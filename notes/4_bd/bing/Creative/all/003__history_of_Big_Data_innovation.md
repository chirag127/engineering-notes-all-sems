### History of Big Data Innovation

- The term Big Data was coined by Roger Mougalas from OReilly Media in 2005, only a year after they created the term Web 2.0. It refers to a large set of data that is almost impossible to manage and process using traditional business intelligence tools.
- However, the origins of large data sets go back to the 1960s and ‘70s when the first data centers and the development of the relational database were established.
- Some of the milestones that led to today's big data revolution are:
  - 1663: John Graunt introduces statistical data analysis with the bubonic plague. He published the first collection of public health records by recording death rates and their variations during the bubonic plague in England.
  - 1865: Richard Millar Devens coins the term "business intelligence" in his "Cyclopædia of Commercial and Business Anecdotes". He described how a banker used information from his environment to turn a profit.
  - 1884: Herman Hollerith invents the punch card tabulating machine, marking the beginning of data processing. He founded the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company, which would eventually become IBM.
  - 1926: Nikola Tesla predicts humans will one day have access to large swaths of data via an instrument that can be carried "in [one's] vest pocket". He foresaw the modern affinity for smartphones and other handheld devices based on his understanding of how wireless technology would change the world.
  - 1928: Fritz Pfleumer invents a way to store information on tape. His process for putting metal stripes on magnetic papers eventually led him to create magnetic tape, which formed the foundation for video cassettes, movie reels and more.
  - 1943: The U.K. created a theoretical computer and one of the first data processing machines to decipher Nazi codes during WWII. The machine, called Colossus, was able to process 5,000 characters per second.
  - 1956: The term "artificial intelligence" is coined by John McCarthy at a conference at Dartmouth College. He defined it as "the science and engineering of making intelligent machines".
  - 1970: Edgar F. Codd proposes the relational model for database management, which revolutionizes data storage and retrieval. He introduced the concept of tables, rows, columns, keys, and joins, which are still widely used today.
  - 1976: Bill Gates and Paul Allen found Microsoft, which would become one of the leading companies in developing software for personal computers and data processing.
  - 1979: Oracle releases the first commercial relational database management system (RDBMS), based on Codd's model. Oracle would later become one of the dominant players in the database market.
  - 1989: Tim Berners-Lee invents the World Wide Web, which enables the sharing and linking of information across the internet. He also created the first web browser, web server, and web page.
  - 1991: The term "data warehouse" is coined by Bill Inmon, who defined it as "a subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant and non-volatile collection of data in support of management's decision making process".
  - 1995: Yahoo! is founded by Jerry Yang and David Filo, who created a web directory to organize and navigate the growing number of websites. Yahoo! would later become one of the most popular search engines and web portals.
  - 1996: Sergey Brin and Larry Page develop the PageRank algorithm, which ranks web pages based on their relevance and popularity. They later founded Google, which would become the most dominant search engine and a leader in big data analytics.
  - 1997: Michael Lesk publishes "How Much Information is There in the World?", one of the first attempts to estimate the amount and growth of data in the world. He estimated that the world's total data was about 12 exabytes (12 billion gigabytes).
  - 1999: The term "big data" is used for the first time in a paper by NASA researchers, who described the challenges of processing large amounts of data generated by scientific experiments. They wrote: "In this paper we describe some of the challenges and opportunities in dealing with big data sets in the Earth and space sciences."
