#### Signal transmission and encoding in Computer Networks

- Signal transmission is the process of sending and receiving data over a physical medium, such as a wire, a fiber optic cable, or a wireless channel.
- Encoding is the process of converting data into a format that can be transmitted as a signal, such as a voltage level, a light pulse, or a radio wave.
- Encoding can be classified into two types: analog-to-digital encoding and digital-to-digital encoding.
- Analog-to-digital encoding is the process of converting an analog signal, such as a voice or a video, into a digital signal, such as a sequence of bits. This involves three steps: sampling, quantization, and encoding.
  - Sampling is the process of measuring the amplitude of the analog signal at regular intervals, called the sampling rate. The sampling rate should be at least twice the highest frequency of the analog signal, according to the Nyquist theorem, to avoid aliasing.
  - Quantization is the process of assigning a discrete value, called a quantization level, to each sample. The quantization level can be represented by a fixed number of bits, called the bit depth. The bit depth determines the resolution and the dynamic range of the digital signal. The quantization error is the difference between the actual sample value and the quantization level.
  - Encoding is the process of converting each quantization level into a binary code, such as a pulse code modulation (PCM) code. The encoding scheme can be uniform or non-uniform, depending on whether the quantization levels are equally or unequally spaced. Non-uniform encoding schemes, such as mu-law and A-law, can reduce the quantization error for low-amplitude signals.
- Digital-to-digital encoding is the process of converting a digital signal, such as a sequence of bits, into another digital signal, such as a series of voltage pulses. This is also known as line coding. The purpose of line coding is to facilitate the transmission, detection, and synchronization of the digital signal. Some common line coding schemes are:
  - Unipolar encoding: The digital signal is represented by two voltage levels, one for 0 and one for 1. For example, non-return-to-zero (NRZ) encoding uses a high voltage for 1 and a low voltage for 0. The advantage of unipolar encoding is its simplicity and low power consumption. The disadvantage is its lack of synchronization and its susceptibility to noise and baseline wander.
  - Bipolar encoding: The digital signal is represented by three voltage levels, one for 0 and two for 1. For example, alternate mark inversion (AMI) encoding uses a high voltage for 1, a low voltage for 0, and alternates the polarity of the voltage for consecutive 1s. The advantage of bipolar encoding is its better synchronization and noise immunity. The disadvantage is its higher power consumption and its problem of long sequences of 0s.
  - Manchester encoding: The digital signal is represented by a transition in the voltage level at the middle of each bit period. For example, a high-to-low transition represents 1, and a low-to-high transition represents 0. The advantage of Manchester encoding is its inherent synchronization and its self-clocking property. The disadvantage is its double bandwidth requirement and its complexity.
- Some mnemonics and learning tricks for signal transmission and encoding in computer networks are:
  - To remember the Nyquist theorem, think of "twice as nice" as the sampling rate should be twice the highest frequency of the analog signal.
  - To remember the difference between PCM and AMI, think of "PCM is plain, AMI is alternating" as PCM uses a uniform encoding scheme, while AMI uses a non-uniform encoding scheme that alternates the polarity of the voltage for consecutive 1s.
  - To remember the difference between NRZ and Manchester encoding, think of "NRZ is no return, Manchester is mid transition" as NRZ encoding does not return to the zero voltage level, while Manchester encoding has a transition at the middle of each bit period.
