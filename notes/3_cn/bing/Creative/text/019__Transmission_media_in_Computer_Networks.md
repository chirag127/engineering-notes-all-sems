#### Transmission media in Computer Networks

Transmission media in computer networks are the physical paths or channels that connect the sender and the receiver of data. They can be classified into two broad categories: guided media and unguided media.

- Guided media: These are also known as wired or bounded media. They use physical links or wires to direct and confine the signals being transmitted. They have high speed, security, and reliability, but they are also limited by distance and cost. Some examples of guided media are:

  - Twisted pair cable: This consists of two insulated copper wires twisted around each other to reduce interference and crosstalk. It is the most widely used transmission media for telephone and LAN networks. It can be unshielded (UTP) or shielded (STP) depending on the presence of a protective jacket. UTP is cheaper, easier to install, and has a high-speed capacity, but it is also more susceptible to external interference and attenuation. STP has better performance, eliminates crosstalk, and is faster, but it is also more expensive, difficult to install, and bulky.
  - Coaxial cable: This consists of a central conductor surrounded by an insulating layer, a metallic shield, and an outer plastic covering. It can transmit data in two modes: baseband (dedicated bandwidth) and broadband (shared bandwidth). It is used for cable TV, digital audio, and Ethernet networks. It has high bandwidth, better noise immunity, easy installation, and low cost, but it also has the drawback of single cable failure affecting the entire network.
  - Optical fiber cable: This uses the principle of light refraction to transmit data through a core made of glass or plastic. It has two types: single-mode (one light path) and multi-mode (multiple light paths). It is used for long-distance, high-speed, and secure communication. It has the advantages of very high bandwidth, low attenuation, immunity to electromagnetic interference, and security, but it also has the disadvantages of high cost, fragility, and difficulty of installation and maintenance.

- Unguided media: These are also known as wireless or unbounded media. They use electromagnetic waves to transmit data through the air or space. They have the benefits of mobility, flexibility, and scalability, but they also face the challenges of interference, attenuation, security, and regulation. Some examples of unguided media are:

  - Radio waves: These are electromagnetic waves with frequencies ranging from 3 kHz to 300 GHz. They can travel long distances and penetrate through walls and buildings. They are used for AM and FM radio, TV, cellular phones, and wireless LANs. They have the advantages of low cost, easy installation, and wide coverage, but they also have the disadvantages of low bandwidth, high attenuation, interference, and security issues.
  - Microwaves: These are electromagnetic waves with frequencies ranging from 300 MHz to 300 GHz. They can travel in straight lines and require line-of-sight communication. They are used for satellite communication, radar, and microwave ovens. They have the advantages of high bandwidth, low attenuation, and long-distance transmission, but they also have the disadvantages of high cost, interference, and environmental impact.
  - Infrared waves: These are electromagnetic waves with frequencies ranging from 300 GHz to 400 THz. They can travel in short distances and require line-of-sight communication. They are used for remote controls, wireless keyboards and mice, and wireless headphones. They have the advantages of low cost, easy installation, and security, but they also have the disadvantages of low bandwidth, high attenuation, and interference from other sources of infrared light.
