#### Signal transmission and encoding in Computer Networks

- Signal transmission is the process of sending digital or analog data over a physical medium such as copper wire, optical fiber, or wireless channel.
- Encoding is the process of converting data into a specific format that can be recognized and interpreted by the receiver.
- Encoding can be done for both analog and digital data, depending on the type of signal and the medium used for transmission.
- Encoding techniques can be classified into three categories: analog data to analog signal, digital data to analog signal, and digital data to digital signal.
- Analog data to analog signal encoding involves modulating the amplitude, frequency, or phase of a continuous carrier wave to represent the analog data. Examples of this technique are amplitude modulation (AM), frequency modulation (FM), and phase modulation (PM).
- Digital data to analog signal encoding involves mapping the binary data into discrete levels of amplitude, frequency, or phase of a carrier wave. Examples of this technique are amplitude shift keying (ASK), frequency shift keying (FSK), and phase shift keying (PSK).
- Digital data to digital signal encoding involves mapping the binary data into discrete levels of voltage or current that can be transmitted over a digital medium. Examples of this technique are non-return to zero (NRZ), bi-phase encoding, and block encoding.
- Non-return to zero (NRZ) encoding is a simple technique that uses two voltage levels (positive and negative) to represent binary 1 and 0. NRZ has two variants: NRZ-Level (NRZ-L) and NRZ-Inverted (NRZ-I). In NRZ-L, the voltage level determines the bit value; in NRZ-I, the change or lack of change in the voltage level determines the bit value.
- Bi-phase encoding is a technique that uses transitions in the voltage level to synchronize the sender and receiver clocks. Bi-phase encoding has two variants: Manchester encoding and differential Manchester encoding. In Manchester encoding, a transition from low to high represents a 0, and a transition from high to low represents a 1. In differential Manchester encoding, a transition at the beginning of a bit period represents a 0, and no transition at the beginning of a bit period represents a 1.
- Block encoding is a technique that uses a fixed number of bits to represent a larger number of bits, with some redundancy added for error detection and correction. Block encoding has several variants, such as 4B/5B encoding, 8B/10B encoding, and 64B/66B encoding. In 4B/5B encoding, for example, every 4 bits of data are encoded into 5 bits of signal, with some patterns reserved for control and synchronization purposes.
