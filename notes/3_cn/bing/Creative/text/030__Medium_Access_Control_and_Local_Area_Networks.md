#### Medium Access Control and Local Area Networks

- Medium Access Control (MAC) is a sublayer of the data link layer that regulates the access of multiple devices to a shared medium, such as a wireless channel or a wired network.
- MAC protocols are designed to avoid or resolve collisions, which occur when two or more devices transmit data at the same time on the same medium.
- There are two main approaches to MAC protocols: contention-based and token-passing.
  - Contention-based protocols allow any device to transmit data whenever the medium is idle, but require a mechanism to detect and recover from collisions. Examples of contention-based protocols are Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection (CSMA/CD), used in Ethernet networks, and Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA), used in wireless networks.
  - Token-passing protocols use a special message, called a token, that circulates among the devices and grants the right to transmit data. Only the device that holds the token can transmit data, and it must release the token after a certain time or amount of data. Examples of token-passing protocols are Token Ring and Token Bus.
- Local Area Networks (LANs) are networks that connect devices within a limited geographic area, such as a building or a campus. LANs typically use MAC protocols to coordinate the access of devices to a common medium, such as a coaxial cable, a twisted pair, a fiber optic cable, or a radio frequency band.
- LANs can be classified into different types based on their topology, which is the physical layout of the devices and the connections among them. Some common LAN topologies are bus, ring, star, and mesh.
  - Bus topology uses a single cable that connects all the devices in a linear fashion. Each device can transmit data on the cable, but only one device can transmit at a time. A device must listen to the cable before transmitting to avoid collisions. An example of a bus LAN is Ethernet.
  - Ring topology uses a closed loop of cable that connects all the devices in a circular fashion. Data is transmitted in one direction along the ring, and each device can receive and forward data to the next device. A device can transmit data only when it receives a token from the previous device. An example of a ring LAN is Token Ring.
  - Star topology uses a central device, called a hub or a switch, that connects all the other devices with individual cables. Data is transmitted from one device to the hub or switch, and then to the destination device. The hub or switch can coordinate the access of devices to the medium and prevent collisions. An example of a star LAN is Ethernet with a switch.
  - Mesh topology uses multiple cables that connect each device to some or all of the other devices. Data can be transmitted along multiple paths from one device to another, which increases the reliability and performance of the network. An example of a mesh LAN is a wireless mesh network.
