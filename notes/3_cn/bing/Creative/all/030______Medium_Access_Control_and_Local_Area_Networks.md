#### Medium Access Control and Local Area Networks

- Medium Access Control (MAC) is a sublayer of the data link layer that defines how multiple devices share the same medium (such as a cable or a wireless channel) in a local area network (LAN).
- MAC protocols are responsible for resolving contention, avoiding collisions, and ensuring fairness among the devices that want to transmit data over the medium.
- There are two main approaches to MAC in LANs: contention-based and token-passing.
  - Contention-based MAC protocols allow any device to transmit data whenever the medium is idle, but they have to deal with the possibility of collisions (when two or more devices transmit at the same time) and retransmissions. An example of a contention-based MAC protocol is Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection (CSMA/CD), which is used in Ethernet networks.
  - Token-passing MAC protocols use a special frame called a token that circulates among the devices in a logical ring or bus topology. Only the device that holds the token can transmit data, and it has to pass the token to the next device after it finishes. This way, collisions are avoided and each device gets a fair chance to transmit. An example of a token-passing MAC protocol is Token Ring, which was developed by IBM.
- MAC protocols can also be classified as deterministic or probabilistic, depending on how they allocate the medium to the devices.
  - Deterministic MAC protocols guarantee that each device will get access to the medium within a bounded time, but they may waste bandwidth if some devices have nothing to transmit. An example of a deterministic MAC protocol is Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA), which divides the medium into fixed time slots and assigns them to the devices in a round-robin fashion.
  - Probabilistic MAC protocols do not guarantee that each device will get access to the medium, but they adapt to the traffic load and use the medium more efficiently. An example of a probabilistic MAC protocol is CSMA/CD, which uses a random backoff algorithm to reduce the probability of collisions.
- MAC protocols can also be designed to support different types of traffic and quality of service (QoS) requirements, such as voice, video, and data. For example, IEEE 802.11 is a standard that defines the MAC and physical layer specifications for wireless LANs (WLANs). It includes several MAC enhancements to support QoS, such as the Enhanced Distributed Channel Access (EDCA) and the Hybrid Coordination Function (HCF) Controlled Channel Access (HCCA).
