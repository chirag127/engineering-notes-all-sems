#### Organization of the Internet

- The Internet is a global network of interconnected networks that supports host-to-host communication through voluntary adherence to open protocols and procedures defined by Internet Standards.
- The Internet is not controlled by any single entity or organization, but rather by a decentralized and distributed system of various actors and stakeholders, such as network operators, service providers, content providers, application developers, users, governments, and civil society.
- The Internet can be organized into four levels, based on the different aspects of control and regulation that affect its functioning:
  - **Internet hardware**: This level includes the physical infrastructure and devices that enable the transmission and reception of data over the Internet, such as cables, routers, switches, servers, computers, and mobile phones. The availability and accessibility of Internet hardware depends on various factors, such as geography, economy, politics, and technology. For example, some countries may have limited or no access to submarine cables that carry Internet traffic across continents, or may face challenges in deploying broadband networks in rural areas. Some governments may also attempt to restrict or disrupt Internet access by cutting off or tampering with Internet hardware, especially during times of political unrest or social movements.
  - **Internet access and Internet service providers (ISPs)**: This level includes the companies and organizations that provide services for users to connect to the Internet, such as dial-up, broadband, wireless, satellite, or mobile. ISPs are responsible for managing the traffic and bandwidth of their networks, as well as providing various features and functionalities, such as email, web hosting, domain name registration, and security. ISPs may also have the ability to monitor, filter, block, or prioritize certain types of content or applications on their networks, depending on their policies, agreements, or regulations. For example, some ISPs may offer faster or cheaper access to certain websites or services, or may censor or throttle access to others, based on their own interests or external pressures. This may affect the quality, diversity, and neutrality of the Internet experience for users.
  - **Navigation within the Internet**: This level includes the systems and mechanisms that enable users to find and access information and resources on the Internet, such as domain names, IP addresses, search engines, web browsers, and hyperlinks. These systems and mechanisms are governed by various technical standards and protocols, as well as by various organizations and entities that manage and coordinate them, such as the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN), the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), and the Internet Society (ISOC). These systems and mechanisms may also influence the visibility, accessibility, and ranking of different types of content or applications on the Internet, depending on their design, implementation, or usage. For example, some search engines may use algorithms or criteria that favor or exclude certain websites or sources, or may personalize or customize the results based on the user's preferences or behavior. Some web browsers may also have built-in features or extensions that enhance or limit the functionality or security of certain websites or services.
  - **The use of the Internet through forums and communities**: This level includes the individual sites and communities that users create, join, or participate in on the Internet, such as blogs, social media platforms, online forums, wikis, chat rooms, and online games. These sites and communities may have their own rules and terms of usage, as well as their own cultures and norms, that shape the behavior and interaction of their members and visitors. These rules and terms of usage may also affect the content and quality of the information and resources that are shared and exchanged within these sites and communities, as well as the rights and responsibilities of the users and the site owners or administrators. For example, some sites and communities may have strict moderation or verification policies that prevent or remove spam, misinformation, or abusive content, or may have reputation or reward systems that encourage or discourage certain types of contributions or feedback. Some sites and communities may also have privacy or security settings that protect or expose the identity or data of the users or the site itself.
