### Congestion control algorithms in computer networks

Congestion control algorithms are mechanisms that regulate the flow of data packets in a network, to prevent congestion and improve performance. Congestion occurs when the demand for network resources exceeds the available capacity, resulting in packet loss, delay, and reduced throughput. Congestion control algorithms aim to balance the trade-off between network utilization and fairness among competing users.

There are two main types of congestion control algorithms: **end-to-end** and **network-assisted**. End-to-end algorithms are implemented by the end hosts, such as the sender and receiver of a TCP connection, and rely on feedback signals from the network, such as packet loss and delay, to adjust the sending rate. Network-assisted algorithms are implemented by the network devices, such as routers and switches, and use explicit signals, such as congestion notification or pricing, to regulate the traffic entering the network.

Some of the most common end-to-end congestion control algorithms are:

- **Slow start**: This algorithm is used by TCP to probe the network capacity at the beginning of a connection or after a packet loss. The sender starts with a small congestion window (CWND), which is the number of packets that can be sent without waiting for an acknowledgment, and doubles it every round-trip time (RTT), until it reaches a threshold (ssthresh) or experiences a packet loss. This algorithm allows the sender to quickly ramp up its sending rate until it reaches the network capacity or causes congestion.
- **Additive increase/multiplicative decrease (AIMD)**: This algorithm is used by TCP to adjust the CWND in response to packet loss or acknowledgment. The sender increases the CWND by one packet per RTT, until it experiences a packet loss, which indicates congestion. Then, the sender reduces the CWND by half, to avoid further congestion. This algorithm allows the sender to gradually approach the network capacity and share it fairly with other users.
- **Congestion avoidance**: This algorithm is used by TCP to avoid entering congestion by estimating the available bandwidth in the network. The sender uses a variable called congestion avoidance factor (CAF), which is the fraction of the CWND that can be increased per RTT, and adjusts it based on the feedback from the network. The sender increases the CWND by CAF packets per RTT, until it experiences a packet loss, which indicates congestion. Then, the sender reduces the CWND by half, and sets the CAF to one packet. This algorithm allows the sender to avoid congestion by adapting to the network conditions.
- **Fast retransmit and fast recovery**: These algorithms are used by TCP to recover from packet loss without reducing the CWND too much. The sender uses a variable called duplicate acknowledgment threshold (DUPACK), which is the number of duplicate acknowledgments that indicate a packet loss, and sets it to three. When the sender receives three duplicate acknowledgments, it assumes that the corresponding packet is lost, and retransmits it immediately, without waiting for a timeout. This is called fast retransmit. Then, the sender reduces the CWND by half, but not below the DUPACK value, and sets the ssthresh to the new CWND. This is called fast recovery. These algorithms allow the sender to quickly recover from packet loss and maintain a high sending rate.

- **Random early detection (RED)**: This algorithm is used by routers to detect and prevent congestion by dropping packets probabilistically before the queue overflows. The router maintains an average queue length (AQL), which is the weighted average of the current queue length and the previous AQL, and compares it with two thresholds: minimum threshold (minth) and maximum threshold (maxth). When the AQL is below minth, the router does not drop any packet. When the AQL is between minth and maxth, the router drops a packet with a probability that increases linearly with the AQL. When the AQL is above maxth, the router drops every packet. This algorithm allows the router to signal congestion to the senders and encourage them to reduce their sending rate.
