#### Switching techniques and multiplexing in Computer Networks

Switching techniques are methods of selecting a path for data transmission in a network. Multiplexing techniques are methods of combining multiple signals into a single channel or carrier. Both techniques are used to improve the efficiency and performance of network communication.

There are three main types of switching techniques: circuit switching, message switching, and packet switching.

- Circuit switching: This technique establishes a dedicated path or channel between the sender and receiver for data transmission, and once a dedicated path is established then it does not terminate it until and unless the connection between the two data transmission point terminates. For example, this technique is used in the public telephonic network for audio transmission. Circuit switching has three phases: circuit establishment, data transmission, and circuit termination. Circuit switching can use two methods of multiplexing: frequency division multiplexing (FDM) and time division multiplexing (TDM).
  - Frequency division multiplexing (FDM): This method divides the total bandwidth of a channel into a series of non-overlapping frequency sub-bands, where each sub-band carries a different signal for data transmission. FDM is used for analog signals, such as radio and optical fiber transmission.
  - Time division multiplexing (TDM): This method divides the data transmission into time frames, where each signal is assigned a fixed time slot for data transmission. TDM is used for digital signals, such as computer networks and digital telephony.
- Message switching: This technique does not establish a dedicated path or channel between the sender and receiver, but instead, it stores and forwards the entire message as a unit from one node to another until it reaches the destination. For example, this technique is used in email and text messaging. Message switching has two advantages: it can handle variable-length messages and it can avoid congestion by buffering the messages. However, it also has some disadvantages: it requires more storage space and it introduces more delay and overhead.
- Packet switching: This technique is similar to message switching, but instead of storing and forwarding the entire message, it divides the message into smaller units called packets, which are then transmitted independently across the network. For example, this technique is used in the internet and most computer networks. Packet switching has some advantages over message switching: it can handle dynamic routing, it can reduce delay and overhead, and it can support error detection and correction. However, it also has some disadvantages: it can cause packet loss, duplication, and reordering, and it can introduce variable delay and jitter. Packet switching can use two methods of multiplexing: frequency division multiplexing (FDM) and statistical multiplexing.
  - Frequency division multiplexing (FDM): This method is the same as the one used in circuit switching, but it is applied to packet switching. FDM is used for analog signals, such as radio and optical fiber transmission.
  - Statistical multiplexing: This method is different from the one used in circuit switching, as it does not assign fixed frequency sub-bands or time slots to each signal, but instead, it dynamically allocates the channel bandwidth according to the demand and availability of the signals. Statistical multiplexing is used for digital signals, such as computer networks and internet. Statistical multiplexing can increase the efficiency of network, as it can maximize the utilization of bandwidth. However, it also requires more complex algorithms and protocols to manage the channel allocation and synchronization.
