### Cryptography in application layer

- Cryptography in application layer refers to the use of cryptographic techniques and protocols to provide security and privacy for applications that run on top of the network layer, such as web browsers, email clients, instant messaging, etc.
- Cryptography in application layer can be divided into two categories: end-to-end encryption and link encryption.
  - End-to-end encryption means that the data is encrypted and decrypted only by the sender and the receiver of the communication, and not by any intermediate nodes or servers. This ensures that the data is protected from eavesdropping, tampering, or modification by third parties. Examples of end-to-end encryption protocols are PGP (Pretty Good Privacy) for email, Signal for instant messaging, and HTTPS (Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure) for web browsing.
  - Link encryption means that the data is encrypted and decrypted at each hop of the communication, by the nodes or servers that relay the data. This ensures that the data is protected from unauthorized access or interception by the nodes or servers themselves, but not from the endpoints of the communication. Examples of link encryption protocols are SSL (Secure Sockets Layer) and TLS (Transport Layer Security) for web browsing, SMTPS (Simple Mail Transfer Protocol Secure) and IMAPS (Internet Message Access Protocol Secure) for email, and IPSec (Internet Protocol Security) for network layer security.
- Cryptography in application layer can provide various security services, such as confidentiality, integrity, authentication, non-repudiation, and access control.
  - Confidentiality means that the data is hidden from unauthorized parties, and can only be read by the intended recipients. Encryption algorithms, such as AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) and RSA (Rivest-Shamir-Adleman), are used to transform the data into an unreadable form, and decryption algorithms are used to reverse the process.
  - Integrity means that the data is protected from unauthorized modification, deletion, or insertion, and can be verified by the recipients. Hash functions, such as SHA (Secure Hash Algorithm) and MD5 (Message Digest 5), are used to generate a unique fingerprint of the data, and digital signatures, such as DSA (Digital Signature Algorithm) and ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm), are used to sign the data with the sender's private key and verify it with the sender's public key.
  - Authentication means that the identity of the sender and the receiver of the data can be confirmed and verified. Symmetric key algorithms, such as DES (Data Encryption Standard) and 3DES (Triple Data Encryption Standard), are used to generate and share a secret key between the sender and the receiver, and asymmetric key algorithms, such as RSA and ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography), are used to generate and exchange public and private keys between the sender and the receiver.
  - Non-repudiation means that the sender and the receiver of the data cannot deny their involvement in the communication. Digital signatures, as mentioned above, are used to provide proof of origin and proof of delivery of the data, and timestamps, such as TSP (Time Stamp Protocol), are used to provide proof of existence and proof of order of the data.
  - Access control means that the data can only be accessed by authorized parties, and can be restricted or revoked by the owner of the data. Encryption algorithms, as mentioned above, are used to prevent unauthorized access to the data, and access control mechanisms, such as ACL (Access Control List) and RBAC (Role-Based Access Control), are used to grant or deny access to the data based on the identity, role, or attributes of the parties.
