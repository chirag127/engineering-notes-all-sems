#### Channel allocation in medium access control

- Channel allocation is the process of assigning channels (frequency bands, time slots, code sequences, etc.) to different users or applications that share a common communication medium.
- Medium access control (MAC) is the sublayer of the data link layer that coordinates the access to the shared medium by multiple users or devices, while ensuring good performance, fairness, low latency and energy efficiency.
- Channel allocation and medium access control are closely related, as the MAC protocol may depend on the channel allocation scheme, and vice versa.
- There are three main types of channel allocation schemes: fixed, dynamic and hybrid.
  - Fixed channel allocation (FCA) assigns a fixed number of channels to each user or application, regardless of the traffic demand or channel conditions. FCA is simple and avoids collisions, but may result in low channel utilization and poor adaptability to traffic variations.
  - Dynamic channel allocation (DCA) assigns channels to users or applications on demand, based on the traffic load, channel quality, priority, etc. DCA is more flexible and efficient, but may incur higher overhead and complexity, and may suffer from collisions or interference.
  - Hybrid channel allocation (HCA) combines the advantages of FCA and DCA, by dividing the channels into two sets: one for fixed allocation and one for dynamic allocation. HCA can achieve a balance between simplicity and adaptability, but may require coordination and synchronization between the two sets of channels.
- There are different types of MAC protocols that can be used for channel allocation, depending on the characteristics of the medium, the network topology, the traffic pattern, the quality of service requirements, etc. Some examples are:
  - Contention-based MAC protocols, such as ALOHA, CSMA, CSMA/CA, CSMA/CD, etc., where users or devices compete for the channel access, and may experience collisions or backoffs. These protocols are suitable for random and bursty traffic, but may have low throughput and high delay under high load or noisy conditions.
  - Reservation-based MAC protocols, such as TDMA, FDMA, CDMA, OFDMA, etc., where users or devices reserve the channel access in advance, and may use a central controller or a distributed algorithm to coordinate the reservations. These protocols are suitable for periodic and predictable traffic, but may have high overhead and low flexibility under dynamic or heterogeneous conditions.
  - Hybrid MAC protocols, such as PRMA, DAMA, MACA, etc., where users or devices use a combination of contention and reservation to access the channel, and may switch between different modes depending on the situation. These protocols are suitable for mixed and variable traffic, but may have high complexity and trade-offs between performance and overhead.
