#### Cohesion Measures in Software Design

- Cohesion is a measure of how strongly related and focused the various responsibilities of a software module are.
- A software module can be a class, a package, a component, a subsystem or a system.
- A software module has high cohesion if all its elements (such as instructions, data, functions, etc.) are closely related to each other and to the main purpose of the module.
- A software module has low cohesion if its elements are loosely related or unrelated to each other or to the main purpose of the module.
- High cohesion is desirable in software design because it is associated with several benefits, such as :
  - Robustness: High cohesion reduces the chances of errors and bugs in the module, as the elements are well-defined and consistent.
  - Reliability: High cohesion increases the confidence in the correctness and functionality of the module, as the elements are well-tested and verified.
  - Reusability: High cohesion makes the module more suitable for reuse in different contexts, as the elements are self-contained and independent.
  - Understandability: High cohesion makes the module easier to understand and maintain, as the elements are clear and coherent.
- Low cohesion is undesirable in software design because it is associated with several drawbacks, such as :
  - Complexity: Low cohesion increases the complexity and size of the module, as the elements are diverse and scattered.
  - Coupling: Low cohesion increases the dependency and interaction between the module and other modules, as the elements are not isolated and encapsulated.
  - Modifiability: Low cohesion makes the module harder to modify and extend, as the elements are interdependent and inconsistent.
  - Testability: Low cohesion makes the module harder to test and debug, as the elements are ambiguous and unpredictable.
- Cohesion is an ordinal type of measurement and is usually described as “high cohesion” or “low cohesion”. However, there are also different levels or types of cohesion that can be used to classify and compare software modules, such as:
  - Functional cohesion: The highest level of cohesion, where the module performs a single and specific function.
  - Sequential cohesion: The second highest level of cohesion, where the module performs a series of related actions that follow a logical sequence.
  - Communicational cohesion: The third highest level of cohesion, where the module performs a set of related actions that operate on the same data or input/output.
  - Procedural cohesion: The fourth highest level of cohesion, where the module performs a set of related actions that are grouped together by control flow or procedure.
  - Temporal cohesion: The fifth highest level of cohesion, where the module performs a set of related actions that are grouped together by time or event.
  - Logical cohesion: The sixth highest level of cohesion, where the module performs a set of related actions that are grouped together by logic or condition.
  - Coincidental cohesion: The lowest level of cohesion, where the module performs a set of unrelated or arbitrary actions that are grouped together by coincidence or convenience.
- Cohesion can be measured using different metrics or techniques, such as:
  - Slice-based cohesion: A metric that measures the cohesion of a module based on the number and size of its executable slices, which are subsets of statements that affect or are affected by a given variable.
  - Information-flow-based cohesion: A metric that measures the cohesion of a module based on the amount and direction of information flow between its elements, such as data, control, and invocation.
  - Semantic cohesion: A metric that measures the cohesion of a module based on the similarity of meaning or purpose of its elements, such as keywords, identifiers, and comments.
  - Structural cohesion: A metric that measures the cohesion of a module based on the structure and organization of its elements, such as hierarchy, modularity, and encapsulation.
