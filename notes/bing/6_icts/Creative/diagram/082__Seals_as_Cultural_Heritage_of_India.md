Seals are small objects that have been used for various purposes in different cultures and periods of history. In India, seals have been found in abundance in the sites of the Indus Valley Civilization, which flourished between 2500 and 1900 BCE. These seals are made of steatite, a soft stone that can be easily carved, and have various designs and inscriptions on them. The seals are usually square or rectangular in shape, and measure about 2-3 cm on each side. Some of the seals have a knob or a boss on the back, which may have been used to hold them or attach them to a cord.

The seals of the Indus Valley Civilization are considered as cultural heritage of India because they provide valuable information about the society, economy, religion, and art of this ancient civilization. The seals depict various animals, such as bulls, elephants, rhinoceroses, tigers, buffaloes, and unicorns, as well as human figures, trees, and geometric patterns. Some of the seals also have a script, which has not been deciphered yet, but may represent the names of individuals, places, or gods. The seals were used for stamping clay tablets, cloth, or other materials, and may have served as identity cards, trade marks, or tokens of authority. The seals also show the contacts and exchanges that the Indus people had with other regions, such as Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf, where similar seals have been found.
