Purusārtha is a key concept in Hinduism that refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. These are Dharma (righteousness, moral values), Artha (prosperity, economic values), Kama (pleasure, love, psychological values) and Moksha (liberation, spiritual values).

In ancient India, the concept of Purusārtha was used to guide the social ethics and individual conduct of different classes and stages of life. The four goals were not mutually exclusive, but rather interdependent and complementary. However, different goals were given different priorities depending on the varna (social class) and ashrama (stage of life) of a person. +------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
```
|                  |                  |                  |                  |
|      Dharma      |      Artha       |       Kama       |      Moksha      |

+------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
|                  |                  |                  |                  |
|     Brahmins     |     Kshatriyas   |     Vaishyas     |     Shudras      |
|                  |                  |                  |                  |
+------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
|                  |                  |                  |                  |
|    Brahmacharya  |    Grihastha     |    Vanaprastha   |    Sannyasa      |
|                  |                  |                  |                  |
+------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
```

The diagram shows the four goals of Purusārtha along the top row, and the four varnas and the four ashramas along the left and right columns, respectively. The diagram also shows the relative importance of each goal for each class and stage of life, indicated by the size of the cell. For example, Dharma was the most important goal for Brahmins (priests and teachers), who were expected to uphold the moral and religious laws of the society. Artha was the most important goal for Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), who were responsible for protecting and governing the society. Kama was the most important goal for Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), who were engaged in economic and productive activities. Moksha was the most important goal for Shudras (servants and laborers), who were considered to be the lowest in the social hierarchy and had limited rights and opportunities. Similarly, Brahmacharya (student stage) was the stage of life where Dharma was the primary focus, Grihastha (householder stage) was the stage where Artha and Kama were the main pursuits, Vanaprastha (retired stage) was the stage where Moksha became more important, and Sannyasa (renunciant stage) was the stage where Moksha was the sole aim. The diagram also shows that the goals of Purusārtha were not fixed or rigid, but rather flexible and adaptable to the changing circumstances and needs of a person. For example, a person could pursue Moksha at any stage of life, or a person could switch from one varna to another based on their qualifications and actions. The concept of Purusārtha was thus a dynamic and holistic framework for leading a balanced and meaningful life in ancient India.
