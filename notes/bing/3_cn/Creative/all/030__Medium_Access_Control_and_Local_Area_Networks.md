#### Medium Access Control and Local Area Networks

- Medium Access Control (MAC) is a sublayer of the data link layer that defines how multiple devices share the same medium (such as a cable or a wireless channel) to communicate in a local area network (LAN).
- MAC protocols are responsible for addressing, framing, error control, flow control, and channel access in LANs.
- There are two main approaches to MAC protocols in LANs: contention-based and token-passing.
  - Contention-based protocols allow any device to transmit whenever the channel is idle, but they have to deal with collisions (when two or more devices transmit at the same time) and retransmissions. Examples of contention-based protocols are Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection (CSMA/CD), used in Ethernet, and Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA), used in wireless LANs.
  - Token-passing protocols use a special frame called a token that circulates among the devices in a logical ring or bus topology. Only the device that holds the token can transmit, and it has to pass the token to the next device after transmission. This avoids collisions, but introduces some delay and overhead. Examples of token-passing protocols are Token Ring and Fiber Distributed Data Interface (FDDI).
- MAC addresses are unique identifiers assigned to each device in a LAN. They are usually 48 bits long and written in hexadecimal notation, such as 00:0A:95:9D:68:16. MAC addresses are used to deliver frames to the correct destination in a LAN, and they are also used for filtering and security purposes.
- MAC protocols can also support quality of service (QoS) requirements for different types of applications, such as voice, video, and data. QoS refers to the ability of a network to provide different levels of performance guarantees, such as bandwidth, delay, jitter, and packet loss. Some MAC protocols that support QoS are IEEE 802.11e for wireless LANs, IEEE 802.1Q for VLANs, and IEEE 802.1p for priority tagging.

Some mnemonics and learning tricks for MAC and LANs are:

- MAC is the acronym for Medium Access Control, and it also sounds like "make", which reminds us that MAC protocols make the communication possible in a LAN.
- CSMA/CD and CSMA/CA are the two most common contention-based protocols, and they can be remembered by the words "collision detection" and "collision avoidance". CD means that the devices detect collisions and back off for a random time before retransmitting. CA means that the devices avoid collisions by using a handshake mechanism before transmitting.
- Token-passing protocols use a token to control the access to the channel, and they can be remembered by the word "token". A token is a small piece of data that represents the right to transmit, and it is passed from one device to another in a logical ring or bus.
- MAC addresses are unique identifiers for each device in a LAN, and they can be remembered by the word "address". An address is a way of locating someone or something, and MAC addresses are used to locate the destination device in a LAN.
