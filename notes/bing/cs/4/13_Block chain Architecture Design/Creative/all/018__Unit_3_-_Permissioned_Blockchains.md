## Unit 3 - Permissioned Blockchains

- Permissioned blockchains are a type of blockchain network that restricts who can participate in the consensus process, read the data, or write new transactions.
- Permissioned blockchains are also known as private or consortium blockchains, depending on the degree of centralization and control.
- Permissioned blockchains are suitable for use cases that require compliance, governance, privacy, or scalability, such as enterprise applications, regulated industries, or cross-organizational collaboration.
- Some of the characteristics of permissioned blockchains are:

  - They have a predefined set of validators or nodes that are authorized to validate transactions and create new blocks. These validators may be selected by a central authority, a consortium of stakeholders, or a voting mechanism.
  - They have a permission layer that defines who can access the network, read the data, or submit transactions. This layer may use cryptographic techniques such as digital signatures, encryption, or zero-knowledge proofs to ensure data confidentiality and integrity.
  - They may use different consensus algorithms than public blockchains, such as Byzantine fault tolerance, proof of authority, or proof of stake, to achieve faster and more efficient consensus among the validators.
  - They may have different data structures than public blockchains, such as directed acyclic graphs, hash trees, or sharding, to improve scalability and performance.
  - They may have different smart contract platforms than public blockchains, such as Hyperledger Fabric, Corda, or Quorum, to enable more complex and customized business logic and interoperability.

- Some of the advantages of permissioned blockchains are:

  - They can provide higher transaction throughput and lower latency than public blockchains, as they do not need to broadcast every transaction to the entire network and reach consensus among a large number of nodes.
  - They can offer more privacy and security than public blockchains, as they can limit who can see or modify the data and use encryption or zero-knowledge proofs to protect sensitive information.
  - They can enable more compliance and governance than public blockchains, as they can enforce rules and policies on the network participants and transactions, and audit the network activity and performance.
  - They can foster more collaboration and innovation than public blockchains, as they can allow multiple parties to share data and execute transactions in a trusted and transparent manner, and integrate with existing systems and standards.

- Some of the disadvantages of permissioned blockchains are:

  - They can suffer from more centralization and control than public blockchains, as they depend on a limited number of validators or authorities that may have more power or influence over the network.
  - They can face more legal and regulatory challenges than public blockchains, as they may have to comply with different laws and regulations in different jurisdictions and sectors, and deal with potential disputes and liabilities among the network participants.
  - They can encounter more technical and operational issues than public blockchains, as they may have to manage the network infrastructure and maintenance, ensure the network availability and reliability, and cope with potential bugs and attacks.

- Some of the examples of permissioned blockchains are:

  - Hyperledger Fabric: A modular and flexible blockchain platform that supports pluggable consensus algorithms, smart contract languages, and data formats, and enables permissioned and private transactions among the network participants.
  - Corda: A distributed ledger platform that focuses on enabling interoperability and scalability among business networks, and supports smart contracts, asset issuance and transfer, and data privacy and confidentiality.
  - Quorum: A fork of Ethereum that adds features such as private transactions, permissioned network management, and enterprise-grade performance and security, and supports Ethereum smart contracts and tools.
