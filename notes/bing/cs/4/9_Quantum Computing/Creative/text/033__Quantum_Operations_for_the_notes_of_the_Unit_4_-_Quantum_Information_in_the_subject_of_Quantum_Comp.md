### Quantum Operations

- Quantum operations are mathematical transformations that describe how a quantum system can evolve or change over time. They are also used to manipulate quantum bits (qubits) in a quantum circuit.  
- Quantum operations are formulated in terms of the density operator, which is a matrix that represents the state of a quantum system. A density operator can be written as a weighted sum of pure states, which are vectors that describe the possible outcomes of a quantum measurement. 
- A quantum operation is a linear, completely positive map from the set of density operators into itself. This means that a quantum operation preserves the properties of being a density operator, such as being positive, trace one, and Hermitian. 
- A quantum operation can be represented by a unitary matrix, which is a matrix that preserves the length and angle of vectors. A unitary matrix can be decomposed into a product of quantum gates, which are elementary quantum operations that act on one or more qubits.  
- Quantum gates can be classified into single-qubit gates and multi-qubit gates. Single-qubit gates act on one qubit and can be further divided into Clifford gates and non-Clifford gates. Clifford gates include the Pauli gates, the Hadamard gate, and the phase gate. Non-Clifford gates include the T gate, which is also known as the pi/8 gate. Multi-qubit gates act on two or more qubits and can implement entanglement, which is a quantum phenomenon that creates correlations between qubits. Examples of multi-qubit gates are the CNOT gate, the SWAP gate, and the Toffoli gate.  
- Quantum operations can also include measurements, which are operations that collapse the quantum state into one of the possible outcomes and return a classical bit of information. Measurements can be performed on one or more qubits and can be either projective or non-projective. Projective measurements are irreversible and destroy the quantum state, while non-projective measurements are reversible and preserve the quantum state. 
- Quantum operations can also include conditional operations, which are operations that depend on the outcome of a previous measurement or a classical input. Conditional operations can be used to implement classical logic and control flow in a quantum circuit. An example of a conditional operation is the IF operation, which applies a quantum gate only if a classical bit is true.
