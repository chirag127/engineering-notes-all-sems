### Measurement for the notes of the Unit 2 - Quantum Computation in the subject of Quantum Computing

- Measurement is a fundamental operation in quantum computation, where the state of a quantum system is observed and a classical outcome is obtained.
- Measurement can be used to perform quantum logic operations, manipulate entanglement, and extract information from quantum states.
- Measurement can also be used as the basis of a quantum computation model, where the computation is driven by local measurements on a highly entangled state of qubits, such as a cluster state or a graph state.
- This model is called measurement-based quantum computation (MBQC) or one-way quantum computation, and it has some advantages over the standard circuit model, such as fault-tolerance, parallelism, and adaptivity.
- The basic steps of MBQC are:
  - Prepare a source state of qubits, which is usually a cluster state or a graph state, where each qubit is connected to its neighbors by a controlled-Z (CZ) gate.
  - Measure some of the qubits in a specific order and basis, which are determined by the desired computation. The measurement outcomes are used to adjust the basis of the subsequent measurements.
  - Correct the final outcomes by applying classical operations, such as XOR or Z-flip, based on the measurement outcomes.
- The measurement basis can be either the computational basis (Z-basis) or the Hadamard basis (X-basis), or a combination of both. The choice of the basis affects the type and the direction of the quantum operation that is implemented by the measurement.
- For example, measuring a qubit in the Z-basis removes it from the cluster state and applies a Z-rotation to its neighbors, while measuring it in the X-basis removes it from the cluster state and applies an X-rotation to its neighbors. The angle of the rotation depends on the measurement outcome, which can be either 0 or 1.
- The measurement order is important, as it determines the causal structure of the computation. The qubits that are measured earlier can affect the qubits that are measured later, but not vice versa. The qubits that are measured simultaneously can be independent or correlated, depending on the entanglement of the source state.
- The measurement outcomes are random, but they can be used to control the computation adaptively. For example, the measurement basis of a qubit can depend on the previous outcomes, or the computation can branch or terminate based on the outcomes.
- The measurement outcomes also introduce errors in the computation, which can be corrected by applying classical operations to the final outcomes. The correction operations depend on the measurement outcomes and the structure of the source state.
- The source state can be any entangled state that allows universal quantum computation, but some states are more efficient or robust than others. The most common choice is the cluster state, which is a two-dimensional lattice of qubits, where each qubit is connected to its four nearest neighbors by a CZ gate. The cluster state can implement any quantum circuit by choosing the appropriate measurement basis and order.
- Another common choice is the graph state, which is a generalization of the cluster state, where the qubits are arranged in an arbitrary graph, and each qubit is connected to its neighbors by a CZ gate. The graph state can implement any quantum circuit by choosing the appropriate measurement basis and order, but it can also implement some quantum operations more directly or efficiently than the cluster state, such as the Toffoli gate or the quantum Fourier transform.
- The advantages of MBQC over the circuit model include:
  - Fault-tolerance: The source state can be prepared in advance and protected by error correction techniques, such as surface codes or topological codes. The measurement errors can be corrected by classical operations, which are easier and cheaper than quantum operations. The measurement outcomes can also be verified by classical communication, which can detect and correct any malicious or erroneous measurements.
  - Parallelism: The measurement operations can be performed in parallel, as long as they do not affect each other causally. The parallelism can speed up the computation and reduce the decoherence effects.
