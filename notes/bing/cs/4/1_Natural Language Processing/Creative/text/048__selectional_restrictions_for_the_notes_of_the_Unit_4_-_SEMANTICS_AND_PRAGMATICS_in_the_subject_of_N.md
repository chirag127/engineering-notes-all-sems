### Selectional restrictions

- Selectional restrictions are constraints on the possible combinations of words in a phrase or sentence, based on their semantic properties or categories.
- Selectional restrictions are a type of semantic rule that can be used to test the acceptability or grammaticality of a sentence.
- Selectional restrictions can be violated by using words that do not belong to the expected semantic categories, resulting in semantic anomalies or oddities.
- For example, the verb "devour" has a selectional restriction that its subject should be an animate entity and its object should be an edible entity. Therefore, the sentence "The lion devoured the antelope" is acceptable, but the sentence "The antelope devoured the lion" or "The lion devoured the rock" is not.
- Selectional restrictions can be expressed using semantic features or predicates that describe the properties or relations of words. For example, the verb "devour" can be represented as:

```
devour(x, y) -> ANIMATE(x) & EDIBLE(y)
```

- This means that the verb "devour" takes two arguments, x and y, and that x must be an animate entity and y must be an edible entity.
- Selectional restrictions can also be violated for rhetorical or humorous effects, such as in metaphors, hyperboles, or jokes. For example, the sentence "The book devoured him" is a metaphor that implies that the book was very engaging or captivating for the reader.
