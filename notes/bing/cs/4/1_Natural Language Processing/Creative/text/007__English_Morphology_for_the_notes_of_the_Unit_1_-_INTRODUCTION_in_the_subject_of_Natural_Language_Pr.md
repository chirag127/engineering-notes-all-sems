### English Morphology

- Morphology is the study of the internal structure and formation of words.
- Words are the smallest units of meaning that can stand alone in a language.
- Words can be composed of smaller units called morphemes, which are the smallest meaningful units in a language.
- Morphemes can be classified into two types: roots and affixes.
- Roots are the core of a word, carrying the main meaning and lexical category (noun, verb, adjective, etc.).
- Affixes are added to roots to modify their meaning or function, or to create new words. Affixes can be further classified into prefixes (before the root), suffixes (after the root), and infixes (within the root).
- Some examples of morphemes and words in English are:

| Word | Morphemes | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| unhappy | un- + happy | not happy |
| happiness | happy + -ness | the state of being happy |
| rewrite | re- + write | write again |
| writer | write + -er | someone who writes |
| unthinkable | un- + think + -able | not able to be thought |
| fan-tas-tic | fan + -tas- + -tic | very good |

- Morphology also studies the rules and patterns of how morphemes are combined and arranged to form words, such as:

  - The order of affixes: e.g., un-happy-ness, not *ness-un-happy or *happy-un-ness
  - The compatibility of affixes and roots: e.g., un-happy, not *un-red or *un-write
  - The changes in spelling or pronunciation of morphemes: e.g., electric + -ity -> electricity, not *electricty or *electricity
  - The productivity of affixes: e.g., -ness can be added to many adjectives to form nouns, but -ity can only be added to a few adjectives
  - The semantic relations between words: e.g., happy and unhappy are antonyms, happiness and unhappiness are also antonyms, but happy and happiness are not antonyms

- Morphology is an important component of natural language processing, as it helps to analyze, generate, and understand words and their meanings in a language.
