### Acoustics Of Speech Production

- Acoustics of speech production is the study of how speech sounds are generated and modified by the human vocal tract and how they are perceived by the listeners.
- The acoustic theory of speech production is a model that describes speech as a result of a source of sound energy (e.g. the larynx) modulated by a filter function determined by the shape of the supralaryngeal vocal tract (e.g. the tongue, lips, palate, etc.) .
- The source of sound energy can be either periodic (e.g. for voiced sounds like vowels) or aperiodic (e.g. for voiceless sounds like fricatives). The source produces a complex waveform that contains a fundamental frequency (F0) and its harmonics (multiples of F0).
- The filter function is the frequency response of the vocal tract, which determines how much each frequency component of the source is amplified or attenuated. The filter function depends on the length, shape, and area of the vocal tract, which vary according to the articulatory movements of the speech organs.
- The filter function produces a shaped spectrum with peaks and valleys, called formants, which are the resonant frequencies of the vocal tract. The formants are the main acoustic cues for vowel identification and quality. The first three formants (F1, F2, F3) are usually sufficient to distinguish most vowels.
- The output of the filter function is the acoustic speech signal, which is transmitted through the air and received by the listener's ear. The listener's auditory system analyzes the speech signal and extracts the relevant information for speech perception and recognition.
- The acoustic theory of speech production is based on the assumption that the source and the filter are independent of each other, meaning that the source does not affect the filter and vice versa. This assumption is called the source-filter theory or the linear theory of speech production .
- The acoustic theory of speech production is useful for understanding the acoustic properties of speech sounds, but it has some limitations and simplifications. For example, it does not account for the nonlinear effects of the vocal tract, such as turbulence, friction, and coupling between different cavities. It also does not consider the feedback mechanisms between the auditory and the motor systems, which are important for speech production and learning.
