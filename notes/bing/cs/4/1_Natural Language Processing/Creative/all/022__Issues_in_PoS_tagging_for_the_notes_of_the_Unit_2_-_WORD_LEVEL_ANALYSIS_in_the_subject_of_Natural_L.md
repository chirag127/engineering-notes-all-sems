### Issues in PoS tagging for the notes of the Unit 2 - WORD LEVEL ANALYSIS in the subject of Natural Language Processing

- Part-of-Speech (PoS) tagging is the process of assigning a word or a token a grammatical category based on its definition and context in a sentence .
- PoS tagging is an important step in many natural language processing tasks, such as syntactic parsing, information extraction, sentiment analysis, machine translation, etc.
- PoS tagging is not a trivial task, as it faces many challenges and difficulties, such as   :
  - **Ambiguity**: Many words can have multiple meanings and therefore multiple PoS tags depending on the context. For example, the word "book" can be a noun or a verb, the word "race" can be a noun or a verb, the word "down" can be an adverb, a preposition, or an adjective, etc. A PoS tagger has to resolve this ambiguity accurately based on the context of use.
  - **Unknown words**: A PoS tagger may encounter words that are not in its vocabulary or training data, such as new words, proper nouns, foreign words, typos, etc. A PoS tagger has to assign a reasonable tag to these words based on some heuristics or rules, such as morphology, capitalization, suffixes, etc.
  - **Variation in tag sets**: Different PoS taggers may use different sets of tags to represent the grammatical categories of words. Some tag sets may be coarse-grained and use less than 20 tags, while others may be fine-grained and use more than 400 tags. A PoS tagger has to be consistent with the tag set it uses and be able to map between different tag sets if needed.
  - **Domain adaptation**: A PoS tagger may perform well on one domain or genre of text, but poorly on another. For example, a PoS tagger trained on news articles may not work well on social media posts, as they may have different styles, vocabularies, structures, etc. A PoS tagger has to be able to adapt to different domains and genres of text and learn from new data.
- Some possible solutions or approaches to address these issues are   :
  - **Rule-based PoS taggers**: These taggers use hand-crafted rules or patterns to assign tags to words based on their morphology, context, or other features. For example, a rule-based tagger may assign the tag NN (singular noun) to any word that ends with -tion, or the tag VB (base form verb) to any word that precedes "to". Rule-based taggers are easy to implement and interpret, but they may not cover all the cases and exceptions, and they may be hard to maintain and generalize to new data.
  - **Probabilistic PoS taggers**: These taggers use statistical models to assign tags to words based on their probabilities or likelihoods. For example, a probabilistic tagger may use a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) to compute the probability of a tag given the previous tag and the current word, or a Conditional Random Field (CRF) to compute the probability of a tag given the entire sentence. Probabilistic taggers are more robust and flexible than rule-based taggers, but they may require a large amount of annotated data to train and they may not handle unknown words well.
  - **Neural PoS taggers**: These taggers use neural networks to assign tags to words based on their embeddings or representations. For example, a neural tagger may use a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) or a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to encode the sequential information of a sentence, or a Transformer or a BERT model to encode the contextual information of a word. Neural taggers are more powerful and accurate than probabilistic taggers, but they may require more computational resources and they may not be easily interpretable or explainable.
- A mnemonic to remember the main issues in PoS tagging is **AUV** (Ambiguity, Unknown words, Variation in tag sets). A learning trick to remember the main approaches to PoS tagging is **RPN** (Rule-based, Probabilistic, Neural).
