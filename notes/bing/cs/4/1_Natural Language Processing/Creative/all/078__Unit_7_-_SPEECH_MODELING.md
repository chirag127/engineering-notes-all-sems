## Unit 7 - SPEECH MODELING

Speech modeling is the process of representing speech signals in a mathematical or statistical way. Speech modeling can be used for various purposes, such as speech recognition, speech synthesis, speech enhancement, speech compression, speech coding, speech analysis, and speech understanding.

There are different types of speech models, depending on the level of abstraction and the features of the speech signal that are captured by the model. Some of the common types of speech models are:

- **Waveform models**: These models represent the speech signal as a sequence of samples or coefficients that describe the shape of the waveform. Waveform models can be either parametric or non-parametric. Parametric models use a small number of parameters to describe the waveform, such as linear predictive coding (LPC) or discrete cosine transform (DCT). Non-parametric models use the original samples or a large number of coefficients, such as pulse-code modulation (PCM) or wavelet transform. Waveform models are useful for speech compression and coding, as they can preserve the quality of the speech signal with low bit rates. However, they are not very effective for speech recognition or synthesis, as they do not capture the linguistic or acoustic features of the speech signal.

- **Spectral models**: These models represent the speech signal as a sequence of spectra or frequency-domain features that describe the energy distribution of the signal over different frequency bands. Spectral models can be either static or dynamic. Static models use a single spectrum or a set of spectra to represent a speech segment, such as short-time Fourier transform (STFT) or mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC). Dynamic models use a sequence of spectra or a trajectory of spectral features to represent a speech segment, such as hidden Markov models (HMM) or dynamic time warping (DTW). Spectral models are useful for speech recognition and synthesis, as they can capture the acoustic and phonetic features of the speech signal. However, they are not very effective for speech compression or coding, as they require high bit rates to preserve the quality of the speech signal.

- **Articulatory models**: These models represent the speech signal as a sequence of articulatory gestures or movements of the vocal tract organs that produce the speech sounds. Articulatory models can be either physical or abstract. Physical models use a detailed description of the anatomy and physiology of the vocal tract, such as the area function or the vocal tract transfer function. Abstract models use a simplified representation of the vocal tract, such as the source-filter model or the distinctive feature model. Articulatory models are useful for speech analysis and understanding, as they can capture the linguistic and phonological features of the speech signal. However, they are not very effective for speech recognition or synthesis, as they are difficult to estimate and manipulate.

- **Prosodic models**: These models represent the speech signal as a sequence of prosodic features or variations of the speech parameters over time, such as pitch, intensity, duration, and stress. Prosodic models can be either continuous or discrete. Continuous models use a function or a curve to describe the prosodic contour of a speech segment, such as the fundamental frequency (F0) or the intensity envelope. Discrete models use a set of symbols or labels to describe the prosodic events or patterns of a speech segment, such as the tone or the accent. Prosodic models are useful for speech synthesis and understanding, as they can capture the emotional and pragmatic features of the speech signal. However, they are not very effective for speech recognition or compression, as they are noisy and redundant.

Some of the mnemonics and learning tricks for the unit 7 - speech modeling are:

- To remember the types of speech models, use the acronym WASP: Waveform, Articulatory, Spectral, and Prosodic.
- To remember the difference between parametric and non-parametric models, use the phrase "parameters are parsimonious": parametric models use fewer parameters than non-parametric models.
- To remember the difference between static and dynamic models, use the phrase "static is stable, dynamic is changing": static models use a single or a fixed set of features, while dynamic models use a sequence or a variable set of features.
- To remember the difference between physical and abstract models, use the phrase "physical is realistic, abstract is simplified": physical models use a detailed and accurate description of the speech production, while abstract models use a general and approximate representation of the speech production.
- To remember the difference between continuous and discrete models, use the phrase "continuous is smooth, discrete is sharp": continuous models use a function or a curve to describe the prosodic contour, while discrete models use a set of symbols or labels to describe the prosodic events.
