### Articulatory Phonetics for the notes of the Unit 5 - BASIC CONCEPTS of Speech Processing in the subject of Natural Language Processing

- Articulatory phonetics is the branch of phonetics that studies how humans produce speech sounds by using the organs of speech, such as the tongue, lips, teeth, jaw, palate, etc.   
- Articulatory phonetics is concerned with the transformation of aerodynamic energy (airflow through the vocal tract) into acoustic energy (sound waves)  
- Articulatory phonetics can be divided into two main categories: segmental and suprasegmental. Segmental phonetics deals with the individual speech sounds (phonemes) and their features, such as place, manner and voicing of articulation. Suprasegmental phonetics deals with the features that span over multiple speech sounds, such as stress, intonation, pitch, duration and loudness. 
- Articulatory phonetics is important for speech processing because it provides a way to describe and classify the speech sounds that are used in different languages and dialects. It also helps to understand how speech sounds are affected by coarticulation, assimilation, elision and other phonological processes.  
- Articulatory phonetics is also useful for speech synthesis and recognition, as it can help to model the speech production system and to generate or analyze the acoustic features of speech sounds.  

Some possible mnemonics and learning tricks for articulatory phonetics are:

- To remember the three main places of articulation for consonants, think of the acronym LAB: Labial (lips), Alveolar (alveolar ridge), Bilabial (both lips).
- To remember the three main manners of articulation for consonants, think of the acronym FAS: Fricative (airflow is partially obstructed), Affricate (airflow is completely obstructed and then released), Stop (airflow is completely obstructed).
- To remember the difference between voiced and voiceless consonants, place your hand on your throat and feel the vibration. Voiced consonants vibrate the vocal cords, voiceless consonants do not.
- To remember the difference between high and low vowels, think of the position of your jaw. High vowels are produced with a high jaw position, low vowels are produced with a low jaw position.
- To remember the difference between front and back vowels, think of the position of your tongue. Front vowels are produced with the tongue near the front of the mouth, back vowels are produced with the tongue near the back of the mouth.
