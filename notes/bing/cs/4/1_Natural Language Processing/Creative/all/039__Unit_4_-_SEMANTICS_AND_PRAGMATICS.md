## Unit 4 - SEMANTICS AND PRAGMATICS

Semantics and pragmatics are two branches of linguistics that deal with the meaning of language. Semantics studies the literal meaning of words, phrases, sentences, and texts, while pragmatics studies the meaning in context, taking into account the speaker's intention, the listener's inference, the background knowledge, and the social norms.

Some of the main topics in semantics and pragmatics are:

- **Sense and reference**: Sense is the abstract concept or idea that a word or expression conveys, while reference is the actual entity or object that a word or expression denotes in the real world. For example, the word "dog" has the sense of a four-legged animal that barks, but its reference can vary depending on the context, such as a specific dog, a group of dogs, or a metaphorical dog.

- **Truth conditions**: Truth conditions are the conditions under which a sentence or a proposition is true or false. For example, the sentence "It is raining" is true if and only if it is raining in the actual world. Truth conditions can be used to analyze the logical structure and validity of arguments, as well as the entailment and contradiction relations between sentences.

- **Semantic roles**: Semantic roles are the roles that different participants play in an event or a situation, such as the agent, the patient, the instrument, the location, the source, the goal, etc. For example, in the sentence "John hit the ball with a bat", John is the agent, the ball is the patient, and the bat is the instrument. Semantic roles can help to identify the meaning and the structure of sentences, as well as the relations between them.

- **Speech acts**: Speech acts are the actions that speakers perform by using language, such as asserting, questioning, requesting, promising, apologizing, etc. For example, the sentence "Can you pass me the salt?" is a speech act of requesting, while the sentence "I hereby declare you husband and wife" is a speech act of declaring. Speech acts can be analyzed in terms of their illocutionary force (the intention of the speaker) and their perlocutionary effect (the outcome of the speech act).

- **Implicature**: Implicature is the implied meaning that goes beyond the literal meaning of an utterance, based on the context, the background knowledge, and the cooperative principle. For example, the sentence "Some students passed the exam" implicates that not all students passed the exam, even though it does not explicitly say so. Implicature can be divided into two types: conversational implicature (based on the general rules of conversation) and conventional implicature (based on the specific words or expressions used).

- **Presupposition**: Presupposition is the background assumption that a speaker makes when uttering a sentence, and that the listener accepts as given. For example, the sentence "The king of France is bald" presupposes that there is a king of France, even though it does not assert it. Presupposition can be triggered by various linguistic phenomena, such as definite descriptions, factive verbs, cleft sentences, etc.

Some of the mnemonics and learning tricks for semantics and pragmatics are:

- To remember the difference between sense and reference, think of the acronym SIR: Sense Is Reference-independent.

- To remember the difference between illocutionary and perlocutionary, think of the words illusion and peril: Illocutionary is the intention or the illusion of the speaker, while perlocutionary is the outcome or the peril of the speech act.

- To remember the difference between conversational and conventional implicature, think of the words conversation and convention: Conversational implicature is based on the general rules of conversation, while conventional implicature is based on the specific words or expressions used.
