### Syntax-Driven Semantic analysis for the notes of the Unit 4 - SEMANTICS AND PRAGMATICS in the subject of Natural Language Processing

- Syntax-driven semantic analysis is a method of deriving the meaning of natural language sentences from their syntactic structure, using the rules of a formal grammar.
- The main steps of syntax-driven semantic analysis are:
  - Tokenization: Splitting the input text into words, punctuation marks, and other meaningful units.
  - Morphological analysis: Identifying the root forms and grammatical features of the tokens, such as tense, number, gender, etc.
  - Part-of-speech tagging: Assigning a word class to each token, such as noun, verb, adjective, etc.
  - Syntactic parsing: Building a hierarchical structure that represents the grammatical relations between the tokens, such as subject, object, modifier, etc.
  - Semantic parsing: Mapping the syntactic structure to a logical representation that captures the meaning of the sentence, such as predicate-argument structure, lambda expressions, etc.
- Syntax-driven semantic analysis relies on the assumption that there is a direct correspondence between the syntactic and semantic structures of natural language sentences.
- Syntax-driven semantic analysis can be performed using different types of formal grammars, such as context-free grammars, dependency grammars, lexical-functional grammars, etc.
- Syntax-driven semantic analysis can be used for various natural language processing tasks, such as information extraction, question answering, machine translation, text summarization, etc.
- Syntax-driven semantic analysis has some advantages and disadvantages, such as :
  - Advantages:
    - It can capture the compositional nature of natural language meaning, i.e., how the meaning of a sentence is derived from the meanings of its parts and their syntactic relations.
    - It can handle ambiguity and anaphora resolution, i.e., how to determine the intended meaning of a word or phrase that has multiple possible interpretations or refers to a previous entity in the text.
    - It can leverage existing linguistic resources, such as dictionaries, grammars, and ontologies, to enrich the semantic representation of natural language sentences.
  - Disadvantages:
    - It can be computationally expensive and complex, as it requires parsing large and diverse natural language sentences with various grammatical rules and exceptions.
    - It can be prone to errors and inconsistencies, as it depends on the quality and coverage of the linguistic resources and the formal grammar used for the analysis.
    - It can be limited in capturing the pragmatic aspects of natural language meaning, i.e., how the meaning of a sentence is influenced by the context, the speaker's intention, the listener's background knowledge, etc.
