### Grammar-based LM for the notes of the Unit 1 - INTRODUCTION in the subject of Natural Language Processing

- A grammar-based language model (LM) is a type of probabilistic model that assigns probabilities to sentences or sequences of words based on a set of rules or constraints that define the structure and syntax of a language.
- A grammar-based LM can be formal or informal, depending on the level of detail and rigor of the rules or constraints.
- A formal grammar is a mathematical system that consists of a set of symbols (called terminals), a set of rules (called productions) that specify how symbols can be combined to form strings (called sentences), and a special symbol (called the start symbol) that indicates the beginning of a sentence.
- A formal grammar can be represented by a tuple G = (N, T, P, S), where N is the set of non-terminal symbols, T is the set of terminal symbols, P is the set of productions, and S is the start symbol.
- A formal grammar can generate a language, which is the set of all sentences that can be derived from the start symbol by applying the productions. A language generated by a grammar G is denoted by L(G).
- A formal grammar can also recognize a language, which is the process of determining whether a given sentence belongs to the language generated by the grammar. A grammar G recognizes a sentence w if there exists a sequence of productions that can derive w from the start symbol.
- A formal grammar can be classified into different types or classes based on the form and complexity of the productions. The most common types are regular, context-free, context-sensitive, and unrestricted grammars, which correspond to different levels of the Chomsky hierarchy.
- A regular grammar is the simplest type of formal grammar, where the productions have the form A -> aB or A -> a, where A and B are non-terminals and a is a terminal. A regular grammar can generate a regular language, which is the set of all sentences that can be recognized by a finite state machine (FSM) or a regular expression (RE).
- A context-free grammar (CFG) is a type of formal grammar where the productions have the form A -> alpha, where A is a non-terminal and alpha is a string of terminals and non-terminals. A CFG can generate a context-free language (CFL), which is the set of all sentences that can be recognized by a pushdown automaton (PDA) or a context-free grammar (CFG).
- A context-sensitive grammar (CSG) is a type of formal grammar where the productions have the form alpha A beta -> alpha gamma beta, where A is a non-terminal and alpha, beta, and gamma are strings of terminals and non-terminals. A CSG can generate a context-sensitive language (CSL), which is the set of all sentences that can be recognized by a linear bounded automaton (LBA) or a context-sensitive grammar (CSG).
- An unrestricted grammar (UG) is the most general type of formal grammar, where the productions have no restrictions on the form. A UG can generate a recursively enumerable language (REL), which is the set of all sentences that can be recognized by a Turing machine (TM) or an unrestricted grammar (UG).
- A grammar-based LM can use any type of formal grammar to model the structure and syntax of a natural language, but the most common and practical choice is a CFG, since it can capture many aspects of natural language syntax, such as phrase structure, recursion, and agreement, while being relatively simple and efficient to parse and generate.
- A grammar-based LM can assign probabilities to sentences or sequences of words by using a probabilistic grammar, which is a formal grammar that assigns a probability to each production. The probability of a sentence or a sequence of words is then the product of the probabilities of the productions that derive it from the start symbol.
- A probabilistic grammar can be estimated from a corpus of sentences or sequences of words by using various methods, such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), Bayesian inference, or expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm. The estimation methods can also incorporate smoothing or regularization techniques to deal with data sparsity and overfitting issues.
- A grammar-based LM can be used for various natural language processing (NLP) tasks, such as parsing, generation, translation, summarization, and speech recognition. A grammar-based LM can provide syntactic information and constraints that can improve the quality and diversity of the outputs. A grammar-based LM can also be combined with other types of LMs, such as n-gram or neural LMs, to leverage both syntactic and semantic information.
