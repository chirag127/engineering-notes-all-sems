Grid computing is a type of distributed computing that uses a network of heterogeneous computers to perform large-scale tasks that require high performance, availability, or scalability. Grid computing can be classified into three main types based on the architecture and functionality of the nodes: CPU scavenging, cluster, and service grids.

CPU scavenging grids have many regular computers that are idle or underutilized most of the time. These computers can be used to perform computations in the background when they are not in use by their owners. CPU scavenging grids are also known as desktop grids, volunteer computing, or opportunistic grids. An example of a CPU scavenging grid is the SETI@home project, which uses the idle time of millions of computers to search for extraterrestrial intelligence.

Cluster grids have a group of dedicated computers that are connected by a high-speed network and act as a single system. Cluster grids are also known as parallel computing, high-performance computing, or supercomputing. Cluster grids can be used to perform complex calculations that require a large amount of processing power and memory. An example of a cluster grid is the Titan supercomputer, which has over 18,000 nodes and can perform more than 27 petaflops.

Service grids have a collection of computers that provide different types of services to the users or applications. Service grids are also known as utility computing, cloud computing, or on-demand computing. Service grids can be used to access various resources such as storage, software, data, or hardware over the network. An example of a service grid is the Amazon Web Services, which offers a range of cloud-based services such as computing, storage, database, analytics, and machine learning.
