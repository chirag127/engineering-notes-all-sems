## Unit 1 - Overview of Grid Computing Technology

- Grid computing is a type of distributed computing that uses a network of computers to perform large-scale tasks that require high performance, such as scientific simulations, data analysis, or weather modeling .
- Grid computing differs from cluster computing, which uses a set of identical or similar computers that are tightly coupled and share resources, such as memory, disk, or network. Grid computing uses heterogeneous computers that are loosely coupled and may have different operating systems, architectures, or configurations.
- Grid computing also differs from cloud computing, which provides on-demand access to computing resources via the internet, such as servers, storage, or applications. Grid computing usually involves a specific goal or problem that requires a large amount of computing power, and may use dedicated or volunteered resources that are not necessarily available on demand.
- Grid computing can be seen as a form of parallel computing, which uses multiple processors or computers to execute a program or task simultaneously. However, grid computing faces more challenges in terms of coordination, communication, security, and fault tolerance, due to the diversity and dynamism of the grid environment.
- Grid computing can be classified into different types, such as computational grids, data grids, or service grids, depending on the main focus or functionality of the grid. Computational grids provide access to distributed computing power for CPU-intensive tasks, such as scientific simulations or mathematical problems. Data grids provide access to distributed data sources or storage for data-intensive tasks, such as data mining or analysis. Service grids provide access to distributed services or applications for service-oriented tasks, such as web services or workflows.
- Grid computing can also be classified into different models, such as public grids, private grids, or hybrid grids, depending on the ownership or access of the grid resources. Public grids use resources that are donated or shared by the public, such as the SETI@home or Folding@home projects, which use idle cycles of personal computers to search for extraterrestrial intelligence or study protein folding. Private grids use resources that are owned or controlled by a specific organization or group, such as the TeraGrid or the LHC Computing Grid, which use dedicated supercomputers or clusters to support scientific research or experiments . Hybrid grids use a combination of public and private resources, such as the World Community Grid or the Open Science Grid, which use both volunteered and institutional resources to support humanitarian or scientific projects.
- Grid computing can provide many benefits, such as scalability, efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and collaboration, for various domains and applications that require large-scale computing. However, grid computing also faces many challenges, such as heterogeneity, security, reliability, interoperability, and usability, that require advanced technologies and standards to overcome.
