- Security attacks are any actions that compromise the security of information or systems. They can be classified into two types: passive attacks and active attacks.
  - Passive attacks are those that do not affect the normal functioning of the system, but only attempt to eavesdrop or monitor the communication. Examples of passive attacks are traffic analysis, wiretapping, and cryptanalysis.
  - Active attacks are those that alter or disrupt the normal functioning of the system, such as modifying, deleting, or injecting data, or denying service. Examples of active attacks are replay, masquerade, modification, and denial of service.
- Security services are the measures that are taken to protect the system from security attacks. They can be classified into five categories: confidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication, and non-repudiation.
  - Confidentiality is the service that ensures that the information is only accessible to the authorized parties. It can be achieved by using encryption techniques, access control mechanisms, or physical protection.
  - Integrity is the service that ensures that the information is not modified or corrupted by unauthorized parties. It can be achieved by using checksums, digital signatures, or message authentication codes.
  - Availability is the service that ensures that the information and the system are accessible and functional to the authorized parties. It can be achieved by using redundancy, fault tolerance, or backup systems.
  - Authentication is the service that ensures that the identity of the parties involved in the communication is verified. It can be achieved by using passwords, biometrics, or digital certificates.
  - Non-repudiation is the service that ensures that the parties involved in the communication cannot deny their participation or the validity of the information. It can be achieved by using digital signatures, timestamps, or audit trails.
- Security mechanisms are the tools or methods that are used to implement the security services. They can be classified into two types: preventive mechanisms and detective mechanisms.
  - Preventive mechanisms are those that aim to prevent or deter the security attacks from happening. Examples of preventive mechanisms are encryption, access control, or firewalls.
  - Detective mechanisms are those that aim to detect or identify the security attacks that have happened or are happening. Examples of detective mechanisms are intrusion detection systems, antivirus software, or audit logs.
- Encryption techniques are the methods that are used to transform the information into an unreadable form, so that only the authorized parties can decrypt and read it. They can be classified into two types: symmetric encryption and asymmetric encryption.
  - Symmetric encryption is the technique that uses the same key for both encryption and decryption. The key is shared by the sender and the receiver, and must be kept secret from anyone else. Examples of symmetric encryption are Caesar cipher, substitution cipher, transposition cipher, stream cipher, and block cipher.
  - Asymmetric encryption is the technique that uses different keys for encryption and decryption. The keys are called public key and private key, and are mathematically related. The public key is known to everyone, and can be used to encrypt the information. The private key is known only to the owner, and can be used to decrypt the information. Examples of asymmetric encryption are RSA, Diffie-Hellman, or ElGamal.
- Cryptanalysis is the process of breaking or analyzing the encryption techniques, and recovering the original information or the key. It can be classified into two types: ciphertext-only attack and known-plaintext attack.
  - Ciphertext-only attack is the type of cryptanalysis that only uses the encrypted information, and tries to find the key or the plaintext by using statistical analysis, frequency analysis, or brute force.
  - Known-plaintext attack is the type of cryptanalysis that uses some pairs of plaintext and ciphertext, and tries to find the key or the plaintext by using mathematical techniques, such as linear algebra, modular arithmetic, or differential cryptanalysis.
