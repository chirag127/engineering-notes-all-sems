### Classical encryption techniques: substitution ciphers and transposition ciphers

- Classical encryption techniques are methods of encrypting plaintext messages that use simple mathematical operations and do not require complex algorithms or computers.
- The two basic building blocks of all classical encryption techniques are substitution and transposition .
- Substitution ciphers replace each element of the plaintext (such as a letter, a digit, or a symbol) with another element of the ciphertext (such as a different letter, a number, or a symbol) according to a predefined rule or key .
- Transposition ciphers rearrange the order of appearance of the elements of the plaintext according to a predefined rule or key, without changing their identity .
- Substitution and transposition ciphers can be combined to form more complex encryption techniques, called product ciphers.
- Some examples of substitution ciphers are Caesar cipher, monoalphabetic cipher, polyalphabetic cipher, and one-time pad .
- Some examples of transposition ciphers are rail fence cipher, columnar transposition cipher, and permutation cipher .
- The main goal of classical encryption techniques is to provide confidentiality, which means preventing unauthorized parties from reading the plaintext messages.
- The main challenge of classical encryption techniques is to resist cryptanalysis, which is the art of breaking the encryption and recovering the plaintext from the ciphertext .
- Cryptanalysis can be performed by various methods, such as frequency analysis, brute force attack, known plaintext attack, chosen plaintext attack, and ciphertext only attack.
- Classical encryption techniques are now considered outdated and insecure, as they can be easily broken by modern computers and sophisticated algorithms. They are mainly used for historical and educational purposes, or for simple applications that do not require high security .
- Steganography is another technique of hiding information, which is different from encryption. Steganography conceals the existence of the message, rather than its content, by embedding it in another medium, such as an image, a sound, or a text.
