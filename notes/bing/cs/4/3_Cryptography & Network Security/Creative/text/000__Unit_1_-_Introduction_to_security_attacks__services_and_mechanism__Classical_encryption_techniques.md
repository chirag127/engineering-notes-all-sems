- Security attacks are any actions that compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information or resources. Security attacks can be classified into two categories: passive attacks and active attacks. Passive attacks are those that only observe or analyze the data without modifying it, such as eavesdropping or traffic analysis. Active attacks are those that alter or disrupt the data or the system, such as modification, deletion, insertion, replay, denial of service, or masquerade.
- Security services are the countermeasures that protect the information and resources from security attacks. Security services can be categorized into five types: confidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication, and non-repudiation. Confidentiality is the service that ensures that the data is only accessible to authorized parties. Integrity is the service that ensures that the data is not modified or corrupted by unauthorized parties. Availability is the service that ensures that the data or the system is accessible and functional when needed. Authentication is the service that verifies the identity of the parties involved in a communication or transaction. Non-repudiation is the service that prevents the parties from denying their involvement or actions in a communication or transaction.
- Security mechanisms are the methods or tools that implement the security services. Security mechanisms can be classified into two types: preventive mechanisms and detective mechanisms. Preventive mechanisms are those that prevent or deter the security attacks from happening, such as encryption, access control, or digital signatures. Detective mechanisms are those that detect or identify the security attacks that have happened, such as auditing, logging, or intrusion detection.
- Classical encryption techniques are the methods of transforming plaintext (the original message) into ciphertext (the encrypted message) using simple mathematical operations and secret keys. Classical encryption techniques can be divided into two types: substitution ciphers and transposition ciphers. Substitution ciphers are those that replace each letter or symbol of the plaintext with another letter or symbol, according to a fixed rule or a key. Transposition ciphers are those that rearrange the order of the letters or symbols of the plaintext, according to a fixed rule or a key.
- Cryptanalysis is the art or science of breaking the encryption and recovering the plaintext from the ciphertext, without knowing the key or the encryption algorithm. Cryptanalysis can be performed by various methods, such as brute force, frequency analysis, pattern matching, or exploiting the weaknesses of the encryption algorithm or the key.
- Steganography is the technique of hiding a secret message within another message or a medium, such as an image, a video, or a sound file. Steganography differs from encryption in that it does not alter the appearance or the content of the message or the medium, but rather conceals the existence of the secret message. Steganography can be combined with encryption to enhance the security of the secret message.
- Stream and block ciphers are two types of symmetric encryption techniques, where the same secret key is used for both encryption and decryption. Stream ciphers are those that encrypt each bit or byte of the plaintext individually, using a keystream that is generated from the key and a random or pseudo-random number. Block ciphers are those that encrypt each block of the plaintext (typically 64 or 128 bits) as a whole, using a key and a complex mathematical function. Stream ciphers are faster and simpler than block ciphers, but they are more vulnerable to attacks such as repetition or modification.
- Modern block ciphers are the advanced and sophisticated versions of block ciphers that use complex mathematical functions and multiple rounds of encryption to achieve high security and efficiency. Modern block ciphers can be categorized into two types: feistel ciphers and non-feistel ciphers. Feistel ciphers are those that divide each block of the plaintext into two halves, and then apply a round function to one half using a subkey, and then XOR the result with the other half, and then swap the halves. This process is repeated for several rounds, using different subkeys derived from the main key. Non-feistel ciphers are those that do not use the feistel structure, but rather apply different operations to the whole block or to different parts of the block, such as substitution, permutation, rotation, or arithmetic operations.
