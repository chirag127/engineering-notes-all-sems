### TCP over wireless for the notes of the Unit 2 - Wireless Networking, Wireless LAN Overview: MAC issues, IEEE 802.11, Blue Tooth, Wireless in the subject of Mobile Computing

- TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) is a reliable, connection-oriented and end-to-end protocol that provides reliable data delivery, flow control and congestion control over the Internet.
- TCP assumes that most packet losses are due to network congestion and responds by reducing the sending rate (window size) to avoid further losses and congestion.
- However, in wireless networks, packet losses can occur more frequently due to wireless link errors, such as fading, shadowing, interference and noise, rather than congestion.
- TCP cannot distinguish between congestion losses and wireless losses and treats both as signs of congestion, resulting in unnecessary and excessive reduction of the sending rate and poor performance of TCP over wireless networks.
- Moreover, wireless networks have some characteristics that are different from wired networks, such as high and variable delays, asymmetric bandwidths, frequent handoffs and mobility, which can also affect the performance of TCP over wireless networks.
- Therefore, TCP needs to be optimized or adapted for wireless networks to deal with the challenges and improve the efficiency and resource utilization of wireless links.
- Some of the approaches for improving TCP performance over wireless networks are:

  - Link layer solutions: These solutions aim to hide the wireless losses from TCP by using link layer mechanisms, such as error correction, retransmission, interleaving and packet scheduling, to recover from wireless errors at the link layer before TCP detects them. These solutions can preserve the end-to-end semantics of TCP and avoid unnecessary window reduction, but they can also introduce additional delays, overheads and complexity at the link layer and interfere with TCP's congestion control.
  - Split-connection solutions: These solutions split the TCP connection into two sub-connections at an intermediate node, such as a base station or a foreign agent, that connects the wired and wireless parts of the network. The intermediate node acts as a proxy for the TCP endpoints and uses different TCP variants or parameters for each sub-connection. These solutions can isolate the wireless losses from the wired losses and optimize the TCP performance for each part of the network, but they break the end-to-end semantics of TCP and require modifications at the intermediate nodes and the TCP endpoints.
  - End-to-end solutions: These solutions modify the TCP behavior at the sender or the receiver or both to enhance the TCP performance over wireless networks. These solutions can be classified into two categories: loss differentiation and loss recovery. Loss differentiation solutions try to distinguish between congestion losses and wireless losses by using explicit or implicit feedback from the network or the receiver and adjust the TCP response accordingly. Loss recovery solutions try to recover from wireless losses by using selective acknowledgments, fast retransmit and fast recovery, or forward error correction techniques. These solutions maintain the end-to-end semantics of TCP and do not require changes at the intermediate nodes, but they may not be able to cope with all the wireless scenarios and may incur additional overheads or complexity at the TCP endpoints.

- Some of the examples of TCP variants or protocols that use the above approaches are:

  - TCP Snoop: A link layer solution that uses a snoop agent at the base station to cache the TCP packets and retransmit them locally in case of wireless losses, without notifying the TCP sender. It also delays the duplicate acknowledgments from the receiver to avoid triggering the fast retransmit and fast recovery at the sender.
  - I-TCP: A split-connection solution that uses the mobile host's foreign agent as a proxy to split the TCP connection into two sub-connections: one between the sender and the foreign agent using standard TCP, and another between the foreign agent and the mobile host using a modified TCP that can handle wireless losses and handoffs.
  - TCP Westwood: An end-to-end solution that uses a loss differentiation mechanism based on estimating the available bandwidth of the wireless link by measuring the rate of returning acknowledgments. It then adjusts the congestion window and the slow start threshold accordingly after a packet loss, depending on whether it is due to congestion or wireless errors.
  - TCP New Reno: An end-to-end solution that uses a loss recovery mechanism based on selective acknowledgments and fast retransmit and fast recovery. It can recover from multiple packet losses within a single window by retransmitting the lost packets in order and avoiding the slow start phase.
