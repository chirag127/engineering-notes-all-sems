### Applications for the notes of the Unit 2 - Wireless Networking, Wireless LAN Overview: MAC issues, IEEE 802.11, Blue Tooth, Wireless in the subject of Mobile Computing

- Wireless networking is the technology that enables devices to communicate without wires or cables. Wireless networking can be used for various applications, such as:
  - Internet access: Wireless networking can provide access to the internet for devices that are not connected to a wired network, such as laptops, smartphones, tablets, etc. Wireless networking can also extend the coverage of a wired network by using wireless access points or routers.
  - Local area network (LAN): Wireless networking can create a LAN within a limited area, such as a home, office, campus, etc. Wireless networking can provide connectivity and data sharing among devices within the LAN, such as computers, printers, scanners, etc. Wireless networking can also support mobility and roaming of devices within the LAN.
  - Personal area network (PAN): Wireless networking can create a PAN within a very short range, such as a few meters. Wireless networking can enable data transfer and synchronization among personal devices, such as phones, watches, headphones, etc. Wireless networking can also support device discovery and pairing for easy communication.
  - Metropolitan area network (MAN): Wireless networking can create a MAN within a large area, such as a city, region, etc. Wireless networking can provide broadband access and wireless backhaul for devices and networks within the MAN, such as cellular base stations, internet service providers, etc. Wireless networking can also support high-speed data transmission and long-range communication.

- Wireless LAN (WLAN) is a type of wireless networking that creates a LAN using wireless technology. WLAN can have various characteristics and features, such as:
  - IEEE 802.11: This is the most common standard for WLAN, also known as WiFi. IEEE 802.11 defines the medium access control (MAC) and physical (PHY) layer specifications for WLAN. IEEE 802.11 has several sub-standards that specify different frequency bands, modulation schemes, data rates, security protocols, etc. for WLAN, such as 802.11a, 802.11b, 802.11g, 802.11n, 802.11ac, 802.11ax, etc.
  - MAC issues: The MAC layer of WLAN is responsible for coordinating the access and transmission of data among devices in the WLAN. The MAC layer of WLAN faces some challenges and issues, such as:
    - Hidden terminal problem: This occurs when two devices in the WLAN cannot hear each other, but can hear a common device. This can cause collisions and interference when both devices try to transmit to the common device at the same time.
    - Exposed terminal problem: This occurs when two devices in the WLAN can hear each other, but cannot hear a common device. This can cause inefficiency and waste of bandwidth when both devices refrain from transmitting to the common device, even though they do not interfere with each other.
    - Near-far problem: This occurs when two devices in the WLAN have different signal strengths at the receiver. This can cause unfairness and degradation of performance when the device with the stronger signal dominates the channel and prevents the device with the weaker signal from transmitting.
  - IEEE 802.11 solutions: The IEEE 802.11 standard provides some solutions and mechanisms to address the MAC issues of WLAN, such as:
    - CSMA/CA: This stands for carrier sense multiple access with collision avoidance. This is the basic channel access method of WLAN, which requires a device to sense the channel before transmitting. If the channel is busy, the device waits for a random backoff time before trying again. If the channel is idle, the device transmits a short request-to-send (RTS) frame to the intended receiver. The receiver replies with a clear-to-send (CTS) frame if it is ready to receive. The device then transmits the data frame and waits for an acknowledgment (ACK) frame from the receiver. The RTS/CTS/ACK exchange helps to avoid collisions and resolve the hidden terminal problem.
    - NAV: This stands for network allocation vector. This is a timer that indicates the duration of the channel reservation by the RTS/CTS/ACK exchange. The NAV is broadcasted in the RTS/CTS/ACK frames and updated by all devices in the WLAN. The NAV helps to prevent other devices from accessing the channel during the reserved period and resolve the exposed terminal problem.
