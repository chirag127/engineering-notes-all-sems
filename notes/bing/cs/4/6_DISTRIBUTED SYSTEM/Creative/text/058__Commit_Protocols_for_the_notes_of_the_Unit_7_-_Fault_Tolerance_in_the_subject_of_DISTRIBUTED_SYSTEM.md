### Commit Protocols

- Commit protocols are used to ensure the atomicity and consistency of transactions in distributed systems, where multiple processes or sites may be involved in executing a transaction.
- A transaction is a sequence of operations that transforms the system from one consistent state to another. A transaction can either commit, meaning that all its operations are successfully executed and their effects are made permanent, or abort, meaning that none of its operations are executed and their effects are discarded.
- Commit protocols are based on the exchange of messages among the processes or sites involved in a transaction, and they typically use a coordinator process or site to orchestrate the commit decision.
- There are two main types of commit protocols: two-phase commit (2PC) and three-phase commit (3PC).
- Two-phase commit protocol:
  - In the first phase, the coordinator sends a prepare message to all the participants, asking them to vote on whether they are ready to commit or not. The participants execute the transaction up to the point of commit, and then reply with either yes or no.
  - In the second phase, the coordinator collects the votes from all the participants, and decides whether to commit or abort the transaction. If all the votes are yes, the coordinator sends a commit message to all the participants, instructing them to make their changes permanent. If any vote is no, or if the coordinator does not receive a vote from some participant within a timeout, the coordinator sends an abort message to all the participants, instructing them to discard their changes.
  - The two-phase commit protocol ensures atomicity and consistency, but it has some drawbacks, such as blocking and vulnerability to failures. Blocking means that if the coordinator or some participant fails after sending or receiving a prepare message, the other participants may have to wait indefinitely for the commit decision, and they cannot release the resources held by the transaction. Vulnerability to failures means that if the coordinator fails after sending a commit or abort message to some but not all participants, the participants may end up in an inconsistent state, where some have committed and some have aborted the transaction.
- Three-phase commit protocol:
  - The three-phase commit protocol is an extension of the two-phase commit protocol that tries to overcome the drawbacks of blocking and vulnerability to failures. It introduces a third phase, called pre-commit, between the prepare and commit phases.
  - In the first phase, the coordinator sends a prepare message to all the participants, asking them to vote on whether they are ready to commit or not. The participants execute the transaction up to the point of commit, and then reply with either yes or no.
  - In the second phase, the coordinator collects the votes from all the participants, and decides whether to pre-commit or abort the transaction. If all the votes are yes, the coordinator sends a pre-commit message to all the participants, instructing them to prepare to commit. If any vote is no, or if the coordinator does not receive a vote from some participant within a timeout, the coordinator sends an abort message to all the participants, instructing them to discard their changes.
  - In the third phase, the coordinator sends a commit message to all the participants, instructing them to make their changes permanent. The participants acknowledge the receipt of the commit message, and then release the resources held by the transaction.
  - The three-phase commit protocol avoids blocking, because if the coordinator or some participant fails after sending or receiving a pre-commit message, the other participants can decide to commit the transaction on their own, without waiting for the coordinator. It also reduces the vulnerability to failures, because if the coordinator fails after sending a commit or abort message to some but not all participants, the participants can use a majority-based protocol to agree on the commit decision. However, the three-phase commit protocol introduces more message delays and overheads, and it is still not immune to network partitions or simultaneous failures.
