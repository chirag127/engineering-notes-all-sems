### Atomic Commit protocols for the notes of the Unit 9 - Distributed Transactions in the subject of DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM

- Atomic commit protocols are protocols that ensure that a distributed transaction is either committed or aborted in its entirety, even if some of the nodes in the system fail or crash.
- Atomic commit protocols are important for maintaining the consistency and integrity of the data in the system, as well as the isolation and durability properties of transactions.
- There are different types of atomic commit protocols, such as two-phase commit (2PC), three-phase commit (3PC), parallel commit, and failure-aware commit (FLAC).
- Two-phase commit (2PC) is the most widely used atomic commit protocol. It consists of two phases: a prepare phase and a commit phase. In the prepare phase, the coordinator node sends a prepare message to all the participant nodes, asking them to vote on whether they are ready to commit or abort the transaction. In the commit phase, the coordinator node collects the votes from the participants and decides whether to commit or abort the transaction based on the majority rule. The coordinator then sends a commit or abort message to all the participants, instructing them to finalize the transaction accordingly.
- Two-phase commit (2PC) has some drawbacks, such as blocking, vulnerability to network partitions, and high latency. Blocking occurs when the coordinator node fails or becomes unreachable, leaving the participants in an uncertain state, waiting for the final decision. Network partitions can cause the coordinator and some of the participants to be isolated from each other, leading to inconsistent outcomes. High latency is caused by the need for two rounds of message exchange between the coordinator and the participants, as well as the possibility of timeouts and retries.
- Three-phase commit (3PC) is an extension of two-phase commit (2PC) that aims to overcome the blocking problem. It adds a third phase, called the pre-commit phase, between the prepare and the commit phases. In the pre-commit phase, the coordinator node sends a pre-commit message to all the participants, indicating that they have agreed to commit the transaction. The participants then acknowledge the pre-commit message and enter a ready state, where they can commit the transaction without waiting for the coordinator. In the commit phase, the coordinator node sends a commit message to all the participants, confirming the final decision. If the coordinator fails or becomes unreachable, the participants can use a timeout mechanism to commit the transaction independently, avoiding blocking.
- Three-phase commit (3PC) has some drawbacks, such as increased latency, complexity, and vulnerability to network partitions. Increased latency is caused by the need for three rounds of message exchange between the coordinator and the participants, as well as the possibility of timeouts and retries. Complexity is caused by the need for more states and transitions in the protocol, as well as the need for failure detection and recovery mechanisms. Network partitions can still cause inconsistent outcomes, as some of the participants may commit the transaction while others may abort it, depending on their view of the coordinator's status.

Transaction intents are written to the data store along with a transaction record, which contains the transaction's status and dependencies. Once the transaction record is replicated, the transaction is considered committed, and the transaction intents are converted to committed values. If the transaction record is not replicated, the transaction is considered aborted, and the transaction intents are discarded.
- Parallel commit has some advantages, such as low latency, non-blocking, and resilience to network partitions. Low latency is achieved by reducing the number of message exchanges and waiting times in the protocol, as well as by parallelizing the replication of transaction records. Non-blocking is achieved by avoiding the need for a coordinator node and allowing the participants to commit or abort the transaction independently, based on the status of the transaction record. Resilience to network partitions is achieved by using a distributed consensus protocol that guarantees the consistency and availability of the transaction record, regardless of the network conditions.
