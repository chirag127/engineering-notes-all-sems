### Concurrency control in distributed transactions for the notes of the Unit 9 - Distributed Transactions in the subject of DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM

- Concurrency control is the process of managing the simultaneous execution of transactions in a distributed database system, such that the ACID properties are preserved and data consistency is maintained.
- Concurrency control in distributed transactions is more challenging than in centralized transactions, because of the following reasons:
  - The transactions may access data stored in different servers, which may have different concurrency control protocols and locking mechanisms.
  - The communication between the servers may be unreliable, delayed, or lost, which may cause conflicts, deadlocks, or inconsistencies among the servers.
  - The transactions may span multiple servers, which may require coordination and agreement among the servers to commit or abort the transactions.
  - The transactions may encounter failures or crashes of servers, networks, or processes, which may affect the availability and durability of the data and the transactions.
- There are three main approaches to concurrency control in distributed transactions: locking-based, timestamp-based, and optimistic.
  - Locking-based concurrency control protocols use the concept of locking data items or resources to prevent concurrent transactions from accessing or modifying them. Locking can be done at different levels of granularity, such as records, pages, tables, or databases. Locking can also be done in different modes, such as shared, exclusive, or update. Locking-based protocols require a mechanism to grant, release, and manage locks, and to detect and resolve deadlocks. Examples of locking-based protocols are two-phase locking (2PL), distributed two-phase locking (D2PL), and tree-structured locking (TSL).
  - Timestamp-based concurrency control algorithms use a transaction’s timestamp to order and serialize the transactions. A timestamp is a unique identifier that reflects the start time or the priority of a transaction. Timestamp-based algorithms compare the timestamps of transactions to determine whether they can read or write a data item, or whether they should abort and restart. Timestamp-based algorithms do not require locking, but they may cause more aborts and restarts than locking-based algorithms. Examples of timestamp-based algorithms are basic timestamp ordering (BTO), distributed timestamp ordering (DTO), and multiversion timestamp ordering (MVTO).
  - Optimistic concurrency control algorithms assume that conflicts among transactions are rare, and allow transactions to execute without locking or checking timestamps. However, before committing, transactions have to validate their read and write sets to ensure that they do not violate the serializability of the transactions. If a validation fails, the transaction has to abort and restart. Optimistic algorithms reduce the overhead of locking and timestamping, but they may cause more aborts and restarts than locking-based or timestamp-based algorithms. Examples of optimistic algorithms are basic optimistic concurrency control (BOCC), distributed optimistic concurrency control (DOCC), and multiversion optimistic concurrency control (MVOCC).
- A mnemonic to remember the three approaches to concurrency control in distributed transactions is: **LTO** (Locking, Timestamp, Optimistic).
- A learning trick to understand the trade-offs among the three approaches is to use the analogy of a library. Suppose there are multiple borrowers who want to borrow or return books from the library, and there are multiple librarians who manage the books and the transactions. The three approaches can be compared as follows:
  - Locking-based approach: The librarians lock the books that are being borrowed or returned by a borrower, and do not allow other borrowers to access them until the transaction is completed. This ensures that the books are always consistent, but it may cause delays, conflicts, or deadlocks among the borrowers and the librarians.
  - Timestamp-based approach: The librarians assign a timestamp to each borrower when they enter the library, and use the timestamps to order and serialize the transactions. The librarians allow a borrower to borrow or return a book only if their timestamp is smaller than the timestamp of the last transaction on that book. This avoids locking the books, but it may cause some borrowers to abort and restart their transactions if their timestamps are larger than the last transaction on the book they want to access.
  - Optimistic approach: The librarians do not lock or check the timestamps of the books, and allow the borrowers to borrow or return the books freely. However, before completing the transaction, the librarians validate the books that the borrower has accessed, and make sure that they have not been modified by other transactions. If a validation fails, the borrower has to abort and restart their transaction. This reduces the overhead of locking and timestamping, but it may cause more aborts and restarts if there are many conflicts among the transactions.
