### Atomic Commit in Distributed Database System

- Atomic commit is an operation that applies a set of distinct changes as a single operation. If the changes are applied, then the atomic commit is said to have succeeded. If the changes are not applied, then the atomic commit is said to have failed or aborted.
- Atomic commit is essential to maintain the atomicity of distributed transactions, which are transactions that span multiple database sites .
- Atomicity means that either all the changes of a transaction are applied, or none of them are. Atomicity ensures the consistency and integrity of the database.
- Atomic commit is challenging in distributed database systems, because some of the sites participating in the transaction may fail or become unreachable during the commit process. Such failures may cause the transaction to be blocked or to be partially committed, violating the atomicity property .
- To cope with failures, distributed database systems use atomic commit protocols (ACPs), which are algorithms that coordinate the commit decision among the participating sites. ACPs ensure that all the sites agree on the same outcome (commit or abort) for the transaction, even in the presence of failures  .
- ACPs can be classified into two categories: blocking and non-blocking. Blocking ACPs may block the commit decision of a transaction if some of the sites fail. Non-blocking ACPs guarantee that the commit decision of a transaction is always made, regardless of failures .
- Blocking ACPs are simpler and more efficient than non-blocking ACPs, but they have lower availability and fault-tolerance. Non-blocking ACPs are more complex and less efficient than blocking ACPs, but they have higher availability and fault-tolerance .
- The most common blocking ACP is the two-phase commit protocol (2PC), which consists of two phases: prepare and commit. In the prepare phase, a coordinator site asks all the participating sites to vote on whether they are ready to commit or not. In the commit phase, the coordinator site decides to commit or abort the transaction based on the votes, and informs all the participating sites of the decision.
- The most common non-blocking ACP is the three-phase commit protocol (3PC), which consists of three phases: prepare, pre-commit and commit. In the prepare phase, a coordinator site asks all the participating sites to vote on whether they are ready to commit or not. In the pre-commit phase, the coordinator site decides to commit or abort the transaction based on the votes, and informs all the participating sites of the tentative decision. In the commit phase, the coordinator site confirms the decision, and informs all the participating sites of the final decision.
- A mnemonic to remember the difference between 2PC and 3PC is that 2PC has two phases and 3PC has three phases. Another mnemonic is that 2PC is blocking and 3PC is non-blocking.
- A possible learning trick to understand the concept of atomic commit is to imagine a group of friends who want to go to a movie together. They need to agree on whether they all go or none of them go. They can use a blocking protocol, such as 2PC, or a non-blocking protocol, such as 3PC, to reach a consensus. A blocking protocol may cause some of them to wait indefinitely if some of them do not respond. A non-blocking protocol may cause some of them to miss the movie if some of them change their mind.
