#### CO-4 Determine and calculate various physical properties of a given material by using

- Physical properties of a material are those that can be observed or measured without changing the identity or composition of the material. Some examples are density, color, hardness, melting point, electrical conductivity, etc.
- To determine and calculate various physical properties of a given material, different methods and instruments can be used depending on the type and nature of the property. Some common methods are:

  - Density: Density is the mass per unit volume of a material. It can be calculated by dividing the mass of the material by its volume. The mass can be measured using a balance or a scale, and the volume can be measured using a graduated cylinder, a ruler, or a caliper. The unit of density is kg/m^3 or g/cm^3.
  - Color: Color is the visual perception of the wavelength of light reflected or transmitted by a material. It can be determined by using a colorimeter, a spectrophotometer, or a color chart. The unit of color is usually expressed in terms of hue, saturation, and brightness.
  - Hardness: Hardness is the resistance of a material to deformation, indentation, or scratching. It can be measured by using a hardness tester, such as a Rockwell, Brinell, Vickers, or Mohs tester. The unit of hardness is usually expressed in terms of a hardness scale or a hardness number.
  - Melting point: Melting point is the temperature at which a solid material changes to a liquid state. It can be determined by using a melting point apparatus, a thermometer, or a differential scanning calorimeter. The unit of melting point is °C or K.
  - Electrical conductivity: Electrical conductivity is the ability of a material to conduct electric current. It can be calculated by dividing the electric current by the potential difference across the material. The electric current can be measured using an ammeter, and the potential difference can be measured using a voltmeter. The unit of electrical conductivity is S/m or Ω^-1 m^-1.
