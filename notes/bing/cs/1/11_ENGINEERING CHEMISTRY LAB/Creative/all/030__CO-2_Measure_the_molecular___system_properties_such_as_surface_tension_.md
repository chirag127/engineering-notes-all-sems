### CO-2 Measure the molecular / system properties such as surface tension

- Surface tension is the energy, or work, required to increase the surface area of a liquid by a given amount. It is a measure of the cohesive forces between the molecules in the liquid. 
- Surface tension depends on the nature and strength of the intermolecular interactions in the liquid. Generally, the stronger the intermolecular forces, the higher the surface tension. For example, water has strong hydrogen bonds and a high surface tension of about 70 dynes/cm, while carbon dioxide has weak dispersion forces and a low surface tension of about 2 dynes/cm.  
- Surface tension can be measured by various methods, such as the force balance method, the drop weight method, the pendant drop method, the bubble pressure method, and the Wilhelmy plate method. These methods involve measuring the force, weight, shape, or pressure of a liquid drop or bubble at the interface with another fluid (usually air or water). 
- Surface tension can be affected by various factors, such as temperature, pressure, solutes, and surfactants. Generally, surface tension decreases with increasing temperature and pressure, as the kinetic energy of the molecules overcomes the intermolecular forces. Solutes can either increase or decrease the surface tension, depending on their polarity and concentration. Surfactants are molecules that reduce the surface tension of polar liquids like water by adsorbing at the interface and disrupting the intermolecular forces. Examples of surfactants are soaps, detergents, and emulsifiers.   
- Surface tension is an important property that affects many phenomena, such as capillary action, wetting, spreading, adhesion, cohesion, bubbles, foams, emulsions, and droplet formation. Surface tension also plays a role in biological systems, such as the alveoli in the lungs, the tear film in the eyes, and the cell membranes.
