## LIST OF EXPERIMENTS

- An experiment is a scientific procedure that is carried out to test a hypothesis, observe a phenomenon, or measure a variable.
- Experiments usually involve manipulating one or more independent variables and measuring their effects on one or more dependent variables.
- Experiments can be classified into different types based on their design, purpose, or outcome. Some common types of experiments are:

  - Controlled experiments: These are experiments where the researcher controls all the factors that could affect the outcome, except for the independent variable that is being tested. The researcher also uses a control group that does not receive the treatment or manipulation of the independent variable, and compares it with an experimental group that does receive it. This allows the researcher to isolate the causal effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable. For example, a controlled experiment to test the effect of a new drug on blood pressure would involve randomly assigning participants to either receive the drug or a placebo, and measuring their blood pressure before and after the treatment.

  - Natural experiments: These are experiments where the researcher does not manipulate the independent variable, but observes its variation in a natural setting. The researcher also tries to find a control group that is similar to the experimental group in all other aspects, except for the independent variable. This allows the researcher to infer the causal effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable, as if it were a controlled experiment. For example, a natural experiment to test the effect of a natural disaster on economic growth would involve comparing the regions that were affected by the disaster with the regions that were not, and measuring their economic indicators before and after the disaster.

  - Quasi-experiments: These are experiments where the researcher does not randomly assign the participants to the experimental or control groups, but uses some other criteria to create the groups. The researcher also does not have full control over the independent variable, but can manipulate it to some extent. This reduces the internal validity of the experiment, as there could be confounding factors that affect the outcome, but increases the external validity, as the experiment is more realistic and generalizable. For example, a quasi-experiment to test the effect of a training program on employee performance would involve selecting the employees who volunteered for the program as the experimental group, and the employees who did not as the control group, and measuring their performance before and after the program.

  - Field experiments: These are experiments that are conducted in a natural setting, rather than in a laboratory or a controlled environment. The researcher still manipulates the independent variable and measures the dependent variable, but does not have full control over the other factors that could affect the outcome. This increases the ecological validity of the experiment, as it reflects the real-world conditions and behaviors, but reduces the internal validity, as there could be confounding factors that affect the outcome. For example, a field experiment to test the effect of a social norm on littering would involve placing signs that either encourage or discourage littering in different public places, and counting the number of litter items in each place.

  - Laboratory experiments: These are experiments that are conducted in a controlled environment, such as a laboratory or a simulated setting. The researcher has full control over the independent variable and the other factors that could affect the outcome, and measures the dependent variable with precision and accuracy. This increases the internal validity of the experiment, as it eliminates the confounding factors that affect the outcome, but reduces the ecological validity, as it does not reflect the real-world conditions and behaviors. For example, a laboratory experiment to test the effect of a stimulus on brain activity would involve exposing the participants to different types of stimuli, such as images, sounds, or words, and recording their brain activity with an electroencephalogram (EEG).

  - Randomized controlled trials (RCTs): These are experiments that are used to test the efficacy and safety of a new intervention, such as a drug, a device, a procedure, or a policy. The researcher randomly assigns the participants to either receive the intervention or a placebo, or to a control group that receives the standard treatment or no treatment. The researcher also ensures that the participants, the intervention providers, and the outcome assessors are blinded to the group assignment, to avoid bias and placebo effects. The researcher then measures the outcome of interest, such as the health status, the behavior, or the satisfaction of the participants, and compares the groups to determine the effect of the intervention. For example, a randomized controlled trial to test the effect of a new vaccine on COVID-19 infection would involve randomly assigning the participants to either receive the vaccine or a placebo, and measuring their infection status after a certain period of time.
