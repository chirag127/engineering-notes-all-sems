### 10. Determination of chloride content in water sample.

- Chloride is one of the major anions found in natural waters. It originates from various sources, such as seawater intrusion, industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, and domestic sewage.
- Chloride can affect the taste, corrosion, and biological activity of water. Therefore, it is important to monitor and control its concentration in drinking water and wastewater.
- One of the common methods for determining chloride content in water sample is the Mohr method, which is based on the precipitation of silver chloride by adding silver nitrate solution in the presence of potassium chromate indicator.
- The procedure of the Mohr method is as follows:

  1. Pipette 50 mL of the water sample into a 250 mL conical flask and add 2-3 drops of potassium chromate indicator. The solution should have a pale yellow color.
  2. Titrate the solution with 0.02 M silver nitrate solution from a burette until a permanent brick-red precipitate of silver chromate appears. Record the final burette reading.
  3. Repeat the titration with two more aliquots of the water sample and take the average of the three readings.
  4. Calculate the chloride content in mg/L using the following formula:

     Chloride (mg/L) = (V x N x 35.45) / 50

     where V is the average volume of silver nitrate solution used in mL, N is the normality of silver nitrate solution, and 35.45 is the equivalent weight of chloride.

- Some sources of error and precaution in this method are:

  - The end point of the titration is not very sharp and depends on the amount of indicator used. Therefore, it is advisable to use a small amount of indicator and to observe the color change under a bright light.
  - The silver nitrate solution should be standardized against a primary standard, such as sodium chloride, before use. The silver nitrate solution should also be protected from light and air to prevent decomposition.
  - The water sample should be free from interfering substances, such as bromide, iodide, sulfide, and cyanide, which can also react with silver nitrate and cause errors. If such substances are present, they should be removed by appropriate methods before the titration.
