### 1. Study of various types of Active & Passive Components based on their ratings.

- Active and passive components are the two main types of electronic circuit elements that have different functions and characteristics.
- Active components are devices that can produce or control the flow of electric current or voltage, such as transistors, diodes, integrated circuits, etc. They usually require an external source of power to operate and can provide power gain or amplification to the circuit  .
- Passive components are devices that can store or dissipate electric energy, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, etc. They do not need an external source of power to function and cannot provide power gain or amplification to the circuit  .
- Active components are usually non-linear, meaning that the output signal is not proportional to the input signal. Passive components are usually linear, meaning that the output signal is proportional to the input signal and follows Ohm's law .
- Active and passive components can be classified based on their ratings, which are the maximum values of voltage, current, power, frequency, etc. that they can handle without damage or degradation. The ratings depend on the type, size, material, and design of the component and are usually specified by the manufacturer.
- Some examples of ratings for active and passive components are:

  - Voltage rating: the maximum voltage that can be applied across the component terminals. For example, a resistor may have a voltage rating of 250 V, meaning that it can withstand up to 250 V across its terminals without burning out. A diode may have a voltage rating of 50 V, meaning that it can block up to 50 V in the reverse direction without breaking down.
  - Current rating: the maximum current that can flow through the component. For example, a wire may have a current rating of 10 A, meaning that it can carry up to 10 A of current without overheating. A transistor may have a current rating of 100 mA, meaning that it can allow up to 100 mA of current to flow through its collector-emitter junction without damage.
  - Power rating: the maximum power that can be dissipated by the component. For example, a resistor may have a power rating of 0.25 W, meaning that it can dissipate up to 0.25 W of power as heat without melting. An integrated circuit may have a power rating of 1 W, meaning that it can consume up to 1 W of power from its supply without overheating.
  - Frequency rating: the maximum frequency that can be applied to or passed by the component. For example, a capacitor may have a frequency rating of 100 kHz, meaning that it can block or store alternating currents up to 100 kHz without losing its capacitance. A filter may have a frequency rating of 10 MHz, meaning that it can attenuate or pass signals up to 10 MHz without distortion.
