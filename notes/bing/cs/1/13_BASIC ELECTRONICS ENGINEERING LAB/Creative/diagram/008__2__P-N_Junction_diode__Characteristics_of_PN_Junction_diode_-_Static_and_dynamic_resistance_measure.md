A PN junction diode is a semiconductor device that allows current to flow in one direction only. It has two terminals, anode (p-side) and cathode (n-side), and a depletion region that acts as a potential barrier between them. The VI characteristics of a PN junction diode show how the current and voltage vary depending on the biasing mode of the diode.

The VI characteristics of a PN junction diode can be obtained by plotting the current (I) on the y-axis and the voltage (V) on the x-axis for different values of applied voltage. The characteristics can be divided into three regions: forward bias, reverse bias, and breakdown.

In forward bias, the anode is connected to the positive terminal of the battery and the cathode is connected to the negative terminal. This reduces the potential barrier and allows the current to flow from the anode to the cathode. The current increases exponentially with the voltage, but there is a small voltage drop across the diode, called the cut-in voltage or the threshold voltage, which is typically 0.7 V for silicon diodes and 0.3 V for germanium diodes. The slope of the forward characteristic curve shows the dynamic resistance of the diode, which is the ratio of the change in voltage to the change in current. The dynamic resistance is inversely proportional to the current and decreases as the current increases. The static resistance of the diode is the ratio of the voltage to the current at a given point on the curve. The static resistance is also inversely proportional to the current and decreases as the current increases.

In reverse bias, the anode is connected to the negative terminal of the battery and the cathode is connected to the positive terminal. This increases the potential barrier and prevents the current from flowing from the anode to the cathode. The current is very small and almost constant, called the reverse saturation current, which is due to the thermal generation of minority carriers in the semiconductor. The reverse saturation current depends on the temperature and the doping level of the semiconductor. The reverse characteristic curve is almost horizontal, indicating that the diode has a very high resistance in reverse bias. The slope of the reverse characteristic curve shows the dynamic resistance of the diode, which is the ratio of the change in voltage to the change in current. The dynamic resistance is directly proportional to the voltage and increases as the voltage increases. The static resistance of the diode is the ratio of the voltage to the current at a given point on the curve. The static resistance is also directly proportional to the voltage and increases as the voltage increases.

This is due to the avalanche effect or the Zener effect, which cause the depletion region to collapse and the minority carriers to multiply rapidly. The breakdown voltage depends on the doping level and the thickness of the depletion region.
