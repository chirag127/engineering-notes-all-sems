##### 1. Kirchhoff‟s laws.

Kirchhoff's laws are two rules that describe the conservation of electric charge and energy in electrical circuits. They are named after Gustav Kirchhoff, a German physicist who formulated them in 1845.

- Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) states that the algebraic sum of currents entering and leaving a node (or junction) in a circuit is zero. This means that the total charge flowing into a node is equal to the total charge flowing out of it. Mathematically, KCL can be written as:

  $$\sum_{k=1}^n I_k = 0$$

  where $I_k$ is the current flowing through the $k$-th branch connected to the node, and $n$ is the number of branches. The sign of the current depends on the direction of the flow: positive if it enters the node, and negative if it leaves the node.

- Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) states that the algebraic sum of voltages around any closed loop in a circuit is zero. This means that the total energy gained or lost by the charges in a loop is zero. Mathematically, KVL can be written as:

  $$\sum_{k=1}^n V_k = 0$$

  where $V_k$ is the voltage across the $k$-th element in the loop, and $n$ is the number of elements. The sign of the voltage depends on the polarity of the element: positive if it is in the same direction as the loop, and negative if it is in the opposite direction.

Kirchhoff's laws can be used to analyze any electrical circuit, such as resistive, capacitive, inductive, or mixed circuits. They can also be applied to AC or DC circuits, as long as the currents and voltages are properly defined.

Some mnemonics and learning tricks for Kirchhoff's laws are:

- For KCL, remember that "what goes in must come out" at a node. Alternatively, you can use the phrase "currents in a node sum to zero" (CINASZ) to recall the equation.
- For KVL, remember that "what goes up must come down" in a loop. Alternatively, you can use the phrase "voltages in a loop sum to zero" (VILASZ) to recall the equation.
- To apply Kirchhoff's laws, you need to assign a reference direction for the currents and voltages in the circuit. A common convention is to use clockwise direction for the loops and positive direction for the branches. However, you can choose any direction as long as you are consistent. If you choose the wrong direction, you will get a negative sign in the equation, which means that the actual direction is opposite to your assumption.
