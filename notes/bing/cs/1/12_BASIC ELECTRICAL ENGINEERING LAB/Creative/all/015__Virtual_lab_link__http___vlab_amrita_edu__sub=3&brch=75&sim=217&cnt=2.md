##### Virtual lab link: http://vlab.amrita.edu/?sub=3&brch=75&sim=217&cnt=2

- The link leads to a virtual lab simulation of a **solar cell I-V characteristic** experiment, which is part of the **Electrical Engineering** branch of the **Virtual Labs** project.
- The simulation allows the user to **vary the parameters** of a solar cell, such as the **light intensity**, the **temperature**, the **series resistance**, the **shunt resistance**, and the **load resistance**, and observe the **output voltage** and **current** on a graph.
- The simulation also provides the **theoretical background** of the experiment, the **procedure** to follow, the **self evaluation** questions, and the **references** for further reading.
- The simulation can be used to **understand** the **basic principles** of a solar cell, such as the **photovoltaic effect**, the **equivalent circuit model**, the **I-V characteristic curve**, and the **maximum power point**.
- The simulation can also be used to **compare** the **experimental results** with the **theoretical predictions**, and to **analyze** the **effect** of **different factors** on the **performance** of a solar cell.

Some possible mnemonics and learning tricks for the virtual lab are:

- To remember the **formula** for the **output current** of a solar cell, use the acronym **SILLY**:

  - **S**hort circuit current
  - **I**deal diode current
  - **L**oad voltage
  - **L**ight intensity
  - **Y**ield factor

  - The formula is: I = I_sc - I_d * (e^(V_L / (n * V_t)) - 1)

- To remember the **factors** that affect the **output voltage** of a solar cell, use the acronym **STARS**:

  - **S**eries resistance
  - **T**emperature
  - **A**rea
  - **R**everse saturation current
  - **S**hadowing

  - The output voltage decreases with increasing series resistance, temperature, reverse saturation current, and shadowing, and increases with increasing area.

- To remember the **shape** of the **I-V characteristic curve** of a solar cell, use the analogy of a **mountain**:

  - The curve starts from the **origin** (zero voltage and current) and rises **steeply** until it reaches the **peak** (maximum power point).
  - The curve then falls **gradually** until it reaches the **horizontal axis** (open circuit voltage and zero current).
  - The curve resembles the **profile** of a **mountain** as seen from the side.
