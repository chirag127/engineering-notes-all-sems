### Maxwell equations in vacuum and in conducting medium for the notes of the Unit 2 - Electromagnetic Field Theory in the subject of ENGINEERING PHYSICS

- Maxwell equations are a set of four partial differential equations that describe the relationship between electric and magnetic fields in space and time.
- They are named after James Clerk Maxwell, who formulated them in the 19th century by combining and modifying the earlier laws of Gauss, Faraday, and Ampere.
- Maxwell equations can be written in two forms: differential form and integral form. The differential form uses the operators of vector calculus, such as divergence and curl, to express the equations in terms of local field quantities. The integral form uses the theorems of vector calculus, such as Gauss's theorem and Stokes' theorem, to express the equations in terms of line, surface, and volume integrals of the field quantities.
- Maxwell equations can be applied to different types of media, such as vacuum, conducting medium, or dielectric medium. Depending on the medium, the equations may have different forms and constants.
- In vacuum, the medium is assumed to be empty of any charge or current, and the electric and magnetic fields are independent of the material properties. The Maxwell equations in vacuum are:

  - Gauss's law for electricity: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0$
  - Gauss's law for magnetism: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$
  - Faraday's law of induction: $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$
  - Ampere-Maxwell law: $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$

  where $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field, $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field, $\mu_0$ is the magnetic permeability of vacuum, and $\epsilon_0$ is the electric permittivity of vacuum.
- In a conducting medium, the medium is assumed to have free charges and currents that can respond to the applied electric and magnetic fields. The Maxwell equations in a conducting medium are:

  - Gauss's law for electricity: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$
  - Gauss's law for magnetism: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$
  - Faraday's law of induction: $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$
  - Ampere-Maxwell law: $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \left( \mathbf{J} + \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} \right)$

  where $\rho$ is the charge density, $\mathbf{J}$ is the current density, and the other symbols have the same meaning as before.
- Some mnemonics and learning tricks for the Maxwell equations are:

  - To remember the signs and directions of the curl operations, use the right-hand rule: curl your fingers in the direction of the rotation, and your thumb will point in the direction of the resulting vector.
  - To remember the order of the fields in the curl operations, use the alphabetical order: E comes before B, so the curl of E is related to the time derivative of B, and vice versa.
  - To remember the constants in the equations, use the units: $\mu_0$ has the unit of henry per meter (H/m), which is the same as the unit of inductance, and $\epsilon_0$ has the unit of farad per meter (F/m), which is the same as the unit of capacitance.
  - To remember the difference between the equations in vacuum and in conducting medium, use the presence or absence of charge and current: in vacuum, there is no charge or current, so the divergence of E and the curl of B are zero, and in conducting medium, there is charge and current, so the divergence of E and the curl of B are nonzero.
