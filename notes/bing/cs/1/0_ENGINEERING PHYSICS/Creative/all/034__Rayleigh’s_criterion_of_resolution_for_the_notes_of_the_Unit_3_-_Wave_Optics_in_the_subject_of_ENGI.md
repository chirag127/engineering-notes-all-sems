### Rayleigh’s criterion of resolution for the notes of the Unit 3 - Wave Optics in the subject of ENGINEERING PHYSICS

- Rayleigh's criterion of resolution is a measure of the ability of an optical system to distinguish two point sources of light that are close together.
- According to Rayleigh's criterion, two point sources are just resolvable when the central maximum of the diffraction pattern of one source coincides with the first minimum of the diffraction pattern of the other source .
- The minimum angular separation θ between the two point sources that can be resolved by an optical system with a circular aperture of diameter D is given by :

  θ = 1.22 λ / D

  where λ is the wavelength of the light.

- Rayleigh's criterion is based on the assumption that the human eye can detect the dip in intensity between the overlapping diffraction patterns of the two sources. However, this may not always be the case, especially if the sources have different brightness or color.
- A more rigorous criterion of resolution is based on the modulation transfer function (MTF) of the optical system, which quantifies the contrast between the resolved images of the sources.
- Rayleigh's criterion is important for applications such as microscopy, astronomy, photography, and radar, where the resolution of an optical system determines the quality and accuracy of the images.

Some mnemonics and learning tricks for Rayleigh's criterion of resolution are:

- Remember the formula θ = 1.22 λ / D by associating it with the phrase "The angle is 1.22 times the ratio of the light to the diameter".
- Remember that the criterion is named after Lord Rayleigh, who was a British physicist and Nobel laureate. You can use the acronym BRAN (British Rayleigh ANgle) to recall his name and the concept of angular separation.
- Remember that the criterion is based on the overlap of the diffraction patterns of the two sources. You can use the analogy of two coins that are placed close together on a table. If you look at them from above, you can see the gap between them, but if you look at them from the side, they appear to touch. Similarly, if the diffraction patterns of the two sources are too close, they appear to merge and cannot be resolved.
