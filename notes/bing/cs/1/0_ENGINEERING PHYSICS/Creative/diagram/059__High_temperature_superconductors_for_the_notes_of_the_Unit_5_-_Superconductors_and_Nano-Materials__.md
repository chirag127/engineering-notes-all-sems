High temperature superconductors are materials that can conduct electricity without resistance at temperatures above 77 K, which is much higher than conventional superconductors. They are mainly composed of copper oxides with other metals, such as yttrium, barium, iron, etc. They have many potential applications in power transmission, magnetic levitation, quantum computing, etc.

The diagram shows a unit cell of YBCO, which consists of three CuO2 planes, two BaO planes, two CuO planes, and one Y plane. The CuO2 planes are the main source of superconductivity, as they host the Cooper pairs of electrons that can move without resistance. The BaO and Y planes act as charge reservoirs and provide structural stability. The CuO planes are insulating and separate the CuO2 planes from each other. The distance between the CuO2 planes is a key factor that determines the critical temperature of the superconductor. The higher the distance, the lower the critical temperature. This is because the Cooper pairs are more likely to break apart when they are farther away from each other. Therefore, the optimal structure of a high temperature superconductor is one that has a high density of CuO2 planes with a minimal distance between them.
