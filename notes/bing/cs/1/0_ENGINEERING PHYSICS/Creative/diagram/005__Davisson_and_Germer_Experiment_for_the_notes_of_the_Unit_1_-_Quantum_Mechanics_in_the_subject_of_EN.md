The Davisson and Germer experiment was performed in 1927 to demonstrate the wave nature of electrons. The experiment involved firing a beam of electrons at a nickel target and measuring the angular distribution of the scattered electrons. The experiment showed that the electrons diffracted from the nickel crystal in a pattern similar to that of X-rays, confirming the de Broglie hypothesis that matter has wave-like properties.

The electron beam is generated by a filament and an anode, and is accelerated by a high voltage power supply. The beam enters a vacuum chamber where it hits a nickel target. The nickel target is a thin layer of nickel atoms arranged in a regular lattice. The electrons are scattered by the nickel atoms in different directions, depending on the angle of incidence and the spacing of the atoms. The scattered electrons are detected by a galvanometer, which measures the current of electrons as a function of the angle. The galvanometer shows a peak in the current when the angle satisfies the Bragg's condition for constructive interference:

nλ = 2d sin θ

where n is an integer, λ is the wavelength of the electrons, d is the spacing between the nickel atoms, and θ is the angle of scattering.

The experiment was repeated by varying the voltage of the power supply, which changed the kinetic energy and the wavelength of the electrons. The experiment found that the peak angle shifted with the voltage, confirming that the electrons had a variable wavelength that depended on their momentum. The experiment also found that the wavelength of the electrons matched the de Broglie relation:

λ = h/p

where h is the Planck's constant and p is the momentum of the electrons.

The Davisson and Germer experiment was one of the first direct evidences of the wave nature of matter, and it supported the development of quantum mechanics. The experiment also showed the possibility of using electron diffraction as a tool to study the structure of materials.
