Fraunhofer diffraction is a type of diffraction that occurs when plane waves are incident on a diffracting object, and the diffraction pattern is viewed at a sufficiently long distance from the object. Fraunhofer diffraction can be observed at a single slit or a double slit.

Fraunhofer diffraction at a single slit:

A single slit of width d is illuminated by a parallel beam of monochromatic light of wavelength λ. According to Huygen's principle, every point on the slit acts as a secondary source of light, and the light waves interfere at a point P on a distant screen. The angle θ between the central axis and the point P is called the diffraction angle.

The intensity of light at P depends on the path difference between the waves from the two edges of the slit. The path difference is given by δ = d sin θ. If δ is an integer multiple of λ, the waves are in phase and the intensity is maximum. If δ is an odd multiple of λ/2, the waves are out of phase and the intensity is minimum. The intensity distribution is given by the formula:

I(θ) = I0 (sin(πd sin θ/λ) / (πd sin θ/λ))^2

where I0 is the intensity at the center of the diffraction pattern.
