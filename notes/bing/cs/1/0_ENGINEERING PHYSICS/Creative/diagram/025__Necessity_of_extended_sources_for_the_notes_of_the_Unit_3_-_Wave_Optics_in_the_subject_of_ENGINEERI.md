The necessity of extended sources for the notes of the Unit 3 - Wave Optics in the subject of ENGINEERING PHYSICS is to explain the phenomenon of interference of light waves. Interference occurs when two or more coherent sources of light superpose and produce a resultant intensity distribution that depends on the phase difference between the sources. Coherent sources are those that have a constant phase difference and a fixed frequency. 

A common example of interference is the formation of Newton's rings, which are circular fringes of alternating bright and dark regions observed when a plano-convex lens is placed on a glass plate. The interference is due to the difference in the optical path length of the light rays reflected from the upper and lower surfaces of the air film between the lens and the plate.

To observe interference, the coherent sources must be extended, meaning that they cover a large area of the screen where the interference pattern is formed. If the sources are point-like, the interference pattern will be very faint and difficult to detect. This is because the intensity of light from a point source decreases rapidly with distance, according to the inverse square law. Therefore, to obtain a clear and bright interference pattern, the sources must be extended and have a high intensity.

One way to obtain extended sources is to use a single source of light and split it into two coherent beams using a device such as a beam splitter, a thin film, or a diffraction grating. Another way is to use two identical sources of light that are synchronized in frequency and phase, such as two lasers.
