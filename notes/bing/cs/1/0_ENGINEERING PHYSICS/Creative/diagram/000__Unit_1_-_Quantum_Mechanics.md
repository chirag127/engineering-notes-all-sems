## Unit 1 - Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the behavior and interactions of matter and light on the atomic and subatomic scale. It is based on the concepts of wave-particle duality, uncertainty principle, superposition, and entanglement. Quantum mechanics also provides the foundation for quantum field theory, quantum chemistry, and quantum information.

One of the ways to represent quantum mechanical states and processes is by using diagrams. There are different types of diagrams that are used for different purposes, such as angular momentum diagrams, Feynman diagrams, and circuit diagrams. Here, we will focus on Feynman diagrams, which are graphical representations of the mathematical expressions describing the behavior and interaction of subatomic particles.

A Feynman diagram consists of lines and vertices. The lines represent the propagation of particles in space and time, and the vertices represent the interactions between them. The direction of the lines indicates the direction of time, and the type of the lines indicates the type of the particles. For example, a solid line represents a fermion (such as an electron or a quark), a wavy line represents a boson (such as a photon or a gluon), and a dashed line represents a scalar (such as a Higgs boson). The lines can also have arrows to indicate the charge or spin of the particles. The vertices are labeled by the coupling constants that determine the strength of the interactions.

A Feynman diagram can be interpreted as a term in the perturbative expansion of the S-matrix, which is a mathematical object that encodes the probabilities of the outcomes of a scattering experiment. The S-matrix can be calculated by summing over all possible Feynman diagrams that contribute to a given process. The more vertices a diagram has, the higher the order of perturbation theory it corresponds to, and the smaller its contribution to the S-matrix.

The following diagram illustrates an example of a Feynman diagram for the process of electron-positron annihilation into two photons:

```
   /\
  /  \
 /    \
e-    e+
 \    /
  \  /
   \/
```

In this diagram, an electron (e-) and a positron (e+) collide and annihilate each other, producing two photons (/\). The vertex is labeled by the fine structure constant (α), which is the coupling constant for the electromagnetic interaction. This diagram is a first-order diagram, meaning that it involves only one vertex. It is also a tree diagram, meaning that it has no loops. Loops are diagrams that involve particles that are not observed in the final state, such as virtual particles. Loops are more complicated to calculate and require regularization and renormalization techniques to deal with infinities.

Feynman diagrams are useful tools for visualizing and calculating quantum mechanical processes, but they are not the only way to do so. There are other methods, such as path integrals, operator methods, and algebraic methods, that can also be used to study quantum mechanics. Feynman diagrams are not meant to be realistic pictures of what happens at the subatomic level, but rather symbolic representations of the mathematical formalism of quantum theory.
