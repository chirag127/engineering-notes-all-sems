The Compton effect is the phenomenon of the increase in wavelength of X-rays and other energetic electromagnetic radiations that have been elastically scattered by electrons. It is a consequence of the particle nature of light and the conservation of energy and momentum in the collision between a photon and an electron.

The following diagram illustrates the basic geometry of the Compton effect using ASCII characters:

```
    k1
    |\
    | \
    |  \    P1
    |   \   /\
    |    \ /  \
    |     X    \
    |    / \    \
    |   /   \    \
    |  /     \    \
    | /       \    \
    |/         \    \
    k2          P2   theta
```

In this diagram:

- k1 is the wave vector of the incident photon with energy E1 and wavelength lambda1.
- k2 is the wave vector of the scattered photon with energy E2 and wavelength lambda2.
- P1 is the momentum vector of the initial electron at rest with energy mec2, where me is the electron mass and c is the speed of light.
- P2 is the momentum vector of the final electron with energy Ee and speed ve.
- X is the point of collision between the photon and the electron.
- theta is the angle between k1 and k2.
- phi is the angle between P1 and P2 (not shown in the diagram).

The Compton effect can be described by the following equations:

- Conservation of energy: E1 + mec2 = E2 + Ee
- Conservation of momentum: k1 = k2 + P2
- Compton formula: lambda2 - lambda1 = (h/mec)(1 - cos(theta)), where h is the Planck constant.

The Compton effect is important for understanding the interaction of high-energy photons with matter and the nature of light as both a wave and a particle.
