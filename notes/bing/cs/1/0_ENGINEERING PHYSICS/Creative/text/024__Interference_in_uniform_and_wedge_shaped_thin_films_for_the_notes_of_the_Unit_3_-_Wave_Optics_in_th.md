### Interference in uniform and wedge shaped thin films

- Interference is the phenomenon of superposition of two or more waves of the same frequency and same polarization, resulting in a new wave pattern.
- Interference can be observed in thin films of transparent materials, such as soap bubbles, oil films, or air films between glass plates.
- Thin films have two surfaces that reflect and refract light waves, creating multiple beams that can interfere with each other.
- The interference pattern depends on the thickness of the film, the wavelength of light, the angle of incidence, and the refractive indices of the film and the surrounding media.

#### Interference in uniform thin films

- A uniform thin film is a film of constant thickness and refractive index, bounded by two plane surfaces.
- A ray of monochromatic light incident on the film is partly reflected and partly refracted at both surfaces, creating two reflected rays and two refracted rays.
- The two reflected rays can interfere with each other, depending on the phase difference between them.
- The phase difference depends on two factors: the optical path difference and the phase change upon reflection.
- The optical path difference is the difference in the distances traveled by the two rays in the film and the surrounding media. It is given by:

  $$\Delta = 2t \cos r - \lambda \frac{n_1}{n_0} \sin i \sin r$$

  where $t$ is the thickness of the film, $r$ is the angle of refraction, $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light in vacuum, $n_0$ is the refractive index of the incident medium, $n_1$ is the refractive index of the film, and $i$ is the angle of incidence.
- The phase change upon reflection occurs when light reflects from a medium of higher refractive index than the incident medium. In this case, the reflected ray undergoes a phase shift of $\pi$ radians or 180 degrees. This is equivalent to adding a path difference of $\frac{\lambda}{2}$.
- The condition for constructive interference is:

  $$\Delta = m \lambda + \frac{\lambda}{2}$$

  where $m$ is an integer. This implies that the two rays have a phase difference of an odd multiple of $\pi$ radians, and their amplitudes add up.
- The condition for destructive interference is:

  $$\Delta = m \lambda$$

  where $m$ is an integer. This implies that the two rays have a phase difference of an even multiple of $\pi$ radians, and their amplitudes cancel out.
