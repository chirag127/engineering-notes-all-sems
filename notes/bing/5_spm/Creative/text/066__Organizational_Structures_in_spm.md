### Organizational Structures in SPM

Organizational structures in SPM (Supplier Relationship Management) are the ways in which the organization arranges its resources and activities to manage its relationships with its suppliers. Different organizational structures have different advantages and disadvantages for SPM, depending on the context and the goals of the organization.

Some of the common organizational structures for SPM are:

- **Functional/Hierarchical**: This structure is based on the division of labor and specialization of functions, such as procurement, finance, quality, etc. Each function has its own authority and responsibility for SPM activities, and reports to a higher-level manager. This structure is simple and clear, but it may create silos and conflicts among functions, and reduce the coordination and integration of SPM across the organization.

- **Matricial**: This structure is based on the combination of functional and project-based units, where each project has a cross-functional team of experts from different functions, led by a project manager. The project manager has the authority and responsibility for SPM activities related to the project, and reports to a higher-level manager. This structure is flexible and responsive, but it may create ambiguity and confusion about the roles and responsibilities of the project and functional managers, and increase the complexity and overhead of SPM.

- **RACI**: This structure is based on the assignment of four roles for each SPM activity: Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. The Responsible role is the person who performs the activity, the Accountable role is the person who approves and oversees the activity, the Consulted role is the person who provides input and feedback for the activity, and the Informed role is the person who is notified of the outcome of the activity. This structure is useful for clarifying and communicating the expectations and accountability of each stakeholder for SPM, but it may require a lot of coordination and communication, and it may not reflect the actual power and influence of the stakeholders.

- **Agile**: This structure is based on the adoption of agile principles and practices for SPM, such as self-organizing teams, iterative and incremental delivery, customer collaboration, and continuous improvement. The SPM team is composed of representatives from different functions and suppliers, who work together to deliver value to the customer and the organization. The SPM team has the authority and responsibility for SPM activities, and reports to a product owner or a sponsor. This structure is adaptive and innovative, but it may challenge the traditional culture and processes of the organization, and require a high level of trust and commitment from the team and the suppliers.
