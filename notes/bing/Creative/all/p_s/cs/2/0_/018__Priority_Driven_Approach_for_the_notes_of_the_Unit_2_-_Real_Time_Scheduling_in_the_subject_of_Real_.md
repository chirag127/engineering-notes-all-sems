- The priority driven approach is a class of scheduling algorithms that never leave any resource idle intentionally, unless there is no ready job that requires the resource.
- The priority driven approach is based on assigning a priority level to each task or job, and executing the highest priority job that is ready at any given time.
- The priority driven approach can be useful for dynamic real-time systems, where the system must adapt to changing conditions and events, and where some tasks are more important or urgent than others.
- The priority driven approach can be classified into two types: static priority and dynamic priority.
  - Static priority: the priority of each task or job is fixed and does not change during the execution. Examples of static priority algorithms are rate monotonic (RM), deadline monotonic (DM), and earliest deadline first (EDF).
  - Dynamic priority: the priority of each task or job can change during the execution, depending on some factors such as deadline, execution time, or resource availability. Examples of dynamic priority algorithms are least laxity first (LLF), least slack time (LST), and resource reservation (RR).

The priority driven approach can also provide better resource utilization, as it does not waste any idle time.
  - Disadvantages: the priority driven approach can suffer from priority inversion, which is a situation where a low priority job blocks a high priority job from executing, due to a shared resource. The priority driven approach can also suffer from unbounded priority inversion, which is a situation where a chain of low priority jobs blocks a high priority job indefinitely. The priority driven approach can also have higher overhead, as it requires more computation and context switching to determine the next job to execute.
