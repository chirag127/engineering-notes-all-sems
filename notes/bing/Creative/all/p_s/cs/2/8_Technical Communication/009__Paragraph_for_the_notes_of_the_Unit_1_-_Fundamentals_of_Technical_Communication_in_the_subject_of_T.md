### Paragraph

A paragraph is a group of related sentences that support one main idea. It is a basic unit of technical communication that can define, instruct, inform, or persuade the audience. A well-written paragraph has a clear structure, a coherent topic, and a consistent style.

#### Structure of a Paragraph

In general, paragraphs consist of three parts: the topic sentence, body sentences, and the concluding or the bridge sentence.

- The topic sentence introduces the main idea of the paragraph and tells the reader what the paragraph is about. It usually comes at the beginning of the paragraph, but it can also appear in the middle or at the end, depending on the purpose and context of the paragraph.
- The body sentences develop and support the main idea of the paragraph with evidence, examples, explanations, or arguments. They should be logically organized and connected with transitions, such as words, phrases, or sentences that show the relationship between the sentences.
- The concluding or the bridge sentence summarizes the main point of the paragraph and/or connects it to the next paragraph or section. It can also provide a recommendation, a prediction, a question, or a call to action, depending on the goal of the paragraph.

#### Example of a Paragraph

Here is an example of a paragraph from a technical report on the benefits of solar energy:

Solar energy is a renewable and clean source of power that can reduce greenhouse gas emissions and fossil fuel dependence. Unlike coal, oil, or natural gas, solar energy does not produce any harmful pollutants or greenhouse gases that contribute to global warming. Moreover, solar energy is abundant and inexhaustible, as the sun provides more than enough energy to meet the world's demand. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), the sun could provide more than 25% of global electricity by 2050, if the current trends of solar technology development and deployment continue. Therefore, solar energy is a viable and attractive option for addressing the global energy and environmental challenges.

Mnemonics and learning tricks can be very helpful for memorizing facts, concepts, or processes, as long as they are easy to remember and make sense to you. Some examples of mnemonics and learning tricks are:

- Acronyms: using the first letter of each word in a list or phrase to form a new word, such as ROYGBIV for the colors of the rainbow, or PEMDAS for the order of operations in math.
- Acrostics: using the first letter of each word in a list or phrase to form a new sentence, such as Every Good Boy Deserves Fudge for the notes on the treble clef, or My Very Eager Mother Just Served Us Nine Pizzas for the order of the planets.
- Rhymes: using words that sound similar to help you remember something, such as Thirty days hath September, April, June, and November, or In fourteen hundred ninety-two, Columbus sailed the ocean blue.
- Chunking: breaking down a large amount of information into smaller, more manageable groups, such as grouping the digits of a phone number into three parts, or using the first letter of each word to form a word, such as HOMES for the Great Lakes.
- Visualization: creating a mental image or story to help you remember something, such as imagining a giant spider web to remember the parts of a web page, or picturing a fish in a tank to remember the formula for volume.
