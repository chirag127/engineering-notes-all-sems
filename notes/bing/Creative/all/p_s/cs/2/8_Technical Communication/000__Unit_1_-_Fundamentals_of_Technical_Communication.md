## Unit 1 - Fundamentals of Technical Communication

Technical communication is the process of conveying information that is relevant, accurate, clear, concise, and user-centered to a specific audience for a specific purpose. Technical communication can take various forms, such as manuals, reports, proposals, memos, presentations, websites, blogs, podcasts, etc. Technical communication often involves communicating expert, technical, or highly complex information to a non-expert or more general audience. Technical communication often works to convince an audience to take some specific action. Technical communication might define, instruct, inform, or persuade.

The fundamentals of technical communication skills include the following:

- Understanding the target audience: Technical communicators need to analyze the needs, expectations, background, and preferences of their audience before creating any document or product. They need to tailor their message and tone to suit the audience's level of knowledge, interest, and attitude. They also need to consider the context and purpose of the communication, such as the goals, constraints, and expectations of the situation.
- Understanding technical communication products: Technical communicators need to be familiar with the different types of documents and products that they may create or use in their field, such as proposals, manuals, reports, memos, presentations, websites, blogs, podcasts, etc. They need to know the conventions, formats, styles, and standards of each type of product, and how to use them effectively and ethically. They also need to be aware of the advantages and disadvantages of each type of product, and how to choose the most appropriate one for their audience and purpose.
- Gathering technical knowledge: Technical communicators need to have a solid understanding of the subject matter that they are communicating, and be able to research, analyze, synthesize, and evaluate information from various sources. They need to be able to use appropriate methods and tools to collect, organize, and document data and evidence. They also need to be able to verify the accuracy, reliability, and validity of the information that they use and present.
- Background research before writing: Technical communicators need to conduct a thorough background research before writing any document or product, to ensure that they have a clear and comprehensive understanding of the topic, the audience, the purpose, and the context of the communication. They need to identify the main objectives, questions, problems, and solutions that they want to address in their communication. They also need to review the existing literature, sources, and documents related to the topic, and identify the gaps, limitations, and opportunities for improvement.
- Adopting communication skills: Technical communicators need to develop and apply various communication skills, such as writing, speaking, listening, reading, and visualizing, to create and deliver effective and engaging technical communication products. They need to use clear, concise, precise, and coherent language, and avoid jargon, ambiguity, and errors. They need to use appropriate tone, style, and voice, and adapt them to the audience and purpose. They need to use effective strategies and techniques to organize, structure, and format their communication products, and to emphasize the main points, arguments, and evidence. They also need to use appropriate and relevant visual aids, such as graphs, charts, tables, diagrams, images, etc., to enhance and complement their verbal communication.
- The writing tools: Technical communicators need to be proficient in using various writing tools, such as word processors, spreadsheets, presentation software, web design software, etc., to create and edit their communication products. They need to know how to use the features and functions of these tools, such as formatting, editing, proofreading, spell-checking, grammar-checking, etc., to improve the quality and appearance of their communication products. They also need to know how to use these tools to collaborate with other writers, reviewers, and stakeholders, and to share and distribute their communication products.

- Acronyms: Using the first letter of each word in a phrase or list to form a new word. For example, ROYGBIV is an acronym for the colors of the rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.
- Acrostics: Using the first letter of each word in a phrase or list to form a sentence. For example, Every Good Boy Deserves Fudge is an acrostic for the notes on the treble clef: E, G, B, D, F.
- Rhymes: Using words that sound alike to help you remember information. For example, In 1492, Columbus sailed the ocean blue is a rhyme for the year that Christopher Columbus discovered America.
- Chunking: Breaking down a large piece of information into smaller, manageable units. For example, to remember a phone number, you can chunk it into three parts: area code, prefix, and suffix.
- Visualization: Creating a mental image of the information you want to remember. For example, to remember the names of the planets in order from the sun, you can visualize a scene with Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.
- Peg words: Using a list of words that rhyme with numbers to help you remember a sequence of items. For example, to remember the order of the presidents, you can use the peg words: one is a bun, two is a shoe, three is a tree, four is a door, etc. Then, you can associate each president with a peg word. For example, one is a bun, Washington; two is a shoe, Adams; three is a tree, Jefferson; etc.
- Method of loci: Using a familiar place or route to help you remember a sequence of items. For example, to remember the order of the presidents, you can use your house as a method of loci. You can imagine walking through your house and seeing each president in a different room. For example, you can see Washington in the living room, Adams in the kitchen, Jefferson in the bathroom, etc.
