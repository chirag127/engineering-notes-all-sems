### Techniques for the notes of the Unit 3 - Technical Presentation in the subject of Technical Communication

A technical presentation is a type of oral communication that aims to convey technical information or concepts to a specific audience, such as engineers, scientists, managers, or customers. Technical presentations require careful planning, preparation, and delivery to achieve the desired objectives and outcomes. Some of the techniques for the notes of the technical presentation are:

- **Identify the main subject and the purpose of the presentation.** The first step is to define the topic and the goal of the presentation, such as informing, persuading, or demonstrating something. This will help to narrow down the scope and focus of the presentation, and to tailor it to the needs and expectations of the audience.
- **Create an outline and structure the presentation.** The second step is to organize the presentation into three main sections: introduction, body, and conclusion. The introduction should capture the attention of the audience, provide some background information, and state the main points or the thesis statement. The body should develop the main points with supporting evidence, examples, or data, and use transitions to connect them logically. The conclusion should summarize the main points, restate the thesis statement, and provide a call to action or a recommendation.
- **Use visual aids and the 10/20/30 rule.** The third step is to enhance the presentation with visual aids, such as slides, graphs, charts, diagrams, or images, that complement the verbal message and make it easier to understand and remember. The 10/20/30 rule, proposed by Guy Kawasaki, suggests that a technical presentation should have no more than 10 slides, last no more than 20 minutes, and use a minimum of 30 font size. This rule helps to keep the presentation concise, clear, and engaging.
- **Practice and record yourself.** The fourth step is to rehearse the presentation several times, preferably in front of a mirror, a friend, or a colleague, and record yourself using a camera or a voice recorder. This will help to improve the delivery, the timing, the tone, the pace, and the body language of the presentation, and to identify and correct any mistakes or weaknesses.
- **Get feedback and revise.** The fifth step is to seek feedback from others, such as peers, mentors, or instructors, and use it to revise and refine the presentation. Feedback can help to improve the content, the structure, the style, and the delivery of the presentation, and to address any questions or concerns that the audience may have.
- **Prepare for the Q&A session.** The sixth step is to anticipate and prepare for the questions and answers session that usually follows a technical presentation. This involves researching the topic, the audience, and the context of the presentation, and preparing some possible questions and answers in advance. It also involves listening carefully to the questions, clarifying them if needed, and answering them confidently and respectfully.
- **Use open body language and eye contact.** The seventh and final step is to use open body language and eye contact to establish rapport and credibility with the audience, and to convey confidence and enthusiasm. Open body language includes smiling, nodding, gesturing, and moving around the stage or the room. Eye contact involves looking at the audience members, not at the slides or the notes, and shifting the gaze from one person to another.

Mnemonics are memory tricks that can help you remember new information by connecting it to something you already know. There are different types of mnemonics, such as rhymes, acronyms, diagrams, or keywords. For example, you can use the acronym HOMES to remember the names of the Great Lakes: Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, and Superior. Or you can use the rhyme "Thirty days hath September, April, June, and November" to remember how many days are in each month. 

Some mnemonics are easy to remember because they are catchy, funny, or make sense. Others may be harder to remember because they are too long, complicated, or don't relate to the topic. The best mnemonics are the ones that work for you, so you may want to create your own or modify the ones you find online. The more you practice and repeat your mnemonics, the more likely you are to remember them.
