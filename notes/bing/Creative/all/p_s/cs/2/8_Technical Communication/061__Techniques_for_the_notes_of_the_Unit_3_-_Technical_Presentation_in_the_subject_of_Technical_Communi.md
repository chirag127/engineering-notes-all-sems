### Techniques for the notes of the Unit 3 - Technical Presentation in the subject of Technical Communication

A technical presentation is a form of oral communication that aims to convey technical information or ideas to a specific audience. Technical presentations can be informative, persuasive, or demonstrative, depending on the purpose and context of the presentation. Some common features of technical presentations are:

- They use technical language and terminology that are appropriate for the audience and the topic.
- They rely on visual aids, such as graphs, charts, diagrams, tables, or images, to support the verbal message and enhance the understanding of the audience.
- They follow a clear and logical structure, such as introduction, body, and conclusion, to organize the main points and subpoints of the presentation.
- They include transitions, signposts, and summaries to guide the audience through the presentation and highlight the key takeaways.
- They use rhetorical techniques, such as ethos, pathos, and logos, to establish credibility, appeal to emotions, and provide evidence and reasoning for the arguments.

Some techniques for the notes of technical presentations are:

- Identify the main subject and provide a summary of its broad definition and why it has become the subject of the presentation.
- Create an outline to distill your message into 3-5 key points that you want the audience to remember.
- Use the 10/20/30 rule, which suggests that a technical presentation should have 10 slides, last 20 minutes, and use a minimum of 30 font size, to force yourself to be concise and avoid information overload.
- Brainstorm and use visual aids that are relevant, clear, and simple to illustrate your points and make your presentation more engaging and memorable.
- Structure your presentation into three main parts: introduction, body, and conclusion. In the introduction, capture the attention of the audience, state the purpose and objectives of the presentation, and provide an overview of the main points. In the body, develop each point with supporting details, examples, and evidence. In the conclusion, summarize the main points, restate the purpose and objectives, and provide a call to action or a recommendation.
- Record yourself and practice your presentation several times, preferably in front of a mirror or a friend, to improve your delivery, timing, and confidence.
- Seek feedback from others and revise your presentation accordingly to address any gaps, errors, or weaknesses.
- Use open body language, such as eye contact, gestures, and facial expressions, to convey enthusiasm, confidence, and rapport with the audience.

Mnemonics and learning tricks can be very helpful for memorizing and recalling information, especially if they are catchy, funny, or meaningful to you. Some examples of mnemonics and learning tricks are:

- Acronyms: using the first letter of each word in a list or phrase to form a new word, such as ROYGBIV for the colors of the rainbow (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet).
- Acrostics: using the first letter of each word in a list or phrase to form a new sentence, such as Every Good Boy Deserves Fudge for the notes on the treble clef (E, G, B, D, F).
- Rhymes: using words that sound similar to create a memorable phrase, such as In 1492, Columbus sailed the ocean blue.
- Chunking: breaking down a large amount of information into smaller, manageable units, such as grouping digits in a phone number or a social security number.
- Visualization: creating a mental image or a story that connects the information you want to remember, such as imagining a giant spider web to remember the parts of a web page (header, footer, sidebar, content, etc.).
- Association: linking the information you want to remember with something you already know, such as using the word HOMES to remember the names of the Great Lakes (Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, Superior).
