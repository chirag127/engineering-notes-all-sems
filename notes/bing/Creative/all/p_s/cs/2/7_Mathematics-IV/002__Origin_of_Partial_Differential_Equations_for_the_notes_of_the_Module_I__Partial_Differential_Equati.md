### Origin of Partial Differential Equations

- Partial differential equations (PDEs) are equations that involve partial derivatives of functions of several variables.
- PDEs arise naturally in many fields of science and engineering, such as physics, chemistry, biology, fluid mechanics, elasticity, electromagnetism, etc.
- The history of PDEs can be traced back to the 17th century, when some of the first PDEs were discovered by mathematicians such as Isaac Newton, Gottfried Leibniz, Leonhard Euler, and Joseph-Louis Lagrange.
- Some of the earliest PDEs were related to geometric problems, such as finding the shortest curve between two points (the brachistochrone problem), or finding the curve that minimizes the area of a surface of revolution (the catenary problem).
- These problems led to the development of the calculus of variations, which is a branch of mathematics that deals with finding extrema of functionals, which are functions of functions.
- Another source of PDEs was the study of physical phenomena, such as heat conduction, wave propagation, fluid flow, elasticity, gravity, and electricity and magnetism.
- These phenomena can be modeled by PDEs that describe how the state of a system changes over space and time, such as the heat equation, the wave equation, the Laplace equation, the Poisson equation, the Navier-Stokes equations, etc.
- The solution of these PDEs can provide insight into the behavior and properties of the physical system, such as the temperature distribution, the sound intensity, the electric potential, the fluid velocity, etc.
- The solution of PDEs can also be used to test and validate physical theories and laws, such as the conservation of energy, the principle of least action, the Maxwell equations, etc.
- The solution of PDEs can be challenging, as they often involve complicated and nonlinear expressions, boundary and initial conditions, and multiple dimensions.
- Some PDEs can be solved analytically, by using methods such as separation of variables, Fourier series, Laplace transform, Green's function, etc.
- Other PDEs can only be solved numerically, by using methods such as finite difference, finite element, spectral, etc.
- The study of PDEs is an active and rich area of mathematics, with many open problems and applications.

Some possible mnemonics and learning tricks for the topic are:

- To remember the order of the most common types of PDEs, use the acronym **HELP**:

  - **H**yperbolic: second order PDEs with two real and distinct characteristic roots, such as the wave equation.
  - **E**lliptic: second order PDEs with no real characteristic roots, such as the Laplace equation.
  - **L**aplace: a special case of elliptic PDEs, where the coefficients are constant and the highest order terms have equal coefficients, such as the Laplace equation.
  - **P**arabolic: second order PDEs with one real and repeated characteristic root, such as the heat equation.

- To remember the formula for the Laplace transform of a function f(t), use the acronym **LIFT**:

  - **L**aplace transform: L{f(t)} = F(s)
  - **I**ntegral: F(s) = integral from 0 to infinity of f(t) * e^(-st) dt
  - **F**unction: f(t) is the function to be transformed
  - **T**ime: t is the time variable

- To remember the formula for the Fourier series of a periodic function f(x) with period 2L, use the acronym **FAT**:

  - **F**ourier series: f(x) = a0/2 + sum from n=1 to infinity of (an * cos(n * pi * x / L) + bn * sin(n * pi * x / L))
  - **A**n: an = (1/L) * integral from -L to L of f(x) * cos(n * pi * x / L) dx
  - **B**n: bn = (1/L) * integral from -L to L of f(x) * sin(n * pi * x / L) dx
