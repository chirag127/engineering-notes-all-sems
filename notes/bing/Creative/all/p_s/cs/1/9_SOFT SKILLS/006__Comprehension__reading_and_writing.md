### Comprehension, reading and writing

- Comprehension is the ability to understand and interpret written or spoken texts.
- Reading comprehension is the process of extracting meaning from written texts by connecting them to one's prior knowledge and experience, using various strategies and skills.
- Writing comprehension is the process of expressing one's thoughts and ideas in written texts, using appropriate language, structure, and conventions.
- Comprehension, reading and writing are interrelated and mutually reinforcing skills that are essential for academic success and lifelong learning.
- Some of the benefits of developing comprehension, reading and writing skills are:
  - They enhance one's critical thinking, creativity, and communication abilities.
  - They enable one to access, analyze, and evaluate information from various sources and perspectives.
  - They foster one's curiosity, interest, and enjoyment of learning.
  - They prepare one for various academic, professional, and personal situations and challenges.

- Some of the strategies and skills that can improve one's comprehension, reading and writing are:
  - Before reading or writing, activate prior knowledge, set a purpose, and make predictions.
  - During reading or writing, monitor comprehension, ask and answer questions, and use context clues.
  - After reading or writing, summarize, synthesize, and reflect on the main ideas and messages.
  - Use graphic organizers, such as charts, diagrams, and maps, to organize and visualize information.
  - Use different types of texts, such as fiction, nonfiction, poetry, and multimedia, to explore various topics and genres.
  - Use various resources, such as dictionaries, thesauruses, and online tools, to expand vocabulary and improve grammar and spelling.
  - Practice reading and writing regularly and frequently, and seek feedback and guidance from others.

Some of the mnemonics and learning tricks for comprehension, reading and writing are:

- Keyword mnemonics: This strategy involves using a word or phrase that sounds similar to the new word or concept and associating it with a visual image. For example, to remember that the word "alacrity" means "eagerness", one can use the keyword "alligator" and imagine an alligator eagerly chasing its prey.
- Rhyming mnemonics: This strategy involves using a rhyme or a song to remember a list of words or a rule. For example, to remember the spelling rule "i before e except after c", one can use the rhyme "i before e except after c, or when sounded like a as in neighbor and weigh".
- Acrostic mnemonics: This strategy involves using the first letter of each word in a list or a sentence to form a new word or phrase. For example, to remember the order of operations in algebra, one can use the acrostic "Please Excuse My Dear Aunt Sally", which stands for Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication, Division, Addition, and Subtraction.
- Acronym mnemonics: This strategy involves using the first letter of each word in a list or a sentence to form a new word that can be pronounced. For example, to remember the names of the Great Lakes, one can use the acronym "HOMES", which stands for Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, and Superior.
- Music mnemonics: This strategy involves setting a list of words or a rule to a familiar tune or a catchy song. For example, to remember the alphabet, one can use the "ABC" song. To remember the states in alphabetical order, one can use the "50 Nifty United States" song.
- Name mnemonics: This strategy involves using the first letter of each word in a list or a sentence to form a name of a person or a thing. For example, to remember the colors of the rainbow, one can use the name "Roy G. Biv", which stands for Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, and Violet.
- Graphic mnemonics: This strategy involves using a visual representation, such as a chart, a diagram, or a map, to organize and recall information. For example, to remember the parts of a story, one can use a story map that shows the setting, the characters, the problem, the events, and the solution.
- Verbal mnemonics: This strategy involves using a verbal cue, such as a definition, a synonym, or an antonym, to remember a word or a concept. For example, to remember that the word "benevolent" means "kind", one can use the verbal cue "benevolent is the opposite of malevolent".
- Auditory mnemonics: This strategy involves using a sound or a noise, such as a clap, a snap, or a whistle, to remember a word or a concept. For example, to remember that the letter "c" makes the /k/ sound, one can use the auditory cue "c is for clap" and clap while saying the sound.
