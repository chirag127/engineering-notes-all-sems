# Write in detail

Writing in detail means to give many examples or cover many aspects of a subject. It is a way of showing your thorough understanding and knowledge of a topic. Writing in detail can help you create a vivid and engaging story, essay, report, or any other type of text. Here are some tips on how to write in detail:

- Use the five senses. When describing a scene, a person, an object, or an emotion, try to include details of sight, sound, touch, smell, and taste. This can help your reader imagine and feel what you are writing about. For example, instead of writing "She was scared", you could write "She felt a cold sweat on her forehead, her heart pounding in her chest, and her mouth dry as dust. She heard a faint scratching sound behind the door, and smelled something rotten and metallic."
- Use specific and concrete words. Avoid vague and abstract words that can have different meanings for different readers. Choose words that are precise and clear, and that convey your exact meaning. For example, instead of writing "He was a nice guy", you could write "He was a generous and kind-hearted person who always helped others in need."
- Use comparisons and contrasts. When explaining a concept, a process, or a phenomenon, you can use comparisons and contrasts to make it easier for your reader to understand. You can compare something to something else that is similar or familiar, or contrast something to something else that is different or opposite. For example, instead of writing "The sun is a star", you could write "The sun is a star, just like the ones we see in the night sky, but much bigger and closer to us. The sun is so bright and hot that it makes life possible on Earth, while most other stars are too dim and cold to support any living beings."
- Use examples and evidence. When making a claim, an argument, or a point, you should always support it with examples and evidence. Examples and evidence can be facts, statistics, quotations, anecdotes, or observations that back up your statement. They can help you persuade, inform, or entertain your reader. For example, instead of writing "Reading is good for you", you could write "Reading is good for you because it improves your vocabulary, your memory, your critical thinking, and your creativity. According to a study by the University of Oxford, reading for just 30 minutes a day can reduce your stress levels by 68%, and increase your happiness by 21%."

# Mnemonics and learning tricks

Mnemonics and learning tricks are techniques that can help you remember information more easily and effectively. They can be words, phrases, sentences, images, or actions that link the information you want to remember to something that is more familiar or memorable. Here are some examples of mnemonics and learning tricks for different subjects:

- For the order of operations in mathematics, you can use the phrase "Please Excuse My Dear Aunt Sally" to remember the sequence of Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication, Division, Addition, and Subtraction.
- For the colors of the rainbow, you can use the name "Roy G. Biv" to remember the order of Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, and Violet.
- For the planets of the solar system, you can use the sentence "My Very Eager Mother Just Served Us Nine Pizzas" to remember the order of Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, and Pluto.
- For the names of the Great Lakes in North America, you can use the word "HOMES" to remember the initials of Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, and Superior.
- For the musical notes on the lines of the treble clef, you can use the sentence "Every Good Boy Deserves Fudge" to remember the order of E, G, B, D, and F.
- For the spelling of the word "arithmetic", you can use the phrase "A Rat In The House May Eat The Ice Cream" to remember the letters A, R, I, T, H, M, E, T, and I.
