### Light-Emitting Diodes

- A light-emitting diode (LED) is a semiconductor device that emits light when current flows through it.
- LEDs are made from extrinsic semiconductors, usually with a p-n junction diode structure.
- When a forward bias voltage is applied to the p-n junction, electrons from the n-type region and holes from the p-type region are injected into the depletion region.
- Some of the electrons and holes recombine in the depletion region, releasing energy in the form of photons.
- The wavelength of the emitted light depends on the energy band gap of the semiconductor material, which can be varied by using different dopants or alloys.
- LEDs can emit light across the ultraviolet, visible, and infrared spectrums, depending on the semiconductor material and the doping level.
- LEDs have many advantages over conventional light sources, such as low power consumption, long lifetime, high efficiency, fast switching, and environmental friendliness.
- LEDs have many applications in various fields, such as displays, indicators, lighting, communication, sensing, and medical devices.

Some examples of LED devices are:

- Digital clocks, calculators, and watches that use seven-segment displays or dot-matrix displays.
- Remote controls, optical mice, and fiber-optic communication systems that use infrared LEDs.
- Traffic lights, brake lights, and emergency lights that use red, yellow, and green LEDs.
- Flashlights, lamps, and decorative lights that use white LEDs or RGB LEDs.
- Phototherapy, pulse oximetry, and endoscopy that use blue, red, and green LEDs respectively.
