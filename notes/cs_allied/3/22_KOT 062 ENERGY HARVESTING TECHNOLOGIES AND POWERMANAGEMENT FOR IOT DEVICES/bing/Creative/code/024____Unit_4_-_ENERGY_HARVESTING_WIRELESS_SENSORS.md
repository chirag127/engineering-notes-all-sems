## Unit 4 - Energy Harvesting Wireless Sensors

- Energy harvesting wireless sensors are devices that can collect ambient energy from the environment and convert it into electrical power for wireless communication and sensing applications.
- Energy harvesting wireless sensors can operate without batteries or wires, which reduces maintenance costs and environmental impact, and enables new applications in remote or inaccessible areas.
- Energy harvesting wireless sensors can use various sources of ambient energy, such as solar, thermal, vibration, wind, radio frequency, and human motion.
- Energy harvesting wireless sensors consist of four main components: an energy harvester, an energy storage unit, a sensor, and a wireless transceiver.
- An energy harvester is a device that converts ambient energy into electrical power. The output power of an energy harvester depends on the type and intensity of the energy source, the conversion efficiency, and the load impedance.
- An energy storage unit is a device that stores the electrical power generated by the energy harvester and provides a stable supply for the sensor and the wireless transceiver. The energy storage unit can be a capacitor, a battery, or a supercapacitor.
- A sensor is a device that measures a physical or chemical property of interest, such as temperature, humidity, pressure, light, gas, or sound. The sensor can be passive or active, depending on whether it requires external power or not.
- A wireless transceiver is a device that transmits and receives data wirelessly using radio waves. The wireless transceiver can use different protocols and standards, such as Bluetooth, ZigBee, Wi-Fi, or LoRaWAN. The wireless transceiver consumes the most power among the components of an energy harvesting wireless sensor.
- Energy harvesting wireless sensors face several challenges, such as low and intermittent power availability, power management, data quality, security, and scalability. Various techniques and methods have been proposed to address these challenges, such as adaptive duty cycling, power-aware routing, data compression, encryption, and clustering.
