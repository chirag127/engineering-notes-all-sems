# Case Studies for the Notes of the Unit 4 - Energy Harvesting Wireless Sensors

Energy harvesting wireless sensors (EHWS) are devices that can collect ambient energy from various sources such as light, heat, vibration, or radio frequency, and use it to power their sensing and communication functions. EHWS can offer many advantages over conventional battery-powered sensors, such as longer lifetime, reduced maintenance, lower cost, and environmental friendliness. EHWS can be applied to various domains, such as industrial, environmental, medical, and smart city applications. Here are some examples of case studies that illustrate the use and benefits of EHWS in different scenarios.

## Case Study 1: Using Energy Harvesting and Radio Technologies to Power Wireless Sensors in Industrial Applications

This case study describes how energy harvesting and radio technologies can be used to power wireless sensors in industrial applications, such as monitoring the condition of machines, equipment, and processes. The main challenges in this domain are the harsh and dynamic environment, the high data rate and reliability requirements, and the limited access and space for battery replacement. The case study presents two solutions that address these challenges:

- The first solution is based on vibration energy harvesting, which converts the mechanical motion of machines into electrical energy. The vibration energy harvester (VEH) consists of a piezoelectric element, a rectifier, and a storage capacitor. The VEH can provide enough power to operate a wireless sensor node that measures acceleration, temperature, and humidity, and transmits the data using Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) protocol. The VEH can harvest up to 100 microwatts of power from a vibration frequency of 50 Hz and an amplitude of 0.5 g. The wireless sensor node can operate continuously with a data rate of 1 Hz and a transmission range of 10 meters.

- The second solution is based on radio frequency energy harvesting, which converts the electromagnetic waves from nearby sources, such as WiFi routers, cellular base stations, or TV transmitters, into electrical energy. The radio frequency energy harvester (RFEH) consists of an antenna, a rectifier, and a storage capacitor. The RFEH can provide enough power to operate a wireless sensor node that measures temperature and humidity, and transmits the data using ZigBee protocol. The RFEH can harvest up to 10 microwatts of power from a radio frequency of 2.4 GHz and a power density of -10 dBm. The wireless sensor node can operate intermittently with a data rate of 0.1 Hz and a transmission range of 100 meters.

The case study demonstrates that energy harvesting and radio technologies can enable maintenance-free wireless sensors for industrial applications, with different trade-offs between power, data rate, and transmission range, depending on the energy source and the communication protocol.

## Case Study 2: A Study on Piezoelectric Energy-Harvesting Wireless Sensor Networks Deployed in a Weak Vibration Environment

This case study investigates the performance of piezoelectric energy-harvesting wireless sensor networks (PEHWSNs) deployed in a weak vibration environment, such as a bridge or a building. The main challenges in this domain are the low and irregular vibration intensity, the high energy consumption of the wireless sensor nodes, and the network scalability and reliability. The case study presents a PEHWSN that addresses these challenges:

- The PEHWSN consists of multiple wireless sensor nodes that measure strain and acceleration, and a base station that collects and analyzes the data. Each wireless sensor node is equipped with a piezoelectric energy harvester, a rechargeable battery, a microcontroller, a transceiver, and a sensor. The piezoelectric energy harvester converts the vibration energy into electrical energy, and charges the battery. The microcontroller controls the sensing and communication operations, and adjusts the duty cycle and the transmission power according to the battery level and the network conditions. The transceiver uses a low-power and long-range protocol, such as LoRa, to communicate with the base station. The sensor measures the strain and acceleration of the structure, and sends the data to the base station.
