### Survey routing protocols for IoT

- Routing protocols are responsible for finding and maintaining routes between nodes in a network, especially in low-power and lossy networks (LLNs) that are common in IoT applications.
- Routing protocols for IoT must consider the challenges and requirements of IoT networks, such as heterogeneity, scalability, mobility, energy efficiency, security, and quality of service (QoS).
- There are different types of routing protocols for IoT, depending on the network topology, routing strategy, and objective function. Some of the common types are  :
  - **Flat routing protocols**: These protocols do not use any hierarchy or clustering among the nodes, and treat all nodes equally. They are suitable for small and homogeneous networks, but may suffer from scalability and overhead issues in large and heterogeneous networks. Examples of flat routing protocols are AODV, DSR, and OLSR.
  - **Hierarchical routing protocols**: These protocols divide the network into clusters or groups of nodes, and assign different roles and responsibilities to the nodes based on their cluster membership. They are suitable for large and heterogeneous networks, but may suffer from cluster formation and maintenance issues. Examples of hierarchical routing protocols are LEACH, PEGASIS, and HEED.
  - **Geographic routing protocols**: These protocols use the location information of the nodes to make routing decisions, and do not rely on any topology or network state information. They are suitable for mobile and dynamic networks, but may suffer from location accuracy and privacy issues. Examples of geographic routing protocols are GPSR, GEAR, and GAF.
  - **Data-centric routing protocols**: These protocols use the content or attributes of the data to make routing decisions, and do not rely on any address or identity information of the nodes. They are suitable for query-based and event-driven applications, but may suffer from data redundancy and aggregation issues. Examples of data-centric routing protocols are SPIN, Directed Diffusion, and COUGAR.
- The IETF has standardized the IPv6 Routing Protocol for Low-Power and Lossy Networks (RPL) as the main routing protocol for IoT networks. RPL is a distance vector protocol that uses a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) as the routing topology, and supports both point-to-point and point-to-multipoint communication .
- RPL has several advantages, such as support for IPv6, multiple objective functions, loop avoidance, and local repair. However, RPL also has several limitations, such as lack of mobility support, security vulnerabilities, and overhead issues .
- Several extensions and modifications have been proposed to improve RPL performance and security, such as MRPL, RPL-PSO, RPL-SEC, and RPL-DSR .
- Other routing protocols that have been proposed or adapted for IoT networks include 6LoWPAN, CoAP, MQTT, XMPP, and AMQP .
