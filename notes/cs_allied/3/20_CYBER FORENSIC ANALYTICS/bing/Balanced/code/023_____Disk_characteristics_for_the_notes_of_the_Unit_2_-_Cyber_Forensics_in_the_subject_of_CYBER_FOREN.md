### Disk characteristics

- A disk is a circular device that stores and retrieves data on a computer.
- A disk can be either magnetic or optical, depending on the type of material and technology used to store data.
- Magnetic disks use a thin layer of magnetic material to store data as tiny magnetized regions called bits. Optical disks use a laser beam to store data as tiny pits or bumps on a reflective surface.
- Some common types of magnetic disks are hard disk drives (HDDs), floppy disks, and zip disks. Some common types of optical disks are compact discs (CDs), digital versatile discs (DVDs), and Blu-ray discs (BDs).
- Disks have several characteristics that affect their performance and capacity, such as:

  - Size: The physical diameter of the disk. Larger disks can store more data, but may also be slower and more prone to damage.
  - Density: The amount of data that can be stored on a unit area of the disk. Higher density disks can store more data, but may also require more precise and sensitive read/write mechanisms.
  - Tracks: The concentric circles on the disk surface where data is stored. Each track is divided into sectors, which are the smallest units of data that can be accessed by the disk.
  - Cylinders: The set of tracks that are aligned vertically across the disk platters. A disk may have one or more platters, which are the thin metal or glass plates that make up the disk.
  - Heads: The devices that read and write data on the disk surface. A disk may have one or more heads, which are attached to an arm that moves across the disk.
  - Spindle speed: The rotational speed of the disk. Higher spindle speed disks can access data faster, but may also generate more heat and noise.
  - Seek time: The time it takes for the head to move to the desired track on the disk. Lower seek time disks can access data faster, but may also consume more power and wear out faster.
  - Transfer rate: The speed at which data can be transferred between the disk and the computer. Higher transfer rate disks can process data faster, but may also require more bandwidth and compatibility.

- Disks are important for cyber forensics because they can contain valuable evidence of digital activities, such as files, logs, metadata, deleted data, and hidden data.
- Cyber forensic analysts use various tools and techniques to acquire, analyze, and present disk data as evidence, such as:

  - Disk imaging: The process of creating an exact copy of the disk data, including the unused and unallocated space, for forensic analysis.
  - Disk cloning: The process of creating a functional duplicate of the disk data, including the operating system and applications, for forensic testing.
  - Disk hashing: The process of generating a unique numerical value, called a hash, that represents the disk data, for forensic verification and integrity.
  - Disk carving: The process of recovering data from the disk that is not referenced by the file system, such as deleted or fragmented files, for forensic recovery.
  - Disk encryption: The process of protecting the disk data from unauthorized access, using a secret key or password, for forensic security.
  - Disk wiping: The process of erasing the disk data, using various methods, such as overwriting, degaussing, or destroying, for forensic sanitization.
