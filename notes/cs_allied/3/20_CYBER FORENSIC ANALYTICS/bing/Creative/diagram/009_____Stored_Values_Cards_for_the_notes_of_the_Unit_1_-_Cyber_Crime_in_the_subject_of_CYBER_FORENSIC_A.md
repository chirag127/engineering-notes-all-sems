# Stored Values Cards

- Stored value cards (SVCs) are cards with data encoded in either a magnetic strip or a computer chip that are preloaded with a fixed amount of electronic currency or value.
- SVCs can be classified into three types: closed system, semi-closed system, and open system.
- Closed system cards are issued by a single merchant or group of merchants and can only be used to purchase goods or services from them. Examples are gift cards, phone cards, and transit cards.
- Semi-closed system cards are issued by a non-financial entity and can be used to purchase goods or services from a network of merchants that accept the card. Examples are loyalty cards, campus cards, and payroll cards.
- Open system cards are issued by a financial institution and can be used to purchase goods or services from any merchant that accepts the card. Examples are prepaid debit cards, travel cards, and remittance cards.
- SVCs offer convenience, security, and flexibility to consumers and merchants, but they also pose money laundering and cybercrime risks .
- The widespread availability of SVCs (particularly at non-financial outlets), the high loading and card balance value limits of open system cards, and the anonymity offered by closed and semi-closed system cards could be abused by organised criminals for illicit financial transactions, money laundering and bulk cash smuggling, particularly as value limits increase.
- SVCs have been identified in several reports as a potential tool for organised crime groups to launder their illicit crime proceeds.
- A study on cross-border electronic funds transfer systems raises similar concerns: "The use of stored value cards to move funds across borders is a relatively new phenomenon, but one that is likely to grow in the future. Stored value cards are attractive to money launderers because they are easy to obtain, can store large amounts of value, and can be used anonymously. They also allow money launderers to avoid the reporting and record-keeping requirements that apply to other forms of cross-border funds transfers."
- Cybercriminals can also exploit SVCs to commit fraud, theft, and counterfeiting. For example, cybercriminals can compromise a legitimate user’s account credentials and transfer funds to a SVC, or counterfeit a SVC and use it to make purchases or withdraw cash.
- The US Secret Service (USSS) operates Electronic Crimes Task Forces (ECTFs) to assist local law enforcement in investigating computer crimes, including those involving SVCs.
- The US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has also issued regulations to implement the provisions of the USA PATRIOT Act that require financial institutions to establish anti-money laundering programs and report suspicious activities involving SVCs.
- The Australian Institute of Criminology (AIC) has published a paper that describes the nature of the money laundering risks of SVCs and considers whether existing regulatory measures are adequate to address them.
- The paper concludes that "there is a need for a more comprehensive and coordinated approach to the regulation of stored value cards, both at the domestic and international levels. This would involve enhancing the existing anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing regime, as well as developing new measures to address the specific risks posed by stored value cards."
