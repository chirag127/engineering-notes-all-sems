### Search Strategies

- A search strategy is a method for finding a solution to a problem by exploring the space of possible states and actions.
- A search strategy consists of two components: a search algorithm and a search heuristic.
- A search algorithm is a systematic procedure that defines how to generate and expand the nodes of a search tree or graph.
- A search heuristic is a function that estimates the cost or quality of a node or a path in the search space, and guides the search algorithm towards the most promising directions.
- There are different types of search strategies, depending on the characteristics of the search space and the goal of the problem.
- Some of the common types of search strategies are:

  - Uninformed search: a search strategy that does not use any domain-specific knowledge or heuristic information, and relies only on the problem definition and the search algorithm. Examples of uninformed search algorithms are breadth-first search, depth-first search, uniform-cost search, iterative deepening search, and bidirectional search.
  - Informed search: a search strategy that uses heuristic information to guide the search algorithm towards the goal state, and to prune the search space by eliminating unpromising nodes or paths. Examples of informed search algorithms are greedy search, A* search, hill-climbing search, beam search, and genetic algorithms.
  - Local search: a search strategy that operates on a single current state, rather than a search tree or graph, and moves to a neighboring state that improves the objective function. Local search algorithms are useful for finding approximate solutions to optimization problems, where the goal is to maximize or minimize a function. Examples of local search algorithms are hill-climbing search, simulated annealing, tabu search, and genetic algorithms.
  - Adversarial search: a search strategy that considers the actions of other agents or opponents, and tries to find the best move for oneself in a competitive or cooperative game. Adversarial search algorithms are based on the minimax principle, which assumes that each player tries to minimize the maximum possible loss or maximize the minimum possible gain. Examples of adversarial search algorithms are minimax search, alpha-beta pruning, expectimax search, and Monte Carlo tree search.
