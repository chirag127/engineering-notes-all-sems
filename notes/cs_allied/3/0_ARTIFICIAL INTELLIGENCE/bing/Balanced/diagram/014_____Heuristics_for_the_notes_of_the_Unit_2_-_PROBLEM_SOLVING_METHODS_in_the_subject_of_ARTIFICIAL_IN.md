### Heuristics

- Heuristics are rules of thumb or shortcuts that guide the search for solutions in problem solving.
- Heuristics can reduce the search space and speed up the search process, but they do not guarantee optimal or complete solutions.
- Heuristics can be domain-specific or domain-independent, depending on whether they apply to a particular type of problem or to any problem in general.
- Some examples of domain-specific heuristics are:
  - In chess, prefer moves that capture the opponent's pieces or threaten the opponent's king.
  - In crossword puzzles, try filling in the longest words first or the words with the most uncommon letters.
  - In traveling salesman problem, start from the nearest city or the city with the lowest cost.
- Some examples of domain-independent heuristics are:
  - Hill climbing: Choose the action that leads to the highest increase in the objective function or the lowest decrease in the cost function.
  - Best first search: Expand the node that has the lowest value of a heuristic function that estimates the distance to the goal.
  - Simulated annealing: Randomly choose an action with a probability that depends on the difference in the objective function or the cost function and a decreasing temperature parameter.
- Heuristics can be evaluated based on their effectiveness, efficiency, and admissibility.
  - Effectiveness measures how often the heuristic leads to a solution or a good solution.
  - Efficiency measures how much the heuristic reduces the search time or the search space.
  - Admissibility measures whether the heuristic is consistent and never overestimates the distance to the goal.
