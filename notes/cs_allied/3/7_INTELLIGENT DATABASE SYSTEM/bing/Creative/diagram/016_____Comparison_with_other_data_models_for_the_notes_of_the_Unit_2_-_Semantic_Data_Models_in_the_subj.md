### Comparison with other data models

Semantic data models (SDMs) are high-level data models that capture the meaning and structure of data in an application domain. They differ from other data models in several aspects, such as:

- SDMs support relationships, data abstraction, inheritance, constraints, unstructured objects, and dynamic properties, which are not directly supported by other data models such as relational, hierarchical, or network models.
- SDMs use natural language and graphical notation to represent data and their semantics, which makes them more understandable and expressive than other data models that use formal or mathematical notation.
- SDMs are based on the concepts of entities, attributes, and relationships, which are closer to the real-world objects and phenomena than the concepts of tables, columns, and keys used by other data models.
- SDMs can be mapped to different logical and physical data models, such as object-oriented, relational, or XML, which makes them more flexible and adaptable than other data models that are tied to a specific implementation.

Some examples of semantic data models are:

- Entity-relationship model (ERM): A graphical model that represents data as entities (things or objects) and relationships (associations or links) between them. Entities have attributes (properties or characteristics) and relationships have cardinalities (number of occurrences) and roles (functions or meanings) .
- Object-role model (ORM): A graphical model that represents data as objects (things or concepts) and roles (ways of participating in relationships) between them. Objects have predicates (properties or facts) and roles have constraints (rules or conditions) .
- Unified Modeling Language (UML): A graphical model that represents data as classes (types or categories) and associations (relationships or connections) between them. Classes have attributes (properties or fields) and operations (methods or functions) and associations have multiplicities (number of instances) and names (labels or identifiers) .
