### Inference and Bayesian Networks

- Inference refers to the process of deriving unknown information from known information. It is used to draw conclusions from a set of observations or facts.
- Bayesian networks are graphical models that use Bayes' theorem to compute conditional probabilities. They are composed of nodes that represent variables and directed edges that represent conditional dependencies between variables.
- Bayes' theorem is used to update the probability of an event based on new evidence or information. It calculates the posterior probability, which is the probability of an event after taking into account the related evidence. The theorem requires specification of the prior probability and likelihood to compute the posterior probability.
- There are two types of inference with Bayesian networks:

1. Forward inference: Computing the probability of observing some variables given the values of other variables. It is used to diagnose the causes of observed symptoms or effects.
2. Backward inference: Computing the probability distribution of unobserved variables given the values of observed variables. It is used to predict unknown variables based on known information.

- Advantages of Bayesian networks include the ability to handle incomplete data, incorporate expert knowledge, and perform causal reasoning. However, they may not scale well to large networks and the accuracy depends on the correct specification of conditional probabilities.
