### Space Limitation

- Space limitation refers to the constraint of the available space on a display device or a visualization medium to present data effectively and efficiently.
- Space limitation can affect the quality, readability, and usability of data visualizations, especially when dealing with large or complex data sets.
- Some of the challenges and solutions related to space limitation are:

  - **Overplotting**: This occurs when too many data points or graphical elements are plotted in the same area, causing occlusion and clutter. Overplotting can reduce the visibility and distinguishability of the data, and make it difficult to identify patterns, trends, outliers, or correlations. Some possible solutions for overplotting are:
    - Using transparency or color blending to show the density or distribution of the data points.
    - Applying filtering, aggregation, or sampling techniques to reduce the number of data points or graphical elements.
    - Using interactive techniques such as zooming, panning, brushing, or linking to explore the data at different levels of detail or focus on regions of interest.
    - Using alternative visual encodings such as glyphs, icons, or symbols to represent the data points or graphical elements more compactly or expressively.
  - **Labeling**: This refers to the process of adding text annotations to the data visualizations to provide additional information or context, such as the names, values, or categories of the data points or graphical elements. Labeling can enhance the interpretability and communication of the data visualizations, but it can also introduce challenges such as:
    - Finding enough space to place the labels without overlapping or obscuring the data or other labels.
    - Choosing the appropriate font size, style, color, and orientation for the labels to make them readable and consistent with the data visualizations.
    - Balancing the trade-off between the amount and the relevance of the information provided by the labels. Too few or too many labels can affect the clarity and comprehensibility of the data visualizations. Some possible solutions for labeling are:
      - Using layout algorithms or heuristics to optimize the placement and alignment of the labels.
      - Using interactive techniques such as tooltips, pop-ups, or mouse-over effects to show the labels on demand or in response to user actions.
      - Using abbreviations, acronyms, or symbols to shorten the labels or convey more information with less text.
      - Using visual cues such as lines, arrows, or connectors to link the labels to the data points or graphical elements.
  - **Multivariate data**: This refers to the data that has more than two variables or dimensions. Multivariate data can provide rich and comprehensive information, but it can also pose challenges for data visualization, such as:
    - Finding suitable visual encodings or mappings to represent the multiple variables or dimensions of the data. Some common visual encodings are position, size, shape, color, orientation, texture, or motion. However, not all visual encodings are equally effective or perceptually accurate for different types of data or tasks. For example, position and size are more effective for quantitative data than color or shape, and color and shape are more effective for categorical data than size or orientation.
    - Finding enough space to display the multiple variables or dimensions of the data without compromising the legibility or aesthetics of the data visualizations. Some possible solutions for multivariate data are:
      - Using dimensionality reduction or feature selection techniques to reduce the number of variables or dimensions of the data or focus on the most relevant or informative ones.
      - Using multiple coordinated views or small multiples to show different variables or dimensions of the data in separate but linked visualizations.
      - Using composite or derived visual encodings or mappings to combine or integrate multiple variables or dimensions of the data into a single visualization. For example, using color hue and saturation to represent two variables, or using glyphs or icons to represent multiple variables.
