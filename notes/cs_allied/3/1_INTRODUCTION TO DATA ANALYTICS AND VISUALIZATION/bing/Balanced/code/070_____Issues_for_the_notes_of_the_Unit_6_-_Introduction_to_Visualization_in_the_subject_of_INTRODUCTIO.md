### Issues for the notes of the Unit 6 - Introduction to Visualization in the subject of INTRODUCTION TO DATA ANALYTICS AND VISUALIZATION

- Data visualization is the process of transforming data into graphical or pictorial representations that can communicate information effectively and efficiently to the audience.
- Data visualization can have many benefits, such as revealing patterns, trends, outliers, correlations, and causations in the data, enhancing the understanding and retention of the data, and facilitating the decision-making process based on the data.
- However, data visualization can also have many issues, such as misleading, confusing, or deceiving the audience, violating the ethical or aesthetic principles of design, or failing to address the needs and expectations of the audience    .
- Some of the common issues for data visualization are:

  - **Correlation is not causation**: Correlations make us see false relationships between variables that may not have any causal link. For example, a graph that shows a correlation between ice cream sales and shark attacks may imply that eating ice cream causes shark attacks, which is obviously not true.
  - **Similarities now do not mean similarities forever**: A relationship happening within our observational set does not imply a relationship always. For example, a graph that shows a similarity between the GDP of two countries in a certain year may not reflect the similarity in the future years, as the GDP may change due to various factors.
  - **Axes make all the difference**: The size and number of axes can change the way we interpret data. For example, a graph that uses a truncated or distorted axis may exaggerate or minimize the differences between the data points, or a graph that uses multiple axes may confuse the audience or create false comparisons .
  - **Abusing the Law of Large Numbers (LLN)**: The LLN states that as the sample size increases, the sample mean approaches the population mean. However, some data visualizations may abuse this law by using large numbers to imply precision or significance that may not exist. For example, a graph that shows the population of a city as 8,175,133 may imply a high level of accuracy, but it may not reflect the uncertainty or variability of the data.
  - **Misleading color contrast**: Color is among the most persuasive design elements. Even subtle shade variations elicit strong emotional responses. In data visualization, high degrees of color contrast may cause viewers to believe that value disparities are greater than they really are. For example, a map that uses a red-green color scheme to show the political preferences of different regions may create a false impression of polarization or homogeneity.
  - **Using an unsuitable format**: The nature of the data usually dictates the format of the visualization. The most important characteristic is whether the data is qualitative (it describes or categorizes) or quantitative (meaning, it’s measurable). For example, a pie chart is suitable for showing the proportions of qualitative data, but not for showing the trends of quantitative data over time. Using an unsuitable format may misrepresent the data or obscure the message.
  - **Lack of better data visualization literacy**: A lack of proper knowledge of data visualization can do more harm than good. First, it confuses the audience, or worse, it misleads them. Additionally, a poorly structured data visualization denotes a loss of time and effort, which delays the decision-making process in deadline-driven projects. Therefore, data visualization literacy is essential for both the creators and the consumers of data visualizations.
  - **Failing to address the audience's needs and expectations**: Data visualization is not only about presenting data, but also about telling a story with data. Therefore, it is important to consider the audience's needs and expectations, such as their background, context, goals, questions, and preferences. For example, a data visualization that is too complex or too simple, too technical or too vague, too detailed or too general, may not engage or inform the audience effectively.
