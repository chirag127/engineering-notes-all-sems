## Unit 7 - Human Vision

Human vision is the ability to perceive and interpret visual information from the environment. It involves the interaction of the eyes, the brain, and the nervous system. Human vision has the following features and limits:

- **Binocular vision**: Humans have two eyes that are located on the left and right sides of the face. Each eye forms a different image on its retina, which is the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. The brain combines these two images to create a single perception of vision that allows humans to see objects in three dimensions and estimate their distance and depth.
- **Eye structure**: The human eye is a complex organ that consists of several parts, such as the cornea, the pupil, the iris, the lens, the vitreous, the retina, the optic nerve, and the extraocular muscles. Each part has a specific function in the process of vision. For example, the cornea is the clear front layer of the eye that bends light to help the eye focus. The pupil is the opening in the center of the iris that controls the amount of light that enters the eye. The lens is the transparent structure behind the pupil that changes shape to focus light onto the retina. The retina is the layer of nerve cells that converts light into electrical signals that are sent to the brain via the optic nerve. The extraocular muscles are the six muscles that control the movements of the eye.
- **Color perception**: Humans can perceive a range of colors that are derived from the combination of three primary colors: red, green, and blue. The retina contains two types of photoreceptors: rods and cones. Rods are more sensitive to light and are responsible for night vision and peripheral vision. Cones are less sensitive to light but can detect different wavelengths of light and are responsible for color vision and central vision. There are three types of cones: L-cones, M-cones, and S-cones, which respond to long, medium, and short wavelengths of light, respectively. The brain interprets the signals from the cones to create the sensation of color.
- **Visual acuity**: Visual acuity is the measure of the sharpness and clarity of vision. It depends on several factors, such as the size and contrast of the object, the distance from the object, the lighting conditions, and the health of the eye. Visual acuity is usually measured by using a Snellen chart, which consists of rows of letters of different sizes. The smallest row of letters that a person can read at a certain distance is used to determine their visual acuity. Normal visual acuity is 20/20, which means that a person can read the 20th row of letters at a distance of 20 feet. A person with 20/40 vision can only read the 20th row of letters at a distance of 40 feet, which means that their vision is less sharp than normal.
- **Visual field**: Visual field is the area of the environment that a person can see at any given moment. It includes the central and peripheral vision. The central vision is the area that a person can see when they focus on a specific point. The peripheral vision is the area that a person can see around the central vision. The normal visual field for each eye is about 170 degrees horizontally and 100 degrees vertically. The visual field of both eyes overlaps by about 120 degrees, which creates the binocular vision. The visual field can be affected by various factors, such as eye diseases, brain injuries, or medications.
