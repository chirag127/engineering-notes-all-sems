### Space Limitation

- Space limitation refers to the constraint of the available space on a display device or a medium to present data and information effectively.
- Space limitation can affect the design, layout, and interactivity of data visualizations, as well as the perception and cognition of the viewers.
- Some of the factors that influence space limitation are:
  - The size and resolution of the display device or medium, such as a monitor, a projector, a paper, or a mobile phone.
  - The amount and complexity of the data and information to be shown, such as the number of variables, dimensions, categories, or values.
  - The type and purpose of the data visualization, such as exploratory, explanatory, descriptive, or prescriptive.
  - The intended audience and context of the data visualization, such as the level of expertise, interest, and attention of the viewers, and the environment and situation where the visualization is used or viewed.
- Some of the challenges and trade-offs that arise from space limitation are:
  - How to balance the trade-off between detail and overview, such as showing more data points or more aggregated summaries, or using zooming, panning, or filtering techniques to navigate the data.
  - How to balance the trade-off between simplicity and complexity, such as using fewer or more visual elements, colors, shapes, or symbols, or using annotations, legends, or tooltips to provide additional information.
  - How to balance the trade-off between aesthetics and functionality, such as using more or less white space, alignment, or symmetry, or using more or less interactive features, such as menus, buttons, or sliders.
  - How to balance the trade-off between accuracy and distortion, such as using more or less precise scales, axes, or labels, or using more or less distorted representations, such as maps, charts, or graphs.
- Some of the strategies and techniques that can help overcome space limitation are:
  - Using multiple views, such as coordinated, linked, or juxtaposed views, to show different aspects, perspectives, or levels of detail of the data.
  - Using hierarchical or nested structures, such as treemaps, sunbursts, or icicle plots, to show the part-whole relationships and the distribution of the data.
  - Using small multiples, such as sparklines, glyphs, or icons, to show the patterns, trends, or outliers of the data in a compact and consistent way.
  - Using visual encoding, such as size, color, shape, or position, to show the differences, similarities, or correlations of the data in a meaningful and efficient way.
