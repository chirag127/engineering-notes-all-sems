# History

- Wearable computing is the field of technology that involves devices that can be worn on the body, such as smartwatches, glasses, headsets, rings, etc.
- Wearable computing has a long and diverse history, dating back to ancient times when people used beads, rings, and abacuses to perform calculations and store information.
- Some of the earliest examples of wearable computing are:

  - The abacus ring, a 16th-century device that allowed the wearer to perform arithmetic operations by sliding beads along a wire.
  - The Queen Elizabeth I's watch, a 16th-century device that was given to her by Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, and was one of the first wristwatches in history.
  - The covert timing devices, a 20th-century device that was hidden in shoes and used by Edward Thorp and Claude Shannon to cheat at roulette by predicting the outcome of the wheel.

- Some of the pioneers of wearable computing are:

  - Steve Mann, a professor at the University of Toronto, who is considered the father of wearable computing and has been developing and wearing various wearable devices since the late 1970s, such as the EyeTap, a head-mounted camera that records and displays the wearer's view.
  - Thad Starner, a professor at Georgia Tech, who is one of the founders of the MIT Wearable Computing Project and has been wearing a wearable computer system since 1993, consisting of a head-mounted display, a keyboard, and a backpack computer.
  - Alex Pentland, a professor at MIT, who is the director of the MIT Media Lab and has been leading research on wearable computing, such as the MIThril, a modular wearable computer platform that supports various sensors and applications.

- Some of the current trends and challenges of wearable computing are:

  - The miniaturization and integration of wearable devices, such as smartwatches, smart glasses, smart earbuds, etc., that can perform various functions and communicate with other devices and the cloud.
  - The user interface and interaction design of wearable devices, such as voice, gesture, touch, etc., that can provide natural and intuitive ways of controlling and accessing information and services.
  - The privacy and security of wearable devices, such as the protection of personal data, the prevention of unauthorized access, the regulation of data collection and sharing, etc., that can ensure the trust and safety of the users and the society.
