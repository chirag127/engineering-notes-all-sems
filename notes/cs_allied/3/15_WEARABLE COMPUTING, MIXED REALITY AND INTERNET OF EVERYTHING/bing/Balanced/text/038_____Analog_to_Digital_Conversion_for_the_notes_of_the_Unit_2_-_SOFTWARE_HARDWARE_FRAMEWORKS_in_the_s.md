### Analog to Digital Conversion

- Analog to digital conversion (ADC) is an electronic process that converts a continuous analog signal, such as a sound, light, or voltage, into a discrete digital signal, such as a binary code, without altering its essential content .
- ADC is a useful feature that allows digital devices, such as microcontrollers, computers, or sensors, to interface with the analog world around us.
- ADC is essential for applications such as digital imaging, radar, signal processing, data acquisition, and communication .
- ADC consists of three main components: a sample and hold circuit, a quantizer, and an encoder.
  - The sample and hold circuit captures the analog signal at a given time and holds it constant until the conversion is complete.
  - The quantizer assigns a discrete value to the sampled signal based on a predefined scale or resolution.
  - The encoder converts the quantized value into a binary code that can be stored or transmitted.
- ADC can be classified into different types based on the conversion technique, such as successive approximation, flash, dual slope, sigma-delta, and pipeline.
- ADC has some important parameters that affect its performance, such as resolution, accuracy, speed, power consumption, and noise .
  - Resolution is the number of bits used to represent the digital output and determines the smallest change in the analog signal that can be detected.
  - Accuracy is the degree of conformity between the digital output and the true value of the analog input and depends on factors such as offset, gain, linearity, and temperature.
  - Speed is the rate at which the ADC can convert the analog signal and is measured by the sampling frequency or the conversion time.
  - Power consumption is the amount of energy required by the ADC to operate and is influenced by the conversion technique, the resolution, and the speed.
  - Noise is the unwanted variation or distortion in the analog signal or the digital output and can be caused by external sources, such as electromagnetic interference, or internal sources, such as quantization error or thermal noise.
