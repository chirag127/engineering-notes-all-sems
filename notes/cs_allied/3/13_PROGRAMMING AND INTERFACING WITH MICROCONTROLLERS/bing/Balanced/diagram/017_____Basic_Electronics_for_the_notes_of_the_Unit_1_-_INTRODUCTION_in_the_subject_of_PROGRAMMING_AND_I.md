# Basic Electronics

## Unit 1 - Introduction

### What is Electronics?

- Electronics is the branch of science and engineering that deals with the study and application of devices, circuits, and systems that operate by controlling the flow of electrons or other electrically charged particles.
- Electronics can be divided into two main categories: analog and digital.
  - Analog electronics use continuous signals that vary in amplitude or frequency to represent information, such as sound, light, or temperature.
  - Digital electronics use discrete signals that have only two states, on or off, to represent information, such as binary digits (bits), logic gates, or microprocessors.

### What are the Basic Components of Electronics?

- The basic components of electronics are the building blocks of any electronic device, circuit, or system. They can be classified into two types: passive and active.
  - Passive components do not require any external power source to operate and do not amplify or generate signals. They include resistors, capacitors, inductors, diodes, and transformers.
  - Active components require an external power source to operate and can amplify or generate signals. They include transistors, integrated circuits (ICs), operational amplifiers (op-amps), and microcontrollers.

### What are the Basic Laws of Electronics?

- The basic laws of electronics are the mathematical rules that describe the behavior and interaction of the basic components and the electric current and voltage in a circuit. They include Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws, and Thevenin's theorem.
  - Ohm's law states that the electric current (I) flowing through a resistor (R) is directly proportional to the voltage (V) applied across it, and inversely proportional to the resistance. The formula is: V = IR
  - Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) states that the algebraic sum of the currents entering and leaving a node (a point where two or more components are connected) in a circuit is zero. The formula is: ∑I = 0
  - Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) states that the algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed loop (a path that starts and ends at the same node) in a circuit is zero. The formula is: ∑V = 0
  - Thevenin's theorem states that any linear circuit with two terminals can be replaced by an equivalent circuit consisting of a single voltage source (Vth) in series with a single resistor (Rth), where Vth is the open-circuit voltage across the terminals and Rth is the equivalent resistance seen from the terminals. The formula is: Vth = Voc and Rth = Voc/Isc, where Voc is the open-circuit voltage and Isc is the short-circuit current.
