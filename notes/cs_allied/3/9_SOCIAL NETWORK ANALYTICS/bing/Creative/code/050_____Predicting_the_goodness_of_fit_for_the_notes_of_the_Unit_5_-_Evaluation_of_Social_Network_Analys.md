# Predicting the goodness of fit for the notes of the Unit 5 - Evaluation of Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is a method of studying the structure and dynamics of social relationships among individuals or groups.
- SNA can be used to understand various aspects of social phenomena, such as communication, collaboration, influence, diffusion, contagion, and cohesion.
- SNA can also be used to evaluate the effectiveness and impact of interventions that aim to change or improve social networks, such as public health programs, community development projects, or organizational change initiatives.
- To evaluate SNA interventions, it is important to measure the changes in the network structure and attributes over time, and to compare them with the expected or desired outcomes.
- One way to do this is to use statistical models that can capture the essential features of the network data, such as the distribution of ties, the presence of clusters or communities, the patterns of reciprocity or transitivity, and the effects of individual or group characteristics.
- These models are called exponential random graph models (ERGMs) or stochastic block models (SBMs), depending on the level of complexity and generality they can handle.
- ERGMs and SBMs are both probabilistic models that specify the likelihood of observing a given network configuration, based on a set of parameters that represent the network properties of interest.
- To fit these models to the network data, one needs to estimate the parameters using methods such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or Bayesian inference.
- However, fitting a model to the data is not enough to ensure that the model is appropriate and valid for the data. One also needs to assess the goodness of fit (GOF) of the model, which is the degree to which the model can reproduce the observed features of the data.
- GOF can be measured using various indices or criteria, such as the likelihood ratio test, the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the deviance information criterion (DIC), or the posterior predictive check (PPC).
- These indices or criteria can be used to compare different models for the same data, or to evaluate the fit of a single model to the data.
- The choice of the GOF index or criterion depends on the type of model, the type of data, and the research question or objective.
- Some GOF indices or criteria are more suitable for ERGMs, while others are more suitable for SBMs. Some GOF indices or criteria are more sensitive to the overall fit of the model, while others are more sensitive to the local fit of the model.
- The GOF indices or criteria can also be complemented by graphical or visual methods, such as plotting the observed and simulated network statistics, or comparing the observed and simulated network structures.
- These methods can help to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the model, and to diagnose the sources of misfit or discrepancy.
- Predicting the GOF of a model is a challenging task, as it depends on many factors, such as the size and complexity of the network, the number and type of parameters, the estimation method, and the sampling variability.
- However, some general guidelines can be followed to improve the prediction of the GOF, such as:

  - Choosing a model that is parsimonious, interpretable, and consistent with the research question or objective.
  - Using a large and representative sample of the network data, or applying appropriate weighting or correction methods for small or biased samples.
  - Using a reliable and efficient estimation method, such as MLE or Bayesian inference, and checking the convergence and stability of the estimates.
  - Using multiple GOF indices or criteria, and comparing them with the theoretical or empirical distributions or thresholds.
  - Using graphical or visual methods, and inspecting the observed and simulated network statistics and structures for any anomalies or outliers.
  - Reporting the GOF results in a transparent and comprehensive way, and discussing the implications and limitations of the model and the data.
