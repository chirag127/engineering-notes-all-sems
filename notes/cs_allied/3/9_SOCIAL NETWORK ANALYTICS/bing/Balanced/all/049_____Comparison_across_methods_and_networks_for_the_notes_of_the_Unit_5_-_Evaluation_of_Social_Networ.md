# Comparison across methods and networks for the notes of the Unit 5 - Evaluation of Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is a set of techniques for studying the structure and dynamics of social relationships among actors, such as individuals, groups, organizations, or countries.
- SNA can be applied to various types of networks, such as communication, collaboration, affiliation, citation, or trade networks, depending on the research question and data availability.
- SNA methods can be classified into three broad categories: descriptive, inferential, and generative.
  - Descriptive methods aim to summarize and visualize the main features of a network, such as its size, density, degree distribution, centrality, clustering, and community structure.
  - Inferential methods aim to test hypotheses and estimate parameters based on network data, such as the effects of network structure on individual or group outcomes, the similarity or difference between networks, or the evolution of networks over time.
  - Generative methods aim to explain and simulate the underlying mechanisms that produce observed network patterns, such as the effects of individual preferences, social influence, homophily, reciprocity, or triadic closure.
- SNA methods can be compared across different criteria, such as their assumptions, data requirements, strengths, limitations, and applications  .
  - For example, descriptive methods are relatively simple and intuitive, but they may not capture the complexity and uncertainty of network data, or the causal relationships between network structure and behavior.
  - Inferential methods are more rigorous and robust, but they may require more data and computational resources, or rely on strong assumptions that may not hold in reality.
  - Generative methods are more flexible and realistic, but they may be difficult to validate, generalize, or compare with alternative models.
- SNA methods can also be compared across different networks, such as their domain, level, scope, or mode  .
  - For example, communication networks are networks of information exchange, collaboration networks are networks of joint work, affiliation networks are networks of membership, citation networks are networks of intellectual influence, and trade networks are networks of economic exchange.
  - Networks can be analyzed at different levels, such as individual, dyadic, triadic, subgroup, or global.
  - Networks can have different scopes, such as ego-centric, socio-centric, or open.
  - Networks can have different modes, such as unimodal, bimodal, or multimodal.
