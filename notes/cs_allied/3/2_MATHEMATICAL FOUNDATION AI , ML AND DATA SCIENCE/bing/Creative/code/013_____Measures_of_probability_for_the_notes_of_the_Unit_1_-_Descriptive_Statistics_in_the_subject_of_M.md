### Measures of probability

- Probability is a measure of how likely an event is to occur in a random experiment.
- Probability can be expressed as a number between 0 and 1, where 0 means impossible and 1 means certain.
- There are different ways to assign probabilities to events, depending on the nature of the experiment and the available information.
- Some of the common methods are:

  - **Classical probability**: This method assumes that all possible outcomes of the experiment are equally likely, and assigns probabilities based on the ratio of favorable outcomes to total outcomes. For example, the probability of getting a head when tossing a fair coin is 1/2, because there are two possible outcomes (head or tail) and one of them is favorable (head).
  - **Empirical probability**: This method estimates probabilities based on the relative frequency of observed outcomes in a large number of trials. For example, the probability of getting a 6 when rolling a fair die can be estimated by rolling the die many times and counting how many times 6 occurs, then dividing by the total number of trials.
  - **Subjective probability**: This method assigns probabilities based on personal judgment, belief, or opinion, without relying on any formal rules or data. For example, the probability of rain tomorrow can be based on one's own experience, weather forecast, or intuition.
  - **Axiomatic probability**: This method defines probability as a set of axioms (basic rules) that any reasonable measure of uncertainty must satisfy. For example, one of the axioms is that the probability of any event is between 0 and 1, inclusive. Another axiom is that the probability of the union of two mutually exclusive events is the sum of their probabilities.

- Some of the important concepts and properties of probability are:

  - **Sample space**: The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. For example, the sample space of tossing a coin is {head, tail}.
  - **Event**: A subset of the sample space. For example, the event of getting a head when tossing a coin is {head}.
  - **Complement**: The set of outcomes that are not in the event. For example, the complement of getting a head when tossing a coin is {tail}.
  - **Union**: The set of outcomes that are in either of the two events. For example, the union of getting a head or a tail when tossing a coin is {head, tail}.
  - **Intersection**: The set of outcomes that are in both of the two events. For example, the intersection of getting a head and a tail when tossing two coins is empty, because there is no outcome that satisfies both conditions.
  - **Mutually exclusive**: Two events are mutually exclusive if they have no common outcomes. For example, getting a head and getting a tail when tossing a coin are mutually exclusive events.
  - **Independent**: Two events are independent if the occurrence of one event does not affect the probability of the other event. For example, getting a head when tossing a coin and getting a 6 when rolling a die are independent events, because the outcome of one does not influence the outcome of the other.
  - **Conditional probability**: The probability of an event given that another event has occurred. For example, the probability of getting a head when tossing a coin given that it is raining is the same as the probability of getting a head when tossing a coin without any condition, because the two events are independent. However, the probability of getting a 6 when rolling a die given that the previous roll was a 6 is different from the probability of getting a 6 when rolling a die without any condition, because the two events are dependent.
