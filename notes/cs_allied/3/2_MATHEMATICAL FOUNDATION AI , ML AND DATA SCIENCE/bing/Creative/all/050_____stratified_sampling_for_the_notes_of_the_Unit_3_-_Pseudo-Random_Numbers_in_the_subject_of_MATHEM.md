# Stratified Sampling

Stratified sampling is a method of sampling from a population that can be divided into subgroups or strata that share some common characteristics. The purpose of stratified sampling is to ensure that the sample represents the diversity and variability of the population, and to reduce the sampling error and bias.

Some examples of stratified sampling are:

- A survey of students' opinions on a new policy, where the population is stratified by grade level and a random sample of students from each grade is selected.
- A simulation of a physical phenomenon, where the population is stratified by different regions or conditions and a random sample of points from each stratum is used to estimate the population statistics.
- A clinical trial of a new drug, where the population is stratified by age, gender, and health status and a random sample of patients from each stratum is assigned to the treatment or control group.

## Advantages and Disadvantages of Stratified Sampling

Some advantages of stratified sampling are:

- It can improve the precision and accuracy of the estimates by reducing the sampling variability within each stratum.
- It can ensure that the sample covers all the relevant subgroups of the population and reflects their proportions.
- It can allow for separate analysis and comparison of the results for each stratum.

Some disadvantages of stratified sampling are:

- It requires prior knowledge of the population characteristics and the appropriate stratification criteria.
- It can be more complex and costly to implement than simple random sampling, especially when there are many strata or the strata are not easily identifiable.
- It can introduce bias if the strata are not mutually exclusive or collectively exhaustive, or if the sampling method within each stratum is not random.

## Types of Stratified Sampling

There are two main types of stratified sampling: proportionate and disproportionate.

- Proportionate stratified sampling is when the sample size for each stratum is proportional to the population size of that stratum. For example, if the population consists of 40% males and 60% females, and the sample size is 100, then the sample should include 40 males and 60 females.
- Disproportionate stratified sampling is when the sample size for each stratum is not proportional to the population size of that stratum, but is based on some other criterion, such as the expected variability, the importance, or the cost of sampling from that stratum. For example, if the population consists of 40% males and 60% females, but the males have more variation in their opinions than the females, and the sample size is 100, then the sample might include 60 males and 40 females.
