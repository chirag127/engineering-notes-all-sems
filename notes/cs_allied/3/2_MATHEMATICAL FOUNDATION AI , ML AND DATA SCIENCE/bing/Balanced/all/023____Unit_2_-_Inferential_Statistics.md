## Unit 2 - Inferential Statistics

- Inferential statistics are methods of drawing conclusions about a population based on a sample of data from that population.
- Inferential statistics are useful when it is impractical or impossible to measure every individual in a population of interest.
- Inferential statistics rely on the assumption that the sample is representative of the population, meaning that it reflects the characteristics and variability of the population.
- Inferential statistics can be divided into two main types: hypothesis testing and estimation.
- Hypothesis testing is a procedure of testing a claim or a statement about a population parameter (such as the mean, the proportion, the variance, etc.) using sample data.
- Hypothesis testing involves four steps: stating the null and alternative hypotheses, choosing a significance level, calculating the test statistic and the p-value, and making a decision based on the p-value and the significance level.
- The null hypothesis is the default or status quo statement that is assumed to be true unless there is strong evidence against it. The alternative hypothesis is the statement that contradicts the null hypothesis and is what the researcher wants to prove or support.
- The significance level, denoted by alpha, is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true. It is also the maximum acceptable risk of making a type I error, which is rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true.
- The test statistic is a numerical value that measures the strength of the evidence against the null hypothesis. It is calculated from the sample data and the assumed value of the population parameter under the null hypothesis.
- The p-value is the probability of obtaining a test statistic as extreme or more extreme than the observed one, assuming that the null hypothesis is true. It is also the smallest significance level at which the null hypothesis can be rejected.
- The decision rule for hypothesis testing is to reject the null hypothesis if the p-value is less than or equal to the significance level, and to fail to reject the null hypothesis otherwise.
- Estimation is a procedure of using sample data to estimate a population parameter (such as the mean, the proportion, the variance, etc.) with a certain degree of confidence or accuracy.
- Estimation can be divided into two types: point estimation and interval estimation.
- Point estimation is the process of finding a single value that best represents the population parameter. The point estimate is usually the sample statistic, such as the sample mean, the sample proportion, the sample variance, etc.
- Interval estimation is the process of finding a range of values that contains the population parameter with a certain level of confidence. The interval estimate is usually the point estimate plus or minus a margin of error, which depends on the sampling distribution of the point estimate, the sample size, and the confidence level.
- The confidence level, denoted by 1 - alpha, is the probability that the interval estimate contains the population parameter. It is also the complement of the significance level used in hypothesis testing.
- The confidence interval is the interval estimate with the confidence level attached to it. It can be interpreted as the range of plausible values for the population parameter based on the sample data.
