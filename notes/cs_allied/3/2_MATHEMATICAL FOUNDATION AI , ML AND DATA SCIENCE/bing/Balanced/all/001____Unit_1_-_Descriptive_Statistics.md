## Unit 1 - Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics are methods of summarizing and displaying data in a concise and informative way. They help us to understand the main features and patterns of a data set, such as its center, spread, shape, and outliers. Descriptive statistics do not involve making any inferences or generalizations beyond the data.

Some of the topics covered in this unit are:

- **Types of data**: Data can be classified into different types based on the level of measurement, such as nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio. Each type of data has different properties and methods of analysis.
- **Frequency distributions**: A frequency distribution is a table that shows how often each value or category of a variable occurs in a data set. It can be displayed as a relative frequency distribution, which shows the proportion or percentage of each value or category, or a cumulative frequency distribution, which shows the total number or proportion of values or categories up to a certain point.
- **Histograms**: A histogram is a graphical display of a frequency distribution of a quantitative variable. It shows the shape and spread of the data, as well as any gaps, outliers, or skewness. A histogram consists of bars that represent the frequency or relative frequency of each class interval of the variable. The class intervals are chosen to cover the range of the data and have equal widths.
- **Measures of central tendency**: Measures of central tendency are numerical values that describe the center or typical value of a data set. The most common measures of central tendency are the mean, the median, and the mode. The mean is the average of all the data values, the median is the middle value when the data are ordered, and the mode is the most frequent value. Each measure of central tendency has its advantages and disadvantages, depending on the type and shape of the data.
- **Measures of variability**: Measures of variability are numerical values that describe the spread or dispersion of a data set. The most common measures of variability are the range, the interquartile range, the variance, and the standard deviation. The range is the difference between the maximum and minimum values, the interquartile range is the difference between the first and third quartiles, the variance is the average of the squared deviations from the mean, and the standard deviation is the square root of the variance. Each measure of variability has its advantages and disadvantages, depending on the type and shape of the data.
- **Boxplots**: A boxplot is a graphical display of the five-number summary of a quantitative variable. The five-number summary consists of the minimum, the first quartile, the median, the third quartile, and the maximum. A boxplot shows the center, spread, and skewness of the data, as well as any outliers. A boxplot consists of a box that represents the interquartile range, a line that represents the median, and whiskers that extend to the minimum and maximum values or to the most extreme values within 1.5 times the interquartile range. Outliers are marked as individual points beyond the whiskers.
- **Z-scores**: A z-score is a standardized value that measures how many standard deviations a data value is above or below the mean of the data set. A z-score can be used to compare values from different data sets that have different means and standard deviations, or to identify outliers in a data set. A z-score is calculated by subtracting the mean from the data value and dividing by the standard deviation. A z-score of zero means that the data value is equal to the mean, a positive z-score means that the data value is above the mean, and a negative z-score means that the data value is below the mean. The magnitude of the z-score indicates how far the data value is from the mean. A z-score of 1.96 or -1.96 corresponds to the 97.5th and 2.5th percentiles of a normal distribution, respectively.
