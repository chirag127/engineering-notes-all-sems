### Constructive Cost Models (COCOMO) in software project management

- Constructive Cost Models (COCOMO) are a family of procedural cost estimation models for software projects, developed by Barry Boehm in the late 1970s and updated in the 1990s and 2000s .
- COCOMO models are based on the number of lines of code (LOC) as the main measure of software size, and use regression formulas to estimate the effort, cost, and schedule of software development based on historical data from previous projects  .
- COCOMO models can be applied to different types of software projects, such as organic, semi-detached, and embedded, which have different characteristics and complexity levels .
- COCOMO models consist of three levels of increasing detail and accuracy: Basic, Intermediate, and Detailed .
  - Basic COCOMO model: It provides a rough order of magnitude estimate of the software cost, based on a simple formula that uses the LOC and a constant factor that depends on the project type .
  - Intermediate COCOMO model: It refines the basic model by introducing 15 cost drivers that account for various attributes of the project, such as product, hardware, personnel, and project factors .
  - Detailed COCOMO model: It further improves the intermediate model by dividing the software development process into four phases: planning and requirements, system design, detailed design, and coding and testing, and applying different effort multipliers for each phase .
- COCOMO models are widely used and documented in software engineering literature, and have been validated and calibrated with empirical data from hundreds of projects .
- COCOMO models have some limitations and challenges, such as:
  - They rely on the LOC as the main measure of software size, which may not be available or accurate in the early stages of software development, and may vary depending on the programming language, coding style, and level of abstraction .
  - They assume a linear relationship between the software size and the software cost, which may not hold for very large or very small projects, or for projects that use different development methodologies, such as agile or iterative .
  - They may not capture all the factors that influence the software cost, such as quality, risk, uncertainty, learning, reuse, and innovation .
  - They may not reflect the current trends and practices in software engineering, such as code reusability, off-the-shelf components, distributed development, and cloud computing .
