### Desktop Grids for the notes of the Unit 1 - Overview of Grid Computing Technology in the subject of High Performance Computing

Desktop Grids, also known as Volunteer Computing, is a type of Grid Computing that utilizes the idle computing resources of a large number of volunteer computers connected to the Internet. These idle resources include CPU cycles, storage, and network bandwidth. Desktop Grids are different from traditional Grid Computing, which involves the use of high-end supercomputers and dedicated clusters of computers.

Here are some important points to remember about Desktop Grids:

1. Architecture: Desktop Grids consist of a large number of volunteer computers connected to the Internet, which are not owned by the same organization. These computers are usually desktops or laptops, and can be located in different parts of the world. The architecture of a Desktop Grid is decentralized, and there is no central authority controlling the resources.

2. Software: Desktop Grids use software called middleware to manage the distribution of computing tasks among the volunteer computers. The middleware is responsible for dividing the tasks into smaller pieces, sending them to the volunteer computers, and collecting the results. Examples of middleware used in Desktop Grids include BOINC (Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing) and XtremWeb.

3. Applications: Desktop Grids are used for a variety of scientific and research applications, such as climate modeling, protein folding, and drug discovery. These applications require a large amount of computing power, which can be provided by the idle resources of volunteer computers.

4. Advantages: Desktop Grids have several advantages over traditional Grid Computing, such as lower cost, wider availability of computing resources, and the ability to harness the power of distributed computing. Desktop Grids also allow volunteers to contribute to scientific research and make a positive impact on society.

5. Disadvantages: Desktop Grids have some disadvantages, such as security concerns, lack of control over the volunteer computers, and the potential for slower processing times due to the use of desktop computers with lower processing power.

Mnemonics and learning tricks for Desktop Grids:

1. Remember the acronym BOINC, which stands for Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing. This is a popular middleware used in Desktop Grids.

2. Think of Desktop Grids as a way to "crowdsource" computing power, similar to how crowdfunding works for raising funds.

3. Imagine a "grid" of volunteer computers spread out all over the world, connected together to form a powerful computing network.

Overall, Desktop Grids are an important part of Grid Computing and offer a unique way to harness the power of distributed computing. By utilizing the idle resources of volunteer computers, Desktop Grids can provide a cost-effective and efficient solution for many scientific and research applications.
