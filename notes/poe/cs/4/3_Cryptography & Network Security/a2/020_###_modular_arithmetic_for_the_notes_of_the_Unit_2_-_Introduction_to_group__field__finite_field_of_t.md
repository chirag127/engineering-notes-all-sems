### Modular Arithmetic

- Modular arithmetic is a system of arithmetic for integers, where numbers "wrap around" upon reaching a certain value called the modulus.
- The modulus is a positive integer that sets the range of numbers that can be represented. For example, modular arithmetic with a modulus of 5 involves only the integers 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4.
- Any integer that is bigger than or equal to 5 wraps around and starts over from 0. For example, 7 is 2 in this system, 12 is 2, 17 is 2, and so on.
- The modulo operator (%) can be used to calculate the remainder after division of one number by another. For example, 17 % 5 = 2. This is useful for understanding modular arithmetic.
- Applications of modular arithmetic include clock arithmetic, computing digital signatures in RSA, and algorithms used in computer programming.

Mnemonics:
- Modular arithmetic wraps around like a clock. Once it reaches the modulus, it starts over from 0.

Learning tricks:
- Practice doing modular arithmetic with small moduli like 5 or 7 to understand how it works.
- Convert regular arithmetic problems to modular arithmetic problems to see how the remainders change. For example, 17 + 12 = 29, but 29 % 5 = 4 in modular arithmetic with modulus 5.

Advantages:
- Useful for applications where numbers repeat in a cycle, like with clocks.
- Enables efficient algorithms for computing signatures and cryptography.

Disadvantages:
- Can be harder to conceptualize than regular arithmetic.
- Limited to integers and a fixed range of numbers based on the chosen modulus.
