### Object-oriented Analysis and Design (OOAD) Process for the notes of the Unit 2 - Enterprise-Wide SOA in the subject of Service Oriented Architecture

The OOAD process for SOA involves the following steps:

1. Identify Business Objects: Identify the key business objects/entities that are involved in the system. These typically correspond to the major nouns in the problem domain. For example, for an e-commerce system, some key business objects could be Customer, Order, Product, etc.

2. Identify Services: Identify the key services that will be offered by the system. These could be process-centric or entity-centric services. For example, for the e-commerce system, some sample services could be PlaceOrder (process-centric) or CustomerService, OrderService, ProductService (entity-centric).

3. Define Interfaces: Define the interfaces for the services. Specify the input, output, preconditions, and postconditions for each service. Also define data types for parameters and return values.

4. Define Classes: Identify classes within each business object and service. Specify attributes and behaviors (methods) for each class. Define class hierarchies and relationships (generalization, association, dependency, etc.)

5. Define Contracts: Define formal contracts for each service in terms of input/output data and functional/non-functional requirements. These contracts serve as the basis for verifying and testing the services.

Some key advantages of the OOAD process for SOA are:

- It leads to services and interfaces that map well to business concepts and processes.
- It results in loosely coupled services with clear definitions of inputs and outputs.
- The object-oriented concepts of encapsulation, inheritance, and polymorphism can be leveraged.

However, the OOAD process may not be suitable if the organization does not have object-oriented analysis and design expertise or if the problem domain does not lend itself well to an object-oriented conceptualization.
