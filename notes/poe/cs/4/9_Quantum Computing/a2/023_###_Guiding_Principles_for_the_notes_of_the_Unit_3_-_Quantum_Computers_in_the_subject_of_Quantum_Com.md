### Guiding Principles for the notes of the Unit 3 - Quantum Computers in the subject of Quantum Computing

1. Superposition: Quantum bits or 'qubits' can be in a superposition of states, meaning that they can act as both 0 and 1 at the same time. This allows a quantum computer to perform many computations in parallel.

2. Entanglement: Qubits can be entangled, so their states are dependent on each other, even if they are separated. If you measure a property of one qubit, such as spin, you instantly know the corresponding property of the other qubit. No matter how far apart the qubits are, they will always be in this quantum connection. This 'spooky action at a distance' as Einstein called it, is a powerful phenomenon that could be harnessed for quantum computing.

3. Interference: When qubits are entangled, their states can interfere with each other, so that the outcome of a measurement may depend on the order/context in which it is performed. This interference effect is another powerful feature of quantum mechanics that could enable quantum algorithms to solve complex problems that are intractable for classical computers.

4. Decoherence: The fragile quantum states of qubits can easily be disrupted by interactions with the environment. This destroys the quantum nature of the system and introduces errors, so decoherence is one of the major challenges in building a quantum computer. The key is to develop ways to isolate qubits and control decoherence to preserve entanglement and interference effects for as long as possible.

Some Mnemonics -

Superposition - Qubits can be in multiple states at once, like a 'quantum superposition' of Schrodinger's cat being both alive and dead.
Entanglement - 'Spooky action at a distance', if you measure one qubit, the other's state is instantly changed too, even if light years apart.
Interference - The wave nature of quantum particles means their states can 'interfere' with each other, depending on context, enabling quantum algorithms.
Decoherence - The fragile quantum nature is easily 'decohered' by interactions with the environment, introducing errors, so we need to preserve entanglement and interference by isolating qubits.
