### Selectional Restrictions for the notes of the Unit 4 - Semantics and Pragmatics in the subject of Natural Language Processing

Selectional restrictions refer to the constraints on the arguments that a predicate can take. For example, the verb 'eat' typically takes a noun phrase referring to food as its object, rather than an abstract noun or a verb phrase. Violating selectional restrictions often results in ungrammatical or nonsensical sentences.

Some common selectional restrictions are:

- Agents of actions must be animate: 'The ball kicked the boy.' is anomalous.
- Instruments must be physically capable of carrying out the action: 'The flower opened the door.' is anomalous.
- Patients of actions must be logically compatible: 'John ate the song.' is anomalous.

 Mnemonics to remember selectional restrictions:

- Animate agents, capable instruments, compatible patients
- Verbs picky about their friends (agents, instruments, patients)

Advantages of selectional restrictions:

- They help prune the search space for interpretation by eliminating implausible or impossible arguments.
- They capture regularities in lexical semantics that can be useful for tasks like semantic role labeling.

Disadvantages of selectional restrictions:

- They can be too rigid, ruling out plausible but non-stereotypical scenarios.
- They are tricky to state precisely and often rely on world knowledge.
- They can be difficult to learn from data due to sparsity and ambiguity.

Applications of selectional restrictions:

- They are used in semantic role labeling to determine argument plausibility.
- They are useful for disambiguating word senses and parsing.
- They can be used to detect errors or anomalies in text.
