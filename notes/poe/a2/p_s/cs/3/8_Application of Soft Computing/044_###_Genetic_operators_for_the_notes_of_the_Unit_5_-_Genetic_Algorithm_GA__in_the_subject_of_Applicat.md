### Genetic operators

The genetic operators used in genetic algorithms are:

1. Selection: Selecting the fittest individuals from a population to breed a new generation. This is done through fitness proportional selection, where individuals with higher fitness have more chances of getting selected. This ensures that fitter traits are carried over to the next generation.
2. Crossover: Exchanging genetic material between two individuals to produce offspring. This is done through methods like single-point crossover, multi-point crossover, uniform crossover, etc. Crossover produces new offspring which are combinations of their parents' traits.
3. Mutation: Randomly altering the genetic material of an individual. This is done by flipping a gene's state from 0 to 1 or vice-versa with a certain mutation probability. Mutation introduces diversity in the population and prevents premature convergence to local optima.

The right combination of selection, crossover and mutation leads to efficient exploration and exploitation of the search space, thereby finding the global optimum.

Advantages:
- mimics natural evolution
- Does not require gradient information
- Can avoid local optima

Disadvantages:
- GA is probabilistic, no guarantee of finding the global optimum
- Choice of genetic operators and their parameters is tricky
- GA can be computationally expensive for complex problems

Applications:
- Optimization
- Machine Learning
- Robotics
- Scheduling
- etc.
