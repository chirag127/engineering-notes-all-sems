### Council of Ministers

- The Council of Ministers consists of Cabinet Ministers and Ministers of State.
- It is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.
- The prime minister is the head of the council of ministers.
- The prime minister allocates portfolios to the ministers.
- The council of ministers aids and advises the president in the exercise of his functions.
- The council of ministers remains in office as long as it enjoys the confidence of the Lok Sabha.

**Cabinet Ministers**:
- They are senior ministers and heading important ministries.
- They are members of the cabinet.
- They attend the meetings of the cabinet.
- They are generally appointed from the majority party or coalition partners in the Lok Sabha.

**Ministers of State (Independent Charge)**:
- They are in charge of less important departments.
- They are not members of the cabinet.
- They do not attend the meetings of the cabinet.
- They are responsible to their cabinet ministers.

**Ministers of State**:
- They are attached to and work under Cabinet Ministers or Ministers of State (Independent Charge).
- They are not members of the cabinet.
- They do not attend the meetings of the cabinet.
- They assist their senior ministers.

**Advantages of Council of Ministers**:
- It aids and advises the president in exercising his functions.
- It provides stability and continuity to the government as it remains in office as long as it has the confidence of the Lok Sabha.
- It consists of experts and specialists from various fields who can effectively handle their ministerial responsibilities.

**Disadvantages of Council of Ministers**:
- There can be conflicts between ministers belonging to different parties in a coalition government which can affect governance.
- There is a possibility of too much concentration of power in the hands of the prime minister and a few senior cabinet ministers.
- There can be lack of coordination between ministers which can lead to delays and inefficiencies in the system.
