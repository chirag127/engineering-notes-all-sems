#### Contract Theory:

- Contract theory is a framework for analyzing relationships where two or more parties exchange goods, services, or other valuable things. It explores why and under what conditions people and organizations form, maintain, and break mutually-beneficial cooperative relationships.
- According to contract theory, individuals enter into contractual agreements based on a rational calculation of costs and benefits to themselves. If the benefits outweigh the costs, the individuals will work to maximize the benefits from the relationship. However, if circumstances change and the costs begin to outweigh the benefits, the individuals will likely end or renegotiate the relationship.
- Key components of contract theory are:

1. Contracts are based on rational self-interest: The individuals enter a contract because they believe it will benefit them in some way. They weigh the potential gains and losses to themselves before forming and continuing the contract.
2. Contracts involve an exchange of value: The individuals must gain something of value from the contract that makes it worthwhile to them, such as money, goods, services, or other rewards. Without a perception of equal or greater value received, there is no incentive to enter or remain in a contract.
3. The terms of the contract must be enforceable: The contract must include clear expectations and rules that can be enforced through legal, social, or internal means. If the terms are vague or unenforceable, then the contract loses its value because individuals can't ensure that the other parties will hold up their end of the bargain.

- Examples: Employment contracts, purchasing goods/services, peace treaties, etc.
- Advantage: Explains how and why mutually-beneficial cooperative relationships form and persist. It recognizes that individuals act in rational self-interest.
- Disadvantage: It assumes all human behavior is rational and selfish, while in reality emotion, altruism, and other factors also influence human decisions. It also focuses on exchanges of material value while ignoring social and psychological rewards of relationships.
