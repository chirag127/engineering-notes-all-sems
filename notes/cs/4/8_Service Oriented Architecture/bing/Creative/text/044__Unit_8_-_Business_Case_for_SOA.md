## Unit 8 - Business Case for SOA

- SOA stands for Service-Oriented Architecture, which is a design paradigm for building software applications that are composed of loosely coupled, reusable, and interoperable services.
- A service is a self-contained unit of functionality that provides a specific business capability or value to its consumers. A service can be implemented using any technology or platform, and can communicate with other services using standard protocols and formats.
- A business case for SOA is a document that outlines the benefits, costs, risks, and assumptions of adopting SOA in an organization. It also provides a roadmap for the SOA implementation, including the scope, objectives, milestones, and metrics of success.
- Some of the benefits of SOA are:
  - Increased agility: SOA enables faster and easier development, integration, and adaptation of business processes and applications, as services can be reused, composed, and modified according to changing business needs and opportunities.
  - Reduced costs: SOA reduces the duplication of effort and resources, as services can be shared and leveraged across multiple applications and domains. SOA also lowers the maintenance and operational costs, as services can be updated and managed independently, without affecting the consumers or providers of other services.
  - Improved quality: SOA improves the reliability, availability, and performance of applications, as services can be tested, monitored, and governed separately, and can be scaled and distributed according to the demand and load. SOA also enhances the security and compliance of applications, as services can be secured and audited individually, and can enforce common policies and standards.
  - Increased innovation: SOA fosters the collaboration and alignment of business and IT stakeholders, as services can be designed and developed based on the business requirements and goals, and can be exposed and consumed by different users and systems. SOA also enables the exploration and exploitation of new technologies and platforms, as services can be integrated and orchestrated with external or third-party services, such as cloud, mobile, or social media services.
- Some of the costs of SOA are:
  - Initial investment: SOA requires a significant upfront investment in terms of time, money, and resources, as the organization needs to establish the SOA vision, strategy, governance, architecture, infrastructure, and skills. The organization also needs to identify, analyze, design, and implement the services that will provide the most value and return on investment.
  - Cultural change: SOA involves a fundamental shift in the way the organization thinks and operates, as the business and IT stakeholders need to adopt a service-oriented mindset and culture, and collaborate and communicate effectively across different domains and boundaries. The organization also needs to manage the change and resistance that may arise from the existing legacy systems and processes, and the people and roles that may be affected by the SOA transformation.
  - Ongoing management: SOA requires a continuous and proactive management of the services and the SOA environment, as the organization needs to monitor, measure, and optimize the performance, quality, and value of the services, and ensure the alignment and compliance of the services with the business and IT objectives and policies. The organization also needs to deal with the complexity and challenges that may arise from the increased interdependence and variability of the services and the consumers and providers of the services.
