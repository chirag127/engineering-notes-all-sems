### History of Deep Learning

- Deep learning is a branch of machine learning that uses layers of algorithms to process data and imitate the thinking process or develop abstractions.
- Deep learning is often used for tasks such as image recognition, natural language processing, speech recognition, and computer vision.
- Deep learning can be traced back to 1943, when Walter Pitts and Warren McCulloch created a computer model based on the neural networks of the human brain. They used a combination of algorithms and mathematics they called "threshold logic" to mimic the thought process .
- In 1957, Frank Rosenblatt created the perceptron, a new version of the McCulloch-Pitts neuron that had true learning capabilities to do binary classification on its own. This inspired the research of shallow neural networks for years to come, until the first AI winter.
- In 1960, Henry J. Kelley developed the basics of a continuous backpropagation model, which is the backward propagation of errors for purposes of training. In 1962, Stuart Dreyfus simplified the model by using the chain rule instead of dynamic programming. Backpropagation would become a key technique for training deep neural networks in the future.
- In 1965, Alexey Grigoryevich Ivakhnenko and Valentin Grigorʹevich Lapa created the first multilayer neural network that used polynomial activation functions and was trained using the Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH). They used statistical analysis to select the best features from each layer and forward them to the next layer.
- In 1979, Kunihiko Fukushima introduced the concept of convolutional neural networks, which are neural networks with multiple pooling and convolutional layers. Convolutional neural networks are widely used for image processing and computer vision tasks.
- In 1986, Geoffrey Hinton, David Rumelhart, and Ronald Williams published a paper that popularized the use of backpropagation for training multilayer neural networks. They also introduced the concept of distributed representations, which are high-dimensional feature vectors that capture the semantic similarities between inputs.
- In 1989, Yann LeCun, Leon Bottou, Yoshua Bengio, and Patrick Haffner developed LeNet, a convolutional neural network that could recognize handwritten digits. LeNet was one of the first practical applications of deep learning and was used by the US Postal Service for zip code recognition.
- In 1997, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks were proposed by Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber. LSTM networks are a type of recurrent neural networks that can learn long-term dependencies and sequential patterns. LSTM networks are widely used for natural language processing and speech recognition tasks.
- In 2006, Geoffrey Hinton, Simon Osindero, and Yee-Whye Teh introduced the concept of deep belief networks, which are generative models that can learn multiple levels of representation from unlabeled data. They also proposed a fast and efficient way of pre-training deep neural networks using a greedy layer-wise approach.
- In 2012, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton won the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC) using a deep convolutional neural network called AlexNet. AlexNet achieved a significant improvement over the previous state-of-the-art methods and sparked a new wave of interest and research in deep learning.
- In 2014, Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio, and Aaron Courville published a book called "Deep Learning", which is considered to be one of the most comprehensive and authoritative sources on the topic. The book covers the theory, methods, and applications of deep learning in various domains.
- In 2015, Google DeepMind demonstrated the ability of deep reinforcement learning to master complex and challenging games such as Atari and Go. Deep reinforcement learning combines deep neural networks with reinforcement learning, which is a type of learning that is based on rewards and penalties.
- In 2018, OpenAI introduced Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which are a type of generative models that can produce realistic and high-quality images, text, audio, and video. GANs consist of two competing neural networks: a generator that tries to create fake samples, and a discriminator that tries to distinguish between real and fake samples.
