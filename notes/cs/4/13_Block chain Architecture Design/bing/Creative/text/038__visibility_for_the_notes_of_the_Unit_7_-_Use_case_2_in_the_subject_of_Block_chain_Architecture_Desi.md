### Visibility

- Visibility is the ability to see and verify the transactions and events that occur on a blockchain network.
- Visibility is important for use cases that involve multiple parties who need to share information and trust each other, such as supply chain management, trade finance, healthcare, and identity management.
- Visibility can be achieved by using different types of blockchain networks, such as public, private, or consortium, depending on the level of access and control required by the participants.
- Public blockchains are open and transparent, allowing anyone to join and view the transactions and events on the network. However, they may have scalability and privacy issues, as well as high costs and energy consumption.
- Private blockchains are closed and permissioned, allowing only authorized participants to join and view the transactions and events on the network. They offer more scalability and privacy, as well as lower costs and energy consumption, but they may have less security and decentralization.
- Consortium blockchains are hybrid and collaborative, allowing a group of trusted participants to join and view the transactions and events on the network. They offer a balance between scalability, privacy, security, and decentralization, but they may have less flexibility and innovation.
- Visibility can also be enhanced by using different types of consensus mechanisms, such as proof-of-work, proof-of-stake, proof-of-authority, or proof-of-elapsed-time, depending on the trade-offs between speed, security, and fairness.
- Proof-of-work is a consensus mechanism that requires participants to solve complex mathematical puzzles to validate transactions and events on the network. It is secure and fair, but it is slow and energy-intensive.
- Proof-of-stake is a consensus mechanism that requires participants to stake their own tokens or assets to validate transactions and events on the network. It is fast and energy-efficient, but it may have security and fairness issues, such as the "nothing at stake" problem or the "rich get richer" problem.
- Proof-of-authority is a consensus mechanism that requires participants to have a reputation or authority to validate transactions and events on the network. It is fast and scalable, but it may have security and decentralization issues, such as the "single point of failure" problem or the "tyranny of the majority" problem.
- Proof-of-elapsed-time is a consensus mechanism that requires participants to wait for a random amount of time to validate transactions and events on the network. It is fair and energy-efficient, but it may have speed and security issues, such as the "sybil attack" problem or the "51% attack" problem.
