## Unit 3 - Permissioned Blockchains

- Permissioned blockchains are a type of distributed ledger technology (DLT) that allow only authorized participants to join the network, validate transactions, and execute smart contracts.
- Permissioned blockchains are different from permissionless blockchains, such as Bitcoin or Ethereum, which are open to anyone and rely on consensus mechanisms, such as proof-of-work or proof-of-stake, to ensure security and trust.
- Permissioned blockchains are suitable for use cases that require privacy, compliance, scalability, and governance, such as enterprise applications, consortiums, or regulated industries.
- Permissioned blockchains can be classified into two categories: private and consortium.
  - Private blockchains are controlled by a single entity or organization, which decides who can access the network and what roles they can perform. Private blockchains are often used for internal purposes, such as data management, auditing, or supply chain optimization.
  - Consortium blockchains are shared by a group of entities or organizations, which agree on the rules and policies of the network and the roles and permissions of the participants. Consortium blockchains are often used for cross-organizational collaboration, such as trade finance, healthcare, or energy.
- Permissioned blockchains can use various consensus protocols, such as proof-of-authority, proof-of-elapsed-time, or Byzantine fault tolerance, to achieve agreement among the validators and ensure the integrity and finality of the transactions.
- Permissioned blockchains can also implement various privacy and confidentiality features, such as encryption, zero-knowledge proofs, or channels, to protect the data and transactions from unauthorized access or disclosure.
- Some examples of permissioned blockchain platforms are Hyperledger Fabric, Corda, Quorum, and Besu.
