### System models for the notes of the Unit 4 - Agreement Protocols in the subject of DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM

A system model is a descriptive tool that captures the common properties and design choices of a distributed system. It helps to understand the behavior and limitations of a system, and to compare different systems based on their assumptions and guarantees.

There are three main types of system models:

- **Physical models**: capture the hardware composition of a system in terms of computers and other devices and their interconnecting network. They also describe the characteristics of the network, such as its topology, latency, bandwidth, reliability, and security.
- **Node models**: capture the software composition of a system in terms of processes and threads that run on each node. They also describe the characteristics of the nodes, such as their availability, failure modes, synchronization, and communication primitives.
- **Timing models**: capture the temporal aspects of a system, such as the clock synchronization, the message ordering, the event occurrence, and the global state. They also describe the assumptions and guarantees of the system regarding the timing behavior, such as whether it is synchronous, asynchronous, or partially synchronous.

Some examples of system models are:

- **Client-server model**: a physical model that describes a system where clients request services from servers, and servers provide services to clients. The clients and servers are usually located on different nodes and communicate over a network. The client-server model is widely used for web applications, database systems, and remote procedure calls.
- **Peer-to-peer model**: a physical model that describes a system where nodes act as both clients and servers, and cooperate to provide services to each other. The nodes are usually distributed over a large-scale network and communicate using overlay protocols. The peer-to-peer model is widely used for file sharing, content distribution, and decentralized applications.
- **Crash-recovery model**: a node model that describes a system where nodes can fail by crashing, but can recover after some time and resume their normal operation. The nodes are assumed to have persistent storage that can survive crashes and maintain their state. The crash-recovery model is widely used for fault-tolerant systems, such as distributed databases, consensus protocols, and replication schemes.
- **Byzantine model**: a node model that describes a system where nodes can fail in arbitrary ways, such as sending incorrect or malicious messages, deviating from the protocol, or colluding with other nodes. The nodes are assumed to have no persistent storage and no way to verify the correctness of other nodes. The Byzantine model is widely used for security-critical systems, such as distributed ledger systems, cryptographic protocols, and distributed voting schemes.
- **Synchronous model**: a timing model that describes a system where there are known bounds on the clock drift, the message delay, and the event occurrence. The system can rely on these bounds to synchronize the clocks, order the messages, and detect the events. The synchronous model is widely used for real-time systems, such as distributed control systems, sensor networks, and multimedia systems.
- **Asynchronous model**: a timing model that describes a system where there are no known bounds on the clock drift, the message delay, and the event occurrence. The system cannot rely on any timing assumptions and has to cope with uncertainty and unpredictability. The asynchronous model is widely used for general-purpose systems, such as distributed operating systems, distributed algorithms, and distributed middleware.
