### Atomic Commit in Distributed Database system for the notes of the Unit 4 - Agreement Protocols in the subject of DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM

- An atomic commit is an operation that applies a set of distinct changes as a single operation. If the changes are applied, then the atomic commit is said to have succeeded. If there is a failure before the atomic commit can be completed, then all of the changes completed in the atomic commit are reversed. This ensures that the system is always left in a consistent state. 
- Atomic commits are essential for multi-step updates to data, such as transferring money from one account to another, or updating multiple records in a database.  
- Atomic commits fulfill two of the key properties of ACID, atomicity and consistency. Atomicity ensures that either all of the changes are made, or none of them are made. Consistency ensures that the state of data is valid before and after the transaction.  
- In distributed database systems, the primary need for commit protocols is to maintain the atomicity of distributed transactions. A distributed transaction is a transaction that involves multiple servers or sites. The execution of a distributed transaction at many sites must either be committed at all sites or aborted at all sites.  
- In distributed systems, a transaction manager is used to coordinate the distinct operations and then commit/rollback the transaction as needed. At the time of coordination in distributed transactions, one of the servers becomes a coordinator, and the rest of the workers become coordinators. The coordinator keeps track of participating servers, gathers results from workers, and makes a decision to ensure transaction consistency. The workers are aware of the coordinator’s existence and communicate their outcome to the coordinator and then follow the coordinator’s decision.  
- The problem with atomic commits is that they require coordination between multiple systems, which may be unreliable or fail. This means no algorithm can coordinate with all systems as proven in the Two Generals Problem. As databases become more and more distributed, this coordination will increase the difficulty of making truly atomic commits.  
- There are different approaches of commit protocols in distributed systems, such as blocking and non-blocking protocols. Blocking protocols require all participants to wait for the final decision until all failures are resolved. Non-blocking protocols allow some participants to proceed without waiting for the final decision, even if some failures occur.  
- Some examples of blocking protocols are two-phase commit (2PC) and three-phase commit (3PC). Some examples of non-blocking protocols are Paxos commit and Skeen's algorithm.  

Mnemonics and learning tricks:

- To remember the ACID properties of transactions, you can use the mnemonic: A Cat Is Durable. 
- To remember the difference between blocking and non-blocking protocols, you can use the analogy: Blocking protocols are like waiting in a queue until everyone is served. Non-blocking protocols are like ordering online and getting a confirmation later. 
- To remember the steps of two-phase commit, you can use the mnemonic: Vote And Commit. The first phase is the voting phase, where the coordinator asks the workers to vote on whether to commit or abort. The second phase is the commit phase, where the coordinator decides based on the votes and informs the workers.
