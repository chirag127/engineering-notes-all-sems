### Absence of global clock for the notes of the Unit 1 - Characterization of Distributed Systems in the subject of DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM

- A global clock is a system-wide clock that is equally accessible to all processes and can be used to timestamp events and order them in a distributed system.
- In a distributed system, there are many processes that are spatially separated and running independently. Each process has its own local clock that may run at a different rate or granularity, leading to them being asynchronous.
- Due to the absence of a global clock, the notion of common time does not exist in a distributed system; different processes may have different notions of time. As a result, it is not always possible to determine the order in which two events on different processes were executed.
- The absence of a global clock also makes it difficult to obtain a consistent view of the system state, as the states of different processes may not be synchronized with each other. Moreover, the message delays in the communication channels are arbitrary and unpredictable, which further complicates the synchronization problem.
- The absence of a global clock has an impact on both the design and implementation of distributed systems, as it poses challenges for algorithms that rely on temporal ordering of events, such as distributed scheduling, distributed mutual exclusion, distributed consensus, etc.
- To overcome the limitation of the absence of a global clock, different schemes have been proposed that implement an abstract notion of time and can be used to order events in a distributed system. Some of these schemes are:

  - Physical clocks: These are clocks that are based on physical devices, such as atomic clocks, quartz crystals, etc. They try to approximate the real time and synchronize with each other using various protocols, such as NTP, GPS, etc. However, physical clocks are not perfectly accurate or reliable, and they may drift or fail over time. Moreover, physical clocks cannot guarantee a total order of events, as two events may have the same timestamp or the timestamps may not reflect the causal order of events.
  - Logical clocks: These are clocks that are based on logical rules, such as counters, vectors, matrices, etc. They try to capture the causal order of events and assign logical timestamps to them. Logical clocks do not depend on physical devices or synchronization protocols, and they can guarantee a partial order of events that is consistent with causality. However, logical clocks cannot measure the real time or the duration of events, and they may not be able to distinguish concurrent events.
  - Hybrid clocks: These are clocks that combine both physical and logical clocks, such as Lamport timestamps, vector clocks, matrix clocks, etc. They try to provide both causal and total order of events, as well as some notion of real time and duration. Hybrid clocks can be more expressive and precise than physical or logical clocks alone, but they may also incur more overhead and complexity.

- A mnemonic to remember the types of clocks is: **P**hysical clocks are **P**recise but **P**roblematic, **L**ogical clocks are **L**ogical but **L**imited, **H**ybrid clocks are **H**ybrid but **H**eavy.
