### Total order for the notes of the Unit 1 - Characterization of Distributed Systems in the subject of DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM

- A distributed system is a collection of independent processes that communicate by message passing over a network.
- A distributed system is said to have **partial order** if we can have a partial order relationship among the events in the system. A partial order relationship means that some events are ordered by a causal relationship, while others are concurrent or unrelated.
- A distributed system is said to have **total order** if we can establish a causal relationship among all events in the system. A total order relationship means that every event has a unique position in the global sequence of events, and every process agrees on this sequence.
- Total order is very useful for distributed system implementation, as it allows processes to coordinate their actions and maintain a consistent state of the shared resources. For example, total order can be used to implement mutual exclusion, atomic broadcast, consensus, replication, and fault tolerance.
- However, total order is also very challenging to achieve in a distributed system, as it requires processes to synchronize their clocks, exchange messages, and cope with failures and delays. Moreover, total order may not reflect the real-time order of events, as it depends on the logical clocks and the communication medium used by the processes.
- There are different approaches to achieve total order in a distributed system, such as:
  - **Atomic broadcast**: A central process is created that observes every inbound message and stamps it with a sequence number before any other system component is allowed to process it. Other system components are required to process messages in the order given by the sequence number.
  - **Distributed consensus**: A formally proven distributed communication protocol such as RAFT delivers total order over a high speed/low latency medium; business logic is tied directly into the RAFT commands and receives them totally ordered.
  - **Lamport timestamps**: Every process maintains a logical clock that is incremented on every local event and synchronized with other processes on every message exchange. Every message carries the timestamp of the sender, and every process updates its clock to the maximum of its own clock and the received timestamp. The total order of events is determined by comparing their timestamps, and breaking ties with some arbitrary mechanism (e.g. the ID of the process).

- A possible mnemonic to remember the concept of total order is: **T**otal order is **T**ough to achieve, but **T**errific for coordination.
