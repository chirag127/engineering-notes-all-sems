### System models for the notes of the Unit 4 - Agreement Protocols in the subject of DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM

System models are abstract descriptions of the properties and design choices of a distributed system. They help to understand the behavior and limitations of a system, and to reason about its correctness and performance. System models can be classified into three types: architectural models, interaction models, and fault models.

- Architectural models describe the structure and organization of a system in terms of components and their interactions. They also define the responsibilities and roles of each component, and how they are placed and replicated in the system. Some common architectural models are:

  - Client-server model: A system where one or more servers provide services to multiple clients that request them. The servers are usually centralized and have more resources than the clients. The clients are usually distributed and have less resources than the servers. Examples of client-server systems are web applications, email systems, and database systems.
  - Peer-to-peer model: A system where each component acts as both a client and a server, and can provide and request services from other components. The components are usually distributed and have similar resources. There is no central authority or coordination in the system. Examples of peer-to-peer systems are file sharing systems, distributed hash tables, and blockchain systems.
  - Publish-subscribe model: A system where components communicate by publishing messages to topics and subscribing to topics of interest. The messages are delivered to all subscribers of a topic, without the need for direct connections between publishers and subscribers. The system may use a broker or a mediator to route the messages, or use a decentralized approach. Examples of publish-subscribe systems are event-driven systems, message queues, and streaming platforms.

- Interaction models describe the communication and coordination mechanisms used by the components of a system. They also define the assumptions and guarantees about the timing and ordering of messages and events in the system. Some common interaction models are:

  - Synchronous model: A system where there are known bounds on the message delay, the processing time, and the clock drift of the components. The system can use deterministic algorithms and protocols to achieve agreement and consistency. Examples of synchronous systems are real-time systems, embedded systems, and sensor networks.
  - Asynchronous model: A system where there are no known bounds on the message delay, the processing time, and the clock drift of the components. The system cannot rely on deterministic algorithms and protocols, and has to use probabilistic or approximate solutions to achieve agreement and consistency. Examples of asynchronous systems are most distributed systems on the Internet, such as web services, cloud computing, and distributed databases.
  - Partially synchronous model: A system where there are some bounds on the message delay, the processing time, and the clock drift of the components, but they may not hold all the time or for all components. The system has to adapt to the changing conditions and use hybrid algorithms and protocols to achieve agreement and consistency. Examples of partially synchronous systems are mobile systems, wireless networks, and fault-tolerant systems.

- Fault models describe the types and frequency of failures that may occur in a system, and how the system can detect and recover from them. They also define the assumptions and guarantees about the availability and reliability of the system. Some common fault models are:

  - Crash fault model: A system where a component may stop functioning and never resume. The system can detect the failure by using timeouts or heartbeats, and can recover by using backups or replicas. The system can tolerate up to f crash faults if it has at least f+1 components. Examples of systems that use crash fault model are primary-backup systems, quorum systems, and consensus protocols.
  - Byzantine fault model: A system where a component may behave arbitrarily and maliciously, and may send incorrect or conflicting messages to other components. The system cannot easily detect the failure, and has to use cryptographic techniques or voting schemes to verify the messages. The system can tolerate up to f Byzantine faults if it has at least 3f+1 components. Examples of systems that use Byzantine fault model are blockchain systems, secure multiparty computation, and Byzantine agreement protocols.
  - Omission fault model: A system where a component may fail to send or receive some messages, but otherwise behaves correctly. The system can detect the failure by using acknowledgments or retransmissions, and can recover by using retries or timeouts. The system can tolerate up to f omission faults if it has at least 2f+1 components. Examples of systems that use omission fault model are reliable broadcast protocols, distributed transactions, and atomic commit protocols.
