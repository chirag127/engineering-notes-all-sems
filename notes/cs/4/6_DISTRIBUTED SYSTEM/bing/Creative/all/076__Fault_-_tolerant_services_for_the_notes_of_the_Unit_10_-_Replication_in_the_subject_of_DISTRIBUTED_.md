### Fault-tolerant services for the notes of the Unit 10 - Replication in the subject of DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM

- Fault-tolerant services are services that can continue to function correctly even in the presence of failures, such as server crashes, network partitions, or malicious attacks.
- Replication is a powerful technique for achieving fault-tolerance in distributed systems, by creating and maintaining multiple copies of the same service state across different servers or locations.
- Replication can improve the availability and performance of distributed services, by allowing clients to access the closest or most responsive replica, and by masking the failures of some replicas.
- Replication can also introduce challenges and tradeoffs, such as ensuring the consistency and coordination of replicas, dealing with conflicting updates, and balancing the overhead of replication with the benefits of fault-tolerance.
- There are different types of replication techniques, depending on the assumptions, goals, and properties of the system and the service. Some of the main types are:

  - **Primary-backup replication**: In this technique, one replica is designated as the primary, and the others are backups. The primary is responsible for processing all the client requests and updating the backups. The backups are passive and only become active when the primary fails. This technique ensures strong consistency among replicas, but introduces a single point of failure and a performance bottleneck in the primary.  
  - **Active replication**: In this technique, all replicas are active and process the same client requests in the same order. The client sends the request to all replicas or to a group communication system that delivers the request to all replicas. The client waits for a majority of replicas to reply with the same result. This technique tolerates failures of some replicas, but requires more network and computational resources, and may introduce non-determinism or divergence among replicas.  
  - **Causal replication**: In this technique, replicas are updated in a way that preserves the causal order of events, i.e., the order that reflects the potential influence or dependence among events. For example, if a client updates a replica and then reads from another replica, the read should reflect the update. This technique allows replicas to be updated asynchronously and independently, but requires additional metadata to track the causal dependencies among events.  
  - **Fused replication**: In this technique, replicas are updated using a combination of coding theory and replication, to achieve both efficiency and savings in storage and messages during normal operations. Coding theory allows replicas to store and transmit less information than the original state, but still be able to reconstruct the state if some replicas fail. This technique may incur higher overhead during recovery from faults, but that may be acceptable if the probability of fault is low.  

- To implement fault-tolerant replication, some of the main challenges and components are:

  - **Consensus**: Consensus is the problem of reaching agreement among a set of processes or replicas on a value or an action, despite the possibility of failures. Consensus is essential for ensuring the consistency and coordination of replicas, especially in active replication. Consensus is also a fundamental problem in distributed systems, and many other problems can be reduced to consensus. However, consensus is also impossible to solve in some failure scenarios, such as asynchronous systems with unreliable communication. Therefore, various algorithms and protocols have been proposed to solve consensus under different assumptions and models, such as Paxos, Raft, and Byzantine fault tolerance.   
  - **Group communication**: Group communication is the abstraction of sending and receiving messages among a set of processes or replicas, as a group. Group communication can provide various properties and guarantees, such as reliable delivery, ordering, atomicity, and membership. Group communication can simplify the implementation of replication, by providing the adequate multicast primitives to implement either primary-backup replication or active replication. For example, total order multicast ensures that all replicas receive the same messages in the same order, and view synchronous multicast ensures that all replicas have a consistent view of the group membership.
