### Likelihood Distortions for the notes of the Unit 6 - SPEECH-ANALYSIS in the subject of Natural Language Processing

In the field of natural language processing, the concept of likelihood distortions refers to the phenomenon where the likelihood of a particular word or phrase is distorted due to various factors. These factors can include the frequency of occurrence of a particular word, the context in which it appears, and the presence of other words in the same sentence.

Here are some common likelihood distortions that you should be aware of:

1. **Zipf's law:** This law states that the frequency of occurrence of a word is inversely proportional to its rank in the frequency table. In other words, the most common word in a language will occur twice as often as the second most common word, three times as often as the third most common word, and so on. This can lead to distortions in the likelihood of less common words, which may be underestimated in models that rely solely on frequency-based measures.

2. **Contextual bias:** The likelihood of a word can also be affected by the context in which it appears. For example, the word "bank" can refer to a financial institution or the side of a river. The context in which it appears can affect which meaning is more likely, leading to distortions in the likelihood of the word.

3. **Co-occurrence bias:** The presence of other words in the same sentence can also affect the likelihood of a word. For example, the word "car" is more likely to occur in a sentence that also contains the words "drive" or "gasoline". This can lead to distortions in the likelihood of the word, as it may be overrepresented in models that rely solely on co-occurrence measures.

Mnemonics and learning tricks for these likelihood distortions can be helpful in remembering them for exams. One possible mnemonic for Zipf's law is to remember that the most common word in a language is "the", which has only three letters. This can help you remember that the most common word is often short and simple.

For contextual bias, you can remember that the context in which a word appears can affect its meaning. One trick is to imagine the word in different contexts and see how its meaning changes. For example, imagine the word "bank" in the context of a river or in the context of a financial institution.

Finally, for co-occurrence bias, you can remember that the presence of other words in the same sentence can affect the likelihood of a word. One trick is to imagine different words that could appear in the same sentence with the target word and see how that affects its likelihood.
