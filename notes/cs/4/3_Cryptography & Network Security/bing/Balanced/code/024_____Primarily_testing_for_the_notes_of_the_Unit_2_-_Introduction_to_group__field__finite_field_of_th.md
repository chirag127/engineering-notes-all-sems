- This unit covers the basic concepts and techniques of symmetric and asymmetric cryptography, as well as some mathematical tools and algorithms that are essential for understanding and implementing cryptographic algorithms.
- The main topics are:

  - Group, field, and finite field of the form GF(p): These are algebraic structures that consist of a set of elements and one or more operations that satisfy certain properties, such as closure, associativity, commutativity, identity, and inverse. A group is a structure with one operation, a field is a structure with two operations (usually called addition and multiplication), and a finite field is a field with a finite number of elements. A finite field of the form GF(p) is a field with p elements, where p is a prime number. Finite fields are useful for constructing symmetric and asymmetric ciphers, as well as for performing arithmetic operations modulo p.
  - Modular arithmetic: This is a system of arithmetic where numbers are reduced to a fixed range by using the remainder operation. For example, in modulo 7 arithmetic, 8 is equivalent to 1, 9 is equivalent to 2, and so on. Modular arithmetic is widely used in cryptography, as it allows for efficient computation and security against certain attacks. Modular arithmetic can be performed on integers, polynomials, matrices, and other structures.
  - Prime and relative prime numbers: A prime number is a natural number greater than 1 that has no positive divisors other than 1 and itself. A relative prime number is a natural number that has no common positive divisors with another natural number, except for 1. For example, 6 and 35 are relative prime numbers, as their only common divisor is 1. Prime and relative prime numbers are important for cryptography, as they are the building blocks of many cryptographic algorithms and protocols, such as RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and ElGamal.
  - Extended Euclidean Algorithm: This is an algorithm that computes the greatest common divisor (GCD) of two natural numbers, as well as the coefficients of a linear combination of the two numbers that equals the GCD. For example, the GCD of 30 and 18 is 6, and the Extended Euclidean Algorithm can find that 6 = 2 * 30 - 3 * 18. The Extended Euclidean Algorithm is useful for cryptography, as it can be used to find the multiplicative inverse of a number modulo another number, which is needed for encryption and decryption in some ciphers, such as RSA.
  - Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) encryption and decryption: This is a symmetric block cipher that encrypts and decrypts data in blocks of 128 bits, using a secret key of 128, 192, or 256 bits. AES is the most widely used symmetric cipher in the world, as it is fast, secure, and standardized. AES consists of four main steps: key expansion, initial round, rounds, and final round. Each step involves various transformations, such as substitution, permutation, XOR, and multiplication, that are applied to the data and the key. AES can operate in different modes, such as ECB, CBC, CFB, OFB, and CTR, that determine how the blocks are processed and chained together.
  - Fermat’s and Euler’s theorem: These are two theorems that relate the modular exponentiation of a number to its modulus. Fermat’s theorem states that if p is a prime number and a is any integer, then a^p ≡ a (mod p). Euler’s theorem generalizes Fermat’s theorem to any modulus that is not necessarily prime. It states that if a and n are coprime integers, then a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n), where φ(n) is the Euler’s totient function that counts the number of positive integers less than n that are coprime with n. Fermat’s and Euler’s theorem are useful for cryptography, as they can be used to simplify the computation of modular exponentiation, which is a common operation in many cryptographic algorithms, such as RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and ElGamal.
