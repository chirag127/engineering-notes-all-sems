## Unit 2 - Wireless Networking, Wireless LAN Overview: MAC issues, IEEE 802.11, Blue Tooth, Wireless

- Wireless networking is the technology that enables devices to communicate without wires or cables, using radio waves or infrared signals.
- Wireless LAN (WLAN) is a type of wireless network that connects devices within a local area, such as a home, office, or campus.
- WLANs use a medium access control (MAC) sublayer and a physical layer (PHY) to coordinate the access and transmission of data over the wireless medium.
- MAC issues in wireless networking include:
  - Half-duplex operation: Wireless devices can either send or receive data at a given time, but not both simultaneously. This limits the throughput and efficiency of the network.
  - Time-varying channel: Wireless signals are affected by factors such as distance, interference, noise, and fading. This causes the channel quality and capacity to change over time and space, requiring adaptive techniques to cope with the variations.
  - Hidden and exposed terminal problem: Wireless devices may not be able to sense each other's transmissions due to obstacles or different ranges. This can lead to collisions or unnecessary waiting, reducing the network performance.
- IEEE 802.11 is the most widely used standard for WLANs, which defines the MAC and PHY specifications for different frequency bands and modulation schemes.
- IEEE 802.11 MAC sublayer uses a distributed coordination function (DCF) based on carrier sense multiple access with collision avoidance (CSMA/CA) protocol, which allows devices to contend for the channel by sensing the medium before transmitting and backing off if the channel is busy.
- IEEE 802.11 MAC sublayer also supports an optional point coordination function (PCF) based on polling, which allows a central controller (called point coordinator) to grant access to the channel to different devices in a round-robin fashion, avoiding collisions and providing quality of service (QoS) guarantees.
- IEEE 802.11 PHY layer defines several variants for different frequency bands, such as 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 60 GHz, and different modulation schemes, such as frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS), direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS), orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM), and single-carrier frequency-division multiple access (SC-FDMA).
- IEEE 802.11 PHY layer also defines several enhancements for improving the network capacity, such as multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO), beamforming, channel bonding, and spatial reuse.
- IEEE 802.11 has several amendments that add new features and functionalities to the standard, such as 802.11a, 802.11b, 802.11g, 802.11n, 802.11ac, 802.11ad, 802.11ax, 802.11ay, and 802.11az.
- Blue Tooth is another wireless technology that enables short-range communication between devices, such as phones, headsets, keyboards, mice, and printers.
- Blue Tooth uses a frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) technique to avoid interference and increase security, by changing the frequency of transmission every 625 microseconds.
- Blue Tooth devices form a network called a piconet, which consists of one master device and up to seven active slave devices. Multiple piconets can be interconnected to form a scatternet.
- Blue Tooth also has several versions that provide different features and capabilities, such as Blue Tooth Low Energy (BLE), Blue Tooth Mesh, and Blue Tooth 5.
- Wireless networking has many advantages, such as mobility, flexibility, scalability, and cost-effectiveness, but also some challenges, such as security, privacy, interference, and power consumption.
