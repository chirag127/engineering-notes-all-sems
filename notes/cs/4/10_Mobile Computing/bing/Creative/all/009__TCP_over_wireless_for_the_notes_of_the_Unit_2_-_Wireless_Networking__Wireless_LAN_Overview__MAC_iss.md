### TCP over wireless for the notes of the Unit 2 - Wireless Networking, Wireless LAN Overview: MAC issues, IEEE 802.11, Blue Tooth, Wireless in the subject of Mobile Computing

- TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) is a reliable and connection-oriented transport layer protocol that provides end-to-end data delivery and congestion control over wired networks.
- TCP assumes that packet losses are mostly due to network congestion and reacts by reducing its congestion window size and retransmitting the lost packets.
- However, in wireless networks, packet losses can also occur due to wireless link errors, such as bit errors, fading, shadowing, and handoffs. These errors are not related to network congestion and should not trigger TCP's congestion control mechanisms.
- TCP over wireless networks faces several challenges, such as:
  - Reduced throughput and efficiency due to unnecessary window reductions and retransmissions.
  - Increased delay and jitter due to link layer retransmissions and acknowledgements.
  - Increased variance of round-trip time (RTT) estimation due to variable wireless link conditions and reordering of packets.
  - Head-of-line blocking due to in-order delivery requirement of TCP.
  - Asymmetric bandwidth and delay characteristics of uplink and downlink wireless channels.
  - Mobility and handoff issues that cause temporary disconnections and route changes.
- Several mechanisms have been proposed to improve the performance of TCP over wireless networks, such as:
  - Link layer mechanisms: These mechanisms aim to hide the wireless link errors from the TCP sender by using techniques such as forward error correction (FEC), automatic repeat request (ARQ), adaptive link layer strategies, and selective retransmissions. These mechanisms can reduce the packet loss rate and improve the link throughput, but they may also introduce additional delay, overhead, and complexity at the link layer. Moreover, they may not be able to handle all types of wireless errors, such as handoffs and mobility.
  - Split-connection mechanisms: These mechanisms divide the end-to-end TCP connection into two separate connections: one over the wired network and one over the wireless network. The wireless connection can use a different transport protocol or a modified version of TCP that is optimized for wireless links. The wired connection can use the standard TCP protocol. The split-connection mechanisms can isolate the wireless link errors from the wired network and allow application/transport level optimizations, but they also introduce overhead, lack of end-to-end semantics, and potential security and compatibility issues.
  - TCP sender adaptation mechanisms: These mechanisms modify the TCP sender to distinguish between wireless link errors and network congestion and react accordingly. These mechanisms can use techniques such as selective acknowledgements (SACK), explicit loss notification (ELN), or explicit congestion notification (ECN) to inform the TCP sender about the cause and location of packet losses. These mechanisms can preserve the end-to-end semantics and avoid unnecessary window reductions and retransmissions, but they may also require changes in the network infrastructure and the TCP receiver.
  - TCP receiver adaptation mechanisms: These mechanisms modify the TCP receiver to handle the wireless link errors and improve the TCP performance. These mechanisms can use techniques such as delayed acknowledgements, delayed duplicate acknowledgements, local retransmissions, or buffer management to reduce the number of duplicate acknowledgements, avoid spurious timeouts, and smooth the TCP sender's window fluctuations. These mechanisms can improve the TCP throughput and efficiency, but they may also increase the delay and buffer requirements at the TCP receiver.

- A mnemonic to remember the four types of mechanisms is: **L**ink layer, **S**plit-connection, **S**ender adaptation, and **R**eceiver adaptation, or **LSSR**.
