### Quantum Bits

- A quantum bit or qubit is the basic unit of quantum information. It is a two-state quantum system that can exist in a superposition of two basis states, usually denoted as |0> and |1>.
- Unlike a classical bit, which can only store one of the two values 0 or 1, a qubit can store a linear combination of both values, called a quantum state. The quantum state of a qubit can be written as:

  |ψ> = α|0> + β|1>

  where α and β are complex numbers such that |α|^2 + |β|^2 = 1.

- The quantum state of a qubit can be manipulated by applying quantum gates, which are unitary operators that act on one or more qubits. Some examples of quantum gates are the Hadamard gate, the Pauli-X gate, the CNOT gate, and the Toffoli gate.
- The quantum state of a qubit can be measured by projecting it onto one of the basis states, either |0> or |1>. The outcome of the measurement is probabilistic, depending on the values of α and β. The probability of measuring |0> is |α|^2 and the probability of measuring |1> is |β|^2. After the measurement, the qubit collapses to the measured state and loses its superposition.
- The quantum state of a qubit can be represented by a point on the surface of a unit sphere, called the Bloch sphere. The basis states |0> and |1> correspond to the north and south poles of the sphere, respectively. The superposition states correspond to the points on the equator of the sphere, and the general states correspond to the points in between. The Bloch sphere is a useful tool for visualizing the effects of quantum gates and measurements on qubits.
