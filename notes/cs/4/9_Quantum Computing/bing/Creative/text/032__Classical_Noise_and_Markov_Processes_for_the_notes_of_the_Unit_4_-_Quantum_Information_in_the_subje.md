### Classical Noise and Markov Processes

- Classical noise is a random disturbance that affects the state or measurement of a physical system. It can be modeled by a stochastic process, which is a collection of random variables indexed by time or space.
- A Markov process is a special type of stochastic process that satisfies the Markov property, which means that the future state of the system only depends on the present state, and not on the past history. A Markov process can be characterized by a transition matrix that specifies the probabilities of moving from one state to another in a given time interval.
- Classical noise and Markov processes are useful tools for describing the effects of environmental fluctuations on classical systems, such as bits, coins, dice, etc. For example, a bit can be subject to noise that flips its state with some probability, and this can be modeled by a Markov process with two states (0 and 1) and a transition matrix that depends on the noise parameter.
- However, classical noise and Markov processes are not sufficient to capture the full complexity of quantum systems, which can exhibit superposition, entanglement, interference, and other non-classical phenomena. Quantum systems can be affected by quantum noise, which is a disturbance that arises from the interaction with a quantum environment, such as photons, phonons, spins, etc. Quantum noise can induce decoherence, which is the loss of quantum coherence due to the entanglement with the environment, and dissipation, which is the loss of energy or information due to the interaction with the environment.
- Quantum noise and quantum processes are more challenging to model and analyze than classical noise and Markov processes, because they require the use of quantum mechanics and quantum information theory. Quantum processes are generalizations of Markov processes that can describe the evolution of quantum systems under noisy conditions. Quantum processes can be characterized by quantum channels, which are linear maps that transform quantum states, or by quantum operations, which are completely positive and trace-preserving maps that transform quantum states. Quantum processes can also be represented by quantum stochastic processes, which are collections of quantum operations indexed by time or space.
- Quantum processes can be classified into different types according to the degree of memory or correlation of the noise. A quantum process is Markovian if the noise is memoryless, which means that the quantum system only interacts with fresh environmental degrees of freedom at each time step, and the past history of the system and the environment does not affect the future evolution. A quantum process is non-Markovian if the noise has memory, which means that the quantum system interacts with the same or correlated environmental degrees of freedom at different time steps, and the past history of the system and the environment can affect the future evolution. Non-Markovian processes can exhibit quantum non-Markovian phenomena, such as the revival of quantum coherence or entanglement, or the violation of the data processing inequality.
- Quantum processes are important for understanding the behavior and performance of quantum systems in realistic settings, such as quantum computation, quantum communication, quantum metrology, quantum thermodynamics, etc. Quantum processes can also be used to model physical phenomena that involve quantum noise, such as quantum measurement, quantum feedback, quantum error correction, quantum control, quantum cryptography, quantum metrology, quantum thermodynamics, etc. Quantum processes can also be exploited to achieve quantum advantages, such as quantum speedup, quantum error correction, quantum metrology, quantum cryptography, quantum thermodynamics, etc.
