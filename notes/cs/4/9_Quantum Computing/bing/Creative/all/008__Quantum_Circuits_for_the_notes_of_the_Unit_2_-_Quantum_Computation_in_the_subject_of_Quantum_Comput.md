### Quantum Circuits for the notes of the Unit 2 - Quantum Computation in the subject of Quantum Computing

- A quantum circuit is a model for quantum computation, similar to classical circuits, in which a computation is a sequence of quantum gates, measurements, initializations of qubits to known values, and possibly other actions.
- A quantum gate is a basic unitary operation that acts on a fixed number of qubits, usually one or two, and changes their quantum state. Examples of quantum gates are the Hadamard gate, the Pauli-X gate, the CNOT gate, and the Toffoli gate.
- A quantum wire is a physical medium that carries qubits from one gate to another, preserving their quantum coherence. Quantum wires can be implemented using various physical systems, such as photons, electrons, or superconducting qubits.
- A quantum circuit can be represented by a directed acyclic graph (DAG), where the nodes are quantum gates and the edges are quantum wires. The input qubits are at the leftmost nodes and the output qubits are at the rightmost nodes. The order of the gates matters, as quantum gates do not generally commute.
- A quantum circuit can be described by a unitary matrix U that maps the input state vector |psi> to the output state vector |phi> = U|psi>. The unitary matrix U can be decomposed into a product of unitary matrices corresponding to the quantum gates in the circuit.
- A quantum circuit can also be simulated by a classical computer, using a vector of 2^n complex numbers to represent the state of n qubits. However, this simulation is exponentially costly in memory and time, as the size of the vector grows exponentially with the number of qubits.
- A quantum circuit can perform any reversible classical computation, by using reversible gates such as the CNOT and the Toffoli gates. However, a quantum circuit can also perform non-classical computations, such as superposition and entanglement, by using gates such as the Hadamard and the CNOT gates.
- A quantum circuit can implement any unitary transformation on n qubits, by using a universal set of quantum gates, such as the CNOT and the single-qubit gates. However, the number of gates required to approximate a unitary transformation with a given accuracy may vary depending on the choice of the universal set and the decomposition algorithm.
- A quantum circuit can be optimized by reducing the number of gates, the depth of the circuit, the width of the circuit, or the error rate of the circuit, depending on the design goals and the physical constraints. Various optimization techniques can be applied, such as gate cancellation, gate reordering, gate decomposition, gate synthesis, gate compilation, and error correction.
- A quantum circuit can be verified by checking whether the output state vector matches the expected state vector, or whether the output probability distribution matches the expected probability distribution, for a given input state vector or input probability distribution. However, this verification may be hard or impossible to perform in practice, due to the exponential complexity of the quantum state space and the measurement limitations of the quantum devices.
