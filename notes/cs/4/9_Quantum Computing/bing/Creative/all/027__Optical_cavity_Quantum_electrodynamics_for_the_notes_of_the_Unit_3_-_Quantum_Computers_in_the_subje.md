### Optical cavity Quantum electrodynamics for the notes of the Unit 3 - Quantum Computers in the subject of Quantum Computing

- Optical cavity Quantum electrodynamics (QED) is the study of the interaction between light confined in a reflective cavity and atoms or other particles, under conditions where the quantum nature of photons is significant.
- It could in principle be used to construct a quantum computer, by using the cavity photons as qubits and the atoms or other particles as quantum gates.
- The simplest case of optical cavity QED is the Jaynes–Cummings model, which describes the interaction between a single two-level atom and a single cavity mode.
- The atom and the cavity mode can exchange energy and information through the coupling constant g, which depends on the electric dipole moment of the atom and the electric field of the cavity mode at the position of the atom.
- The dynamics of the system can be solved exactly, and one of the most striking phenomena is the vacuum Rabi oscillation, which is the coherent oscillation between the states |e,0> (excited atom, zero photon) and |g,1> (ground atom, one photon).
- The vacuum Rabi oscillation can be used to generate single photons, entangle the atom and the cavity mode, or transfer quantum information between them.
- The frequency of the vacuum Rabi oscillation is given by the Rabi frequency Omega_R = sqrt(2g^2 + Delta^2), where Delta is the detuning between the atom and the cavity mode.
- The vacuum Rabi oscillation can be observed when the system is in the strong coupling regime, where g is much larger than the decay rates of the atom and the cavity mode.
- The strong coupling regime can be achieved by using high-finesse optical cavities, which have low losses and high reflectivity, and by placing the atom at the antinode of the cavity mode, where the electric field is maximum.
- Optical cavity QED has been realized experimentally with various systems, such as atoms, ions, molecules, quantum dots, superconducting qubits, and nanomechanical resonators, coupled to optical or microwave cavities  .
- Optical cavity QED has enabled the observation of many quantum optical effects, such as the Purcell effect, the photon blockade effect, the cavity bistability, the cavity quantum phase transition, the cavity electromagnetically induced transparency, and the cavity optomechanics  .
- Optical cavity QED has also opened the field of chiral quantum optics, which explores the effects of breaking the time-reversal symmetry in the interaction between light and matter.
- One way to break the time-reversal symmetry is to introduce synthetic magnetic fields for photons, which can induce circular orbits and topological phases for the cavity modes.
- Synthetic magnetic fields for photons can be generated by using various techniques, such as modulating the cavity parameters, applying external magnetic fields, or coupling the cavity modes to spin systems.
- Synthetic magnetic fields for photons can lead to novel phenomena, such as the quantum Hall effect, the edge states, the topological insulators, and the topological phase transitions for the cavity modes.
- Synthetic magnetic fields for photons can also enable new applications, such as backscatter-resilient quantum communication, topological quantum computation, and topological quantum metrology.
