### Measurement for the notes of the Unit 2 - Quantum Computation in the subject of Quantum Computing

- Measurement is a fundamental concept in quantum mechanics, where it is used to test or manipulate a physical system and obtain a numerical result.
- Measurement can also be used as a means of performing quantum computation, where entanglement is used as a resource and local measurements on qubits are used to drive the computation. This is known as measurement-based quantum computation (MQC).
- MQC has several advantages over other models of quantum computation, such as the quantum circuit model, the adiabatic quantum computation model, and the quantum Turing machine model. Some of these advantages are:
  - MQC is more robust against noise and errors, as it does not require precise control over the quantum system or long coherence times. It can also implement fault-tolerant schemes using error-correcting codes and post-selection techniques.
  - MQC is more efficient in terms of resources, as it only requires a single entangled state preparation and a sequence of single-qubit measurements, which are easier to implement than multi-qubit gates and operations.
  - MQC is more versatile and flexible, as it can simulate any other model of quantum computation using different types of entangled states and measurement patterns. It can also perform universal quantum computation using only one type of entangled state, such as the cluster state or the graph state.
- The general procedure of MQC consists of three steps: entangle the qubits, measure the ancillae (auxiliary qubits), and correct the outputs. In the first step, the qubits are entangled in order to prepare the source state, which encodes the quantum information and the computation. In the second step, the ancillae are measured in a specific order and basis, which determines the outcome of the computation. In the third step, the outputs are corrected according to the measurement results and the desired logical basis, which yields the final answer of the computation.
- The most prominent example of MQC is the one-way quantum computer, which uses a cluster state as the source state and performs quantum computation by measuring the qubits along the edges of the cluster. The cluster state is a highly entangled state of qubits arranged in a regular lattice, where each qubit is connected to its nearest neighbors by a controlled-Z (CZ) gate. The cluster state can be prepared by applying a Hadamard gate to each qubit followed by a CZ gate to each pair of neighboring qubits. The cluster state has the property that any qubit can be measured in any basis without disturbing the rest of the state, as long as the measurement results are communicated to the other qubits. This allows for a flexible and scalable implementation of quantum computation using only local measurements and classical communication.
- Another example of MQC is the teleportation-based model, which uses a Bell state as the source state and performs quantum computation by teleporting the qubits to different locations and measuring them in different bases. The Bell state is a maximally entangled state of two qubits, which can be written as |Φ+⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. The Bell state can be prepared by applying a Hadamard gate to the first qubit followed by a controlled-NOT (CNOT) gate to the second qubit. The Bell state has the property that any qubit can be teleported to another location by performing a joint measurement on the qubit and one of the Bell state qubits, and communicating the measurement result to the other Bell state qubit. This allows for a distributed and secure implementation of quantum computation using only entanglement, measurement, and classical communication.

- A possible mnemonic to remember the steps of MQC is: EMAC, which stands for Entangle, Measure, Ancillae, Correct. Alternatively, one can use the acronym MEOW, which stands for Measure, Entangle, Outputs, Weights, where the weights refer to the correction factors that depend on the measurement results.
