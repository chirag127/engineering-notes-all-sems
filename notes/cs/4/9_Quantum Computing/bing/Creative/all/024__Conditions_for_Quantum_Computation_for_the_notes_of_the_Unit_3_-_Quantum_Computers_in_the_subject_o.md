### Conditions for Quantum Computation for the notes of the Unit 3 - Quantum Computers in the subject of Quantum Computing

Quantum computation is the process of using quantum systems, such as qubits, to perform operations on data, such as quantum algorithms. Quantum computation can potentially solve some problems faster or more efficiently than classical computation, such as factoring large numbers, searching unsorted databases, or simulating quantum systems. However, quantum computation also faces many challenges, such as noise, decoherence, scalability, and error correction. To construct a practical and reliable quantum computer, certain conditions must be met. These conditions are known as the DiVincenzo criteria, proposed by the theoretical physicist David P. DiVincenzo in 2000. The DiVincenzo criteria consist of seven conditions, of which the first five are necessary for quantum computation, and the last two are necessary for quantum communication. The seven conditions are:

1. **A scalable physical system with well-characterized qubits.** A qubit is the basic unit of quantum information, which can exist in a superposition of two states, usually denoted as |0> and |1>. A quantum computer requires a large number of qubits to perform complex operations, and each qubit must be well-defined and controllable. The physical system that implements the qubits must be scalable, meaning that it can be extended to accommodate more qubits without compromising their quality or performance. Some examples of physical systems that can realize qubits are superconducting circuits, trapped ions, photons, or atoms.

2. **The ability to initialize the state of the qubits to a simple fiducial state.** Before performing any quantum computation, the qubits must be prepared in a known and simple state, such as |0> or |1>. This is called the initialization or reset of the qubits, and it ensures that the quantum computer starts from a clean slate. The initialization process must be fast and reliable, and it must not introduce any unwanted noise or errors to the qubits.

3. **Long relevant decoherence times.** Decoherence is the process by which a quantum system loses its quantum properties, such as superposition and entanglement, due to the interaction with its environment. Decoherence is the main source of noise and error in quantum computation, and it limits the time that a quantum computer can maintain and manipulate quantum information. The decoherence time is the time scale at which a quantum system becomes indistinguishable from a classical system. The relevant decoherence time is the decoherence time that affects the specific quantum operations that are performed by the quantum computer. For example, the phase coherence time is the time scale at which the relative phase between the |0> and |1> states of a qubit is lost, and it affects the single-qubit operations. The relevant decoherence times must be much longer than the time required to perform the quantum operations, and they must be comparable to or longer than the total running time of the quantum algorithm.

4. **A "universal" set of quantum gates.** A quantum gate is an operation that transforms the state of one or more qubits, such as a rotation, a swap, or an entangling operation. A set of quantum gates is universal if it can be used to approximate any arbitrary quantum operation to any desired accuracy. A universal set of quantum gates is necessary for quantum computation, because it allows the quantum computer to implement any quantum algorithm. A universal set of quantum gates can be composed of a finite number of elementary quantum gates, such as the Hadamard, the Pauli, the CNOT, and the Toffoli gates.

5. **A qubit-specific measurement capability.** Measurement is the process of extracting information from a quantum system, such as the state of a qubit. Measurement is essential for quantum computation, because it allows the quantum computer to output the result of the quantum algorithm, or to perform intermediate steps such as error correction or feedback. Measurement is also irreversible and probabilistic, meaning that it collapses the quantum state of the system to one of the possible outcomes, and that the outcome is not deterministic but depends on the probability amplitude of the state. A qubit-specific measurement capability means that the quantum computer can measure the state of any individual qubit, or any subset of qubits, without disturbing the state of the other qubits.

6. **Interconverting stationary and flying qubits and faithfully transmitting flying qubits between specified locations.** These two conditions are necessary for quantum communication, which is the process of transferring quantum information from one location to another, such as in quantum cryptography
