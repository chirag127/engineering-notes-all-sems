### Quantum Operations for the notes of the Unit 4 - Quantum Information in the subject of Quantum Computing

- Quantum operations are mathematical formalisms that describe the transformations that a quantum system can undergo. They include unitary evolution, measurement, and interaction with an environment. 
- Quantum operations are formulated in terms of the density matrix, which is a positive, Hermitian, and trace-one operator that represents the state of a quantum system. 
- A quantum operation is a linear, completely positive map from the set of density matrices to itself. A map is completely positive if it preserves the positivity of any density matrix, even when extended to a larger system. 
- A quantum operation is physical if it satisfies the trace-preserving condition, which means that the probability of the system remains one after the operation. A trace-preserving quantum operation is also called a quantum channel. 
- A quantum operation can be represented by a set of Kraus operators, which are matrices that satisfy the completeness relation. The action of a quantum operation on a density matrix is given by the sum of the products of the Kraus operators and their adjoints. 
- A quantum operation can also be represented by a unitary operator acting on a larger system that includes an ancilla. The ancilla is initially in a fixed state, and the quantum operation is obtained by tracing out the ancilla after the unitary evolution. This is called the Stinespring dilation theorem. 
- A quantum operation can be composed with another quantum operation by multiplying their Kraus operators or by concatenating their unitary operators. The composition of quantum operations is associative but not commutative. 
- A quantum operation can be inverted if it is bijective, meaning that it maps every density matrix to a unique density matrix. A bijective quantum operation is also called an automorphism. An automorphism is unitary if and only if it preserves the purity of the density matrix. 
- A quantum measurement is a special type of quantum operation that yields a classical outcome and collapses the quantum state. A quantum measurement can be described by a set of measurement operators, which are positive operators that satisfy the completeness relation. The probability of obtaining a certain outcome is given by the trace of the product of the measurement operator and the density matrix, and the post-measurement state is given by the normalized product of the measurement operator and the density matrix. 
- A quantum measurement can be generalized to a positive operator-valued measure (POVM), which is a set of positive operators that sum up to the identity operator. A POVM can be implemented by a quantum measurement on a larger system that includes an ancilla. 
- A quantum measurement can be non-destructive if it does not disturb the quantum state. A non-destructive measurement is also called a quantum observable, which is a Hermitian operator that commutes with the density matrix. The expectation value of a quantum observable is given by the trace of the product of the observable and the density matrix. 
- A quantum operation can be characterized by its action on a set of basis states, or by its matrix representation in a certain basis. A quantum operation can also be characterized by its Choi matrix, which is the density matrix of the joint system after applying the quantum operation to one half of a maximally entangled state. The Choi matrix encodes the complete information about the quantum operation, and can be used to check its properties, such as linearity, positivity, and trace-preserving. 
- A quantum operation can be compared with another quantum operation by using various measures of distance or similarity, such as the trace distance, the fidelity, the diamond norm, or the quantum process fidelity. These measures can be used to quantify the noise or the error of a quantum operation, or to evaluate the performance of a quantum algorithm.
