### Classical Noise and Markov Processes for the notes of the Unit 4 - Quantum Information in the subject of Quantum Computing

- Classical noise is a type of disturbance that affects the state of a classical system, such as a bit, a coin, or a dice. Classical noise can be modeled by randomizing the transition probabilities from one state to another, according to some probability distribution. For example, a bit can flip from 0 to 1 or vice versa with some probability p due to noise.
- Markov processes are a class of stochastic processes that have the property of memorylessness, meaning that the future state of the system only depends on the current state, and not on the past history. Markov processes can be described by a transition matrix that specifies the probability of going from one state to another in one step. For example, a coin toss is a Markov process with two states (heads and tails) and a transition matrix that depends on the bias of the coin.
- Classical noise and Markov processes can be used to model the effects of noise on quantum systems, such as qubits, quantum gates, or quantum channels. Quantum systems are subject to both classical and quantum noise, which can degrade the coherence and entanglement of quantum states, and reduce the fidelity and capacity of quantum information processing tasks. 
- To study the effects of classical noise on quantum systems, one can use the formalism of quantum operations, which are linear maps that transform quantum states according to the rules of quantum mechanics. Quantum operations can be represented by Kraus operators, which are matrices that act on the state vector or the density matrix of the quantum system. Quantum operations can also be characterized by their Choi matrix, which is a matrix that encodes the input-output relations of the quantum operation.
- One way to model classical noise on quantum systems is to assume that the noise is Markovian, meaning that it is independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) over time. This implies that the quantum operation that describes the noise is the same at each time step, and that the noise does not introduce any correlations between different time steps. Markovian noise can be described by a single Kraus operator or a single Choi matrix, which can be computed from the transition probabilities of the classical noise. For example, a bit flip noise on a qubit can be modeled by a Markovian quantum operation with a Kraus operator that depends on the bit flip probability p.
- Another way to model classical noise on quantum systems is to assume that the noise is non-Markovian, meaning that it is not i.i.d. over time. This implies that the quantum operation that describes the noise can vary at each time step, and that the noise can introduce correlations between different time steps. Non-Markovian noise can be described by a sequence of Kraus operators or a sequence of Choi matrices, which can be computed from the transition probabilities of the classical noise and the correlation function of the noise. For example, a bit flip noise on a qubit with memory can be modeled by a non-Markovian quantum operation with a sequence of Kraus operators that depend on the bit flip probability p and the memory parameter q.
- The effects of classical noise on quantum systems can be quantified by various measures, such as the fidelity, the entanglement, the capacity, or the filter function. The fidelity is a measure of how close two quantum states are, and it can be used to compare the ideal state of the quantum system with the noisy state after the quantum operation. The entanglement is a measure of how much quantum correlations exist between two or more quantum systems, and it can be used to assess the usefulness of the quantum system for quantum information tasks. The capacity is a measure of how much classical or quantum information can be transmitted through a quantum channel, and it can be used to evaluate the performance of the quantum channel under noise. The filter function is a function that captures the effect of the noise on the quantum system, and it can be used to compute the quantum process of a sequence of quantum operations under correlated noise.
