### Wireless Sensor Networks

Wireless sensor networks (WSNs) are networks of spatially dispersed and dedicated sensors that monitor and record the physical conditions of the environment and forward the collected data to a central location.  WSNs can measure environmental conditions such as temperature, sound, pollution levels, humidity and wind.  WSNs are typically composed of battery-powered sensor nodes that have limited energy, processing, storage and communication capabilities.  WSNs are often deployed in an ad-hoc manner and rely on wireless connectivity and spontaneous formation of networks.  WSNs can support various applications, such as:

- Internet of Things (IoT)
- Surveillance and monitoring for security, threat detection
- Environmental temperature, humidity, and air pressure
- Noise level of the surrounding
- Medical applications like patient monitoring
- Agriculture
- Landslide detection

Some of the challenges of WSNs are:

- Quality of service
- Security
- Energy efficiency
- Network throughput
- Performance
- Ability to cope with node failure
- Cross layer optimisation
- Scalability to large scale of deployment
- Interference
- Reliability

The main components of a WSN are:

- Sensors: Sensors are used to capture the environmental variables and convert them into electrical signals. Sensors can be passive or active, analog or digital, scalar or vector, etc. 
- Radio nodes: Radio nodes are used to receive the data produced by the sensors and send it to the base station or other nodes. Radio nodes can use different wireless technologies, such as Bluetooth, ZigBee, Wi-Fi, etc. Radio nodes can also perform some processing and aggregation of the sensor data. 
- Base station: Base station is the central processing unit of the WSN. It is connected to the internet or other networks and can communicate with the radio nodes. Base station can perform data analysis, storage, mining, and visualization. Base station can also control the sensor nodes and send commands or queries.
