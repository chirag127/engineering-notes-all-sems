### Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) Technology for the Notes of the Unit 2 - Hardware for IoT in the Subject of Internet of Things

- Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) is a form of wireless communication that uses electromagnetic or electrostatic coupling in the radio frequency portion of the electromagnetic spectrum to uniquely identify an object, animal or person. 
- An RFID system consists of three components: a scanning antenna, a transceiver and a transponder. The transponder is also known as the RFID tag, which is attached to the object to be identified. The scanning antenna and the transceiver are often combined into a single device called the RFID reader or interrogator.  
- There are two main types of RFID tags: active and passive. Active tags have their own power source, such as a battery, and can transmit signals over a longer distance. Passive tags do not have a power source and rely on the energy from the reader's radio waves to activate and communicate. There are also semi-passive tags, which have a battery to power the internal circuitry, but use the reader's energy to transmit signals. 
- RFID tags can store different amounts of data, ranging from a few bits to several kilobytes. Some tags are read-only, meaning the data is pre-programmed and cannot be changed. Some tags are read-write, meaning the data can be overwritten by the reader. Some tags are write-once, meaning the data can be written once by the reader and then becomes read-only. 
- RFID tags can also be classified by their frequency range, which affects their read range, data rate, and resistance to interference. There are three main types of RFID systems based on frequency: low frequency (LF), high frequency (HF), and ultra-high frequency (UHF). 

| Frequency | Range | Data Rate | Application |
|-----------|-------|-----------|-------------|
| LF (125-134 kHz) | Up to 10 cm | Low | Animal identification, access control, car immobilizers |
| HF (13.56 MHz) | Up to 1 m | Medium | Smart cards, library books, passports, contactless payment |
| UHF (860-960 MHz) | Up to 12 m | High | Supply chain management, inventory tracking, toll collection, vehicle identification |

- RFID technology has many advantages over other identification methods, such as barcodes or optical character recognition (OCR). Some of the advantages are:  
  - RFID tags can be read without direct line of sight, unlike barcodes or OCR.
  - RFID tags can be read from a greater distance, depending on the type of tag and reader.
  - RFID tags can be read faster and more accurately, as multiple tags can be read simultaneously.
  - RFID tags can store more data and can be updated dynamically, unlike barcodes or OCR.
  - RFID tags can be embedded or attached to various shapes and sizes of objects, and can withstand harsh environments, such as heat, moisture, or chemicals.
- RFID technology also has some disadvantages and challenges, such as:  
  - RFID tags can be affected by interference from other radio sources, such as metal, water, or other RFID systems.
  - RFID tags can be vulnerable to security and privacy issues, such as unauthorized reading, cloning, or tracking of tags.
  - RFID tags can be costly and complex to implement, depending on the type of tag, reader, and system integration.
  - RFID tags can have ethical and social implications, such as animal welfare, human rights, or environmental impact.

- RFID technology has many applications and use cases in various domains, such as:   
  - Manufacturing and logistics: RFID tags can be used to track the production, distribution, and inventory of goods and materials, as well as to automate processes, such as quality control, sorting, or packaging.
  - Retail and commerce: RFID tags can be used to enhance the customer experience, such as by enabling self-checkout, smart fitting rooms, or personalized recommendations, as well as to prevent theft, counterfeiting, or spoilage of products.
  - Transportation and mobility: RFID tags can be used to facilitate the identification, payment, and access of vehicles, drivers, and passengers, such as by enabling electronic toll collection, car sharing, or public transit systems.
