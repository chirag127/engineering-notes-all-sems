### Communicating data with H/W units for the notes of the Unit 7 - IoT Applications in the subject of Internet of Things

- IoT devices are the hardware components that collect, process, and transmit data to or from other devices, applications, or cloud services. They can be sensors, actuators, embedded systems, intelligent devices, or microcontrollers.
- IoT devices communicate data with H/W units using various protocols and standards, depending on the requirements of the application, the power consumption, the security, and the network topology.
- Some of the common IoT protocols and standards are:

  - Wi-Fi: A wireless network protocol that allows devices to connect to the internet or a local area network (LAN) using radio waves. Wi-Fi is widely used for high-bandwidth applications that require fast and reliable data transmission, such as video streaming, gaming, or smart home devices. Wi-Fi can also support mesh networks, where devices can relay data to each other without a central router. However, Wi-Fi consumes more power than other protocols and may not be suitable for battery-operated devices or long-range communication.
  - Bluetooth: A wireless network protocol that allows devices to connect and exchange data over short distances using radio waves. Bluetooth is commonly used for low-power and low-bandwidth applications, such as wearable devices, health monitors, or wireless keyboards. Bluetooth can also support mesh networks, where devices can form a network without a central hub. Bluetooth has low latency and high security, but it has a limited range and may interfere with other devices using the same frequency band.
  - Zigbee: A wireless network protocol that allows devices to form a mesh network using low-power radio waves. Zigbee is designed for low-data-rate and low-cost applications, such as smart lighting, smart metering, or environmental monitoring. Zigbee can support thousands of devices in a network and has a long range and high reliability. Zigbee also has low power consumption and high security, but it may not be compatible with other protocols or devices.
  - LoRa: A wireless network protocol that allows devices to communicate over long distances using low-power radio waves. LoRa is ideal for low-data-rate and low-cost applications, such as smart agriculture, smart city, or asset tracking. LoRa can support millions of devices in a network and has a range of up to 15 km in rural areas and 5 km in urban areas. LoRa also has low power consumption and high security, but it has high latency and may require a gateway to connect to the internet or other networks.
  - Cellular: A wireless network protocol that allows devices to connect to the internet or other networks using cellular towers and radio waves. Cellular is suitable for high-data-rate and high-reliability applications, such as video surveillance, autonomous vehicles, or remote monitoring. Cellular can support a large number of devices in a network and has a global coverage and high speed. Cellular also has high security and low latency, but it consumes more power than other protocols and may incur high costs.

- A mnemonic to remember the five common IoT protocols and standards is: **W**i-Fi, **B**luetooth, **Z**igbee, **L**oRa, **C**ellular. The first letters of each protocol spell out **WBZLC**, which sounds like "wiz lick". You can imagine a wizard licking a device to make it communicate with other devices using different protocols.
- A learning trick to compare the five common IoT protocols and standards is to use a table with the following criteria: data rate, power consumption, range, latency, security, cost, and compatibility. You can assign each protocol a score from 1 to 5 for each criterion, where 1 is the lowest and 5 is the highest. For example, Wi-Fi has a high data rate (5), high power consumption (1), medium range (3), low latency (5), high security (5), medium cost (3), and high compatibility (5).
