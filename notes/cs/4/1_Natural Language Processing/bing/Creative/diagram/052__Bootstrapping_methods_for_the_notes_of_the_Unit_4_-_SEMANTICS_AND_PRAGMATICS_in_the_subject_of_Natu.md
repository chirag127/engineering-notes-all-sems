Bootstrapping methods are linguistic theories that propose how children can acquire the syntax and meaning of a language by using different types of cues and information. There are four main types of bootstrapping methods: semantic, syntactic, prosodic, and pragmatic. 

Semantic bootstrapping is the idea that children can learn the syntactic categories and structures of a language by using their innate knowledge of semantic concepts, such as agents, actions, and objects. For example, a child might assume that a word that refers to an agent is a noun, and that a word that refers to an action is a verb. Semantic bootstrapping also involves learning the thematic roles of words, such as who does what to whom in a sentence. For example, a child might assume that the subject of a sentence is the agent, and the object is the patient. Semantic bootstrapping requires two critical assumptions: that children have innate semantic knowledge, and that there is a universal mapping between semantic and syntactic categories. 

Syntactic bootstrapping is the idea that children can learn the meaning of words, especially verbs, by using the syntactic context in which they occur. For example, a child might infer that the verb "giggle" means something different from the verb "hit" based on the number and type of arguments they take. Syntactic bootstrapping also involves learning the syntactic rules and constraints of a language, such as word order, agreement, and case marking. For example, a child might infer that the word "she" is a subject pronoun, and that it agrees with the verb in number and person. Syntactic bootstrapping requires two critical assumptions: that children have innate syntactic knowledge, and that there is a probabilistic correspondence between syntactic and semantic properties of words. 

Prosodic bootstrapping is the idea that children can use the acoustic and rhythmic features of speech, such as stress, intonation, and pauses, to segment the speech stream and identify linguistic units, such as words, phrases, and clauses. For example, a child might use the stress pattern of a word to determine whether it is a noun or a verb, or use the intonation contour of a sentence to determine whether it is a statement or a question. Prosodic bootstrapping also involves learning the prosodic rules and functions of a language, such as how to mark focus, contrast, and emotion. For example, a child might learn that a rising intonation at the end of a sentence indicates a question, or that a falling intonation indicates a statement. Prosodic bootstrapping requires the assumption that children have innate prosodic knowledge, and that there is a universal mapping between prosodic and syntactic features of speech. 

Pragmatic bootstrapping is the idea that children can use the social and situational context of language use, such as the speaker's intentions, goals, and beliefs, to infer the meaning and function of words and sentences. For example, a child might use the speaker's eye gaze, gestures, and facial expressions to identify the referent of a word, or use the speaker's tone of voice, mood, and attitude to infer the illocutionary force of a sentence. Pragmatic bootstrapping also involves learning the pragmatic rules and conventions of a language, such as how to use politeness, relevance, and implicature. For example, a child might learn that saying "please" and "thank you" is a way of being polite, or that saying "can you pass the salt?" is a way of making a request. Pragmatic bootstrapping requires the assumption that children have innate pragmatic knowledge, and that there is a probabilistic correspondence between pragmatic and semantic properties of language.
