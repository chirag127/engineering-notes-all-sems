Selectional restrictions are the semantic constraints that a word imposes on the environment in which it occurs. For example, a verb like eat requires that its subject refers to an animate entity and its object to something concrete. Selectional restrictions are part of the meanings of specific words, and they are linguistic rather than factual in nature. They are also a type of presupposition, which means they hold even in questions, negative statements, and other non-assertive contexts.

The following diagram illustrates the basic architecture of a selectional restriction:

```
+-----------------+     +-----------------+
|   Predicate     |     |   Argument      |
| (selector)      |     | (selected)      |
+-----------------+     +-----------------+
| Semantic class  | --> | Semantic class  |
| (e.g. animate)  |     | (e.g. concrete) |
+-----------------+     +-----------------+
| Lexical item    | --> | Lexical item    |
| (e.g. eat)      |     | (e.g. sandwich) |
+-----------------+     +-----------------+
```

The arrow indicates the direction of selection, from the predicate to the argument. The semantic class of the predicate restricts the possible semantic classes of the argument. The lexical item of the predicate also restricts the possible lexical items of the argument. A violation of selectional restrictions results in dissonance or unacceptability, unless it is intended as a joke, a metaphor, or a coercion.
