## Unit 5 - BASIC CONCEPTS of Speech Processing

Speech processing is the study of how humans produce, perceive, and understand speech, as well as how speech can be processed by machines. Speech processing has many applications, such as speech recognition, speech synthesis, speech enhancement, speech coding, speech analysis, and speech translation.

Some of the basic concepts of speech processing are:

- **Speech production**: This is the process by which humans generate speech sounds using the vocal tract and the respiratory system. Speech production involves three major levels of processing: conceptualization, formulation, and articulation. Conceptualization is the stage where the speaker decides what to say and selects the relevant concepts and words. Formulation is the stage where the speaker constructs the syntactic, morphological, and phonological structure of the utterance. Articulation is the stage where the speaker produces the speech sounds by controlling the movements of the vocal organs, such as the vocal cords, the tongue, the lips, and the jaw.   

- **Speech sounds**: These are the acoustic signals that result from speech production. Speech sounds can be classified into different types based on their temporal and spectral characteristics. Temporal characteristics refer to the duration and timing of speech sounds, while spectral characteristics refer to the frequency and amplitude of speech sounds. Some of the common types of speech sounds are:

  - **Vowels**: These are speech sounds that are produced with a relatively open vocal tract and a periodic vibration of the vocal cords. Vowels have a long duration and a clear formant structure, which means that they have distinct peaks in their frequency spectrum. Vowels are usually voiced, which means that they have a fundamental frequency (F0) that corresponds to the pitch of the speaker. Vowels are also the most prominent and sonorous speech sounds, which means that they have a high amplitude and energy. Examples of vowels are /a/, /i/, /u/, /e/, and /o/. 

  - **Consonants**: These are speech sounds that are produced with a relatively closed or constricted vocal tract and a variable vibration of the vocal cords. Consonants have a short duration and a complex spectral structure, which means that they have a noisy or irregular frequency spectrum. Consonants can be voiced or voiceless, depending on whether they have a F0 or not. Consonants can also be classified based on their place of articulation (where the vocal tract is constricted), manner of articulation (how the vocal tract is constricted), and voicing (whether the vocal cords vibrate or not). Examples of consonants are /p/, /t/, /k/, /b/, /d/, /g/, /f/, /s/, /m/, /n/, and /l/. 

  - **Silence**: This is the absence of speech sounds, which can occur between words, sentences, or utterances. Silence can also be intentional or unintentional, depending on whether the speaker pauses for a purpose or not. Silence can be used to indicate hesitation, uncertainty, emphasis, or turn-taking in a conversation. Silence can also be filled with non-speech sounds, such as breathing, coughing, or laughing. 

- **Speech analysis**: This is the process of extracting useful information from speech sounds, such as the type, pitch, energy, and duration of speech segments. Speech analysis can be done using various techniques, such as:

  - **Short-time Fourier transform (STFT)**: This is a technique that converts a speech signal from the time domain to the frequency domain, by dividing the signal into short segments (called frames) and applying the Fourier transform to each frame. The result is a spectrogram, which is a graphical representation of the frequency spectrum of the speech signal over time. The spectrogram can show the formants, harmonics, and noise components of speech sounds, as well as the transitions and boundaries between speech segments.
