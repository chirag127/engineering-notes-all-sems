### Likelihood Distortions for the notes of the Unit 6 - SPEECH-ANALYSIS in the subject of Natural Language Processing

- Likelihood distortions are measures of the similarity or dissimilarity between two short-time spectra, which are commonly used in speech recognition systems to compare the input speech with the stored templates or models.
- Likelihood distortions can be derived from the likelihood function of a statistical model, such as the Gaussian or the autoregressive model, which assumes a certain probability distribution for the spectral coefficients.
- Likelihood distortions can be classified into two types: log likelihood ratio (LLR) and likelihood ratio (LR). The former is based on the logarithm of the likelihood function, while the latter is based on the likelihood function itself.
- LLR distortions are more suitable for speech recognition systems that use dynamic time warping (DTW) or hidden Markov models (HMMs), because they are additive and symmetric, which means that they satisfy the triangle inequality and the commutative property. Examples of LLR distortions are the Itakura-Saito (IS) distortion, the cepstral (CEP) distortion, and the weighted slope metric (WSM) distortion.
- LR distortions are more suitable for speech recognition systems that use vector quantization (VQ) or neural networks, because they are multiplicative and asymmetric, which means that they do not satisfy the triangle inequality and the commutative property. Examples of LR distortions are the likelihood ratio (LR) distortion and the weighted likelihood ratio (WLR) distortion.
- Likelihood distortions can be modified by incorporating perceptual or suprasegmental information, such as the Bark-scale frequency warping, the gain, the absolute loudness, or the energy. These modifications can improve the performance of speech recognition systems by reducing the effects of noise, channel distortion, or speaker variability.
- Likelihood distortions can be evaluated by their effects on the performance of speech recognition systems, such as the recognition accuracy, the computational complexity, or the robustness to noise. According to a comparative study by Nocerino et al. , the log likelihood ratio and weighted slope metric distortion measures gave the highest recognition accuracy, while the Itakura-Saito distortion measure gave the lowest score.

: Nocerino, N., Soong, F. K., Rabiner, L. R., & Klatt, D. H. (1985). Comparative study of several distortion measures for speech recognition. Speech Communication, 4(4), 317-331.
