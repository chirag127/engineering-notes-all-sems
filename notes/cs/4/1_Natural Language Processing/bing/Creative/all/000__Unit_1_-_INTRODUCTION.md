## Unit 1 - INTRODUCTION

- This unit introduces the basic concepts and principles of artificial intelligence (AI), which is the study of how to create machines and software that can perform tasks that require human intelligence, such as reasoning, learning, planning, perception, and natural language processing.
- The main objectives of this unit are:
  - To understand the definition and scope of AI and its subfields.
  - To learn about the history and evolution of AI and its major achievements and challenges.
  - To appreciate the ethical, social, and philosophical implications of AI and its applications.
  - To familiarize with the methods and tools of AI, such as search, knowledge representation, inference, machine learning, and logic programming.
- The main topics covered in this unit are:
  - What is AI? 
    - AI is the science and engineering of making intelligent machines and software that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence.
    - AI can be divided into two main branches: weak AI and strong AI. Weak AI aims to create systems that can perform specific tasks, such as playing chess, recognizing faces, or translating languages. Strong AI aims to create systems that can exhibit general intelligence, such as understanding, reasoning, and learning from any domain.
    - AI can also be classified into four categories based on the type and degree of intelligence exhibited by the systems: systems that think like humans, systems that act like humans, systems that think rationally, and systems that act rationally.
  - History of AI
    - The term AI was coined by John McCarthy in 1956 at the Dartmouth Conference, where the foundations of AI were laid by researchers from various disciplines, such as mathematics, logic, psychology, and computer science.
    - The early years of AI (1950s-1960s) were marked by optimism and enthusiasm, as many achievements were made in areas such as game playing, theorem proving, natural language processing, and neural networks. Some of the pioneers of AI in this period were Alan Turing, Claude Shannon, Marvin Minsky, Herbert Simon, and John McCarthy.
    - The first AI winter (1970s) was a period of disillusionment and funding cuts, as many of the initial expectations and promises of AI were not fulfilled, and some of the limitations and difficulties of AI became apparent. Some of the challenges faced by AI in this period were the combinatorial explosion, the frame problem, the common sense problem, and the brittleness problem.
    - The resurgence of AI (1980s) was a period of revival and innovation, as new methods and techniques were developed and applied to various domains, such as expert systems, knowledge engineering, machine learning, computer vision, and robotics. Some of the influential figures of AI in this period were Edward Feigenbaum, John Holland, John Hopfield, Rodney Brooks, and Judea Pearl.
    - The second AI winter (1990s) was a period of consolidation and criticism, as some of the hype and expectations of AI were again tempered by reality and skepticism, and some of the ethical and social issues of AI were raised and debated. Some of the critics of AI in this period were Hubert Dreyfus, John Searle, Roger Penrose, and Bill Joy.
    - The current state of AI (2000s-present) is a period of integration and expansion, as AI has become more interdisciplinary and ubiquitous, and has achieved remarkable results and breakthroughs in areas such as deep learning, natural language processing, computer vision, speech recognition, and self-driving cars. Some of the prominent figures of AI in this period are Geoffrey Hinton, Yann LeCun, Yoshua Bengio, Andrew Ng, and Demis Hassabis.
  - Ethical and social issues of AI
    - AI has many potential benefits and applications for humanity, such as enhancing productivity, improving health care, advancing education, and solving global problems. However, AI also poses many ethical and social challenges and risks, such as displacing human workers, invading privacy, manipulating behavior, creating bias and discrimination, and threatening human dignity and autonomy.
    - Some of the ethical and social questions and dilemmas that AI raises are:
      - How can we ensure that AI systems are aligned with human values and goals, and do not harm or deceive humans?
      - How can we ensure that AI systems are transparent, accountable, and trustworthy, and do not make errors or cause accidents?
      - How can we ensure that AI systems are fair, inclusive, and respectful, and do not discriminate or oppress humans?
      - How can we ensure that AI systems are sustainable, responsible, and beneficial, and do not degrade or endanger the environment or humanity?
