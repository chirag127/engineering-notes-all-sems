### Language Modeling

- Language modeling is the task of estimating the probability of a sequence of words or symbols in a natural language.
- A language model assigns a probability to every possible sentence or text in a language, based on how likely it is to occur in a given corpus or domain.
- Language models are useful for various natural language processing applications, such as speech recognition, machine translation, text summarization, text generation, etc.
- Language models can be classified into two types: **generative** and **discriminative**.
  - Generative models learn the joint probability of the input and output, and can generate new samples from the learned distribution. For example, a generative model can learn the probability of a sentence and its translation, and can generate new translations for unseen sentences.
  - Discriminative models learn the conditional probability of the output given the input, and can predict the most likely output for a given input. For example, a discriminative model can learn the probability of a translation given a sentence, and can predict the best translation for a given sentence.
- Language models can also be classified based on the level of granularity they operate on: **word-level**, **character-level**, or **subword-level**.
  - Word-level models treat each word as an atomic unit, and assign probabilities to sequences of words. Word-level models require a large vocabulary to cover all possible words in a language, and may suffer from data sparsity and out-of-vocabulary issues.
  - Character-level models treat each character as an atomic unit, and assign probabilities to sequences of characters. Character-level models have a smaller vocabulary, and can handle unseen words and morphological variations. However, character-level models may have difficulty capturing long-range dependencies and semantic information.
  - Subword-level models treat each subword or token as an atomic unit, and assign probabilities to sequences of subwords or tokens. Subword-level models can balance between the advantages and disadvantages of word-level and character-level models, and can adapt to different languages and domains. Subword-level models can use various methods to segment words into subwords or tokens, such as byte-pair encoding, unigram language model, or wordpiece.
- Language models can also be classified based on the architecture they use: **n-gram models**, **neural network models**, or **transformer models**.
  - N-gram models are the simplest and most widely used type of language models. N-gram models estimate the probability of a word or symbol based on the previous n-1 words or symbols, using the chain rule of probability and the Markov assumption. N-gram models can use various smoothing techniques to deal with data sparsity and zero probabilities, such as Laplace smoothing, Good-Turing smoothing, Kneser-Ney smoothing, etc.
  - Neural network models are a more advanced type of language models that use neural networks to learn complex and nonlinear relationships between words or symbols. Neural network models can use various architectures, such as feedforward neural networks, recurrent neural networks, long short-term memory networks, gated recurrent unit networks, convolutional neural networks, etc. Neural network models can capture long-range dependencies and semantic information, and can generate more fluent and natural texts.
  - Transformer models are the state-of-the-art type of language models that use the transformer architecture, which is based on the concept of attention. Transformer models can encode and decode sequences of words or symbols using multiple layers of self-attention and cross-attention, without relying on recurrence or convolution. Transformer models can learn from large-scale corpora and generate high-quality texts, and can also perform various natural language understanding and generation tasks, such as question answering, text summarization, text generation, etc. Transformer models include various variants, such as BERT, GPT, T5, BART, etc.
