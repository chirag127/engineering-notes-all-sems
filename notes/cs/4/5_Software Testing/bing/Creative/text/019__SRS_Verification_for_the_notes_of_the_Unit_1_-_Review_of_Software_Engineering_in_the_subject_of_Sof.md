### SRS Verification for the notes of the Unit 1 - Review of Software Engineering in the subject of Software Testing

SRS verification is the process of checking the quality and completeness of the software requirements specification (SRS) document. The SRS document is a comprehensive description of the software system, its features, and its expected behavior. The SRS document is the basis for the software design, development, testing, and maintenance. Therefore, it is important to ensure that the SRS document is correct, consistent, unambiguous, complete, and verifiable.

SRS verification can be done by using various methods, such as peer reviews, walkthroughs, inspections, checklists, etc. These methods involve reviewing the SRS document by the testing person or a group of persons, and identifying any issues or defects in the document. The issues or defects can be related to the content, structure, format, style, or language of the document. The issues or defects can also be related to the requirements themselves, such as missing, unclear, wrong, or conflicting requirements.

One of the common tools used for SRS verification is a checklist. A checklist is a list of critical information or criteria that the SRS document should contain or meet. A checklist can help to make the review more structured and effective, and to ensure that no important aspect of the SRS document is overlooked. A checklist can also help to measure the quality of the SRS document and to provide feedback for improvement.

An SRS document checklist should address the following issues:

- Correctness: The SRS document should correctly represent the expectations and needs of the stakeholders (customers, users, developers, testers, etc.) for the software system. The requirements should be accurate, precise, and valid. The requirements should also be feasible and realistic, and should not violate any constraints or assumptions. The SRS document should also identify and specify all the applicable safety and security requirements for the software system. For example, if the software system is expected to handle sensitive or confidential data, the SRS document should state how the data will be protected and encrypted.

- Consistency: The SRS document should be consistent within itself and with other related documents. The requirements should not contradict or conflict with each other, or with the goals and objectives of the software system. The terminology, notation, and format used in the SRS document should be consistent and uniform throughout the document. The SRS document should also use consistent and clear definitions for the terms, acronyms, and abbreviations used in the document. For example, if the term "user" is used to refer to the end-user of the software system, the same term should not be used to refer to the developer or the tester of the software system.

- Completeness: The SRS document should be complete and cover all the aspects and details of the software system. The requirements should be comprehensive and sufficient for the design, development, testing, and maintenance of the software system. The requirements should also be traceable and testable, and should have clear and measurable criteria for verification and validation. The SRS document should also include all the functional and non-functional requirements for the software system, such as performance, reliability, usability, maintainability, etc. The SRS document should also address all the possible scenarios and situations that the software system may encounter, such as normal, abnormal, exceptional, or error conditions.

- Unambiguity: The SRS document should be unambiguous and clear. The requirements should have only one interpretation and meaning, and should not be vague, confusing, or misleading. The requirements should also be specific and detailed, and should not leave any room for doubt or misunderstanding. The SRS document should also use simple and precise language, and should avoid using jargon, slang, or colloquial expressions. The SRS document should also use diagrams, tables, charts, or other graphical representations to illustrate or clarify the requirements, if necessary.

- Verifiability: The SRS document should be verifiable and testable. The requirements should be stated in such a way that they can be checked and confirmed by using various methods, such as inspection, analysis, demonstration, or testing. The requirements should also have clear and objective criteria for verification and validation, and should not be subjective or opinion-based. The SRS document should also specify the methods, tools, techniques, and standards that will be used for verifying and validating the requirements. For example, if the software system is expected to respond to a user input within a certain time limit, the SRS document should state the exact time limit and the method for measuring the response time.
