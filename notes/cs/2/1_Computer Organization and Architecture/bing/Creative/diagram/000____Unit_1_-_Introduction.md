## Unit 1 - Introduction

- In this unit, you will learn about the basic concepts and principles of artificial intelligence (AI), its history, applications, and challenges.
- AI is the study of how to create machines or systems that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as reasoning, learning, planning, decision making, perception, and natural language processing.
- AI can be classified into different types based on the goals, methods, and capabilities of the systems. Some common types are:
  - Weak AI or Narrow AI: Systems that are designed to perform a specific task or domain, such as playing chess, recognizing faces, or translating languages. They do not have general intelligence or understanding of other domains.
  - Strong AI or Artificial General Intelligence (AGI): Systems that can perform any intellectual task that a human can do, such as understanding natural language, solving complex problems, or creating art. They have general intelligence and can adapt to new situations and domains.
  - Artificial Superintelligence (ASI): Systems that can surpass human intelligence and capabilities in all domains, such as inventing new technologies, creating new knowledge, or manipulating reality. They have superintelligence and can potentially outsmart or control humans.
- AI can also be classified into different types based on the degree of human involvement or intervention in the systems. Some common types are:
  - Symbolic AI or Classical AI: Systems that use symbols and rules to represent and manipulate knowledge, such as logic, search, and expert systems. They rely on human-defined symbols and rules and do not learn from data or experience.
  - Subsymbolic AI or Connectionist AI: Systems that use artificial neural networks to learn from data and experience, such as deep learning, reinforcement learning, and evolutionary algorithms. They do not rely on human-defined symbols and rules and can learn from data or experience.
  - Hybrid AI: Systems that combine both symbolic and subsymbolic approaches to leverage the strengths and overcome the limitations of each approach, such as neural-symbolic systems, neuroevolution, and neuro-fuzzy systems. They can use both human-defined symbols and rules and learn from data or experience.
- AI has a long and rich history that spans from ancient myths and legends to modern science and technology. Some of the milestones and achievements in the history of AI are:
  - 1950: Alan Turing proposed the Turing test, a criterion for judging whether a machine can exhibit human-like intelligence, based on its ability to converse with a human through text.
  - 1956: John McCarthy coined the term "artificial intelligence" at the Dartmouth Conference, where he and other researchers discussed the goals and challenges of creating intelligent machines.
  - 1965: Joseph Weizenbaum created ELIZA, one of the first chatbots, that could simulate a psychotherapist by using pattern matching and natural language processing techniques.
  - 1972: Alain Colmerauer and Philippe Roussel developed Prolog, one of the first logic programming languages, that could be used for creating expert systems and natural language processing applications.
  - 1979: Douglas Hofstadter published Gödel, Escher, Bach, a book that explored the connections between mathematics, art, music, and AI, and introduced the concept of strange loops and self-referential systems.
  - 1985: Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published Perceptrons, a book that analyzed the limitations and possibilities of artificial neural networks, and sparked the interest in connectionist AI.
  - 1997: IBM's Deep Blue, a chess-playing computer, defeated the world champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match, demonstrating the power of brute-force search and evaluation techniques.
  - 2005: Stanley, a self-driving car developed by Stanford University, won the DARPA Grand Challenge, a competition for autonomous vehicles, by navigating a 212-km desert course in less than seven hours.
  - 2011: IBM's Watson, a question-answering system, won the Jeopardy! quiz show, beating the human champions Ken Jennings and Brad Rutter, by using natural language processing and machine learning techniques.
  - 2016: Google's AlphaGo, a deep reinforcement learning system, defeated the world champion Lee Sedol in the game of Go, a complex board game that requires intuition and creativity, by using neural networks and Monte Carlo tree search techniques.
  - 2020: OpenAI's GPT-3, a deep learning system, generated coherent and diverse texts on various topics and tasks
