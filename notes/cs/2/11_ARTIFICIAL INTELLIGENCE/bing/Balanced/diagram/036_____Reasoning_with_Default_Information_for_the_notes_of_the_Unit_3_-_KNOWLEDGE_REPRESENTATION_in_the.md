### Reasoning with Default Information

- Default reasoning is a form of nonmonotonic reasoning where plausible conclusions are inferred based on general rules which may have exceptions (defaults) .
- It is nonmonotonic in the sense that additional information may force us to withdraw earlier conclusions, namely whenever the additional information shows that the case at hand is exceptional .
- For example, a default rule may state that birds can fly, but this rule may have exceptions such as penguins or ostriches. If we encounter a new bird, we may assume by default that it can fly, unless we learn otherwise.
- Default reasoning is useful for representing and reasoning with incomplete or uncertain knowledge, which is common in artificial intelligence .
- Default reasoning requires two facilities, one that forces conclusions to be retracted in light of new refuting evidence and another that protects conclusions from retraction in light of new but irrelevant evidence .
- Commonsense reasoning evidently involves two types of default rules: expectation-evoking and explanation-evoking .
- Expectation-evoking rules are those that generate predictions or expectations about the world, such as "birds can fly" or "people are honest". They are often expressed in the form of conditional statements with a normality condition, such as "normally, if X then Y".
- Explanation-evoking rules are those that generate explanations or justifications for observed facts, such as "the grass is wet because it rained" or "the car won't start because the battery is dead". They are often expressed in the form of causal or diagnostic rules, such as "X causes Y" or "if Y then X is a possible cause".
- Different formalisms and systems have been proposed for default reasoning in artificial intelligence, such as default logic, circumscription, autoepistemic logic, nonmonotonic modal logic, and defeasible logic .
- Default logic is one of the most influential and widely studied formalisms for default reasoning . It consists of a set of facts and a set of default rules, each of the form "if A and normally B, then C". A default rule can be applied if its precondition A is true and its normality condition B is consistent with the current facts. The application of a default rule adds its conclusion C to the facts, unless it is contradicted by another default rule or a fact. A default theory may have multiple extensions, which are sets of facts that are closed under the application of default rules. An extension represents a possible way of reasoning with the default theory, depending on which default rules are applied and which are not.
- Circumscription is another formalism for default reasoning, based on the idea of minimizing the extension of certain predicates . It allows us to express the closed world assumption, which states that what is not known to be true is false, and the domain closure assumption, which states that the domain of discourse is finite and known. Circumscription can also be used to express other types of default rules, such as the frame default for causal worlds, which states that the effects of an action are only those that are explicitly specified .
- Autoepistemic logic is a formalism for default reasoning that incorporates a modal operator L, which stands for "the agent believes" . It allows us to express the introspective reasoning of an agent, such as "if I do not believe that X is true, then I believe that X is false". Autoepistemic logic can also be used to express other types of default rules, such as exceptions as defaults, which state that an exception to a general rule is true unless there is evidence to the contrary .
- Nonmonotonic modal logic is a generalization of autoepistemic logic that allows for multiple modal operators, representing different sources or modes of belief . It can be used to express the interaction and conflict between different default rules, such as "I believe that X is true, unless I have reason to doubt it" or "I believe that X is true, unless someone else tells me otherwise".
