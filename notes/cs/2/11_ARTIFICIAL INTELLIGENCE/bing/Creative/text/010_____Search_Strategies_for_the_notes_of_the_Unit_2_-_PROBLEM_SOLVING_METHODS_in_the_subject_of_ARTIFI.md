### Search Strategies

- A search strategy is a method for finding a solution to a problem by exploring the space of possible states and actions.
- A search strategy consists of two components: a search algorithm and a search heuristic.
- A search algorithm is a systematic procedure that determines which state to expand next from a set of candidate states, called the frontier or the open list.
- A search heuristic is a function that estimates the cost or the quality of a state or an action, often based on some domain knowledge or approximation.
- There are different types of search strategies, depending on how they use the search heuristic and how they order the frontier.
- Some common types of search strategies are:

  - Uninformed search: These are search strategies that do not use any heuristic information. They only rely on the problem definition and the search algorithm. Examples of uninformed search are breadth-first search, depth-first search, uniform-cost search, and iterative deepening search.
  - Informed search: These are search strategies that use heuristic information to guide the search. They often use a heuristic function to evaluate the states or the actions and select the most promising ones. Examples of informed search are greedy search, A* search, hill-climbing search, and beam search.
  - Local search: These are search strategies that operate on a single current state and try to improve it by applying some local changes. They do not keep track of the path or the frontier. They are often used for optimization problems, where the goal is to find the best state rather than a specific state. Examples of local search are hill-climbing search, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and tabu search.
  - Adversarial search: These are search strategies that deal with problems involving multiple agents with conflicting goals, such as games. They often use a minimax algorithm to find the optimal move for the agent, assuming that the opponent will play optimally as well. They may also use alpha-beta pruning to reduce the search space. Examples of adversarial search are minimax search, alpha-beta search, and expectimax search.
