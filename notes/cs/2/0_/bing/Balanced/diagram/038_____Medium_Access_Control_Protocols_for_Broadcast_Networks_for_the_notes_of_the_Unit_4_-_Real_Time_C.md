### Medium Access Control Protocols for Broadcast Networks

- Medium access control (MAC) protocols are mechanisms that allow several users or transmitters to access a common medium or channel, such as a wireless network or a shared bus.
- MAC protocols play an important role in the development and performance of both wired and wireless networks, as they determine how the channel capacity is shared among the users and how the collisions or interference are avoided or resolved .
- MAC protocols can be classified into two main categories: random access and scheduling .
  - Random access protocols allow users to transmit whenever they have data to send, without any coordination with other users or a central controller. However, this may result in collisions or interference among the users, which degrade the network performance and require retransmission mechanisms. Examples of random access protocols are ALOHA, slotted ALOHA, and carrier-sense multiple access (CSMA) protocols .
  - Scheduling protocols require users to follow some rules or algorithms to determine when they can transmit, based on some criteria such as priority, demand, or channel quality. Scheduling protocols can avoid or reduce collisions or interference, but they may introduce some overhead or delay in the network. Examples of scheduling protocols are time-division multiple access (TDMA), frequency-division multiple access (FDMA), and code-division multiple access (CDMA) protocols .
- MAC protocols for broadcast networks are designed to handle the challenges and requirements of wireless communication, such as limited bandwidth, variable channel conditions, mobility, and energy efficiency .
  - MAC protocols for broadcast networks can be centralized or distributed. Centralized protocols rely on a base station or an access point to coordinate the channel access among the users, while distributed protocols allow users to communicate directly with each other without any central controller .
  - MAC protocols for broadcast networks can be contention-based or reservation-based. Contention-based protocols use random access methods to compete for the channel, while reservation-based protocols use scheduling methods to reserve the channel in advance .
  - MAC protocols for broadcast networks can be adaptive or fixed. Adaptive protocols can adjust their parameters or behavior according to the network conditions or the user demands, while fixed protocols use predefined or static settings .
- MAC protocols for broadcast networks have various applications and objectives, such as improving the network throughput, fairness, reliability, latency, or energy consumption .
  - Some examples of MAC protocols for broadcast networks are:
    - IEEE 802.11: a family of standards for wireless local area networks (WLANs) that use CSMA/CA (collision avoidance) and RTS/CTS (request to send/clear to send) mechanisms to reduce collisions and hidden terminal problems.
    - IEEE 802.15.4: a standard for low-rate wireless personal area networks (LR-WPANs) that use CSMA/CA and slotted or unslotted modes to support low-power and low-data-rate applications.
    - IEEE 802.16: a standard for wireless metropolitan area networks (WMANs) that use TDMA and OFDMA (orthogonal frequency-division multiple access) to provide broadband wireless access to fixed or mobile users.
    - ABROAD: an adaptive MAC protocol for reliable broadcast in wireless networks that uses a feedback-based scheme to adjust the transmission rate and the number of retransmissions according to the channel conditions and the packet loss rate.
