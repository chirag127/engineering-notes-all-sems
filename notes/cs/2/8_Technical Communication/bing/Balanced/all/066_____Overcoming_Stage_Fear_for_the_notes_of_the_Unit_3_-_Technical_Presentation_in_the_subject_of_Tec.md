# Overcoming Stage Fear

- Stage fear, also known as performance anxiety, is a common problem that affects many people who have to speak or perform in front of an audience.
- Stage fear can cause physical symptoms such as trembling, sweating, dry mouth, nausea, rapid heartbeat, and shortness of breath.
- Stage fear can also affect the quality of the presentation, such as causing nervousness, forgetfulness, stuttering, lack of eye contact, and poor body language.
- Stage fear can be overcome by following some strategies before, during, and after the presentation.

## Before the presentation

- Prepare well: Know your topic, audience, and purpose. Research, organize, and rehearse your content. Use visual aids, notes, or cue cards to support your speech. Practice in front of a mirror, a friend, or a video camera. Anticipate possible questions and objections and prepare your answers.
- Relax: Do some breathing, stretching, or meditation exercises to calm your nerves. Avoid caffeine, alcohol, or nicotine, which can increase anxiety. Listen to some soothing music, read a positive affirmation, or visualize a successful outcome.
- Arrive early: Check the venue, equipment, and seating arrangement. Familiarize yourself with the environment and the audience. Chat with some friendly faces or introduce yourself to the host or moderator. This will help you feel more comfortable and confident.

## During the presentation

- Focus on your message, not on yourself: Remember that the audience is interested in what you have to say, not in how you look or sound. Concentrate on delivering your main points, supporting evidence, and examples. Use humor, anecdotes, or stories to engage the audience and make your presentation more memorable.
- Use positive body language: Smile, make eye contact, and use gestures to express your enthusiasm and confidence. Avoid fidgeting, crossing your arms, or looking at the floor. Move around the stage or walk towards the audience to create rapport and energy.
- Control your voice: Speak clearly, loudly, and slowly. Vary your tone, pitch, and pace to avoid monotony and boredom. Pause for emphasis, effect, or transition. Use rhetorical questions, quotations, or statistics to capture the audience's attention. Avoid filler words, such as "um", "uh", or "like".

## After the presentation

- Thank the audience, the host, and the moderator for their time and attention. Invite feedback, questions, or comments from the audience. Answer politely, honestly, and briefly. If you don't know the answer, admit it and offer to follow up later. If you encounter a hostile or challenging question, stay calm and respectful. Don't argue, interrupt, or insult the questioner. Acknowledge their point of view and provide a counterargument or a compromise.
- Evaluate your performance: Review your presentation and identify your strengths and weaknesses. Ask for constructive criticism from a trusted friend, colleague, or mentor. Watch or listen to a recording of your presentation and note your areas of improvement. Celebrate your achievements and learn from your mistakes. Set goals and action plans for your next presentation.
