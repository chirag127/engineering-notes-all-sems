### Correctness for the notes of the Unit 2 - Forms of Technical Communication

Correctness is one of the six qualities of effective communication, along with clarity, conciseness, completeness, coherence, and courtesy. Correctness means that the details of a message are accurate, the grammar and punctuation are correct, and the appearance and tone are appropriate for the audience and purpose.

Correctness is especially important in technical communication, which aims to convey precise and concise information to a specific audience. Technical communication has three specific considerations with regards to grammatical correctness, no matter the document type:

- Passive voice: The passive voice is often frowned upon in business writing because it is a denser, more complex, and less direct verb structure. However, in technical writing, the passive voice can be useful to emphasize the action rather than the actor, or to avoid assigning blame or responsibility. For example, "The experiment was conducted by the researchers" is passive, while "The researchers conducted the experiment" is active. The passive voice can also be used to avoid using personal pronouns, such as "I" or "we", which may not be appropriate for some technical documents.
- Commands and directions: The next quirk of technical writing is that it often contains a lot of commands and directions. These are imperative sentences that tell the reader what to do or how to do something. For example, "Press the red button to start the machine" or "Follow these steps to install the software". Commands and directions should be clear, concise, and consistent. They should also use parallel structure, which means that each item in a list or series has the same grammatical form. For example, "To make a cake, you need to: measure the ingredients, mix them together, pour the batter into a pan, and bake it in the oven" is parallel, while "To make a cake, you need to: measuring the ingredients, mix them together, pour the batter into a pan, and baking it in the oven" is not parallel.
- Bullets and lists: Technical writing often relies heavily on bulleted or numbered lists. These are useful to organize and present information in a clear and concise way. However, bullets and lists should also follow some rules of correctness. First, they should have a clear and consistent format, such as indentation, punctuation, and capitalization. Second, they should have a logical order, such as chronological, alphabetical, or numerical. Third, they should have a clear and consistent relationship with the main text, such as an introductory sentence, a colon, and a parallel structure. For example, "The benefits of using solar energy are: - It is renewable and sustainable. - It reduces greenhouse gas emissions. - It lowers electricity bills." is a correct bulleted list, while "The benefits of using solar energy are: - renewable and sustainable. - It reduces greenhouse gas emissions - lower electricity bills" is not a correct bulleted list.

Correctness is not only about grammar and punctuation, but also about context and content. Contextual correctness means that the information is relevant, accurate, and up-to-date for the intended audience and purpose. Content correctness means that the information is factual, logical, and consistent throughout the document. To achieve contextual and content correctness, technical writers should do thorough research, use reliable sources, cite their references, and check their facts.

Correctness is essential for technical communication, as it affects the credibility, reliability, and usability of the information. Technical writers should strive to achieve correctness at all levels, from the overall document, to paragraphing, to sentence structure, to word choice, and even to punctuation. Correctness can be improved by revising, editing, and proofreading the document, as well as by seeking feedback from peers, experts, or users.
