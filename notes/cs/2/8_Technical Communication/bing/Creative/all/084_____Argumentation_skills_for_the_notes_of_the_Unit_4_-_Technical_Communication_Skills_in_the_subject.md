# Argumentation skills for the notes of the Unit 4 - Technical Communication Skills

Argumentation skills are the abilities to construct and evaluate arguments, to use evidence and reasoning to support claims, and to communicate effectively with others who may have different opinions or perspectives. Argumentation skills are essential for technical communication, as they help to:

- Persuade the audience of the validity, feasibility, or desirability of a proposal, solution, or recommendation.
- Explain the rationale, assumptions, and limitations of a technical process, method, or product.
- Compare and contrast the advantages and disadvantages of different alternatives or options.
- Address and refute potential objections, criticisms, or counterarguments.
- Demonstrate critical thinking, creativity, and problem-solving skills.

Some of the key aspects of argumentation skills are:

- Style: The choice of words, tone, and structure that suit the purpose, audience, and context of the argument. A good style should be clear, concise, coherent, and courteous. It should also avoid logical fallacies, emotional appeals, or personal attacks that may weaken the argument or alienate the audience.
- Devices: The techniques or strategies that help to enhance the argument, such as definitions, examples, analogies, statistics, quotations, or rhetorical questions. Devices should be relevant, accurate, and credible. They should also be used sparingly and appropriately, as too many or too few devices may reduce the impact or clarity of the argument.
- Analysis: The process of breaking down the argument into its main components, such as the claim, the evidence, the warrant, the backing, the qualifier, and the rebuttal. Analysis helps to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the argument, as well as the gaps, inconsistencies, or flaws that may need to be addressed or revised.
- Cohesion and Emphasis: The use of transitions, connectors, signposts, or headings that help to link the parts of the argument and guide the reader through the logic and flow of the argument. Cohesion and emphasis also help to highlight the main points, key terms, or important information that the reader should pay attention to or remember.
- Critical Thinking: The ability to think independently, objectively, and creatively about the argument and the topic. Critical thinking involves asking questions, challenging assumptions, examining evidence, evaluating sources, considering alternatives, and drawing conclusions based on logic and reason. Critical thinking also helps to avoid biases, prejudices, or stereotypes that may interfere with the quality or credibility of the argument.
- Nuances: The recognition and appreciation of the subtleties, complexities, or uncertainties that may exist in the argument or the topic. Nuances help to acknowledge the limitations, exceptions, or qualifications that may apply to the argument, as well as the perspectives, values, or interests that may influence the argument or the audience. Nuances also help to avoid oversimplification, generalization, or absolutism that may distort or misrepresent the argument or the topic.
