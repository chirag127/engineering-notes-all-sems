### Impersonal Technical Communication

- Impersonal technical communication is a style of writing or speaking that avoids using personal pronouns (I, you, he, she, we, etc.) and focuses on conveying data and important information.
- Impersonal technical communication is often used in engineering, science, and business contexts, where the purpose is to inform, persuade, or instruct the audience, rather than to express personal opinions or emotions.
- Impersonal technical communication can have several advantages, such as:
  - It can create a sense of objectivity and credibility, as the writer or speaker appears to rely on facts and evidence, rather than personal bias or preference.
  - It can avoid confusion or ambiguity, as the writer or speaker clearly identifies the subject and the action, rather than using vague or passive constructions.
  - It can maintain a professional and respectful tone, as the writer or speaker avoids addressing the audience directly or making assumptions about their knowledge or background.
- Impersonal technical communication can also have some disadvantages, such as:
  - It can sound dry or boring, as the writer or speaker lacks personality or voice, and does not engage the audience's interest or curiosity.
  - It can create a distance or barrier, as the writer or speaker does not establish a rapport or relationship with the audience, and does not show empathy or understanding of their needs or concerns.
  - It can limit the scope or depth, as the writer or speaker does not provide any personal insights or perspectives, and does not invite feedback or dialogue from the audience.
- Impersonal technical communication can be achieved by using various strategies, such as:
  - Using the passive voice, where the subject is acted upon by the verb, rather than performing the action. For example, "The wire was cut" instead of "I cut the wire".
  - Using the third person, where the writer or speaker refers to people or things by their names or nouns, rather than by pronouns. For example, "The engineer tested the prototype" instead of "He tested the prototype".
  - Using the impersonal pronoun "one", where the writer or speaker refers to a general or hypothetical person, rather than a specific or known person. For example, "One should always follow the safety guidelines" instead of "You should always follow the safety guidelines".
  - Using the indefinite article "a" or "an", where the writer or speaker refers to a singular or unspecified person or thing, rather than a definite or particular one. For example, "A report was submitted" instead of "The report was submitted".
  - Using the plural form, where the writer or speaker refers to a group or category of people or things, rather than a single or individual one. For example, "Researchers have found that..." instead of "A researcher has found that...".
  - Using technical terms, jargon, or acronyms, where the writer or speaker uses specialized or abbreviated language that is common or familiar to the field or discipline, rather than using plain or simple language that is accessible or understandable to a wider audience. For example, "The CPU performed the calculations" instead of "The central processing unit performed the calculations".

  - The audience: Who are they? What are their expectations, needs, and preferences? How much do they know about the topic? How do they want to be addressed or involved?
  - The purpose: What is the goal or objective of the communication? What is the message or information to be conveyed? What is the tone or attitude to be expressed?
  - The context: What is the situation or environment of the communication? What is the medium or channel of communication? What are the constraints or limitations of the communication?
