## Unit 2 - Concurrent Processes

Unit 2 of the Operating System course covers the topic of concurrent processes. Concurrent processes are multiple processes that are executing simultaneously on a computer. This can lead to various challenges, such as race conditions, deadlocks, and starvation.

The operating system must manage concurrent processes in a way that ensures that they are executed efficiently and correctly. To do this, the operating system uses various synchronization mechanisms, such as semaphores, monitors, and message passing.

Semaphores are a type of synchronization mechanism that are used to control access to shared resources. They allow processes to signal each other and to wait for each other, ensuring that shared resources are used in a controlled and synchronized manner.

Monitors are a type of synchronization mechanism that are used to control access to shared resources. They provide a mechanism for processes to wait for each other and to signal each other, ensuring that shared resources are used in a controlled and synchronized manner.

Message passing is a type of synchronization mechanism that allows processes to communicate with each other by passing messages. This allows processes to coordinate their actions and to share information, ensuring that they are executed efficiently and correctly.

In the context of the Operating System course, students will learn about concurrent processes and the synchronization mechanisms used to manage them through interactive activities and hands-on experiences. This may include working with simulations of concurrent processes, writing code to implement synchronization mechanisms, and analyzing the behavior of concurrent processes in response to different inputs.

In summary, Unit 2 of the Operating System course covers the topic of concurrent processes and the synchronization mechanisms used to manage them, including semaphores, monitors, and message passing. Students will learn about these topics through interactive activities and hands-on experiences.
