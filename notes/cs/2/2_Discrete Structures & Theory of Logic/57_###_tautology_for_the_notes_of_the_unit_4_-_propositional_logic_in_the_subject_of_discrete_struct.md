### Tautology for the notes of the Unit 4 - Propositional Logic in the subject of Discrete Structures & Theory of Logic

In propositional logic, a tautology is a formula that is always true, regardless of the truth values assigned to its propositions. A tautology is considered to be a self-evident truth that can be deduced from the axioms and rules of propositional logic.

A tautology can be expressed using logical connectives such as AND, OR, NOT, IMPLIES, and IFF (if and only if). Some common examples of tautologies include:

1. A AND NOT A = 0
2. A OR NOT A = 1
3. (A IMPLIES B) AND (B IMPLIES A) = A IFF B

Tautologies are important in the study of propositional logic because they provide a way to test the validity of a formula. If a formula is a tautology, it is considered to be logically valid and can be used to deduce other truths.

In summary, a tautology is a formula in propositional logic that is always true, regardless of the truth values assigned to its propositions. Tautologies are considered to be self-evident truths and are useful in testing the validity of a formula.
