### Contradiction for the notes of the Unit 4 - Propositional Logic in the subject of Discrete Structures & Theory of Logic

In propositional logic, a contradiction is a statement that contradicts itself. A contradiction is a logical statement that can be both true and false at the same time, which is impossible. A contradiction is often represented by the symbol "⊥".

A contradiction can be derived from a set of premises if they imply the negation of one of the premises. This is known as the law of non-contradiction, which states that a proposition cannot be both true and false at the same time.

Contradictions are used in mathematical proof to show that a statement is false. For example, if a statement leads to a contradiction, then it must be false. This is known as proof by contradiction.

In addition to proof by contradiction, contradictions are also used in the study of formal systems, such as first-order logic and modal logic, to study the consistency and completeness of the system. A formal system is considered consistent if it does not contain any contradictions, and it is considered complete if it can prove all logically valid statements.

In this unit, we will study the concept of contradiction and its use in propositional logic and formal systems. We will also study the law of non-contradiction and its implications for the study of logic and mathematics.
