### First order predicate for the notes of the Unit 5 - Predicate Logic in the subject of Discrete Structures & Theory of Logic

First-order predicate logic, also known as first-order logic or first-order theory, is a formal logical system that extends propositional logic to include predicates and quantifiers. In first-order predicate logic, predicates are used to represent properties or relations between objects, and quantifiers are used to specify the scope of the predicates.

In first-order predicate logic, a statement is made up of a subject term and a predicate, which is a property or relation that the subject term satisfies. For example, the statement "John is a student" can be represented as "Student(John)". The subject term "John" is the argument of the predicate "Student".

First-order predicate logic also includes two types of quantifiers: the universal quantifier (∀) and the existential quantifier (∃). The universal quantifier is used to express that a property holds for all objects in a domain, while the existential quantifier is used to express that there exists an object in a domain that satisfies a property.

First-order predicate logic is used in many areas of mathematics and computer science, including artificial intelligence, automated theorem proving, and formal verification. It is also used as a foundation for more advanced logical systems, such as higher-order logic and modal logic.
