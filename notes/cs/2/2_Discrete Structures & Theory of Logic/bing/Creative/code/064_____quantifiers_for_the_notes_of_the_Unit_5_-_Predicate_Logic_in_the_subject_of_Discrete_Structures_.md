# Quantifiers for the notes of the Unit 5 - Predicate Logic in the subject of Discrete Structures & Theory of Logic

- Predicate logic is a branch of logic that deals with predicates, which are expressions that can be true or false depending on the values of their variables.
- Quantifiers are symbols that specify how many or how much of something satisfies a given predicate.
- There are two main types of quantifiers in predicate logic: universal and existential.
- The universal quantifier, denoted by ∀, means "for all" or "for every". It states that the predicate is true for every element in the domain of discourse.
- The existential quantifier, denoted by ∃, means "there exists" or "there is some". It states that the predicate is true for at least one element in the domain of discourse.
- Quantifiers can be used to create propositions that involve entire sets of objects, some of them, or none of them.
- Quantifiers have a scope, which is the part of the formula that they apply to. The scope is usually indicated by parentheses.
- Quantifiers can be nested, meaning that one quantifier can be inside the scope of another. The order of the quantifiers matters, as it can change the meaning of the proposition.
- Quantifiers can be combined with logical connectives, such as negation, conjunction, disjunction, implication, and equivalence, to form more complex propositions.
- Quantifiers can also be used with an identity predicate, denoted by =, to express equality or inequality between objects.
