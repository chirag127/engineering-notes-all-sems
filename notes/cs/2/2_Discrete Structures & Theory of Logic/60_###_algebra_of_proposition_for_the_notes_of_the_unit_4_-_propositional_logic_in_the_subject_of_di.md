### Algebra of proposition for the notes of the Unit 4 - Propositional Logic in the subject of Discrete Structures & Theory of Logic

The Algebra of Propositions, also known as Propositional Logic, is a branch of mathematical logic that deals with the manipulation of propositions (statements) using logical connectives (AND, OR, NOT, IMPLIES, etc.) and logical equivalences.

In Propositional Logic, propositions are represented by symbols (e.g. p, q, r, etc.) and the logical connectives are used to form compound propositions. For example, the proposition "p AND q" is a compound proposition formed by the logical connective AND and the propositions p and q.

The following are some of the basic rules and laws of Propositional Logic:
1. Commutativity: p AND q is equivalent to q AND p, and p OR q is equivalent to q OR p.
2. Associativity: (p AND q) AND r is equivalent to p AND (q AND r), and (p OR q) OR r is equivalent to p OR (q OR r).
3. Distributivity: p AND (q OR r) is equivalent to (p AND q) OR (p AND r), and p OR (q AND r) is equivalent to (p OR q) AND (p OR r).
4. Idempotence: p OR p is equivalent to p, and p AND p is equivalent to p.
5. De Morgan's Laws: NOT (p AND q) is equivalent to (NOT p) OR (NOT q), and NOT (p OR q) is equivalent to (NOT p) AND (NOT q).
6. Implication: p IMPLIES q is equivalent to (NOT p) OR q.
7. Equivalence: p is equivalent to q if and only if (p IMPLIES q) AND (q IMPLIES p).

In Propositional Logic, the goal is to determine the truth value (True or False) of a proposition given the truth values of its components. This is done using truth tables, which are tables that show the truth values of a proposition for all possible combinations of truth values of its components.

It is important to understand the concepts and laws of Propositional Logic in order to apply them in various fields and solve problems related to discrete structures, mathematical logic, and computer science.
