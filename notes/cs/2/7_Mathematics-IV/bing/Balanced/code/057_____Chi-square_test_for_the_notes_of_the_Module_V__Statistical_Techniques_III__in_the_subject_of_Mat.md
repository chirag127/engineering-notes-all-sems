# Chi-square test

- A chi-square test is a statistical method that compares the observed frequencies of categorical data with the expected frequencies under a null hypothesis.
- The chi-square test can be used to test the independence of two variables, the goodness of fit of a theoretical distribution, or the homogeneity of proportions across groups.
- The chi-square test statistic is calculated as the sum of the squared differences between the observed and expected frequencies, divided by the expected frequencies.
- The chi-square test statistic follows a chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of categories minus the number of parameters estimated under the null hypothesis.
- The chi-square test can be performed using a table of critical values or a p-value calculator. The null hypothesis is rejected if the test statistic is greater than the critical value or if the p-value is less than the significance level.
- The chi-square test has some assumptions and limitations, such as the requirement of large sample sizes, the sensitivity to outliers, and the inability to handle continuous data.

## Examples of chi-square tests

- One example of a chi-square test is to test whether the proportion of people who are left-handed is the same for Americans and Canadians.
- Another example of a chi-square test is to test whether the blood group of children having one parent of blood type A and the other of blood type B follows the expected ratio of 1:2:1 for A, AB, and B.
- A third example of a chi-square test is to test whether the variance of a normally distributed population has a given value based on a sample variance.
