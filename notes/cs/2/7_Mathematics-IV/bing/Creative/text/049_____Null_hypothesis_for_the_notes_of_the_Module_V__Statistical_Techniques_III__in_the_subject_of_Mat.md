### Null Hypothesis

- A null hypothesis is a **theory** based on insufficient evidence that requires further testing to prove whether the observed data is true or false .
- A null hypothesis is usually a **default** or **baseline** assumption that there is **no effect** or **no relationship** between phenomena or populations .
- A null hypothesis is often denoted by **H0** or **H0** and is contrasted with an alternative hypothesis, which is denoted by **H1** or **H1** .
- An alternative hypothesis is a **competing** or **opposing** claim that there is **some effect** or **some relationship** between phenomena or populations.
- For example, a null hypothesis statement can be “the rate of plant growth is not affected by sunlight”, while an alternative hypothesis statement can be “the rate of plant growth is affected by sunlight”.
- A null hypothesis can be tested using **statistical methods** such as hypothesis testing, significance testing, or confidence intervals .
- A null hypothesis can be either **rejected** or **failed to be rejected** based on the **evidence** from the data .
- A null hypothesis can be rejected if the evidence is **strong enough** to show that the null hypothesis is **unlikely** to be true .
- A null hypothesis can be failed to be rejected if the evidence is **not strong enough** to show that the null hypothesis is **likely** to be false .
- A null hypothesis can never be **proved** or **accepted** as true, only **disproved** or **rejected** as false .
- A null hypothesis is important for **scientific inquiry** and **critical thinking** as it allows researchers to test their assumptions and challenge their claims with empirical data .
