### Control Charts

- Control charts are a graphical tool for statistical process control (SPC), which is the use of various methods and tools to monitor and improve the quality and performance of a process over time .
- Control charts help to determine if a process is in a state of control, which means that the variation in the process is due to common causes that are inherent and random, and not due to special causes that are assignable and preventable .
- Control charts consist of a central line that represents the average or mean of the data, and upper and lower control limits that are calculated from the data and represent the expected range of variation in the process  .
- Control charts can be used to plot different types of data, such as continuous (e.g., height, weight, temperature) or discrete (e.g., defects, errors, complaints) data, and different types of statistics, such as mean, range, standard deviation, proportion, or count  .
- Control charts can be classified into two main categories: variable control charts and attribute control charts. Variable control charts are used for continuous data that can be measured on a scale, such as length, weight, or time. Attribute control charts are used for discrete data that can be counted or classified into categories, such as pass/fail, yes/no, or defective/non-defective  .
- Some examples of variable control charts are X-bar and R charts, X-bar and S charts, and individual and moving range (I-MR) charts. Some examples of attribute control charts are p charts, np charts, c charts, and u charts  .
- Control charts can help to identify and analyze the sources of variation in a process, and to detect any changes or trends that may indicate a shift in the process mean or an increase in the process variation  .
- Control charts can also help to compare the performance of different processes, to evaluate the effects of process improvements, and to maintain the quality of the process output over time  .
- Control charts are based on the assumption that the process data follow a normal distribution, or a bell-shaped curve, where most of the data points are close to the mean and few are far away from it  .
- Control charts use different rules or tests to determine if a process is out of control, which means that there is evidence of special causes of variation in the process. Some common rules are: one point outside the control limits, two out of three points near the control limits, four out of five points beyond the one-sigma limits, eight consecutive points on one side of the central line, six consecutive points increasing or decreasing, or 14 consecutive points alternating up and down  .
- Control charts should be updated regularly with new data and reviewed periodically to ensure that they reflect the current state of the process and that the control limits are valid and accurate  .
