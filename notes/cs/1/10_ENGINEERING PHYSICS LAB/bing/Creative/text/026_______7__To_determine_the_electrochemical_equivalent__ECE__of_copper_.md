##### 7. To determine the electrochemical equivalent (ECE) of copper.

- The electrochemical equivalent (ECE) of a substance is the mass of the substance deposited or liberated at an electrode during electrolysis by one coulomb of charge.
- The ECE of copper can be determined by using a copper voltameter, which consists of two copper electrodes immersed in a solution of copper sulfate.
- The procedure is as follows:
  - Clean and polish the copper electrodes and weigh them accurately.
  - Connect the electrodes to a battery or a power supply and a rheostat. Place an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel with the electrodes.
  - Adjust the rheostat to obtain a constant current and note the readings of the ammeter and the voltmeter.
  - Allow the electrolysis to proceed for a fixed time, say 10 minutes, and then switch off the current.
  - Remove the electrodes, wash them with distilled water, dry them and weigh them again.
  - Calculate the mass of copper deposited or liberated at each electrode and the average mass.
  - Calculate the charge passed through the circuit by multiplying the current and the time.
  - Calculate the ECE of copper by dividing the average mass by the charge.
- The ECE of copper can also be calculated theoretically by using Faraday's laws of electrolysis, which state that:
  - The mass of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the charge passed through the electrolyte.
  - The mass of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to its equivalent weight, which is the ratio of its molar mass to its valency.
- The theoretical formula for the ECE of copper is:

  ECE = (Molar mass of copper / Valency of copper) / Faraday constant

  where Faraday constant is the charge carried by one mole of electrons, which is approximately 96500 C.

- The ECE of copper can be compared with the experimental value and the percentage error can be calculated. The sources of error may include:
  - Impurities in the electrodes or the electrolyte
  - Inaccurate measurements of mass, current, voltage or time
  - Side reactions or gas evolution at the electrodes
  - Temperature variations or heat loss in the circuit
