### V-I characteristics of semiconductor diode

- The V-I characteristics of a semiconductor diode is a curve that shows the relationship between the voltage across the diode and the current through it.
- The V-I characteristics can be obtained by connecting a diode in series with a resistor and a variable voltage source, as shown in the figure below.

![Circuit diagram for V-I characteristics of diode](https://electronicspost.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/VI-characteristics-of-pn-junction-diode.png)

- The V-I characteristics can be divided into two regions: forward bias and reverse bias.
- Forward bias is the condition when the positive terminal of the voltage source is connected to the p-type side of the diode and the negative terminal to the n-type side. In this case, the diode offers a low resistance and allows a large current to flow through it.
- Reverse bias is the condition when the positive terminal of the voltage source is connected to the n-type side of the diode and the negative terminal to the p-type side. In this case, the diode offers a high resistance and allows a very small current to flow through it.
- The V-I characteristics curve for forward and reverse bias is shown in the figure below.

![V-I characteristics curve for diode](https://electronicspost.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/VI-characteristics-of-pn-junction-diode-1.png)

- The forward bias region of the curve has three parts: the cut-in voltage, the linear region, and the saturation region.
- The cut-in voltage is the minimum voltage required to make the diode conduct in the forward direction. It is also called the threshold voltage or the knee voltage. It depends on the type and doping level of the semiconductor material. For silicon diodes, it is about 0.7 V and for germanium diodes, it is about 0.3 V.
- The linear region is the part of the curve where the current increases linearly with the voltage. The slope of the curve in this region is the forward resistance of the diode, which is usually very small.
- The saturation region is the part of the curve where the current reaches a maximum value and does not increase further with the voltage. This is because the diode cannot handle more current without overheating or breaking down. The voltage at this point is called the forward voltage or the forward drop of the diode.
- The reverse bias region of the curve has two parts: the leakage current and the breakdown region.
- The leakage current is the very small current that flows through the diode in the reverse direction. It is due to the thermal generation of electron-hole pairs in the depletion region of the diode. It is usually negligible and can be ignored in most applications.
- The breakdown region is the part of the curve where the current increases rapidly with the voltage in the reverse direction. This is because the diode undergoes a phenomenon called breakdown, which is the breakdown of the depletion region due to a high electric field. There are two types of breakdown: avalanche breakdown and Zener breakdown. Avalanche breakdown occurs when the electrons gain enough kinetic energy to knock out more electrons from the atoms, creating a chain reaction of ionization. Zener breakdown occurs when the electric field is so strong that it pulls out electrons from the valence band, creating a large number of electron-hole pairs. Both types of breakdown result in a large current flow through the diode, which can damage it if not controlled. The voltage at which breakdown occurs is called the breakdown voltage or the Zener voltage of the diode. It depends on the doping level and the thickness of the depletion region of the diode. Some diodes are specially designed to operate in the breakdown region and are called Zener diodes. They are used for voltage regulation and reference purposes.
