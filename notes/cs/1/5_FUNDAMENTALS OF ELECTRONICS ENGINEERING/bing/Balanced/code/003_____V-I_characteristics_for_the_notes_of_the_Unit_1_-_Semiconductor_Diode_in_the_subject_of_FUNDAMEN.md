### V-I characteristics of semiconductor diode

- The V-I characteristics of a semiconductor diode is a curve that shows the relationship between the voltage across the diode and the current through it.
- The V-I characteristics of a diode can be obtained by connecting a variable voltage source, a resistor and an ammeter in series with the diode, and a voltmeter in parallel with the diode, as shown in the figure below.

![Circuit diagram for V-I characteristics of diode](https://electronicspost.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/V-I-characteristics-of-pn-junction-diode.png)

- The diode can be operated in two modes: forward bias and reverse bias.
- In forward bias mode, the positive terminal of the voltage source is connected to the p-type region and the negative terminal to the n-type region of the diode. This reduces the potential barrier at the p-n junction and allows the current to flow from the p-type to the n-type region.
- In reverse bias mode, the polarity of the voltage source is reversed, i.e., the positive terminal is connected to the n-type region and the negative terminal to the p-type region of the diode. This increases the potential barrier at the p-n junction and prevents the current from flowing across the diode, except for a small leakage current.
- The V-I characteristics curve of a diode in both modes is shown in the figure below.

![V-I characteristics curve of diode](https://electronicspost.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/V-I-characteristics-of-pn-junction-diode-1.png)

- The salient features of the V-I characteristics curve of a diode are:

  - In forward bias mode, the diode does not conduct until the applied voltage reaches a certain threshold value, called the cut-in voltage or the knee voltage. This value depends on the type of material and doping level of the diode. For silicon diodes, the cut-in voltage is about 0.7 V, and for germanium diodes, it is about 0.3 V .
  - After the cut-in voltage is reached, the diode current increases rapidly with a small increase in voltage. The diode behaves like a closed switch and offers a very low resistance to the current flow. The forward resistance of a diode is typically in the range of a few ohms.
  - In reverse bias mode, the diode current is very small, in the order of microamperes or nanoamperes, and is called the reverse saturation current. This current is due to the thermal generation of minority carriers in the diode. The reverse saturation current is independent of the applied voltage, but increases with the temperature.
  - If the reverse voltage is increased beyond a certain limit, called the breakdown voltage, the diode current increases abruptly and the diode may be damaged. This phenomenon is called the breakdown or avalanche effect. The breakdown voltage depends on the type and doping level of the diode. Some diodes, such as Zener diodes, are designed to operate in the breakdown region for voltage regulation purposes .
