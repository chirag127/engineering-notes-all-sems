# Op-Amp Basics

- An op-amp (operational amplifier) is a device that amplifies the difference between two input voltages and produces a single-ended output voltage .
- An op-amp has two input terminals, labeled as V+ (non-inverting input) and V- (inverting input), and one output terminal, labeled as Vout .
- An op-amp also has two power supply terminals, labeled as Vs+ (positive supply) and Vs- (negative supply), which provide the maximum and minimum output voltages respectively .
- An op-amp can be considered as a differential-to-single-ended converter, as it converts a differential input signal (V+ - V-) to a single-ended output signal (Vout) .
- An op-amp exhibits common-mode rejection, which means it ignores any voltage component that is common to both input signals, such as noise or a DC offset .
- An op-amp has a very high open-loop gain, which is the ratio of output voltage to input voltage when no feedback is applied. The open-loop gain is typically in the order of 10^5 to 10^6 .
- An op-amp has a very high input impedance, which means it draws very little current from the input sources. The input impedance is typically in the order of 10^6 to 10^12 ohms .
- An op-amp has a very low output impedance, which means it can drive a large current to the load. The output impedance is typically in the order of 10 to 100 ohms .
- An op-amp has a very wide bandwidth, which means it can amplify signals of a wide range of frequencies. The bandwidth is typically in the order of 10^6 to 10^8 Hz .
- An op-amp can be used for various applications, such as amplification, filtering, integration, differentiation, summing, subtracting, etc. by applying different feedback configurations .
