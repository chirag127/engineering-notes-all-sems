# Types of Batteries

Batteries are devices that store electrical energy in the form of chemical energy and convert it into electrical current when needed. Batteries are widely used in electrical engineering for various applications, such as powering portable devices, electric vehicles, backup systems, etc.

There are many types of batteries, but they can be broadly classified into four categories:

- **Primary batteries**: These are non-rechargeable batteries that can only be used once. They have a fixed amount of chemical energy that is consumed during discharge. Examples of primary batteries are alkaline, zinc-carbon, lithium, etc.
- **Secondary batteries**: These are rechargeable batteries that can be used multiple times. They have a reversible chemical reaction that allows them to regain their chemical energy after being recharged. Examples of secondary batteries are lead-acid, nickel-cadmium, nickel-metal hydride, lithium-ion, etc.
- **Reserve batteries**: These are batteries that are activated only when needed. They have a separate electrolyte that is added to the battery when it is required to produce current. Examples of reserve batteries are thermal batteries, water-activated batteries, etc.
- **Fuel cells**: These are batteries that generate electrical energy from the continuous supply of fuel and oxidant. They do not store chemical energy, but rather convert it directly into electrical energy. Examples of fuel cells are hydrogen-oxygen, methanol, etc.

Each type of battery has its own advantages and disadvantages, depending on the application, cost, performance, safety, environmental impact, etc. Some of the factors that are used to compare different types of batteries are   :

- **Voltage**: The potential difference between the terminals of the battery. It determines the amount of electrical energy that can be delivered by the battery. Different types of batteries have different nominal voltages, ranging from 1.2 V to 3.6 V for common batteries.
- **Capacity**: The amount of electrical charge that can be delivered by the battery. It is measured in ampere-hours (Ah) or milliampere-hours (mAh). It depends on the size, chemistry, and discharge rate of the battery. Higher capacity means longer run time for the battery.
- **Energy density**: The amount of energy that can be stored by the battery per unit volume or mass. It is measured in watt-hours per liter (Wh/L) or watt-hours per kilogram (Wh/kg). Higher energy density means smaller and lighter batteries for the same amount of energy.
- **Power density**: The amount of power that can be delivered by the battery per unit volume or mass. It is measured in watts per liter (W/L) or watts per kilogram (W/kg). Higher power density means faster and stronger batteries for the same size and weight.
- **Cycle life**: The number of times that the battery can be charged and discharged before its capacity drops below a certain level. It depends on the depth of discharge, charging method, temperature, etc. Higher cycle life means longer lifespan for the battery.
- **Self-discharge**: The loss of charge that occurs in the battery when it is not in use. It is caused by internal chemical reactions, leakage currents, etc. It is expressed as a percentage of the initial charge per month or year. Lower self-discharge means longer shelf life for the battery.
- **Cost**: The price of the battery per unit of energy or power. It depends on the materials, manufacturing, availability, etc. Lower cost means more affordable batteries for the consumers.
