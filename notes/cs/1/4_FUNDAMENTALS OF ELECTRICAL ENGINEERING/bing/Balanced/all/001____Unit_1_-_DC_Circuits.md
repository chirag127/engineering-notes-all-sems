## Unit 1 - DC Circuits

- A DC circuit is a circuit that consists of direct current (DC) sources, such as batteries or solar cells, and resistors, capacitors, inductors, switches, and other components that are connected by wires or conductors.
- The main characteristics of a DC circuit are:
  - The current flows in one direction only, from the positive terminal of the source to the negative terminal.
  - The voltage across each component is constant and depends on the source voltage and the resistance or reactance of the component.
  - The power dissipated by each component is equal to the product of the voltage across it and the current through it.
- The main concepts and laws that govern the behavior of DC circuits are:
  - Kirchhoff's current law (KCL): The algebraic sum of the currents entering any node or junction in a circuit is zero.
  - Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): The algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed loop in a circuit is zero.
  - Ohm's law: The current through a resistor is directly proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance.
  - Series and parallel connections: Components are connected in series if they share the same current and in parallel if they share the same voltage. The equivalent resistance of a series connection is the sum of the individual resistances, while the equivalent resistance of a parallel connection is the reciprocal of the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances.
  - Voltage and current division: The voltage across a series connection of resistors is divided among them in proportion to their resistances, while the current through a parallel connection of resistors is divided among them in inverse proportion to their resistances.
  - Superposition principle: The voltage or current in any branch of a linear circuit with multiple sources is equal to the algebraic sum of the voltages or currents produced by each source acting alone, with all other sources replaced by their internal resistances.
  - Thevenin's and Norton's theorems: Any linear circuit with a single pair of terminals can be replaced by an equivalent circuit consisting of a voltage source in series with a resistor (Thevenin's equivalent) or a current source in parallel with a resistor (Norton's equivalent). The equivalent voltage or current is the open-circuit voltage or short-circuit current at the terminals, and the equivalent resistance is the resistance seen from the terminals with all sources replaced by their internal resistances.
  - Maximum power transfer theorem: The maximum power is transferred from a source to a load when the load resistance is equal to the source resistance or the Thevenin or Norton equivalent resistance of the source circuit.
  - Capacitors and inductors: Capacitors and inductors are energy storage devices that oppose changes in voltage and current, respectively. The voltage across a capacitor is proportional to the charge stored on it, while the current through an inductor is proportional to the magnetic flux linked by it. The relationship between voltage and current for a capacitor is given by V = Q/C, where V is the voltage, Q is the charge, and C is the capacitance. The relationship between voltage and current for an inductor is given by V = L di/dt, where V is the voltage, i is the current, and L is the inductance. The energy stored in a capacitor is given by W = Q^2/2C, while the energy stored in an inductor is given by W = Li^2/2. In a DC circuit, capacitors act as open circuits and inductors act as short circuits after a long time.
