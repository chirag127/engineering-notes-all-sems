### Apparent, active and reactive power

- Apparent power is the product of voltage and current in an AC circuit, without reference to phase angle. It is measured in volt-amperes (VA) and symbolized by S. It represents the total power supplied to the circuit.
- Active power is the power that does work in the circuit, such as heating, lighting, or motion. It is the component of apparent power that is in phase with the voltage. It is measured in watts (W) and symbolized by P. It represents the real power consumed by the circuit.
- Reactive power is the power that is stored and released by the inductive and capacitive elements of the circuit, such as coils and capacitors. It is the component of apparent power that is out of phase with the voltage by 90 degrees. It is measured in volt-amperes reactive (VAR) and symbolized by Q. It represents the imaginary power that causes voltage and current to oscillate in the circuit.
- The relationship between apparent, active and reactive power can be represented by a right-angled triangle called the power triangle. The hypotenuse is the apparent power, the base is the active power, and the height is the reactive power. The angle between the apparent and active power is called the power factor angle, and it indicates how much of the apparent power is converted into active power. The power factor is the cosine of this angle, and it ranges from 0 to 1. A higher power factor means a more efficient use of power in the circuit.
- The power triangle can be expressed by the following equations:

  - S^2 = P^2 + Q^2
  - P = S cos θ
  - Q = S sin θ
  - cos θ = P / S
  - sin θ = Q / S

- The power factor can be improved by adding or subtracting reactive power to the circuit, using devices such as capacitors or inductors. This can reduce the apparent power and the losses in the circuit, and increase the voltage and the efficiency.
