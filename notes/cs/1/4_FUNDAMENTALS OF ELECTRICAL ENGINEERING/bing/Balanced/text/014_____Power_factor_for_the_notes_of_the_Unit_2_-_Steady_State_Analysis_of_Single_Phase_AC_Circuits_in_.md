### Power factor

- Power factor is a dimensionless quantity that measures how effectively the electric power is converted into useful work in an AC circuit. 
- Power factor is defined as the ratio of real power (P) to apparent power (S) in an AC circuit. Real power is the power that performs useful work, such as turning a motor or lighting a bulb. Apparent power is the product of the voltage and current in the circuit, regardless of the phase angle between them.  
- Power factor can be expressed as: PF = P / S, where P is the real power in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW), and S is the apparent power in volt-amperes (VA) or kilovolt-amperes (kVA).  
- Power factor can also be calculated as the cosine of the phase angle (θ) between the voltage and current waveforms in an AC circuit. If the current is in phase with the voltage, the power factor is 1, which means that all the apparent power is converted into real power. If the current is out of phase with the voltage, the power factor is less than 1, which means that some of the apparent power is wasted as reactive power. Reactive power is the power that oscillates between the source and the load, and does not perform any useful work.  
- Power factor can be expressed as: PF = cos θ, where θ is the phase angle between the voltage and current waveforms. 
- Power factor can range from 0 to 1, depending on the type and configuration of the load in the circuit. A purely resistive load, such as a heater or a lamp, has a power factor of 1, because the current and voltage are in phase. A purely inductive or capacitive load, such as a motor or a capacitor, has a power factor of 0, because the current and voltage are 90 degrees out of phase. A mixed load, such as a fluorescent light or a computer, has a power factor between 0 and 1, because the current and voltage are not in phase but not 90 degrees apart either.  
- Power factor is important for the efficiency and reliability of the power system. A low power factor means that more current is required to deliver the same amount of real power, which increases the losses and heating in the transmission lines and transformers. A low power factor also reduces the capacity of the power system to supply more loads. A high power factor means that less current is required to deliver the same amount of real power, which reduces the losses and heating in the transmission lines and transformers. A high power factor also increases the capacity of the power system to supply more loads.  
- Power factor can be improved by using power factor correction devices, such as capacitors, inductors, or synchronous motors, that can adjust the phase angle between the voltage and current in the circuit. Power factor correction can reduce the apparent power and increase the real power, which improves the efficiency and reliability of the power system.
