## Unit 2 - Steady State Analysis of Single Phase AC Circuits

- Single phase AC circuits are electrical circuits that are powered by alternating current (AC) sources with a single frequency and phase.
- AC sources produce sinusoidal voltages and currents that vary periodically in time and amplitude.
- The frequency of an AC source is the number of cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz).
- The phase of an AC source is the angle that the sinusoidal waveform makes with the horizontal axis at a given instant, measured in degrees or radians.
- The peak value of an AC voltage or current is the maximum amplitude that it reaches in one cycle.
- The root mean square (RMS) value of an AC voltage or current is the effective value that produces the same power as a constant DC voltage or current of the same magnitude.
- The RMS value of a sinusoidal voltage or current is equal to its peak value divided by the square root of 2.
- The average value of an AC voltage or current over one cycle is zero, unless it has a DC component.
- The DC component of an AC voltage or current is the constant value that is added to or subtracted from the sinusoidal waveform.
- The phasor representation of an AC voltage or current is a vector that has a magnitude equal to its RMS value and an angle equal to its phase.
- Phasors are useful for simplifying the analysis of AC circuits, as they allow the use of complex numbers and algebra instead of trigonometry and calculus.
- The impedance of an AC circuit is the ratio of the phasor voltage to the phasor current, measured in ohms (Ω).
- The impedance of an AC circuit depends on the frequency of the AC source and the characteristics of the circuit elements, such as resistance, inductance and capacitance.
- The resistance of an AC circuit is the real part of the impedance, which represents the opposition to the flow of current due to the dissipation of power as heat.
- The reactance of an AC circuit is the imaginary part of the impedance, which represents the opposition to the flow of current due to the storage and release of energy in magnetic or electric fields.
- The inductive reactance of an AC circuit is proportional to the frequency of the AC source and the inductance of the circuit element, which is the property of creating a magnetic field when current flows through it.
- The capacitive reactance of an AC circuit is inversely proportional to the frequency of the AC source and the capacitance of the circuit element, which is the property of storing electric charge when voltage is applied across it.
- The power factor of an AC circuit is the cosine of the angle between the phasor voltage and the phasor current, which indicates how efficiently the power is transferred from the source to the load.
- The power factor of an AC circuit can be improved by adding or subtracting reactive elements, such as capacitors or inductors, to balance the inductive and capacitive reactances.
- The apparent power of an AC circuit is the product of the RMS voltage and the RMS current, measured in volt-amperes (VA).
- The apparent power of an AC circuit is the maximum power that the source can deliver to the load, but not all of it is useful.
- The real power of an AC circuit is the product of the RMS voltage, the RMS current and the power factor, measured in watts (W).
- The real power of an AC circuit is the useful power that the load consumes or the source delivers, which is converted into heat, light, motion or other forms of energy.
- The reactive power of an AC circuit is the product of the RMS voltage, the RMS current and the sine of the angle between the phasor voltage and the phasor current, measured in volt-amperes reactive (VAR).
- The reactive power of an AC circuit is the power that is exchanged between the source and the load due to the reactive elements, which is stored and released in magnetic or electric fields.
- The complex power of an AC circuit is the sum of the real power and the reactive power, measured in volt-amperes complex (VAC).
- The complex power of an AC circuit is the vector representation of the power in the phasor domain, which has a magnitude equal to the apparent power and an angle equal to the power factor angle.
