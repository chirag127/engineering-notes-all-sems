### Maxwell equations in vacuum and in conducting medium

Maxwell equations are a set of four partial differential equations that describe the relationship between electric and magnetic fields in space and time. They are:

- Gauss's law: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$, where $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field, $\rho$ is the charge density, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.
- Gauss's law for magnetism: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$, where $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field.
- Faraday's law of induction: $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$, where $t$ is the time.
- Ampere's law with Maxwell's correction: $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J} + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$, where $\mathbf{J}$ is the current density, and $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space.

In a vacuum, there is no charge or current, so $\rho = 0$ and $\mathbf{J} = 0$. Therefore, the Maxwell equations in vacuum are:

- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0$
- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$
- $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$
- $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$

These equations imply that electric and magnetic fields can propagate in vacuum as electromagnetic waves, with a constant speed given by $c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \epsilon_0}}$, which is the speed of light.

In a conducting medium, there is a non-zero charge and current, so $\rho \neq 0$ and $\mathbf{J} \neq 0$. Therefore, the Maxwell equations in a conducting medium are:

- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon}$, where $\epsilon$ is the permittivity of the medium.
- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$
- $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$
- $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu \mathbf{J} + \mu \epsilon \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$, where $\mu$ is the permeability of the medium.

These equations imply that electric and magnetic fields can propagate in a conducting medium as electromagnetic waves, with a speed that depends on the properties of the medium, such as its conductivity, permittivity, and permeability. The speed of electromagnetic waves in a conducting medium is generally lower than the speed of light in vacuum.
