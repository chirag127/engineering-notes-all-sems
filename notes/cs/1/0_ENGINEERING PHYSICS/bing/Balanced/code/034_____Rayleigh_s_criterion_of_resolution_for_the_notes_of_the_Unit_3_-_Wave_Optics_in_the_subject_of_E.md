### Rayleigh’s criterion of resolution

- Rayleigh's criterion of resolution is a measure of the ability of an optical instrument to distinguish two closely spaced point sources of light, such as stars or distant objects.
- According to Rayleigh's criterion, two point sources are just resolvable when the center of the diffraction pattern of one source coincides with the first minimum of the diffraction pattern of the other source. See Figure 1.
- Rayleigh's criterion can be applied to any aperture shape, but it is usually derived for circular apertures, such as lenses or telescopes. For circular apertures, the diffraction pattern is called an Airy disk, and the angular separation between two resolvable sources is given by:

  θ = 1.22 λ / D

  where θ is the angular separation, λ is the wavelength of light, and D is the diameter of the aperture.

- Rayleigh's criterion is based on the assumption that the human eye can detect the dip in intensity between the two overlapping diffraction patterns. However, this may not always be the case, especially for faint sources or low contrast. Therefore, Rayleigh's criterion is not an absolute limit, but rather a practical guideline for resolution.
- Rayleigh's criterion can also be applied to other types of waves, such as sound waves or electromagnetic waves. In general, the resolution of a wave depends on the wavelength and the size of the aperture or antenna that receives the wave.
