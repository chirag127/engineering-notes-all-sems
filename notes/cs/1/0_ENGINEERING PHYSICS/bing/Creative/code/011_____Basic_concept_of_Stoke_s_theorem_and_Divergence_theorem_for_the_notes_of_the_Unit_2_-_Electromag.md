# Stoke's theorem
- Stoke's theorem relates a surface integral of the curl of a vector field to a line integral of the vector field along the boundary of the surface.
- The surface must be orientable, meaning it has two distinct sides, and the boundary must be a simple closed curve.
- The theorem can be expressed as:

$$\iint_S \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$

- Where $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field, $S$ is a surface, $\partial S$ is the boundary of $S$, and $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ is the curl of $\mathbf{F}$.
- The theorem can be interpreted as the circulation of the vector field around the boundary of the surface equals the sum of the rotations of the vector field over the surface.
- The theorem can be used to simplify the calculation of surface integrals in some cases, such as when the curl of the vector field is easier to compute than the vector field itself.
- The theorem can also be applied to electromagnetism, where the vector field can represent the magnetic field and the surface can represent a loop of wire.

# Divergence theorem
- Divergence theorem relates a volume integral of the divergence of a vector field to a surface integral of the vector field over the boundary of the volume.
- The volume must be a closed region and the surface must be a closed surface.
- The theorem can be expressed as:

$$\iiint_V \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \, dV = \iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

- Where $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field, $V$ is a volume, $\partial V$ is the boundary of $V$, and $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ is the divergence of $\mathbf{F}$.
- The theorem can be interpreted as the net flux of the vector field across the boundary of the volume equals the sum of the sources and sinks of the vector field inside the volume.
- The theorem can be used to simplify the calculation of volume integrals in some cases, such as when the divergence of the vector field is easier to compute than the vector field itself.
- The theorem can also be applied to electromagnetism, where the vector field can represent the electric field and the volume can represent a charge distribution.
