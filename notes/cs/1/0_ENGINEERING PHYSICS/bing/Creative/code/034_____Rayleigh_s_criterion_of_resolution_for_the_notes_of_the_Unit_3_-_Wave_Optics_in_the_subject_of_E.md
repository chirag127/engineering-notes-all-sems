### Rayleigh's criterion of resolution

- Rayleigh's criterion of resolution is a measure of the ability of an optical instrument to distinguish two closely spaced point sources of light, such as stars or distant objects.
- According to Rayleigh's criterion, two point sources are just resolvable when the central maximum of the diffraction pattern of one source coincides with the first minimum of the diffraction pattern of the other source. See Figure 1.
- Rayleigh's criterion can be applied to circular apertures, such as lenses or telescopes, as well as to single slits. For circular apertures, the criterion for resolution is when the center of one Airy disk pattern falls on the first minimum of the Airy disk pattern of the second source. See Figure 2.
- The minimum angular separation θ between two point sources that can be resolved by a circular aperture of diameter D is given by the formula:

    θ = 1.22 λ / D

    where λ is the wavelength of light.

- The smaller the value of θ, the better the resolution of the optical instrument. The resolution depends on the wavelength of light and the size of the aperture. The resolution is better for shorter wavelengths and larger apertures.
- Rayleigh's criterion can be used to calculate the distance between two objects that are barely resolvable by an optical instrument, given their angular separation and their distance from the instrument. The distance s between two objects a distance r away and separated by an angle θ is given by the formula:

    s = r θ

    where r and s are in the same units.

- Rayleigh's criterion is based on the assumption that the point sources are coherent and monochromatic, and that the diffraction patterns are observed in the far field. It is also a subjective criterion, as different observers may have different thresholds of perception for resolving two sources.
