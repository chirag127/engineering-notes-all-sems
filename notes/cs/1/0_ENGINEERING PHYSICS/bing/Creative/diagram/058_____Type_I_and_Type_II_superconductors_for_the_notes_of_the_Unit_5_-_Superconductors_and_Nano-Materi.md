### Type I and Type II Superconductors

- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and perfect diamagnetism below a certain critical temperature.
- Superconductors can be classified into two types based on their behavior in an external magnetic field: type I and type II.
- Type I superconductors have only one critical magnetic field, Hc. When the external magnetic field exceeds Hc, the superconductivity is completely destroyed and the material becomes normal.
- Type I superconductors are usually pure metals, such as mercury, lead, tin, aluminum, etc.
- Type I superconductors have a sharp transition from the superconducting state to the normal state, and they expel all the magnetic flux from their interior (Meissner effect).
- Type II superconductors have two critical magnetic fields, Hc1 and Hc2. When the external magnetic field is between Hc1 and Hc2, the superconductivity is partially preserved and the material becomes a mixed state.
- Type II superconductors are usually metal alloys or complex oxide ceramics, such as niobium, vanadium, technetium, yttrium barium copper oxide, etc.
- Type II superconductors have a gradual transition from the superconducting state to the normal state, and they allow some magnetic flux to penetrate their interior in the form of quantized vortices (Abrikosov effect).
- Type II superconductors have higher critical temperatures and critical fields than type I superconductors, and they are more suitable for practical applications.
