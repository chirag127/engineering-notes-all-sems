### Quantum wires and quantum well

- Quantum wires and quantum well are examples of **quantum confinement** in semiconductor nanostructures, where the motion of charge carriers (electrons and holes) is restricted in one or more dimensions.
- Quantum confinement leads to discrete energy levels and changes in the electronic, optical and transport properties of the nanostructures.
- Quantum wells (QWs) are two-dimensional (2D) structures, where the charge carriers are confined in one direction (usually the growth direction) and free to move in the other two directions (parallel to the surface).
- Quantum wires (QWRs) are one-dimensional (1D) structures, where the charge carriers are confined in two directions (perpendicular to the wire axis) and free to move in one direction (along the wire axis).
- Quantum wells and quantum wires can be fabricated by various techniques, such as molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD), lithography, etching, etc.
- Quantum wells and quantum wires have applications in nanoelectronics, optoelectronics, information technology, and biotechnology, due to their enhanced carrier mobility, reduced scattering, increased carrier density, tunable bandgap, and quantum size effects.

#### Quantum well

- A quantum well is a thin layer of a lower bandgap semiconductor material sandwiched between two layers of a higher bandgap material, forming a heterostructure.
- The charge carriers are confined in the lower bandgap layer (the well) by the potential barriers created by the higher bandgap layers (the barriers).
- The energy levels of the charge carriers in the well are quantized, forming subbands, due to the boundary conditions imposed by the barriers.
- The subband spacing depends on the well width, the effective mass of the charge carriers, and the band offset between the well and the barrier materials.
- The bandgap of the quantum well is effectively increased by the quantum confinement, and can be tuned by changing the well width or the barrier material.
- The density of states (DOS) of the quantum well is a staircase function, with steps corresponding to the subbands, unlike the parabolic DOS of the bulk material.
- The optical absorption and emission of the quantum well are discrete and wavelength-selective, due to the transitions between the subbands, unlike the continuous spectrum of the bulk material.
- The quantum well can be used to make various devices, such as lasers, LEDs, photodetectors, solar cells, modulators, etc.

#### Quantum wire

- A quantum wire is a thin rod or a stripe of a lower bandgap semiconductor material embedded in a higher bandgap material, forming a heterostructure.
- The charge carriers are confined in the lower bandgap material (the wire) by the potential barriers created by the higher bandgap material (the matrix).
- The energy levels of the charge carriers in the wire are quantized in two directions, forming subbands, due to the boundary conditions imposed by the matrix.
- The subband spacing depends on the wire cross-section, the effective mass of the charge carriers, and the band offset between the wire and the matrix materials.
- The bandgap of the quantum wire is effectively increased by the quantum confinement, and can be tuned by changing the wire cross-section or the matrix material.
- The density of states (DOS) of the quantum wire is a series of delta functions, with peaks corresponding to the subbands, unlike the parabolic DOS of the bulk material or the staircase DOS of the quantum well.
- The optical absorption and emission of the quantum wire are discrete and polarization-dependent, due to the transitions between the subbands, unlike the continuous spectrum of the bulk material or the wavelength-selective spectrum of the quantum well.
- The quantum wire can be used to make various devices, such as nanotransistors, nanosensors, nanowire FETs, nanolasers, etc.
