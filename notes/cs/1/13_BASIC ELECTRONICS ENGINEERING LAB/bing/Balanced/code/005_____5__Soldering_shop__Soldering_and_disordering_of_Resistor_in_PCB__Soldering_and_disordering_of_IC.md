### 5. Soldering shop: Soldering and disordering of Resistor in PCB. Soldering and disordering of IC in PCB. Soldering and disordering of Capacitor in PCB

Soldering is the process of joining two or more metal parts by melting and flowing a filler metal (solder) into the joint. Soldering is used to create electrical connections, mechanical joints, or both. Soldering can be done with a soldering iron, a soldering gun, a soldering station, or a soldering torch.

Disordering is the process of removing soldered parts from a circuit board or a joint. Disordering can be done with a disordering pump, a disordering wick, a disordering iron, or a hot air gun.

Resistors, capacitors, and integrated circuits (ICs) are common components that can be soldered and disordering on a printed circuit board (PCB). A PCB is a thin board made of fiberglass, epoxy, or other material that has copper traces and pads on its surface to connect electronic components.

The following are some general steps and tips for soldering and disordering these components on a PCB:

- Before soldering or disordering, make sure the PCB and the components are clean and free of dust, grease, or oxidation. Use a soft brush, alcohol, or flux to clean the surfaces.
- Use a soldering iron or a soldering station with a suitable tip size and temperature for the component and the PCB. A fine tip is better for small components and pads, while a larger tip is better for larger components and pads. The temperature should be high enough to melt the solder quickly, but not too high to damage the component or the PCB. A typical temperature range is 300°C to 400°C.
- Use a good quality solder with a suitable composition and diameter for the component and the PCB. A common solder composition is 60% tin and 40% lead, which has a low melting point and good flowability. A common solder diameter is 0.5 mm to 1 mm, depending on the size of the component and the pad. Avoid using solder that contains acid or water-soluble flux, as they can corrode the PCB and the components.
- Use a soldering stand or a third hand tool to hold the PCB and the component in place. Make sure the component is aligned with the pad and the polarity is correct (if applicable). Apply a small amount of flux to the pad and the component lead (if applicable) to improve the soldering quality and reduce oxidation.
- To solder a component, heat the pad and the component lead (if applicable) with the soldering iron tip for a few seconds, then apply the solder to the joint. The solder should flow smoothly and form a shiny and smooth fillet around the joint. Avoid applying too much or too little solder, as this can cause cold solder joints, solder bridges, or solder balls. Remove the soldering iron tip and let the joint cool down. Do not move the component or the PCB until the solder is solidified.
- To disorder a component, heat the solder joint with the soldering iron tip or the hot air gun until the solder melts, then use a disordering pump or a disordering wick to remove the solder from the joint. The disordering pump is a spring-loaded device that creates a vacuum to suck the molten solder. The disordering wick is a braided copper wire that absorbs the molten solder. Be careful not to damage the pad or the component when using these tools. After disordering, clean the pad and the component lead (if applicable) with a soft brush, alcohol, or flux.
- After soldering or disordering, inspect the joints for any defects, such as cold solder joints, solder bridges, solder balls, or lifted pads. Use a magnifying glass or a microscope to check the joints. If any defects are found, rework the joints by reheating and adding or removing solder as needed. After reworking, clean the joints with a soft brush, alcohol, or flux.
- After soldering or disordering, test the PCB and the components for functionality and performance. Use a multimeter, an oscilloscope, or other testing equipment to measure the voltage, current, resistance, capacitance, or frequency of the circuit. If any problems are found, troubleshoot the circuit by checking the connections, the components, and the solder joints.
