# PN Junction Diode: Characteristics of PN Junction Diode-Static and Dynamic Resistance Measurement from Graph

A PN junction diode is a semiconductor device that allows current to flow in one direction only. It is formed by joining a p-type semiconductor and an n-type semiconductor, creating a depletion region at the junction. The depletion region acts as a potential barrier that prevents the flow of charge carriers across the junction, unless an external voltage is applied.

## VI Characteristics of PN Junction Diode

The VI characteristics of a PN junction diode are the graphs that show the relationship between the voltage applied across the diode and the current flowing through it. There are two types of VI characteristics: forward and reverse.

### Forward Characteristics

The forward characteristics show the behavior of the diode when it is forward biased, meaning that the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the p-type region and the negative terminal to the n-type region. In this case, the external voltage reduces the potential barrier and allows the charge carriers to cross the junction. The current increases exponentially with the voltage, as shown in the graph below.

![Forward characteristics of PN junction diode](https://testbook.com/learn/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Forward-Bias-Characteristics-of-PN-Junction-Diode.png)

The forward voltage, VF, is the minimum voltage required to make the diode conduct. It is typically around 0.7 V for silicon diodes and 0.3 V for germanium diodes.

The forward resistance, RF, is the ratio of the forward voltage to the forward current. It is a measure of how much the diode resists the current flow in the forward direction. It is usually very small, in the order of ohms or milliohms.

### Reverse Characteristics

The reverse characteristics show the behavior of the diode when it is reverse biased, meaning that the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the n-type region and the negative terminal to the p-type region. In this case, the external voltage increases the potential barrier and prevents the charge carriers from crossing the junction. The current is very small, in the order of microamperes or nanoamperes, and is called the reverse saturation current, IR. It is due to the thermal generation of minority carriers in the semiconductor. The graph below shows the reverse characteristics of a PN junction diode.

![Reverse characteristics of PN junction diode](https://testbook.com/learn/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Reverse-Bias-Characteristics-of-PN-Junction-Diode.png)

The reverse voltage, VR, is the maximum voltage that can be applied across the diode without damaging it. It is also called the breakdown voltage or the peak inverse voltage. It depends on the doping level and the thickness of the depletion region. If the reverse voltage exceeds the breakdown voltage, the diode enters the breakdown region, where the current increases rapidly with the voltage. This can cause the diode to overheat and burn out.

The reverse resistance, RR, is the ratio of the reverse voltage to the reverse current. It is a measure of how much the diode resists the current flow in the reverse direction. It is usually very large, in the order of megaohms or gigaohms.

## Static and Dynamic Resistance of PN Junction Diode

The static resistance of a PN junction diode is the resistance at a particular point on the VI characteristics curve. It is calculated by dividing the voltage by the current at that point. For example, the static forward resistance at point A on the graph below is given by RF = VF/IF.

The dynamic resistance of a PN junction diode is the resistance at a small interval around a particular point on the VI characteristics curve. It is calculated by dividing the change in voltage by the change in current at that interval. For example, the dynamic forward resistance at point A on the graph below is given by rF = dV/dI.

![Dynamic resistance of PN junction diode](https://www.electronics-tutorials.ws/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/diode-dio4.gif)

The dynamic resistance is a measure of how the diode responds to small changes in the applied voltage. It is useful for analyzing the diode behavior in AC circuits, where the voltage and current vary sinusoidally. The dynamic resistance is usually much smaller than the static resistance, and it decreases as the current increases.
