### Kreb Cycle

The Kreb Cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, is a series of chemical reactions that occur in the mitochondria of eukaryotic cells. The cycle is responsible for the oxidative metabolism of carbohydrates, fats and proteins, and is a critical component of the process of cellular respiration.

Here are some key points to remember about the Kreb Cycle:

- The cycle begins with the formation of citrate, a compound that is produced when acetyl CoA (a molecule derived from the breakdown of carbohydrates, fats and proteins) combines with oxaloacetate.
- Citrate is then converted into isocitrate through a series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions.
- Isocitrate is then converted into alpha-ketoglutarate, releasing carbon dioxide and producing NADH (a molecule that carries high-energy electrons).
- Alpha-ketoglutarate is then converted into succinyl-CoA, releasing another molecule of carbon dioxide and producing another molecule of NADH.
- Succinyl-CoA is then converted into succinate, producing a molecule of ATP through a process known as substrate-level phosphorylation.
- Succinate is then converted into fumarate, producing another molecule of FADH2 (a molecule that carries high-energy electrons).
- Fumarate is then converted into malate, which is then converted back into oxaloacetate, completing the cycle.

Overall, the Kreb Cycle is an essential component of cellular respiration, providing the energy needed for the synthesis of ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the primary source of energy for cellular processes. Factors that can affect the Kreb Cycle include the availability of oxygen, the levels of NAD+ and FAD, and the availability of substrates such as glucose and fatty acids.
