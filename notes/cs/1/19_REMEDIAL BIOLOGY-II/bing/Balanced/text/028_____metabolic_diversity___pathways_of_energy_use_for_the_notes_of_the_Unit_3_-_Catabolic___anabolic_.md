### Metabolic Diversity and Pathways of Energy Use

- Metabolic diversity mainly refers to the different metabolic strategies that organisms have evolved to obtain energy.
- Metabolic pathways are series of connected chemical reactions that feed one another and convert starting molecules into products.
- Metabolic pathways can be broadly divided into two categories based on their effects:
  - Catabolic pathways break down complex molecules into simpler ones and release energy. Examples are glycolysis, Krebs cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.
  - Anabolic pathways synthesize complex molecules from simpler ones and consume energy. Examples are photosynthesis, gluconeogenesis, and protein synthesis.
- Metabolic pathways evolved among prokaryotes before eukaryotes arose as the result of their interaction and coevolution with changing physicochemical environmental conditions.
- Metabolic diversity is influenced by factors such as oxygen availability, light intensity, temperature, pH, and nutrient sources.
- Some examples of metabolic diversity among prokaryotes are:
  - Aerobic respiration: using oxygen as the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain to produce ATP. Most bacteria and archaea use this pathway.
  - Anaerobic respiration: using other molecules (such as nitrate, sulfate, or carbon dioxide) as the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain to produce ATP. Some bacteria and archaea use this pathway when oxygen is scarce or absent.
  - Fermentation: using organic molecules (such as pyruvate, lactate, or ethanol) as the final electron acceptor in the absence of an electron transport chain to produce ATP. Some bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes use this pathway when oxygen is scarce or absent.
  - Photosynthesis: using light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into organic molecules and oxygen. Some bacteria (such as cyanobacteria) and eukaryotes (such as plants and algae) use this pathway to produce their own food.
  - Chemolithotrophy: using inorganic molecules (such as hydrogen, ammonia, or sulfur) as the electron donor to produce ATP. Some bacteria and archaea use this pathway to obtain energy from the environment.
  - Chemoorganotrophy: using organic molecules (such as glucose, fatty acids, or amino acids) as the electron donor to produce ATP. Most bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes use this pathway to obtain energy from food.
- Metabolic pathways are regulated by various mechanisms, such as feedback inhibition, allosteric regulation, covalent modification, and gene expression.
- Metabolic pathways are integrated and coordinated to maintain homeostasis and respond to environmental changes. For example, the glycolysis pathway can provide intermediates for the Krebs cycle, the pentose phosphate pathway, and the synthesis of amino acids and nucleotides. The Krebs cycle can provide intermediates for the synthesis of amino acids, lipids, and porphyrins. The oxidative phosphorylation pathway can provide ATP for various cellular processes. The photosynthesis pathway can provide organic molecules and oxygen for the respiration pathway. The gluconeogenesis pathway can produce glucose from non-carbohydrate sources when glucose is low. The lipid catabolism pathway can produce acetyl-CoA and glycerol from fatty acids when glucose is low. The protein catabolism pathway can produce amino acids and ammonia from proteins when glucose is low or nitrogen is high. The amino acid synthesis pathway can produce non-essential amino acids from intermediates of glycolysis and the Krebs cycle. The lipid synthesis pathway can produce fatty acids and triglycerides from acetyl-CoA and glycerol. The protein synthesis pathway can produce proteins from amino acids and nucleotides. The nucleotide synthesis pathway can produce nucleotides from intermediates of glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and the amino acid synthesis pathway.
