### Carbohydrate Metabolism

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the whole of the biochemical processes responsible for the metabolic formation, breakdown, and interconversion of carbohydrates in living organisms.
- Carbohydrates are organic molecules composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms. They can be classified into monosaccharides (simple sugars), disaccharides (two monosaccharides linked by a glycosidic bond), oligosaccharides (three to ten monosaccharides), and polysaccharides (more than ten monosaccharides).
- The most common monosaccharides are glucose, fructose, and galactose. The most common disaccharides are sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose), and maltose (glucose + glucose). The most common polysaccharides are starch, glycogen, and cellulose.
- Carbohydrate metabolism begins with digestion in the small intestine where monosaccharides are absorbed into the blood stream. Blood sugar concentrations are controlled by three hormones: insulin, glucagon, and epinephrine. Insulin lowers blood glucose levels by stimulating the uptake of glucose by cells and the storage of glucose as glycogen in the liver and muscles. Glucagon raises blood glucose levels by stimulating the breakdown of glycogen and the release of glucose by the liver. Epinephrine also raises blood glucose levels by stimulating the breakdown of glycogen and the release of glucose by the liver and muscles, as well as inhibiting the uptake of glucose by cells.
- The main pathway of carbohydrate metabolism is glycolysis, which is the breakdown of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, with the net production of two molecules of ATP and two molecules of NADH. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm of all cells and does not require oxygen.
- Pyruvate can have different fates depending on the availability of oxygen and the type of cell. In the presence of oxygen, pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is converted into acetyl-CoA, which then enters the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle). The citric acid cycle is a series of reactions that produces two molecules of CO2, one molecule of ATP, three molecules of NADH, and one molecule of FADH2 for each acetyl-CoA. The NADH and FADH2 then donate their electrons to the electron transport chain, which is a series of protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The electron transport chain transfers the electrons to oxygen, forming water, and generates a proton gradient across the membrane. The proton gradient drives the synthesis of ATP by the enzyme ATP synthase. This process is called oxidative phosphorylation and produces about 32 molecules of ATP for each glucose molecule.
- In the absence of oxygen, pyruvate undergoes anaerobic respiration, which is the reduction of pyruvate to either lactate or ethanol, depending on the type of organism. In animals, pyruvate is reduced to lactate by the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase, which regenerates NAD+ from NADH. This allows glycolysis to continue in the absence of oxygen, but produces only two molecules of ATP for each glucose molecule. This process is called lactic acid fermentation and occurs in the muscles during strenuous exercise, causing muscle fatigue and soreness.
- In some microorganisms, such as yeast, pyruvate is reduced to ethanol and CO2 by the enzymes pyruvate decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase, which also regenerate NAD+ from NADH. This allows glycolysis to continue in the absence of oxygen, but produces only two molecules of ATP for each glucose molecule. This process is called alcoholic fermentation and is used for the production of bread, beer, and wine.
- Carbohydrates can also be synthesized from other sources, such as amino acids, glycerol, and lactate. This process is called gluconeogenesis and occurs mainly in the liver. Gluconeogenesis is the reverse of glycolysis, with some modifications to bypass the irreversible steps of glycolysis. Gluconeogenesis is important for maintaining blood glucose levels during fasting, starvation, or prolonged exercise.
