# Energy Utilization

- Energy utilization is the process of converting chemical energy into useful work by living organisms.
- Energy utilization involves the breakdown of organic molecules, such as glucose, by cellular respiration, which releases energy that can be used to synthesize ATP, the primary energy currency of all cells.
- Energy utilization also involves the relationship of carbohydrate metabolism to other compounds, such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, which can be used as alternative sources of energy or as building blocks for biosynthesis.

## Structure of mitochondria

- Mitochondria are organelles that are the site of most of the energy production in eukaryotic cells.
- Mitochondria have a double membrane structure: an outer membrane that surrounds the organelle and an inner membrane that forms folds called cristae, which increase the surface area for energy production.
- Mitochondria also have a matrix, which is the space enclosed by the inner membrane, where some of the enzymes involved in cellular respiration are located.
- Mitochondria have their own DNA and ribosomes, which allow them to produce some of their own proteins.

## Cellular respiration

- Cellular respiration is the process of oxidizing organic molecules, such as glucose, to carbon dioxide and water, and releasing energy that can be used to synthesize ATP.
- Cellular respiration consists of three main stages: glycolysis, the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle), and the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation.
- Cellular respiration can be summarized by the following equation:

C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy (ATP and heat)

## Relationship of carbohydrate metabolism to other compounds

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the breakdown and synthesis of glucose and other sugars, which are the main source of energy for most cells.
- Carbohydrate metabolism is linked to other compounds, such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, by various metabolic pathways that can interconvert different molecules depending on the cellular needs and availability of substrates.
- For example, excess glucose can be stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles, or converted to fatty acids and stored as triglycerides in adipose tissue. Conversely, when glucose is scarce, glycogen can be broken down to glucose, or fatty acids can be oxidized to acetyl-CoA, which can enter the citric acid cycle.
- Similarly, amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, can be used as a source of energy by deamination, which removes the amino group and produces ammonia, which is excreted as urea. The remaining carbon skeleton can be converted to pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, or other intermediates of the citric acid cycle, depending on the type of amino acid. Conversely, when amino acids are needed for protein synthesis, they can be synthesized from glucose or other intermediates by transamination, which transfers the amino group from one molecule to another.
- Nucleic acids, such as DNA and RNA, can also be degraded and recycled by nucleases, which break down the phosphodiester bonds between the nucleotides, and nucleosidases, which cleave the glycosidic bonds between the nitrogenous bases and the pentose sugars. The resulting nucleosides and nucleobases can be reused for nucleic acid synthesis, or further degraded to produce ribose-5-phosphate, which can enter the pentose phosphate pathway, or uric acid, which is excreted as a waste product.

## Glycolysis

- Glycolysis is the first stage of cellular respiration, which occurs in the cytoplasm of the cell.
- Glycolysis is the process of splitting one molecule of glucose (a six-carbon sugar) into two molecules of pyruvate (a three-carbon compound).
- Glycolysis consists of ten enzymatic reactions, which can be divided into two phases: the preparatory phase and the payoff phase.
- The preparatory phase consumes two molecules of ATP to phosphorylate glucose and its derivatives, making them more reactive and unstable.
- The payoff phase produces four molecules of ATP (a net gain of two ATP) and two molecules of NADH (a reduced form of the electron carrier NAD+) by oxidizing the intermediates and transferring their electrons to NAD+.
- The overall equation of glycolysis is:

Glucose + 2NAD+ + 2ADP + 2Pi -> 2Pyruvate + 2NADH + 2H+ + 2
