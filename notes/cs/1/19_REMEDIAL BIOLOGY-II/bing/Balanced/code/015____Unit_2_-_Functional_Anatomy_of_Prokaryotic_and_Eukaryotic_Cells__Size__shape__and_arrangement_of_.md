## Unit 2 - Functional Anatomy of Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells: Size, shape, and arrangement of bacterial cells. Structure and function of cells

- Prokaryotic cells are cells that lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are usually smaller and simpler than eukaryotic cells, which have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. 
- Eukaryotic cells are cells that have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are usually larger and more complex than prokaryotic cells, which lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. 
- Bacterial cells are prokaryotic cells that belong to the domain Bacteria. They are the most abundant and diverse group of organisms on Earth. They can be found in almost every environment and can perform various metabolic processes. 
- The size of bacterial cells varies from 0.2 to 10 micrometers in diameter, depending on the species and the environmental conditions. The average size of a bacterial cell is about 1 micrometer in diameter. 
- The shape of bacterial cells can be classified into three main categories: cocci, bacilli, and spirilla. 
  - Cocci are spherical or oval-shaped bacteria. They can be arranged in different patterns, such as single, pairs, chains, clusters, or tetrads. Examples of cocci are Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, and Micrococcus. 
  - Bacilli are rod-shaped or cylindrical bacteria. They can be arranged in different patterns, such as single, pairs, chains, or palisades. Examples of bacilli are Escherichia, Bacillus, and Lactobacillus. 
  - Spirilla are spiral-shaped or curved bacteria. They can be arranged in different patterns, such as single, pairs, or chains. Examples of spirilla are Helicobacter, Treponema, and Campylobacter. 
- The structure and function of bacterial cells are determined by their cell envelope, which consists of three layers: the cell wall, the cell membrane, and the capsule. 
  - The cell wall is a rigid layer that surrounds the cell membrane and provides shape, support, and protection to the bacterial cell. It also prevents the cell from bursting due to osmotic pressure. The cell wall is composed of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugar and amino acids. The cell wall can be classified into two types: gram-positive and gram-negative, based on the amount and arrangement of peptidoglycan and the presence or absence of an outer membrane. 
    - Gram-positive cell wall has a thick layer of peptidoglycan and no outer membrane. It also contains teichoic acids, which are polymers of glycerol or ribitol. Gram-positive bacteria stain purple when treated with a Gram stain, a technique that differentiates bacteria based on their cell wall structure. Examples of gram-positive bacteria are Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, and Bacillus. 
    - Gram-negative cell wall has a thin layer of peptidoglycan and an outer membrane. The outer membrane is composed of lipopolysaccharides (LPS), which are molecules of lipid and sugar. The outer membrane acts as a barrier to many substances, such as antibiotics and detergents. Gram-negative bacteria stain pink or red when treated with a Gram stain. Examples of gram-negative bacteria are Escherichia, Salmonella, and Pseudomonas.

It also contains proteins that perform various functions, such as transport, enzymatic, and signal transduction. The cell membrane is also involved in energy production, as it contains the electron transport chain and the ATP synthase, which are involved in cellular respiration.
