### Carbohydrate metabolism

Carbohydrate metabolism is the set of biochemical processes that involve the formation, breakdown, and interconversion of carbohydrates in living organisms. Carbohydrates are organic molecules that consist of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms. They can be classified into simple sugars (monosaccharides) and complex sugars (polysaccharides).

Some of the main functions of carbohydrates are:

- To provide energy for cellular activities through glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.
- To store energy in the form of glycogen in the liver and muscles, and starch in plants.
- To serve as structural components of cell walls, membranes, and extracellular matrices.
- To act as signaling molecules and receptors in cell communication.

The main steps of carbohydrate metabolism are:

- Digestion: The breakdown of complex carbohydrates into simple sugars by enzymes in the mouth, stomach, and small intestine. The main products of digestion are glucose, fructose, and galactose, which are absorbed into the bloodstream.
- Glycolysis: The oxidation of glucose into pyruvate, which yields two molecules of ATP and two molecules of NADH. This process occurs in the cytoplasm of all cells and does not require oxygen. Pyruvate can be further converted into acetyl-CoA, lactate, or ethanol, depending on the availability of oxygen and the type of cell.
- The citric acid cycle: The oxidation of acetyl-CoA into carbon dioxide and water, which yields two molecules of ATP, six molecules of NADH, and two molecules of FADH2. This process occurs in the mitochondria of aerobic cells and requires oxygen. The NADH and FADH2 are used to generate more ATP in the next step.
- Oxidative phosphorylation: The transfer of electrons from NADH and FADH2 to oxygen, which creates a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. This gradient drives the synthesis of ATP by the enzyme ATP synthase. This process produces the most ATP (about 32 molecules per glucose molecule) and water as a byproduct.
- Gluconeogenesis: The synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources, such as amino acids, lactate, glycerol, and pyruvate. This process occurs mainly in the liver and kidneys and requires energy from ATP and NADH. Gluconeogenesis is important for maintaining blood glucose levels during fasting, starvation, or intense exercise.
- Glycogenesis: The synthesis of glycogen from glucose, which is catalyzed by the enzyme glycogen synthase. This process occurs mainly in the liver and muscles and requires energy from ATP and UTP. Glycogenesis is important for storing excess glucose for later use.
- Glycogenolysis: The breakdown of glycogen into glucose, which is catalyzed by the enzyme glycogen phosphorylase. This process occurs mainly in the liver and muscles and releases glucose into the bloodstream. Glycogenolysis is important for providing glucose during periods of high energy demand, such as exercise or stress.
- Pentose phosphate pathway: The conversion of glucose into pentose sugars, such as ribose and deoxyribose, which are used for the synthesis of nucleic acids and coenzymes. This process also generates NADPH, which is used for biosynthesis and detoxification reactions. The pentose phosphate pathway occurs in the cytoplasm of all cells and does not produce ATP.

Carbohydrate metabolism is regulated by various hormones, enzymes, and feedback mechanisms that ensure a balance between glucose supply and demand. Some of the main regulators are:

- Insulin: A hormone secreted by the pancreas in response to high blood glucose levels. Insulin stimulates the uptake of glucose by cells, the synthesis of glycogen, and the inhibition of gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis. Insulin also promotes the use of glucose as the main energy source.
- Glucagon: A hormone secreted by the pancreas in response to low blood glucose levels. Glucagon stimulates the breakdown of glycogen, the synthesis of glucose, and the use of fatty acids and ketone bodies as alternative energy sources. Glucagon also inhibits the uptake of glucose by cells and the synthesis of glycogen.
