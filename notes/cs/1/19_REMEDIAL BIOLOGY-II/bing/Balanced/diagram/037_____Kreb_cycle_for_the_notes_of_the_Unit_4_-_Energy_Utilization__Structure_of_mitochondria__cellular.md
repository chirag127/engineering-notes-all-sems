### Kreb cycle

The Kreb cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle, is a series of chemical reactions that occur in the mitochondria of aerobic organisms. It is part of the process of cellular respiration, which converts glucose and other organic molecules into energy in the form of ATP  .

Some main points about the Kreb cycle are:

- The Kreb cycle requires oxygen and produces carbon dioxide as a waste product  .
- The Kreb cycle begins with the conversion of pyruvate, the product of glycolysis, into acetyl-CoA by the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase complex .
- Acetyl-CoA then combines with oxaloacetate, a four-carbon molecule, to form citrate, a six-carbon molecule  .
- Citrate undergoes a series of eight steps, involving various enzymes and coenzymes, to regenerate oxaloacetate and complete the cycle  .
- The Kreb cycle produces two molecules of ATP, six molecules of NADH, and two molecules of FADH2 per glucose molecule  .
- NADH and FADH2 are electron carriers that transfer their electrons to the electron transport chain, where most of the ATP is generated by oxidative phosphorylation  .
- The Kreb cycle also provides precursors for the synthesis of certain amino acids, as well as other important biomolecules .
- The Kreb cycle is regulated by feedback inhibition, where high levels of ATP, NADH, or citrate inhibit the activity of key enzymes in the cycle  .
