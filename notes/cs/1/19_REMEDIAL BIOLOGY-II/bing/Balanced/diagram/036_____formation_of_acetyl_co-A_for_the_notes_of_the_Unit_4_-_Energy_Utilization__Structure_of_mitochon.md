### Formation of acetyl co-A

- Acetyl co-A is a molecule that serves as a link between glycolysis and the citric acid cycle in cellular respiration.
- Acetyl co-A is formed from the oxidation of pyruvate, the end product of glycolysis, in a process called pyruvate oxidation.
- Pyruvate oxidation occurs in the mitochondrial matrix in eukaryotes and in the cytoplasm in prokaryotes.
- Pyruvate oxidation involves three steps:
  - Pyruvate is decarboxylated, meaning it loses a carbon atom as carbon dioxide (CO2).
  - The remaining two-carbon fragment is oxidized, meaning it loses electrons and hydrogen atoms, which are transferred to a coenzyme called NAD+ to form NADH.
  - The oxidized two-carbon fragment is attached to a molecule called coenzyme A (CoA) to form acetyl co-A.
- The overall reaction of pyruvate oxidation can be written as:

  - Pyruvate + CoA + NAD+ → Acetyl co-A + NADH + H+ + CO2
- Acetyl co-A can also be formed from the breakdown of fatty acids (lipids) in a process called beta-oxidation.
- Beta-oxidation occurs in the mitochondrial matrix in eukaryotes and in the peroxisomes in prokaryotes.
- Beta-oxidation involves four steps:
  - The fatty acid is activated by attaching it to CoA, using two molecules of ATP.
  - The activated fatty acid is oxidized, meaning it loses electrons and hydrogen atoms, which are transferred to FAD and NAD+ to form FADH2 and NADH.
  - The oxidized fatty acid is cleaved, meaning it loses two carbon atoms as acetyl co-A.
  - The remaining fatty acid chain is shortened by two carbons and repeats the cycle until it is completely converted to acetyl co-A.
- The overall reaction of beta-oxidation can be written as:

  - Fatty acid + CoA + FAD + NAD+ → Acetyl co-A + FADH2 + NADH + H+
- Acetyl co-A is then used as a substrate for the citric acid cycle, where it is further oxidized to produce more CO2, NADH, FADH2, and ATP.
