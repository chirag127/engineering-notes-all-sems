### Energy production

- Energy production is the process of converting different sources of energy, such as chemical, light, or mechanical, into a form that can be used by living cells, such as ATP (adenosine triphosphate).
- Energy production involves two types of metabolic pathways: catabolic and anabolic.
- Catabolic pathways are those that break down large molecules into smaller ones, releasing energy in the process. For example, the digestion of food, the breakdown of glucose, and the oxidation of fatty acids are catabolic pathways.
- Anabolic pathways are those that build up large molecules from smaller ones, requiring energy in the process. For example, the synthesis of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids are anabolic pathways.
- Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up the rate of metabolic reactions by lowering the activation energy. Enzymes are specific for their substrates and can be regulated by various factors, such as temperature, pH, inhibitors, and activators.
- Energy production is regulated by the balance between catabolic and anabolic pathways, which depends on the availability of nutrients, hormones, and signals from the environment. The cell maintains a constant level of ATP by adjusting the rate of energy production and consumption.

### Carbohydrate metabolism

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the set of metabolic pathways that involve the breakdown and synthesis of carbohydrates, such as glucose, glycogen, starch, and cellulose.
- The main catabolic pathway of carbohydrate metabolism is glycolysis, which converts glucose into pyruvate, generating ATP and NADH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, reduced form) in the process. Pyruvate can then enter the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle), which produces more ATP, NADH, and FADH2 (flavin adenine dinucleotide, reduced form). The NADH and FADH2 can then donate their electrons to the electron transport chain, which drives the synthesis of ATP by oxidative phosphorylation.
- The main anabolic pathway of carbohydrate metabolism is gluconeogenesis, which converts non-carbohydrate precursors, such as lactate, amino acids, and glycerol, into glucose. Glucose can then be stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles, or as starch in plants. Glucose can also be used to synthesize other carbohydrates, such as ribose, deoxyribose, and cellulose.

### Lipid and protein catabolism

- Lipid and protein catabolism are the metabolic pathways that involve the breakdown of lipids and proteins, respectively, into smaller molecules that can be used for energy production or other purposes.
- Lipid catabolism, also known as lipolysis, involves the hydrolysis of triglycerides (the main form of stored fat) into glycerol and fatty acids. Glycerol can enter glycolysis, while fatty acids can undergo beta-oxidation, which produces acetyl-CoA (acetyl coenzyme A), NADH, and FADH2. Acetyl-CoA can then enter the citric acid cycle, and NADH and FADH2 can enter the electron transport chain, generating ATP.
- Protein catabolism, also known as proteolysis, involves the hydrolysis of proteins into amino acids. Amino acids can then be deaminated, which removes the amino group and produces ammonia and a carbon skeleton. Ammonia can be excreted or converted into urea or uric acid, depending on the organism. The carbon skeleton can enter different metabolic pathways, depending on the type of amino acid. Some amino acids can be converted into pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, or intermediates of the citric acid cycle, and then used for energy production or gluconeogenesis. Other amino acids can be converted into ketone bodies, which can be used as an alternative fuel source by the brain and other tissues.
