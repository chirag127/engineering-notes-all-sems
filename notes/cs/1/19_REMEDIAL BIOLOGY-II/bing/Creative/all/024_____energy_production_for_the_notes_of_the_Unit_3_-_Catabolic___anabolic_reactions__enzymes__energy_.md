# Energy production for the notes of the Unit 3 - Catabolic & anabolic reactions: enzymes, energy production and carbohydrate metabolism. - Energy production is the process of converting different sources of energy, such as chemical, light, or nuclear energy, into a usable form, such as ATP, the universal energy currency of cells.
- Energy production involves various metabolic pathways, which are sequences of enzyme-catalyzed reactions that transform one or more starting molecules into products.
- Metabolic pathways can be classified into two types: catabolic and anabolic.
  - Catabolic pathways are those that break down large molecules into smaller ones, releasing energy in the process. Examples of catabolic pathways are glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation, which are involved in carbohydrate metabolism; beta-oxidation, which is involved in lipid metabolism; and proteolysis, which is involved in protein metabolism.
  - Anabolic pathways are those that synthesize large molecules from smaller ones, requiring energy in the process. Examples of anabolic pathways are gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and glycogenesis, which are involved in carbohydrate metabolism; fatty acid synthesis, which is involved in lipid metabolism; and protein synthesis, which is involved in protein metabolism.
- Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up the rate of metabolic reactions by lowering the activation energy required for the reaction to occur. Enzymes are highly specific for their substrates and products, and can be regulated by various factors, such as temperature, pH, inhibitors, activators, and feedback mechanisms.
- Energy production mechanism is the process of coupling the energy released from catabolic pathways to the energy required for anabolic pathways, mainly through the formation and hydrolysis of ATP. ATP is composed of an adenine base, a ribose sugar, and three phosphate groups. The bonds between the phosphate groups are high-energy bonds that can be broken to release energy for cellular work. ATP can be synthesized by three main mechanisms: substrate-level phosphorylation, oxidative phosphorylation, and photophosphorylation.
  - Substrate-level phosphorylation is the direct transfer of a phosphate group from a substrate molecule to ADP, forming ATP. This occurs in some steps of glycolysis and the citric acid cycle, as well as in some anabolic pathways, such as gluconeogenesis and glycogenesis.
  - Oxidative phosphorylation is the indirect synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate, using the energy derived from the electron transport chain and the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. This occurs in the final stage of carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism, and is the major source of ATP production in aerobic organisms.
  - Photophosphorylation is the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate, using the energy derived from the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis. This occurs in the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts, and is the source of ATP production in photosynthetic organisms.
- Metabolic diversity is the variation in the types and pathways of metabolism among different organisms, depending on their environmental conditions, nutritional requirements, and evolutionary history. Some examples of metabolic diversity are:
  - Fermentation is the partial breakdown of organic molecules without oxygen, producing various end products, such as ethanol, lactate, or acetate, and a small amount of ATP. Fermentation occurs in some bacteria, fungi, and animal cells, such as muscle cells, when oxygen is scarce or absent.
  - Anaerobic respiration is the complete breakdown of organic molecules without oxygen, using an alternative electron acceptor, such as nitrate, sulfate, or carbon dioxide, and producing a moderate amount of ATP. Anaerobic respiration occurs in some bacteria and archaea, such as methanogens, sulfate-reducers, and denitrifiers, that live in oxygen-poor or oxygen-free environments.
  - Chemosynthesis is the synthesis of organic molecules from inorganic molecules, using the energy derived from the oxidation of reduced compounds, such as hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, or iron. Chemosynthesis occurs in some bacteria and archaea, such as sulfur-oxidizers, nitrifiers, and iron-oxidizers, that live in extreme environments, such as hydrothermal vents, volcanic soils, or acidic springs.
