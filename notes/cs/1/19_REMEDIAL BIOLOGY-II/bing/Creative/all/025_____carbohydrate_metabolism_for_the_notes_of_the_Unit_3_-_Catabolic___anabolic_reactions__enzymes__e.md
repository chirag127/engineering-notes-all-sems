# Carbohydrate Metabolism

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the whole of the biochemical processes responsible for the metabolic formation, breakdown, and interconversion of carbohydrates in living organisms.
- Carbohydrates are organic molecules composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms. They can be classified into monosaccharides (simple sugars), disaccharides (two monosaccharides linked by a glycosidic bond), oligosaccharides (three to ten monosaccharides), and polysaccharides (more than ten monosaccharides).
- The most common monosaccharides are glucose, fructose, and galactose. The most common disaccharides are sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose), and maltose (glucose + glucose). The most common polysaccharides are starch, glycogen, and cellulose.
- Carbohydrate metabolism begins with digestion in the small intestine where monosaccharides are absorbed into the blood stream. Blood sugar concentrations are controlled by three hormones: insulin, glucagon, and epinephrine. Insulin lowers blood glucose levels by stimulating the uptake of glucose by cells and the storage of glucose as glycogen in the liver and muscles. Glucagon raises blood glucose levels by stimulating the breakdown of glycogen and the release of glucose by the liver. Epinephrine also raises blood glucose levels by stimulating the breakdown of glycogen and the release of glucose by the liver and muscles, as well as the breakdown of fat and protein for energy.
- The main pathway of carbohydrate metabolism is glycolysis, which is the breakdown of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm of all cells and does not require oxygen. It produces a net gain of two molecules of ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and two molecules of NADH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) per glucose molecule. Pyruvate can then enter different pathways depending on the availability of oxygen and the type of cell.
- If oxygen is present, pyruvate can enter the mitochondria and be converted into acetyl-CoA (acetyl coenzyme A) by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Acetyl-CoA then enters the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle), which is a series of reactions that produce two molecules of CO2 (carbon dioxide), one molecule of ATP, three molecules of NADH, and one molecule of FADH2 (flavin adenine dinucleotide) per acetyl-CoA molecule. The NADH and FADH2 then enter the electron transport chain, which is a series of protein complexes that transfer electrons from NADH and FADH2 to O2 (oxygen), producing H2O (water) and a large amount of ATP. This process is called cellular respiration or aerobic respiration .
- If oxygen is absent or limited, pyruvate can undergo anaerobic respiration or fermentation. In anaerobic respiration, pyruvate is converted into lactate (lactic acid) by lactate dehydrogenase, which regenerates NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) from NADH. This allows glycolysis to continue, but produces less ATP and more lactate, which can cause muscle fatigue and soreness. In fermentation, pyruvate is converted into ethanol (alcohol) and CO2 by pyruvate decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase, which also regenerate NAD+ from NADH. This process occurs in some microorganisms, such as yeast, and is used for the production of alcoholic beverages and bread.
- Carbohydrate metabolism can also involve the interconversion of different carbohydrates, such as the conversion of glucose into galactose or fructose, or the conversion of glucose into ribose or deoxyribose. These processes are catalyzed by specific enzymes, such as hexokinase, phosphoglucose isomerase, phosphofructokinase, aldolase, transketolase, and transaldolase.
