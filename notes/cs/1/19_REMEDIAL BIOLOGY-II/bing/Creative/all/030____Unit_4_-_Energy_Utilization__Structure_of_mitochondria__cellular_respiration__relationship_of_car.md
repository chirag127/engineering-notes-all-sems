# Unit 4 - Energy Utilization

## Structure of mitochondria

- Mitochondria are small, rod-shaped organelles found in both plant and animal cells .
- They have two membranes, an outer membrane and an inner membrane, made of phospholipid layers .
- The outer membrane is smooth and encloses the organelle, while the inner membrane is folded into projections called cristae .
- The cristae increase the surface area of the inner membrane, where the reactions of cellular respiration take place.
- The space between the two membranes is called the intermembrane space, and the space inside the inner membrane is called the matrix .
- The matrix contains enzymes, ribosomes, DNA, and various molecules involved in energy production .
- Mitochondria are the sites of aerobic respiration, which generates adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the main source of chemical energy for the cell .
- Mitochondria have their own DNA and ribosomes, and can replicate independently of the cell .
- Mitochondria are thought to have evolved from ancient bacteria that were engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells, forming a symbiotic relationship . This is known as the endosymbiotic theory.

## Cellular respiration

- Cellular respiration is the process of breaking down organic molecules, such as glucose, to release energy in the form of ATP .
- Cellular respiration can be divided into four stages: glycolysis, formation of acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA), Krebs cycle, and electron transport chain (ETC) and oxidative phosphorylation .
- Glycolysis is the first stage of cellular respiration, and it occurs in the cytoplasm of the cell .
- Glycolysis involves the splitting of one molecule of glucose (a six-carbon sugar) into two molecules of pyruvate (a three-carbon compound), with the net production of two ATP and two NADH (a reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, an electron carrier) .
- Pyruvate then enters the mitochondria, where it is converted into acetyl-CoA, a two-carbon compound that is attached to coenzyme A, a carrier molecule .
- This conversion also produces one NADH and one carbon dioxide (CO2) per pyruvate molecule .
- Acetyl-CoA then enters the Krebs cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which occurs in the matrix of the mitochondria .
- The Krebs cycle is a series of reactions that involves the oxidation of acetyl-CoA and the generation of various intermediates, such as citrate, succinate, and oxaloacetate .
- The Krebs cycle also produces two CO2, one ATP, three NADH, and one FADH2 (a reduced form of flavin adenine dinucleotide, another electron carrier) per acetyl-CoA molecule .
- The NADH and FADH2 produced in glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, and the Krebs cycle then donate their electrons to the ETC, which is located in the inner membrane of the mitochondria .
- The ETC is a series of protein complexes and electron carriers that transfer the electrons from NADH and FADH2 to the final electron acceptor, which is oxygen (O2) .
- The ETC also pumps protons (H+) from the matrix to the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient across the inner membrane .
- The proton gradient drives the synthesis of ATP by a protein complex called ATP synthase, which is embedded in the inner membrane .
