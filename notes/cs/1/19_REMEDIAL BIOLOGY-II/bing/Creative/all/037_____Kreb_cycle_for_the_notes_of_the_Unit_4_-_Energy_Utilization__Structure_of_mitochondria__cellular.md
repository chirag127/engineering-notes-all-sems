# Kreb cycle

The Kreb cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is a series of chemical reactions that occur in the mitochondria of aerobic organisms. It is the second stage of cellular respiration, following glycolysis and preceding the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation. The Kreb cycle produces energy by oxidizing acetyl-CoA, which is derived from the breakdown of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. The Kreb cycle also provides precursors for the synthesis of certain amino acids and other biomolecules, and generates reducing agents (NADH and FADH2) that donate electrons to the electron transport chain.

The main steps of the Kreb cycle are:

- Acetyl-CoA (2 carbon atoms) combines with oxaloacetate (4 carbon atoms) to form citrate (6 carbon atoms) in a condensation reaction catalyzed by citrate synthase. This is the first and rate-limiting step of the cycle.
- Citrate is converted to isocitrate (6 carbon atoms) by a reversible isomerization reaction catalyzed by aconitase. This involves the removal and addition of a water molecule.
- Isocitrate is oxidized to alpha-ketoglutarate (5 carbon atoms) by a dehydrogenation reaction catalyzed by isocitrate dehydrogenase. This is the first oxidative decarboxylation reaction of the cycle, which releases a molecule of carbon dioxide and reduces a molecule of NAD+ to NADH.
- Alpha-ketoglutarate is further oxidized to succinyl-CoA (4 carbon atoms) by another oxidative decarboxylation reaction catalyzed by alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase. This also releases a molecule of carbon dioxide and reduces a molecule of NAD+ to NADH.
- Succinyl-CoA is converted to succinate (4 carbon atoms) by a substrate-level phosphorylation reaction catalyzed by succinyl-CoA synthetase. This involves the transfer of a phosphate group from succinyl-CoA to GDP, forming GTP, which can then be converted to ATP by nucleoside diphosphate kinase. This is the only step of the cycle that directly produces ATP (or GTP).
- Succinate is oxidized to fumarate (4 carbon atoms) by a dehydrogenation reaction catalyzed by succinate dehydrogenase. This is the only step of the cycle that occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where succinate dehydrogenase is part of complex II of the electron transport chain. This reaction reduces a molecule of FAD to FADH2, which can then donate electrons to the electron transport chain.
- Fumarate is converted to malate (4 carbon atoms) by a hydration reaction catalyzed by fumarase. This involves the addition of a water molecule across the double bond of fumarate.
- Malate is oxidized to oxaloacetate (4 carbon atoms) by a dehydrogenation reaction catalyzed by malate dehydrogenase. This reduces a molecule of NAD+ to NADH, which can then donate electrons to the electron transport chain.

The Kreb cycle completes one turn for each molecule of acetyl-CoA that enters it. Since each molecule of glucose produces two molecules of acetyl-CoA in the previous stage of glycolysis and pyruvate oxidation, the Kreb cycle turns twice for each molecule of glucose. The net products of the Kreb cycle per molecule of glucose are:

- 4 molecules of carbon dioxide (released as waste)
- 6 molecules of NADH (carrying high-energy electrons)
- 2 molecules of FADH2 (carrying high-energy electrons)
- 2 molecules of ATP (or GTP) (providing immediate energy)

The Kreb cycle is regulated by several factors, such as the availability of substrates (acetyl-CoA, NAD+, FAD, ADP, etc.), the feedback inhibition by products (NADH, ATP, etc.), and the allosteric modulation by other metabolites (citrate, calcium, etc.). The Kreb cycle is also influenced by hormonal signals, such as insulin and glucagon, that affect the metabolism of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. The Kreb cycle is essential for the energy production and biosynthesis of aerobic organisms, and its dysfunction can lead to various diseases, such as diabetes, cancer, and neurodegeneration.
