# Electron Transport System and Oxidative Phosphorylation

- Electron Transport System (ETS) is a series of proteins and electron carriers located in the inner mitochondrial membrane that transfers electrons donated by the reduced molecules NADH and FADH2 through a series of electron acceptors, to oxygen.
- Oxidative Phosphorylation (OP) is a process involving a flow of electrons through the ETS, which creates a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and the use of this gradient to drive the synthesis of ATP by a protein complex called ATP synthase.
- The ETS consists of four main complexes: Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase), Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase), Complex III (cytochrome bc1 complex), and Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase). There is also a mobile electron carrier called coenzyme Q (or ubiquinone) that shuttles electrons between Complex I and II to Complex III, and another mobile electron carrier called cytochrome c that transfers electrons from Complex III to Complex IV.
- As electrons move through the ETS, they release energy that is used to pump protons from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient. This gradient is also called the proton-motive force, as it drives the protons back into the matrix through the ATP synthase, which uses the energy to phosphorylate ADP to ATP.
- The ETS and OP are coupled by the chemiosmotic theory, which states that the energy released by the electron transport is stored as a proton gradient, and that the energy of the proton gradient is used to drive ATP synthesis.
- The ETS and OP are the final stages of cellular respiration, which is the process of breaking down organic molecules (such as glucose) to release energy in the form of ATP. Cellular respiration consists of four main steps: glycolysis, formation of acetyl co-A, Krebs cycle, and ETS and OP.
- Glycolysis is the first step of cellular respiration, which occurs in the cytoplasm of the cell. It involves the splitting of glucose (a six-carbon sugar) into two molecules of pyruvate (a three-carbon compound), with the net production of 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose molecule.
- Formation of acetyl co-A is the second step of cellular respiration, which occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. It involves the oxidation of pyruvate to acetyl co-A (a two-carbon compound) by an enzyme complex called pyruvate dehydrogenase, with the release of CO2 and the production of 1 NADH per pyruvate molecule.
- Krebs cycle (or citric acid cycle) is the third step of cellular respiration, which also occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. It involves the oxidation of acetyl co-A to CO2 by a series of enzymes, with the production of 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 ATP (or GTP) per acetyl co-A molecule.
- ETS and OP are the fourth and final step of cellular respiration, which occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It involves the transfer of electrons from NADH and FADH2 to oxygen by the ETS, with the generation of a proton gradient and the synthesis of ATP by OP.
- The total yield of ATP from cellular respiration depends on the efficiency of the ETS and OP, as well as the type of shuttle system that transports NADH from the cytoplasm to the mitochondria. In eukaryotic cells, there are two main shuttle systems: the malate-aspartate shuttle and the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle. The malate-aspartate shuttle operates in the liver, heart, and kidney cells, and transfers NADH to the ETS as NADH, resulting in 3 ATP per NADH. The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle operates in the muscle and brain cells, and transfers NADH to the ETS as FADH2, resulting in 2 ATP per NADH.
