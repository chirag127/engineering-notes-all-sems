# ATP and Cellular Respiration

- ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the energy-carrying molecule used in cells because it can release energy very quickly.
- Energy is released from ATP when the end phosphate is removed, forming ADP (adenosine diphosphate) and a free phosphate.
- ATP can be regenerated from ADP and a phosphate by using energy from cellular respiration.
- Cellular respiration refers to the breakdown of glucose and other respiratory substrates to make energy carrying molecules called ATP.
- Cellular respiration is a metabolic pathway that uses glucose to produce ATP, which may be used as energy to power many reactions throughout the body.
- Cellular respiration is a series of chemical reactions that break down glucose and produce ATP, which are:
  - Glycolysis: the splitting of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, producing 2 ATP and 2 NADH.
  - Pyruvate oxidation: the conversion of pyruvate into acetyl-CoA, producing 2 NADH and releasing 2 CO2.
  - Citric acid cycle (or Krebs cycle): the oxidation of acetyl-CoA into 2 CO2, producing 2 ATP, 6 NADH, and 2 FADH2.
  - Oxidative phosphorylation: the transfer of electrons from NADH and FADH2 to oxygen, producing 26-28 ATP and 6 H2O.
- Cellular respiration is a catabolic process, meaning it breaks down molecules and releases energy.
- Cellular respiration can be aerobic (using oxygen as the final electron acceptor) or anaerobic (using other molecules as the final electron acceptor).
- Cellular respiration is influenced by several factors, such as temperature, pH, substrate availability, enzyme activity, and oxygen concentration.
- Cellular respiration is the main source of ATP for most living organisms, and it is essential for maintaining life and homeostasis.
