# Structure of mitochondria

- Mitochondria are small, rod-shaped organelles that are found in most eukaryotic cells, such as plants and animals .
- They are involved in cellular respiration, which is the process of converting organic molecules into energy (ATP) for the cell    .
- They have a double membrane structure, consisting of an outer membrane and an inner membrane    .
- The outer membrane is smooth and surrounds the entire organelle. It is permeable to small molecules and ions  .
- The inner membrane is folded into projections called cristae, which increase the surface area for the reactions of cellular respiration   .
- The space between the outer and inner membranes is called the intermembrane space, and the space enclosed by the inner membrane is called the matrix   .
- The matrix contains various enzymes, molecules, and mitochondrial DNA, which is inherited maternally and encodes some of the proteins involved in cellular respiration   .
- Mitochondria can divide by fission, which is similar to bacterial reproduction, and can fuse with other mitochondria to exchange genetic material and regulate their function .
- Mitochondria are thought to have evolved from ancient bacteria that were engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells and established a symbiotic relationship with them.
- Mitochondria are involved in many cellular processes, such as apoptosis (programmed cell death), calcium signaling, oxidative stress, and aging.
- Mitochondria can also be affected by various diseases, such as mitochondrial disorders, diabetes, cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and cardiovascular diseases .
