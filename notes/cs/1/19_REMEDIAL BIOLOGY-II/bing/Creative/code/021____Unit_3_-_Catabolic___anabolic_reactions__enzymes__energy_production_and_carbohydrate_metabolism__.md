## Catabolic and anabolic reactions

- Catabolic reactions are those that break down complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy in the process. An example of a catabolic reaction is glycolysis, which breaks down glucose into pyruvate and produces ATP and NADH.
- Anabolic reactions are those that build up complex molecules from simpler ones, consuming energy in the process. An example of an anabolic reaction is gluconeogenesis, which synthesizes glucose from non-carbohydrate sources such as lactate and amino acids, using ATP and NADH.
- Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up the rate of chemical reactions by lowering the activation energy. Enzymes are highly specific for their substrates and can be regulated by various factors such as temperature, pH, inhibitors, activators, and feedback mechanisms.
- Energy production is the process of converting the chemical energy stored in the bonds of organic molecules into a more usable form, such as ATP, which is the universal energy currency of the cell. Energy production can occur through different pathways, depending on the availability of oxygen and the type of substrate.

## Carbohydrate metabolism

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the set of reactions that involve the breakdown and synthesis of carbohydrates, such as glucose, glycogen, starch, and cellulose. Carbohydrate metabolism can be divided into three main stages: glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.
- Glycolysis is the first stage of carbohydrate metabolism, which occurs in the cytoplasm of the cell. It converts one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, generating two molecules of ATP and two molecules of NADH. Glycolysis can occur in the presence or absence of oxygen, and can be followed by different pathways depending on the cellular conditions.
- The citric acid cycle, also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is the second stage of carbohydrate metabolism, which occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. It oxidizes the acetyl-CoA derived from pyruvate or fatty acids, producing two molecules of CO2, one molecule of ATP, three molecules of NADH, and one molecule of FADH2 per cycle. The citric acid cycle requires oxygen and is the main source of carbon dioxide and reduced coenzymes for oxidative phosphorylation.
- Oxidative phosphorylation is the third and final stage of carbohydrate metabolism, which occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It uses the electrons from NADH and FADH2 to drive the synthesis of ATP from ADP and Pi, through a series of protein complexes called the electron transport chain and a proton gradient across the membrane. Oxidative phosphorylation is the most efficient way of producing ATP, generating about 28 molecules of ATP per molecule of glucose.

## Lipid and protein catabolism

- Lipid catabolism is the process of breaking down lipids, such as triglycerides, into glycerol and fatty acids, which can then be used as energy sources or substrates for other metabolic pathways. Lipid catabolism can occur through two main mechanisms: lipolysis and beta-oxidation.
- Lipolysis is the hydrolysis of triglycerides into glycerol and fatty acids, which occurs in the cytoplasm of adipose tissue and other cells. Lipolysis is stimulated by hormones such as glucagon, epinephrine, and cortisol, and inhibited by insulin. Glycerol can be converted into glucose by gluconeogenesis, while fatty acids can be transported to the mitochondria for beta-oxidation.
- Beta-oxidation is the oxidation of fatty acids into acetyl-CoA, which occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. Beta-oxidation involves four steps: dehydrogenation, hydration, oxidation, and thiolysis, which are repeated until the fatty acid chain is completely cleaved.
