# Unit 4 - Energy Utilization

## Structure of mitochondria

- Mitochondria are small, rod-shaped organelles found in most eukaryotic cells, such as plants and animals  .
- They have two membranes, an outer membrane and an inner membrane, made of phospholipids  .
- The outer membrane is smooth and surrounds the organelle, while the inner membrane is folded into projections called cristae .
- The space between the two membranes is called the intermembrane space, and the space inside the inner membrane is called the matrix .
- The matrix contains mitochondrial DNA, ribosomes, enzymes, and other molecules involved in energy production  .
- The cristae increase the surface area of the inner membrane, where the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation occur .
- Mitochondria are the sites of cellular respiration, the process of converting organic molecules into ATP, the main energy currency of the cell    .
- Mitochondria can vary in number, shape, and size depending on the cell type and energy demand  .
- Mitochondria have their own genetic material and can replicate independently of the cell cycle.
- Mitochondria are thought to have evolved from ancient bacteria that were engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells in a process called endosymbiosis.

## Cellular respiration

- Cellular respiration is the process of breaking down organic molecules, such as glucose, into carbon dioxide and water, and releasing energy in the form of ATP   .
- Cellular respiration can be divided into four stages: glycolysis, formation of acetyl co-A, Krebs cycle, and electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation   .
- Glycolysis is the first stage of cellular respiration, and it occurs in the cytoplasm of the cell. It involves splitting a molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, and producing two molecules of ATP and two molecules of NADH (a high-energy electron carrier)   .
- Formation of acetyl co-A is the second stage of cellular respiration, and it occurs in the matrix of the mitochondria. It involves converting each molecule of pyruvate into a molecule of acetyl co-A, and releasing one molecule of carbon dioxide and one molecule of NADH   .
- Krebs cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is the third stage of cellular respiration, and it occurs in the matrix of the mitochondria. It involves a series of reactions that use acetyl co-A to produce two molecules of carbon dioxide, one molecule of ATP, three molecules of NADH, and one molecule of FADH2 (another high-energy electron carrier) per cycle   .
- Electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation are the fourth and final stage of cellular respiration, and they occur in the inner membrane of the mitochondria. They involve transferring the electrons from NADH and FADH2 to a series of protein complexes and electron carriers, and using the energy released to pump protons (H+) from the matrix to the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient   . The protons then flow back to the matrix through a protein called ATP synthase, which uses the energy to synthesize ATP from ADP and phosphate. This process is called oxidative phosphorylation because oxygen is the final electron acceptor and water is the final product   .
- The total amount of ATP produced by cellular respiration from one molecule of glucose is about 36 to 38 molecules, depending on the efficiency of the process
