### Relationship of carbohydrate metabolism to other compounds

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the process of breaking down and synthesizing carbohydrates, which are organic molecules composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms.
- Carbohydrates can be classified into simple sugars (monosaccharides and disaccharides) and complex sugars (polysaccharides), such as starch, glycogen, and cellulose.
- Carbohydrate metabolism is connected to the metabolism of other compounds, such as proteins and lipids, through common intermediates and pathways  .
- The main pathway of carbohydrate metabolism is glycolysis, which converts glucose into pyruvate, generating ATP and NADH .
- Pyruvate can then enter the mitochondria and be converted into acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA), which is the entry point for the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle) .
- The citric acid cycle is a series of reactions that produces more ATP, NADH, FADH2, and CO2 .
- The NADH and FADH2 then donate their electrons to the electron transport chain, which is a series of protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane .
- The electron transport chain transfers the electrons along a gradient of proton pumps, creating a proton motive force that drives the synthesis of ATP by the enzyme ATP synthase .
- This process is called oxidative phosphorylation, and it is the major source of ATP production in aerobic organisms .
- The carbohydrates that can also feed into glucose catabolism include galactose, fructose, and glycogen.
- Galactose and fructose can be converted into glucose or intermediates of glycolysis by specific enzymes.
- Glycogen is a branched polymer of glucose that is stored in the liver and muscle cells and can be broken down into glucose by the enzyme glycogen phosphorylase.
- The amino acids from proteins can also connect with glucose catabolism through pyruvate, acetyl CoA, and components of the citric acid cycle  .
- Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins and can be classified into glucogenic, ketogenic, or both, depending on how they are metabolized .
- Glucogenic amino acids can be converted into pyruvate or intermediates of the citric acid cycle, and can be used to synthesize glucose by a process called gluconeogenesis .
- Ketogenic amino acids can be converted into acetyl CoA or ketone bodies, and can be used to generate energy or synthesize fatty acids and cholesterol .
- Some amino acids can be both glucogenic and ketogenic, depending on the metabolic conditions .
- The fatty acids from lipids can also connect with glucose catabolism through acetyl CoA .
- Fatty acids are long chains of hydrocarbons that are the main components of triglycerides and phospholipids.
- Fatty acids can be broken down into acetyl CoA by a process called beta-oxidation, which occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.
- Acetyl CoA can then enter the citric acid cycle or be used to synthesize ketone bodies.
- Fatty acids can also be synthesized from acetyl CoA by a process called fatty acid synthesis, which occurs in the cytosol.
