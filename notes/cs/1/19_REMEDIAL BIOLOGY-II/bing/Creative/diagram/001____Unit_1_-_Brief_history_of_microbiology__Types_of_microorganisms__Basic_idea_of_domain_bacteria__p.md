- Brief history of microbiology
  - Microbiology is the study of microscopic organisms, such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa.
  - Microbiology essentially began with the development of the microscope in the 17th century.
  - Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch draper and hobbyist, was the first to observe and document microorganisms in 1674.
  - Robert Hooke, an English naturalist, was the first to use a microscope to observe living things, such as fungi and cells, in 1665.
  - Ferdinand Cohn, a German botanist, was the founder of bacteriology, a subdiscipline of microbiology, in the 19th century. He described several bacteria, such as Bacillus and Beggiatoa, and classified them based on their shape and motility.
  - Louis Pasteur, a French chemist, was a pioneer of microbiology in the 19th century. He demonstrated the role of microorganisms in fermentation, spoilage, and disease, and developed the germ theory of disease and the technique of pasteurization.
  - Robert Koch, a German physician, was another pioneer of microbiology in the 19th century. He established the criteria for proving the causal relationship between a microbe and a disease, and identified the causative agents of anthrax, tuberculosis, and cholera.
  - Other notable figures in the history of microbiology include Alexander Fleming, who discovered penicillin, the first antibiotic, in 1928; Martinus Beijerinck and Sergei Winogradsky, who developed the field of environmental microbiology in the late 19th and early 20th centuries; and Carl Woese, who proposed the three-domain system of classification for living organisms based on molecular phylogeny in 1977.

- Types of microorganisms
  - Microorganisms are classified into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya.
  - Bacteria are prokaryotic organisms, meaning they lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are the most abundant and diverse group of microorganisms, and can be found in almost every habitat on Earth. They have various shapes, such as rods, spheres, spirals, and filaments, and can be either motile or non-motile. They can also be classified based on their metabolic diversity, such as aerobic or anaerobic, autotrophic or heterotrophic, and chemotrophic or phototrophic.
  - Archaea are also prokaryotic organisms, but they differ from bacteria in their cell structure, membrane composition, and gene expression. They are often found in extreme environments, such as hot springs, salt lakes, and acidic soils, where they can tolerate high temperatures, salinity, and acidity. They can also be classified based on their metabolic diversity, such as methanogens, halophiles, thermophiles, and acidophiles.
  - Eukarya are eukaryotic organisms, meaning they have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They include four kingdoms: Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia.
    - Protista are a diverse group of mostly unicellular organisms, such as algae, protozoa, and slime molds. They can be either autotrophic or heterotrophic, and can be either motile or non-motile. They can also be classified based on their mode of locomotion, such as flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia.
    - Fungi are a group of mostly multicellular organisms, such as mushrooms, molds, and yeasts. They are heterotrophic, meaning they obtain organic matter from other sources, and decompose organic matter by secreting enzymes. They can also be classified based on their morphology, such as hyphae, mycelium, or spores.
