### Carbohydrate Metabolism

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the whole of the biochemical processes responsible for the metabolic formation, breakdown, and interconversion of carbohydrates in living organisms.
- Carbohydrates are organic molecules composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms. They can be classified into monosaccharides (simple sugars), disaccharides (two monosaccharides linked by a glycosidic bond), oligosaccharides (three to ten monosaccharides), and polysaccharides (more than ten monosaccharides).
- The most common and important monosaccharide in living organisms is glucose, which is the main source of energy for cells. Other monosaccharides include fructose, galactose, ribose, and deoxyribose.
- The most common and important disaccharides in living organisms are sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose), and maltose (glucose + glucose).
- The most common and important polysaccharides in living organisms are starch (a storage form of glucose in plants), glycogen (a storage form of glucose in animals), and cellulose (a structural component of plant cell walls).
- Carbohydrate metabolism begins with digestion in the small intestine where monosaccharides are absorbed into the blood stream. Blood sugar concentrations are controlled by three hormones: insulin, glucagon, and epinephrine.
- Insulin is secreted by the pancreas when the blood glucose level is high, and it stimulates the uptake of glucose by cells, especially in the liver, muscle, and adipose tissue. Insulin also promotes the synthesis of glycogen, fat, and protein, and inhibits the breakdown of these molecules.
- Glucagon is secreted by the pancreas when the blood glucose level is low, and it stimulates the breakdown of glycogen in the liver and the release of glucose into the blood. Glucagon also promotes the breakdown of fat and protein, and inhibits the synthesis of these molecules.
- Epinephrine is secreted by the adrenal glands in response to stress, and it enhances the effects of glucagon and inhibits the effects of insulin. Epinephrine also stimulates the breakdown of glycogen in the muscle and the release of glucose and fatty acids into the blood.
- The main pathway of glucose metabolism in cells is glycolysis, which is the breakdown of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, with the net production of two molecules of ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and two molecules of NADH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) per glucose molecule.
- Pyruvate can have different fates depending on the availability of oxygen and the type of cell. In the presence of oxygen, pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is converted into acetyl-CoA (acetyl coenzyme A), which then enters the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle). The citric acid cycle is a series of reactions that produces two molecules of CO2 (carbon dioxide), one molecule of ATP, three molecules of NADH, and one molecule of FADH2 (flavin adenine dinucleotide) per acetyl-CoA molecule.
- The NADH and FADH2 molecules generated by glycolysis and the citric acid cycle are then used to produce more ATP by a process called oxidative phosphorylation, which involves the transfer of electrons from these molecules to oxygen through a series of protein complexes called the electron transport chain. The electron transport chain creates a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, which drives the synthesis of ATP by a protein called ATP synthase.
- In the absence of oxygen, or in some cells that lack mitochondria, pyruvate is converted into lactate (lactic acid) by a process called anaerobic glycolysis, which regenerates NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) from NADH and allows glycolysis to continue. Lactate can be transported to the liver and converted back into glucose by a process called gluconeogenesis, which is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources.
