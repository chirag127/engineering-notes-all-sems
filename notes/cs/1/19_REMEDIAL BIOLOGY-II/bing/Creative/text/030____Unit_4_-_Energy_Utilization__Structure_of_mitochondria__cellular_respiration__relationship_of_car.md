## Unit 4 - Energy Utilization: Structure of mitochondria, cellular respiration, relationship of carbohydrate metabolism to other compounds, Glycolysis, formation of acetyl co-A, Kreb cycle, Electron Transport System and Oxidative Phosphorylation, ATP, factors affecting respiration

- Mitochondria are organelles that are responsible for the production of most of the energy in eukaryotic cells. They have a double membrane structure: an outer membrane that is smooth and an inner membrane that is folded into cristae. The space between the two membranes is called the intermembrane space, and the space inside the inner membrane is called the matrix. The matrix contains enzymes, DNA, and ribosomes.
- Cellular respiration is the process of breaking down organic molecules, such as glucose, to release energy in the form of ATP. It involves three main stages: glycolysis, the Kreb cycle, and the electron transport system and oxidative phosphorylation. Cellular respiration can be aerobic (using oxygen) or anaerobic (without oxygen).
- Carbohydrate metabolism is the conversion of carbohydrates into other compounds, such as pyruvate, acetyl co-A, and carbon dioxide. Carbohydrate metabolism is linked to other metabolic pathways, such as lipid metabolism, protein metabolism, and nucleic acid metabolism. For example, acetyl co-A can be used to synthesize fatty acids, cholesterol, and ketone bodies, or to enter the Kreb cycle. Pyruvate can be converted into amino acids, such as alanine and glutamate, or into lactate under anaerobic conditions. Carbon dioxide can be used for photosynthesis or for the synthesis of nucleotides.
- Glycolysis is the first stage of cellular respiration, in which glucose is split into two molecules of pyruvate. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen. It involves 10 enzymatic reactions, which can be divided into two phases: the preparatory phase and the payoff phase. The preparatory phase consumes 2 ATP molecules to phosphorylate glucose and fructose-6-phosphate. The payoff phase produces 4 ATP molecules and 2 NADH molecules by oxidizing glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and reducing NAD+. The net gain of glycolysis is 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose molecule.
- Formation of acetyl co-A is the link between glycolysis and the Kreb cycle. It occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and requires oxygen. It involves the oxidation of pyruvate by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, which consists of three enzymes and five coenzymes. The products of this reaction are acetyl co-A, carbon dioxide, and NADH. Acetyl co-A can then enter the Kreb cycle or be used for other metabolic pathways.
- The Kreb cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is the second stage of cellular respiration, in which acetyl co-A is oxidized to carbon dioxide and reduced coenzymes. It occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and requires oxygen. It involves eight enzymatic reactions, which can be divided into two phases: the condensation phase and the oxidation phase. The condensation phase involves the formation of citrate from acetyl co-A and oxaloacetate. The oxidation phase involves the oxidation of citrate to oxaloacetate, producing 2 carbon dioxide, 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 ATP (or GTP) per acetyl co-A molecule. The oxaloacetate can then combine with another acetyl co-A to continue the cycle.
- The electron transport system and oxidative phosphorylation is the third and final stage of cellular respiration, in which the reduced coenzymes (NADH and FADH2) are oxidized and the energy released is used to generate ATP. It occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane and requires oxygen. It involves four protein complexes (I, II, III, and IV) and two mobile electron carriers (ubiquinone and cytochrome c). The electrons from NADH and FADH2 are transferred to the protein complexes, which pump protons from the matrix to the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient. The protons then flow back to the matrix through the ATP synthase, which uses the proton motive force to synthesize ATP from ADP and Pi. The final electron acceptor is oxygen, which combines with protons and electrons to form water.
