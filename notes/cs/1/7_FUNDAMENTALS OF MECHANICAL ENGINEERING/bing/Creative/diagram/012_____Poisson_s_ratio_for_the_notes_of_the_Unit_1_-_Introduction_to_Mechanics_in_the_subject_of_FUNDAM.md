### Poisson's ratio

- Poisson's ratio is a measure of the Poisson effect, which is the deformation (expansion or contraction) of a material in directions perpendicular to the direction of loading.
- Poisson's ratio is defined as the negative ratio of the transverse strain to the longitudinal strain of a material when it is stretched or compressed .
- Mathematically, Poisson's ratio can be represented as:

$$\mu = - \frac{\epsilon_{lat}}{\epsilon_{long}}$$

Where,

$\mu$ = Poisson's ratio

$\epsilon_{lat}$ = Lateral strain

$\epsilon_{long}$ = Longitudinal strain

- Strain is the change in the dimension of an object or shape in terms of length, breadth, etc. divided by its original dimension.
- Lateral strain is the change in the width or thickness of an object or shape divided by its original width or thickness.
- Longitudinal strain is the change in the length of an object or shape divided by its original length.
- Poisson's ratio is a dimensionless quantity and has no unit.
- Poisson's ratio can have values between -1 and 0.5 for most materials.
- Poisson's ratio is positive for most materials, which means they contract in the transverse direction when stretched in the longitudinal direction, and vice versa.
- Poisson's ratio is zero for materials that do not change in the transverse direction when stretched or compressed in the longitudinal direction, such as cork.
- Poisson's ratio is negative for materials that expand in the transverse direction when stretched in the longitudinal direction, and vice versa, such as some types of rubber and foam.
- Poisson's ratio is 0.5 for materials that are incompressible, which means their volume does not change when stretched or compressed, such as water and some biological tissues.
- Poisson's ratio is an important property of materials that affects their mechanical behavior, such as stress, stiffness, strength, and fracture.
- Poisson's ratio can be determined experimentally by measuring the change in the dimensions of a material when subjected to a known load, or theoretically by using the elastic constants of a material, such as Young's modulus and shear modulus.
