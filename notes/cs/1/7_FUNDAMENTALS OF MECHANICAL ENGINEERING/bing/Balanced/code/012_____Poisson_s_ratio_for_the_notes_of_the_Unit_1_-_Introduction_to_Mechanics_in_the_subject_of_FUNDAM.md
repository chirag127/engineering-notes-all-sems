### Poisson’s ratio

- Poisson’s ratio is a measure of the Poisson effect, the deformation (expansion or contraction) of a material in directions perpendicular to the specific direction of loading.
- Poisson’s ratio is defined as the ratio of the lateral strain to the longitudinal strain of a material when it is stretched or compressed.
- Poisson’s ratio formula is given by: μ = - εₗₐₜ/εₗₒₙ₉, where μ is Poisson’s ratio, εₗₐₜ is the lateral strain, and εₗₒₙ₉ is the longitudinal strain.
- Poisson’s ratio is a dimensionless quantity and has no units.
- Poisson’s ratio is a property of a material and is constant for isotropic materials.
- Poisson’s ratio can have values between -1 and 0.5 for most materials. A positive value of Poisson’s ratio means that the material contracts laterally when stretched longitudinally, and vice versa. A negative value of Poisson’s ratio means that the material expands laterally when stretched longitudinally, and vice versa.
- Poisson’s ratio is important for engineering applications because it affects the stress distribution, stiffness, and stability of structures and materials.
- Poisson’s ratio can be determined experimentally by measuring the change in dimensions of a specimen under a tensile or compressive load.
- Poisson’s ratio examples: Rubber has a Poisson’s ratio of about 0.5, which means that it does not change in volume when deformed. Cork has a Poisson’s ratio of about 0, which means that it does not change in shape when deformed. Steel has a Poisson’s ratio of about 0.3, which means that it contracts slightly when stretched and expands slightly when compressed.
