### Stress-strain diagram for ductile and brittle materials

- A stress-strain diagram is a graphical representation of the relationship between the applied stress and the resulting strain in a material.
- The stress-strain diagram can be used to determine the mechanical properties of a material, such as the modulus of elasticity, the yield strength, the ultimate strength, and the fracture point.
- The shape of the stress-strain diagram depends on the type of material and the loading conditions. Generally, materials can be classified as ductile or brittle based on their behavior under stress.
- Ductile materials are those that can undergo large plastic deformation before fracture. They have a high toughness and can absorb a lot of energy. Examples of ductile materials are metals, polymers, and some ceramics.
- Brittle materials are those that fracture with little or no plastic deformation. They have a low toughness and can absorb very little energy. Examples of brittle materials are glass, concrete, and some ceramics.
- The stress-strain diagram for a ductile material typically has the following features:

  - A linear elastic region, where the stress is proportional to the strain and the material obeys Hooke's law. The slope of this region is the modulus of elasticity or Young's modulus, which measures the stiffness of the material.
  - A nonlinear elastic region, where the stress is still reversible but not proportional to the strain. The material exhibits some nonlinear elasticity or anelasticity.
  - A yield point, where the stress reaches a critical value and the material starts to deform plastically. The yield strength is the stress at the yield point.
  - A plastic region, where the stress increases with increasing strain but the material undergoes permanent deformation. The material exhibits plasticity or viscoplasticity.
  - A strain hardening region, where the stress increases at a faster rate than the strain due to the increase in dislocation density and the resistance to plastic deformation. The material exhibits strain hardening or work hardening.
  - An ultimate point, where the stress reaches a maximum value and the material can no longer sustain the load. The ultimate strength is the stress at the ultimate point.
  - A necking region, where the cross-sectional area of the material decreases due to the localized plastic deformation. The stress decreases with increasing strain due to the decrease in load-bearing capacity. The material exhibits necking or instability.
  - A fracture point, where the material breaks or ruptures. The fracture strength is the stress at the fracture point.

- The stress-strain diagram for a brittle material typically has the following features:

  - A linear elastic region, where the stress is proportional to the strain and the material obeys Hooke's law. The slope of this region is the modulus of elasticity or Young's modulus, which measures the stiffness of the material.
  - A fracture point, where the material breaks or ruptures with little or no plastic deformation. The fracture strength is the stress at the fracture point.
