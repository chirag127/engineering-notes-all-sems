### Dynamic and Kinematic Viscosity

- Viscosity is a property of fluids that measures their resistance to flow. It is influenced by the intermolecular forces, temperature, and pressure of the fluid.
- There are two types of viscosity: dynamic and kinematic.
- Dynamic viscosity, also known as absolute viscosity, evaluates the internal resistance of a fluid to flow. It is defined as the ratio of the shear stress to the shear rate of the fluid. It is a measure of the force required to make the fluid flow at a certain rate. The SI unit of dynamic viscosity is Pascal-second (Pa-s) or milliPascal-second (mPa-s).  
- Kinematic viscosity describes the ratio of dynamic viscosity to density. It is defined as the ratio of the fluid's momentum diffusivity to its thermal diffusivity. It is a measure of how fast the fluid moves when a certain force is applied. The SI unit of kinematic viscosity is square meter per second (m2/s) or centimeter square per second (cm2/s).  
- Two fluids with the same value of dynamic viscosity can have different values of kinematic viscosity based on their density and vice versa. For example, water and glycerin have similar dynamic viscosity at room temperature, but water has lower density and higher kinematic viscosity than glycerin. Conversely, air and honey have similar kinematic viscosity at room temperature, but air has lower density and higher dynamic viscosity than honey. 
- Some examples of fluids with high dynamic viscosity are honey, molasses, motor oil, and tar. Some examples of fluids with low dynamic viscosity are water, gasoline, alcohol, and air. Some examples of fluids with high kinematic viscosity are honey, glycerin, and motor oil. Some examples of fluids with low kinematic viscosity are water, air, and gasoline.  
- The viscosity of fluids changes with temperature and pressure. Generally, the viscosity of liquids decreases with increasing temperature and pressure, while the viscosity of gases increases with increasing temperature and pressure. This is because the intermolecular forces in liquids are stronger than in gases, and they are weakened by higher temperature and pressure. Conversely, the molecular collisions in gases are more frequent and energetic at higher temperature and pressure, which increases their resistance to flow.
