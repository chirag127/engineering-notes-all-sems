### Systematic and binomial system of nomenclature

- Living organisms are classified into a hierarchy of groups based on similarities and differences. This scientific procedure of naming and classifying organisms is known as taxonomy or systematics.
- The modern taxonomic system was introduced by Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus in the 1700s. He introduced a two-word naming system called binomial nomenclature.
- Each organism is assigned a two-part scientific name:
    - Genus: The first word in the name refers to the genus, which is a group of closely related species. It is always capitalized.
    - Species: The second word refers to the species, which is a subset of the genus and represents organisms that can breed and produce fertile offspring. It is never capitalized.
- For example, humans are classified as:
    - Kingdom: Animalia
    - Phylum: Chordata
    - Class: Mammalia
    - Order: Primates
    - Family: Hominidae
    - Genus: Homo
    - Species: sapiens
- The binomial system allows scientists worldwide to communicate unambiguously about organisms. It indicates evolutionary relationships between organisms.
- The modern taxonomic system arranges species into a hierarchy of groups based on shared characteristics: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. This is known as the taxonomic hierarchy or Linnaean taxonomy.
- The five kingdom classification system groups all living organisms into five broad kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia. These kingdoms are based on fundamental differences in cellular organization, body structure, methods of nutrition, and reproduction.
