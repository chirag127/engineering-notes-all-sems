### Major Groups for the Notes of the Unit 3 - Classification of Living Organisms

- Classification of living organisms is the process of grouping them based on their similarities and differences in various aspects such as morphology, anatomy, physiology, genetics, etc.
- Classification helps to organize the diversity of life, to understand the evolutionary relationships among different groups of organisms, and to provide a universal system of nomenclature for naming and identifying them.
- There are different systems of classification, but one of the most widely accepted and commonly used is the five kingdom classification proposed by Robert H. Whittaker in 1969  .
- The five kingdom classification is based on the following criteria:
  - Cell structure: whether the cells are prokaryotic (lacking a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles) or eukaryotic (having a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles).
  - Mode of nutrition: whether the organisms are autotrophic (capable of synthesizing their own organic molecules from inorganic sources using light or chemical energy) or heterotrophic (dependent on organic molecules from other sources for their nutrition).
  - Thallus organization: whether the organisms are unicellular (consisting of a single cell) or multicellular (consisting of many cells), and whether the multicellular organisms are simple (lacking differentiation of tissues and organs) or complex (having differentiation of tissues and organs).
  - Phylogenetic relationships: whether the organisms are primitive (having a long evolutionary history and few derived features) or advanced (having a short evolutionary history and many derived features).
  - Reproduction: whether the organisms reproduce asexually (without the fusion of gametes) or sexually (with the fusion of gametes), and whether the sexual reproduction involves alternation of generations (a life cycle with two distinct multicellular phases, one haploid and one diploid) or not.

- The five kingdoms are:
  - Kingdom Monera: It includes all the prokaryotic organisms, such as bacteria and cyanobacteria (blue-green algae). They are unicellular, autotrophic or heterotrophic, and reproduce asexually by binary fission or budding. They are the most primitive and diverse group of organisms, and can be found in almost all habitats on earth. Some of them are beneficial, such as nitrogen-fixing bacteria and decomposers, while some of them are harmful, such as pathogens and parasites.
  - Kingdom Protista: It includes all the eukaryotic organisms that are not classified as fungi, plants, or animals. They are mostly unicellular, but some are multicellular or colonial. They are autotrophic or heterotrophic, and reproduce asexually or sexually. They are the most diverse group of eukaryotes, and can be found in aquatic and moist terrestrial habitats. Some of the major groups of protists are:
    - Chrysophytes: They are golden algae (desmids) and diatoms. They are mostly unicellular, autotrophic, and have silica in their cell walls. They are found in freshwater and marine environments, and are important producers of organic matter and oxygen.
    - Dinoflagellates: They are mostly unicellular, autotrophic, and have two flagella for locomotion. They are found in marine and freshwater environments, and some of them can produce bioluminescence and toxins. They are also involved in the formation of red tides, which are harmful algal blooms that affect marine life and human health.
    - Euglenoids: They are mostly unicellular, autotrophic or heterotrophic, and have one or two flagella for locomotion. They are found in freshwater and marine environments, and some of them can change their shape by contracting and expanding their cell membrane. They have a light-sensitive organelle called the eyespot, which helps them to orient themselves towards the light source.
    - Slime molds: They are multicellular or colonial, heterotrophic, and have a complex life cycle involving a plasmodial stage (a mass of cytoplasm with many nuclei) and a sporangial stage (a structure that produces spores). They are found in moist terrestrial habitats, and feed on decaying organic matter. They are also known as acellular or cellular fungi, but they are not true fungi.
