### Concept of animal and plant classification

- Biological classification is the scientific process of grouping living organisms into categories based on their similarities and differences.
- Classification helps to organize the diversity of life and to understand the evolutionary relationships among different groups of organisms.
- The most widely used system of classification is the Linnaean taxonomy, which was developed by the Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus in the 18th century.
- Linnaeus classified all living organisms into two kingdoms – Plantae (plants) and Animalia (animals) – based on their morphology and anatomy.
- He also introduced the binomial system of nomenclature, which assigns a two-part scientific name to each species, consisting of the genus name and the specific epithet (e.g. Homo sapiens, Panthera leo).
- The Linnaean taxonomy has seven hierarchical levels of classification: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. Each level is called a taxon (plural taxa) and represents a group of organisms that share common characteristics.
- The Linnaean taxonomy is also called the rank-based system, because each taxon has a fixed rank and a fixed name. However, this system has some limitations, such as:
  - It does not reflect the evolutionary history and phylogenetic relationships of organisms, which are based on genetic and molecular data.
  - It does not account for the diversity and complexity of life, especially at the lower levels of classification, such as bacteria, archaea, and protists.
  - It does not accommodate the discovery of new species and the revision of existing taxa, which may require changes in the names and ranks of taxa.
- To overcome these limitations, a more modern and dynamic system of classification is the phylogenetic system, which is based on the cladistic approach.
- Cladistics is the study of the evolutionary history and relationships of organisms, using shared derived characters (synapomorphies) as evidence.
- A cladogram is a branching diagram that shows the evolutionary relationships among different groups of organisms, based on their common ancestry and divergence.
- A clade is a group of organisms that share a common ancestor and all its descendants. A clade is also called a monophyletic group, and it is the basic unit of classification in the phylogenetic system.
- The phylogenetic system does not use fixed ranks or names for taxa, but rather uses clade names that reflect their evolutionary history and relationships. For example, the clade name Mammalia refers to the group of animals that share the derived character of mammary glands, and it includes all the living and extinct mammals.
- The phylogenetic system is also called the rank-free system, because each clade can have any rank and any name, depending on the level of resolution and the context of the study.
- The phylogenetic system is more flexible and informative than the rank-based system, because it can accommodate new discoveries and revisions, and it can show the degree of relatedness and divergence among different groups of organisms.
- However, the phylogenetic system also has some challenges, such as:
  - It requires more data and analysis to construct reliable and robust cladograms, which may not always be available or consistent.
  - It may not be compatible with the existing nomenclature and classification of organisms, which may cause confusion and controversy among different users and stakeholders.
  - It may not be practical or convenient to use for some purposes, such as identification, communication, and education, which may require simpler and more stable names and categories.

- Therefore, both the rank-based and the rank-free systems of classification have their advantages and disadvantages, and they may complement each other in different situations and applications.
- The most recent and comprehensive system of classification of living organisms is the five kingdom classification, which was proposed by the American biologist Robert Whittaker in 1969.
- Whittaker recognized five major kingdoms of life, based on their cellular structure, mode of nutrition, and phylogenetic relationships. These are:
  - Monera: The kingdom of prokaryotic organisms, such as bacteria and archaea, which lack a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. They are unicellular and have diverse modes of nutrition, such as photosynthesis, chemosynthesis, and heterotrophy. They are the most ancient and abundant group of organisms on Earth, and they play important roles in the cycling of matter and energy in the biosphere.
