## Transformation of Sentences

- A sentence is a group of words that conveys a complete meaning.
- A sentence can be classified into three types based on its structure: simple, compound and complex.
- A simple sentence consists of one independent clause that expresses a complete thought. For example, "She sings well."
- A compound sentence consists of two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon (;). For example, "She sings well and plays the guitar."
- A complex sentence consists of one independent clause and one or more dependent clauses. A dependent clause is a group of words that cannot stand alone as a complete sentence. It is usually introduced by a subordinating conjunction (such as because, although, when, etc.) or a relative pronoun (such as who, which, that, etc.). For example, "She sings well because she practices every day."
- A sentence can be transformed from one type to another by adding or removing clauses, conjunctions, or punctuation marks. For example, "She sings well because she practices every day." can be transformed into a simple sentence by removing the dependent clause: "She sings well." It can also be transformed into a compound sentence by replacing the subordinating conjunction with a coordinating conjunction: "She practices every day, so she sings well."

## Subject-verb agreement

- Subject-verb agreement is the rule that the verb in a sentence must agree with the subject in number (singular or plural) and person (first, second, or third). For example, "He sings well." (singular subject, singular verb) and "They sing well." (plural subject, plural verb).
- Some common rules for subject-verb agreement are:
  - The verb agrees with the subject, not the complement. For example, "The singer is a woman." (singular subject, singular verb) and "The singers are women." (plural subject, plural verb).
  - The verb agrees with the subject closest to it when the subject is a compound noun joined by and. For example, "The singer and the guitarist are friends." (plural subject, plural verb) and "The singer and her friend is coming." (singular subject, singular verb).
  - The verb agrees with the singular subject when the subject is a compound noun joined by or, nor, either...or, or neither...nor. For example, "Neither the singer nor the guitarist is available." (singular subject, singular verb) and "Either the singer or the guitarist is coming." (singular subject, singular verb).
  - The verb agrees with the plural subject when the subject is a collective noun that refers to a group of individuals. For example, "The team is practicing." (singular subject, singular verb) and "The team are arguing." (plural subject, plural verb).
  - The verb agrees with the singular subject when the subject is an indefinite pronoun that refers to a singular entity. For example, "Someone is calling." (singular subject, singular verb) and "Nobody is here." (singular subject, singular verb).
  - The verb agrees with the plural subject when the subject is an indefinite pronoun that refers to a plural entity. For example, "Some are ready." (plural subject, plural verb) and "Many are coming." (plural subject, plural verb).

## Prefix and Suffix

- A prefix is a word part that is added to the beginning of a word to change its meaning. For example, the prefix un- means not, so adding un- to the word happy makes unhappy, which means not happy.
- A suffix is a word part that is added to the end of a word to change its meaning. For example, the suffix -ful means full of, so adding -ful to the word joy makes joyful, which means full of joy.
