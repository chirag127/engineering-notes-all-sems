# Stereochemistry

Stereochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms and molecules and the effect of this on chemical reactions. Stereochemistry focuses on the relationships between stereoisomers, which are molecules that have the same molecular formula and sequence of bonded atoms, but differ in the orientation of their atoms in space.

Some of the main topics in stereochemistry are:

- **Chirality**: The property of a molecule that makes it non-superimposable on its mirror image. A molecule that has chirality is called a chiral molecule, and its mirror image is called an enantiomer. Chiral molecules usually have one or more asymmetric centers, which are atoms that are bonded to four different groups. For example, the carbon atom in 2-butanol is an asymmetric center, and the molecule has two enantiomers: (R)-2-butanol and (S)-2-butanol.

- **Optical activity**: The ability of a chiral molecule to rotate the plane of polarized light. The direction and degree of rotation depend on the structure and configuration of the molecule, as well as the wavelength and temperature of the light. The specific rotation of a molecule is a constant that can be used to identify and quantify it. Optical activity is measured by a device called a polarimeter.

- **Stereoisomerism**: The classification of stereoisomers based on their structural differences. There are two main types of stereoisomerism: configurational and conformational. Configurational stereoisomers are molecules that have different spatial arrangements of their atoms and cannot be interconverted by rotation around single bonds. They include enantiomers and diastereomers, which are stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other. Conformational stereoisomers are molecules that have different spatial arrangements of their atoms due to rotation around single bonds. They include eclipsed, staggered, gauche, and anti conformations.

- **Stereochemical notation**: The methods and symbols used to represent the three-dimensional structure and configuration of molecules. Some of the common stereochemical notations are:

  - **Fischer projection**: A two-dimensional representation of a molecule that shows the asymmetric centers as points of intersection of horizontal and vertical lines. The horizontal lines indicate bonds that project out of the plane of the paper, and the vertical lines indicate bonds that project into the plane of the paper. The configuration of the asymmetric centers is determined by the priority of the groups attached to them, according to the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules.

  - **Newman projection**: A two-dimensional representation of a molecule that shows the conformation of a single bond as viewed along its axis. The front atom is represented by a point, and the back atom is represented by a circle. The bonds attached to the front and back atoms are shown as lines radiating from the point and the circle, respectively. The angle between the bonds is called the dihedral angle, and it determines the type of conformation.

  - **Sawhorse projection**: A two-dimensional representation of a molecule that shows the conformation of a single bond as viewed from an oblique angle. The front and back atoms are shown as wedges, and the bonds attached to them are shown as lines. The angle between the bonds is also the dihedral angle, and it determines the type of conformation.

  - **Wedge-and-dash notation**: A two-dimensional representation of a molecule that shows the three-dimensional arrangement of the atoms and bonds. The bonds that are in the plane of the paper are shown as solid lines, the bonds that project out of the plane of the paper are shown as wedges, and the bonds that project into the plane of the paper are shown as dashed lines. The configuration of the asymmetric centers is determined by the priority of the groups attached to them, according to the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules.

  - **R/S notation**: A notation that assigns an absolute configuration to an asymmetric center based on the priority of the groups attached to it, according to the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules. The priority of the groups is determined by their atomic number, and if there is a tie, by their substituents. The configuration is determined by the order of the groups in a clockwise or counterclockwise direction around the asymmetric center.
