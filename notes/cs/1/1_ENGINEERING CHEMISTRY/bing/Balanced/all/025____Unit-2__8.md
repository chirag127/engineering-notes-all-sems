## Unit-2: 8

- This unit covers the topic of **data structures and algorithms**.
- Data structures are ways of organizing and storing data in a computer memory, such as arrays, lists, stacks, queues, trees, graphs, etc.
- Algorithms are step-by-step procedures for solving a problem or performing a task, such as sorting, searching, traversing, etc.
- Data structures and algorithms are closely related, as the choice of data structure affects the efficiency and complexity of the algorithm, and vice versa.
- The main goals of data structures and algorithms are to **optimize the time and space** required for solving a problem, as well as the **correctness and robustness** of the solution.
- Some common concepts and techniques in data structures and algorithms are:
  - **Abstract data types (ADTs)**: A way of defining the behavior and operations of a data structure without specifying its implementation details. Examples are stack, queue, set, map, etc.
  - **Recursion**: A way of solving a problem by breaking it down into smaller subproblems that have the same structure as the original problem, and then combining the solutions of the subproblems. Examples are factorial, Fibonacci, binary search, etc.
  - **Divide and conquer**: A way of solving a problem by dividing it into two or more subproblems that are easier to solve, and then combining the solutions of the subproblems. Examples are merge sort, quick sort, binary search tree, etc.
  - **Dynamic programming**: A way of solving a problem by storing and reusing the solutions of overlapping subproblems, instead of recomputing them. Examples are matrix chain multiplication, longest common subsequence, knapsack problem, etc.
  - **Greedy algorithm**: A way of solving a problem by making the locally optimal choice at each step, without considering the global consequences. Examples are activity selection, Huffman coding, Dijkstra's algorithm, etc.
  - **Backtracking**: A way of solving a problem by exploring all possible solutions and discarding the ones that do not satisfy the constraints. Examples are n-queens problem, sudoku, Hamiltonian cycle, etc.
  - **Branch and bound**: A way of solving a problem by pruning the search space based on some criteria, such as lower and upper bounds, heuristic functions, etc. Examples are travelling salesman problem, 0/1 knapsack problem, etc.
  - **Graph algorithms**: A way of solving problems that involve nodes and edges, such as finding the shortest path, the minimum spanning tree, the maximum flow, the connected components, etc. Examples are breadth-first search, depth-first search, Prim's algorithm, Kruskal's algorithm, Ford-Fulkerson algorithm, etc.
  - **Sorting algorithms**: A way of arranging the elements of a sequence in a specific order, such as ascending, descending, alphabetical, etc. Examples are bubble sort, insertion sort, selection sort, heap sort, merge sort, quick sort, radix sort, etc.
  - **Searching algorithms**: A way of finding an element or a set of elements that satisfy some criteria in a sequence or a data structure, such as linear search, binary search, interpolation search, hash table, binary search tree, etc.
  - **Complexity analysis**: A way of measuring the performance and efficiency of an algorithm in terms of the time and space required as a function of the input size. Examples are big-O notation, big-Omega notation, big-Theta notation, etc.
