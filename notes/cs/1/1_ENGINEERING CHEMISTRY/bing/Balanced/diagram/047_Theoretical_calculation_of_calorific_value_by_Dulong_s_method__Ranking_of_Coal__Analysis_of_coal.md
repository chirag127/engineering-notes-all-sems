# Theoretical calculation of calorific value by Dulong’s method, Ranking of Coal, Analysis of coal

## Calorific value of coal
- The calorific value of coal is the amount of heat released by the complete combustion of a unit mass of coal.
- It is usually expressed in kilojoules per kilogram (kJ/kg) or British thermal units per pound (Btu/lb).
- The calorific value of coal depends on its composition, moisture content, ash content, and volatile matter content.
- The calorific value of coal can be measured experimentally by using a bomb calorimeter or estimated theoretically by using empirical formulas based on the ultimate analysis of coal.

## Dulong's method
- Dulong's method is one of the empirical formulas for estimating the calorific value of coal from its ultimate analysis.
- The ultimate analysis of coal is the determination of the mass fractions of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, and ash in coal.
- Dulong's method assumes that the heat of combustion of an organic compound is nearly equal to the heats of combustion of the elements in it, multiplied by their percentage content in the compound.
- Dulong's formula for the higher calorific value (HCV) of coal is:

HCV = 337C + 1442(H - O/8) + 93S kJ/kg

where C, H, O, and S are the mass fractions of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and sulfur in coal, respectively.

- Dulong's formula for the lower calorific value (LCV) of coal is:

LCV = HCV - 22.6H - 5.85M kJ/kg

where H and M are the mass fractions of hydrogen and moisture in coal, respectively.

- Dulong's method is simple and convenient, but it has some limitations and sources of error, such as:
  - It does not account for the variation of the heat of combustion of carbon and hydrogen with coal rank and structure.
  - It does not account for the heat of formation of water vapor and sulfur dioxide from the combustion of hydrogen and sulfur in coal.
  - It does not account for the heat losses due to the incomplete combustion of carbon and hydrogen in coal.
  - It does not account for the heat losses due to the sensible heat of the combustion products.

## Ranking of coal
- The ranking of coal is the classification of coal according to its degree of metamorphism, or the change in physical and chemical properties due to the burial and heating of plant matter over geological time.
- The ranking of coal reflects its origin, composition, maturity, and potential uses.
- The ranking of coal is usually based on parameters such as moisture content, volatile matter content, fixed carbon content, calorific value, and carbon-hydrogen ratio.
- The ranking of coal can be divided into four main categories: lignite, subbituminous, bituminous, and anthracite.

### Lignite
- Lignite is the lowest rank of coal, formed from peat that has been subjected to low pressure and temperature.
- Lignite has a high moisture content (25-40%), a low calorific value (10-20 MJ/kg), and a low carbon-hydrogen ratio (0.8-1.2).
- Lignite is brown or black in color, soft and crumbly in texture, and has a high tendency to ignite spontaneously.
- Lignite is mainly used for power generation and gasification.

### Subbituminous
- Subbituminous is the second lowest rank of coal, formed from lignite that has been subjected to higher pressure and temperature.
- Subbituminous has a lower moisture content (15-30%), a higher calorific value (20-30 MJ/kg), and a higher carbon-hydrogen ratio (1.2-1.8) than lignite.
- Subbituminous is dark brown or black in color, soft and brittle in texture, and has a moderate tendency to ignite spontaneously.
- Subbituminous is mainly used for power generation and gasification.

### Bituminous
- Bituminous is the most common and widely used rank of coal, formed from subbituminous that has been subjected to higher pressure and temperature.
- Bituminous has a lower moisture content (5-15%), a higher calorific value (30-35 MJ/kg), and a higher carbon-hydrogen ratio (1.8-2.5) than subbituminous.
- Bituminous is black in color, hard and shiny in texture, and has a low tendency to ignite spontaneously.
