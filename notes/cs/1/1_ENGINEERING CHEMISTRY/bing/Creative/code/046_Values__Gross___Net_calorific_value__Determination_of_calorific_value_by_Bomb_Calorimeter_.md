# Values, Gross & Net calorific value, Determination of calorific value by Bomb Calorimeter

- Values are the numerical or quantitative expressions of the quality or quantity of something. For example, the value of a car is the price it can be sold for, the value of a fuel is the amount of heat it can produce, the value of a person is the worth they have in society, etc.
- Calorific value is a specific value of a fuel that measures the amount of heat or energy released when the fuel is completely burned. It is usually expressed in units of joules per kilogram (J/kg) or calories per gram (cal/g).
- Gross calorific value (GCV) is the total amount of heat or energy released by the fuel, including the latent heat of water vapour that is formed during combustion. It is also known as the higher heating value (HHV) or the upper heating value (UHV) of the fuel.
- Net calorific value (NCV) is the amount of heat or energy released by the fuel, excluding the latent heat of water vapour that is formed during combustion. It is also known as the lower heating value (LHV) or the lower heating value (LHV) of the fuel.
- The difference between GCV and NCV is the latent heat of water vapour that is either included or excluded in the calculation. The latent heat of water vapour is the amount of heat required to change water from liquid to gas or vice versa. It depends on the temperature and pressure of the water vapour. At 15.5°C and 1 atm, the latent heat of water vapour is 586 cal/g.
- The formula for calculating NCV from GCV is:

  NCV = GCV - 586 (water as moisture + water formed from H2) cal/g

  where water as moisture is the amount of water present in the fuel before combustion, and water formed from H2 is the amount of water produced from the hydrogen in the fuel during combustion.

- A bomb calorimeter is an instrument used to determine the GCV of a fuel by measuring the heat transferred to a known mass of water surrounding a sealed metal container (called the bomb) where the fuel is burned under high pressure and oxygen. The bomb calorimeter consists of the following parts:

  - A metal bomb with a lid that can be screwed tightly. The bomb has two electrodes connected to a fuse wire that ignites the fuel sample inside the bomb. The bomb is filled with pure oxygen at a pressure of about 25 atm.
  - A water jacket that encloses the bomb and contains a known mass of water. The water jacket has a stirrer to ensure uniform temperature distribution and a thermometer to measure the temperature change of the water.
  - A calorimeter vessel that encloses the water jacket and prevents heat loss to the surroundings. The calorimeter vessel has an insulating layer and an air gap to minimize heat transfer.
  - A control panel that supplies electric current to the fuse wire and records the temperature change of the water.

- The working principle of the bomb calorimeter is based on the law of conservation of energy, which states that the energy released by the fuel combustion is equal to the energy gained by the water and the bomb. The GCV of the fuel can be calculated by the following formula:

  GCV = (Qw + Qb) / m

  where Qw is the heat gained by the water, Qb is the heat gained by the bomb, and m is the mass of the fuel sample.

  Qw can be calculated by multiplying the mass of the water (Mw), the specific heat capacity of the water (Cw), and the temperature change of the water (Tw):

  Qw = Mw * Cw * Tw

  Qb can be calculated by multiplying the heat capacity of the bomb (Cb) and the temperature change of the water (Tw):

  Qb = Cb * Tw

  The heat capacity of the bomb (Cb) is a constant that depends on the material and mass of the bomb. It can be determined by calibrating the bomb calorimeter with a standard fuel of known GCV, such as benzoic acid.

  The temperature change of the water (Tw) is the difference between the final and initial temperatures of the water, measured before and after the fuel combustion.

  The mass of the fuel sample (m) is the weight of the fuel placed in the bomb, measured before the combustion.
