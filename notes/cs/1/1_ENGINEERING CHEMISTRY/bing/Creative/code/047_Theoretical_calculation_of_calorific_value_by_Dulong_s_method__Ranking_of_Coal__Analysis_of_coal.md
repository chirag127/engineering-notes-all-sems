## Theoretical calculation of calorific value by Dulong’s method

- The calorific value of a fuel is the amount of heat produced by its complete combustion per unit mass or volume.
- The theoretical calorific value of a fuel can be estimated from its ultimate analysis, which gives the mass fractions of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and sulfur in the fuel.
- Dulong’s formula is a widely used empirical equation that relates the theoretical calorific value of a fuel to its elemental composition.
- The formula is given by:

  Theoretical calorific value of fuel = 33800 C + 144500 (H2 – (O2/8)) + 9300 S kJ/kg

  Where C, H2, O2, and S represent the mass fractions of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and sulfur in the fuel, respectively.

- The formula assumes that all the carbon in the fuel is converted to carbon dioxide, all the hydrogen to water, all the sulfur to sulfur dioxide, and all the oxygen to water.
- The formula also assumes that the water formed by the combustion is in the liquid state, and that the heat of vaporization of water is 2442 kJ/kg.
- The formula is based on the average heats of combustion of the elements in organic compounds, which are:

  Heat of combustion of carbon = 33800 kJ/kg

  Heat of combustion of hydrogen = 144500 kJ/kg

  Heat of combustion of sulfur = 9300 kJ/kg

- The formula is valid for most solid and liquid fuels, but not for gaseous fuels, which have a higher calorific value due to the heat of vaporization of water being released in the combustion process.
- The formula is also not accurate for fuels that contain nitrogen, ash, or moisture, which lower the calorific value of the fuel.
- The formula is an approximation and does not account for the variations in the chemical structure and bonding of different fuels.

## Ranking of Coal

- Coal is a combustible sedimentary rock that is formed from the accumulation and compression of plant matter over millions of years.
- Coal is classified into different ranks based on its degree of metamorphism, which is influenced by the temperature, pressure, and time of burial of the coal.
- The rank of coal indicates its quality, maturity, and suitability for various applications.
- The main ranks of coal are:

  - Peat: The lowest rank of coal, which is not technically a coal, but a partially decomposed plant matter that has a high moisture content and a low calorific value. Peat is used as a fuel and a soil conditioner.
  - Lignite: The lowest rank of true coal, which is brownish-black in color and has a high moisture content and a low calorific value. Lignite is used as a fuel for power generation and as a raw material for synthetic gas and chemicals.
  - Sub-bituminous: A rank of coal that is black in color and has a lower moisture content and a higher calorific value than lignite. Sub-bituminous coal is used as a fuel for power generation and as a raw material for synthetic gas and chemicals.
  - Bituminous: A rank of coal that is black in color and has a low moisture content and a high calorific value. Bituminous coal is the most abundant and widely used rank of coal. It is used as a fuel for power generation, industrial processes, and domestic heating. It is also used as a raw material for coke, metallurgy, and chemicals.
  - Anthracite: The highest rank of coal, which is hard, glossy, and black in color. It has a very low moisture content and a very high calorific value. Anthracite is the most pure and mature form of coal. It is used as a fuel for power generation, industrial processes, and domestic heating. It is also used as a filter media and a reductant in metallurgy.

  - Fixed carbon: The percentage of carbon in the coal that remains after the volatile matter is driven off by heating. Fixed carbon is an indicator of the heating value and combustion efficiency of the coal.
