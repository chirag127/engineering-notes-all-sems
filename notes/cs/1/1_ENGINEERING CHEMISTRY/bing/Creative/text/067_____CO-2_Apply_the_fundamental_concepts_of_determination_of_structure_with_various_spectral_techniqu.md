### CO-2 Apply the fundamental concepts of determination of structure with various spectral techniques

- Spectroscopy is the study of how electromagnetic radiation, across a spectrum of different wavelengths, interacts with molecules - and how these interactions can be quantified, analyzed, and ultimately interpreted to gain information about molecular structure.
- The most important spectroscopic techniques for structure determination are ultraviolet and visible spectroscopy, infrared spectroscopy, and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy.
- Ultraviolet and visible spectroscopy measures the absorption of light by molecules in the range of 200-800 nm. This absorption corresponds to the excitation of electrons from lower to higher energy levels. The absorption spectrum can provide information about the presence of conjugated pi systems, chromophores, and functional groups in the molecule.
- Infrared spectroscopy measures the absorption of light by molecules in the range of 2500-25,000 nm. This absorption corresponds to the vibration of bonds and groups in the molecule. The infrared spectrum can provide information about the types and numbers of bonds and functional groups in the molecule, as well as their relative positions and orientations.
- Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy measures the absorption of radiofrequency waves by nuclei in a magnetic field. This absorption corresponds to the spin transitions of nuclei from lower to higher energy levels. The nuclear magnetic resonance spectrum can provide information about the number and types of atoms in the molecule, their relative positions and environments, and their connectivity and stereochemistry.
- Each spectroscopic technique has its own advantages and limitations, and they can be combined to give complementary and overlapping information about the structure of a molecule. For example, ultraviolet and visible spectroscopy can identify the presence of a benzene ring, but cannot distinguish between different isomers of benzene. Infrared spectroscopy can identify the presence of a carbonyl group, but cannot distinguish between different types of carbonyl compounds. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy can identify the number and types of carbon and hydrogen atoms, but cannot detect atoms with zero or even nuclear spin, such as oxygen and nitrogen.
- To apply the fundamental concepts of determination of structure with various spectral techniques, one should follow a systematic approach that involves the following steps:
  - Obtain and analyze the molecular formula of the compound, if available, and calculate the degrees of unsaturation.
  - Obtain and analyze the ultraviolet and visible spectrum of the compound, if available, and identify the presence of conjugated pi systems, chromophores, and functional groups.
  - Obtain and analyze the infrared spectrum of the compound, and identify the types and numbers of bonds and functional groups.
  - Obtain and analyze the nuclear magnetic resonance spectrum of the compound, and identify the number and types of atoms, their relative positions and environments, and their connectivity and stereochemistry.
  - Compare and integrate the information from different spectroscopic techniques, and propose a possible structure for the compound that is consistent with all the data.
  - Verify the proposed structure by comparing it with known structures or by performing additional experiments, such as mass spectrometry, X-ray crystallography, or electron microscopy.
