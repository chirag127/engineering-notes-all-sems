# Process, Determination of Hardness and Alkalinity, Numerical Problems

- **Process** is a series of actions or steps taken to achieve a desired result or outcome. In the context of water treatment, process refers to the methods and techniques used to purify water for various purposes, such as drinking, irrigation, industrial use, etc.
- **Hardness** is a measure of the concentration of dissolved calcium and magnesium ions in water. Hard water can cause scaling, corrosion, and reduced efficiency of boilers, pipes, and appliances. Hardness can be expressed in various units, such as milligrams per liter (mg/L) of calcium carbonate (CaCO3), grains per gallon (gpg), or degrees of hardness (dH).
- **Alkalinity** is a measure of the capacity of water to neutralize acids. Alkalinity is mainly due to the presence of bicarbonates, carbonates, and hydroxides in water. Alkalinity can also be expressed in various units, such as mg/L of CaCO3, meq/L, or dH.
- **Determination of Hardness and Alkalinity** can be done by various methods, such as titration, colorimetry, or ion exchange. One of the most common methods is the EDTA titration method, which involves adding a standard solution of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) to a water sample until a color change occurs, indicating the end point. The amount of EDTA used is proportional to the hardness or alkalinity of the water sample. The indicator used for hardness titration is Eriochrome Black T (EBT), which changes from red to blue when all the calcium and magnesium ions are complexed by EDTA. The indicator used for alkalinity titration is phenolphthalein, which changes from colorless to pink when the pH reaches 8.3, indicating the end point for bicarbonate alkalinity, and methyl orange, which changes from yellow to orange when the pH reaches 4.5, indicating the end point for total alkalinity.
- **Numerical Problems** are problems that require the application of mathematical formulas and calculations to solve. Some examples of numerical problems related to hardness and alkalinity are:

  - A water sample has a total hardness of 250 mg/L as CaCO3 and a calcium hardness of 150 mg/L as CaCO3. What is the magnesium hardness of the water sample?
    - Solution: Magnesium hardness = Total hardness - Calcium hardness
    - Magnesium hardness = 250 - 150 = 100 mg/L as CaCO3
  - A water sample has a total alkalinity of 300 mg/L as CaCO3 and a bicarbonate alkalinity of 200 mg/L as CaCO3. What is the carbonate alkalinity and the hydroxide alkalinity of the water sample?
    - Solution: Carbonate alkalinity = Total alkalinity - Bicarbonate alkalinity
    - Carbonate alkalinity = 300 - 200 = 100 mg/L as CaCO3
    - Hydroxide alkalinity = Carbonate alkalinity - 2 x Bicarbonate alkalinity
    - Hydroxide alkalinity = 100 - 2 x 200 = -300 mg/L as CaCO3
    - Since hydroxide alkalinity cannot be negative, it is assumed to be zero, and the excess carbonate alkalinity is converted to bicarbonate alkalinity.
    - Bicarbonate alkalinity = 200 + 300 = 500 mg/L as CaCO3
    - Carbonate alkalinity = 0 mg/L as CaCO3
    - Hydroxide alkalinity = 0 mg/L as CaCO3
  - A water sample requires 20 mL of 0.01 M EDTA to reach the end point with EBT indicator for hardness titration. What is the total hardness of the water sample in mg/L as CaCO3 and in dH?
    - Solution: Total hardness = Volume of EDTA x Molarity of EDTA x Equivalent weight of CaCO3 x 1000 / Volume of water sample
    - Equivalent weight of CaCO3 = Molecular weight of CaCO3 / 2 = 100 / 2 = 50
    - Assuming the volume of water sample is 100 mL, the total hardness is:
    - Total hardness = 20 x 0.01 x 50 x 1000 / 100 = 200 mg/L as CaCO3
    - To convert mg/L as CaCO3 to dH, divide by 17.8
    - Total hardness = 200 / 17.8 = 11.24 dH
