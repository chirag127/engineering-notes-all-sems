### Graphite and Fullerene; Introduction, Structure and Applications

- Graphite and fullerene are two forms of carbon that have different structures and properties.
- Graphite has a layered structure in which hexagonal rings of carbon atoms are connected to each other by covalent bonds. The layers are held together by weak van der Waals forces, which allow them to slide over each other. Graphite is a good conductor of electricity and heat, and has a high melting point and lubricating properties.
- Fullerene is a class of molecules that consist of carbon atoms arranged in hollow shapes, such as spheres, tubes, or ellipsoids. The most common and stable fullerene is C60, also known as buckminsterfullerene or buckyball, which has 60 carbon atoms in a spherical shape. Other fullerenes include C70, C76, C84, and nanotubes, which are cylindrical fullerenes.
- Fullerene has a similar structure to graphite, but it also has pentagonal or heptagonal rings that prevent the carbon sheet from being planar. Fullerene has high symmetry, stability, and reactivity, and can form complexes with other atoms or molecules. Fullerene is a semiconductor and can exhibit superconductivity, magnetism, and optical properties .
- Graphene is a single layer of graphite, which is one atom thick. It has a hexagonal lattice structure and is the thinnest and strongest material known. Graphene has exceptional electrical, thermal, mechanical, and optical properties, and is considered a potential material for various applications in electronics, composites, sensors, energy, and biotechnology .
- Some of the applications of graphite are:
  - As electrodes in batteries, fuel cells, and electrolysis
  - As lubricants and additives in polymers and metals
  - As pencils and paints
  - As moderators and reflectors in nuclear reactors
  - As catalysts and adsorbents
- Some of the applications of fullerene are:
  - As drug delivery agents and contrast agents in biomedicine
  - As photovoltaic devices and light-emitting diodes in solar cells and displays
  - As nanoscale transistors and switches in nanoelectronics
  - As hydrogen storage materials and catalysts in energy conversion and storage
  - As sensors and detectors for various analytes
