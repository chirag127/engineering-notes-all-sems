### Atomic and Molecular Structure: Molecular orbitals of diatomic molecules, Bond Order

Molecular orbitals are formed by the combination of atomic orbitals of two or more atoms. In diatomic molecules, the atomic orbitals of the two atoms combine to form molecular orbitals. These molecular orbitals can be bonding, anti-bonding, or non-bonding.

Bond order is a measure of the strength of a chemical bond. It is defined as the difference between the number of bonding electrons and the number of anti-bonding electrons, divided by two. A higher bond order indicates a stronger chemical bond.

In diatomic molecules, the bond order can be calculated using the molecular orbital diagram. The molecular orbitals are arranged in order of increasing energy, with the bonding orbitals lower in energy than the anti-bonding orbitals. The electrons are then placed in the molecular orbitals following the Aufbau principle, Hund's rule, and the Pauli exclusion principle.

For example, in the case of the oxygen molecule (O2), the bond order is 2. This is because there are 8 valence electrons in the oxygen molecule, which are distributed among the molecular orbitals as follows: 2 electrons in the sigma 2s bonding orbital, 2 electrons in the sigma 2s* anti-bonding orbital, 4 electrons in the two pi 2p bonding orbitals, and 0 electrons in the sigma 2p bonding orbital. The bond order is then calculated as (4-2)/2 = 2.

In summary, the molecular orbitals of diatomic molecules are formed by the combination of atomic orbitals, and the bond order is a measure of the strength of the chemical bond. The bond order can be calculated using the molecular orbital diagram and the distribution of electrons among the molecular orbitals. A higher bond order indicates a stronger chemical bond.
