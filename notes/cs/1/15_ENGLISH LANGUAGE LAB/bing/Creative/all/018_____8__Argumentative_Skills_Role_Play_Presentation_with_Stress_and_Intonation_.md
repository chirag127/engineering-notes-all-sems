# 8. Argumentative Skills/Role Play Presentation with Stress and Intonation

- Argumentative skills are the abilities to present and defend a position or opinion on an issue, using evidence and reasoning, in a clear and persuasive way.
- Role play presentation is a technique of simulating a real-life situation or scenario, where the participants act out different roles and perspectives, in order to practice or demonstrate their argumentative skills.
- Stress and intonation are the features of speech that convey the speaker's attitude, emotion, emphasis, and tone, by varying the loudness, pitch, and length of the words and sentences.
- Stress and intonation are important for argumentative skills and role play presentation, because they can help the speaker to:
  - express their confidence, conviction, and credibility
  - highlight the main points and arguments
  - show contrast, comparison, and concession
  - signal agreement, disagreement, or doubt
  - engage the audience and create rapport
  - convey sarcasm, irony, or humor

## How to use stress and intonation for argumentative skills and role play presentation

- To use stress and intonation effectively, the speaker should:
  - identify the purpose and audience of the argument or role play
  - plan and organize the content and structure of the argument or role play
  - choose the appropriate words and phrases to support the argument or role play
  - practice the argument or role play aloud, paying attention to the stress and intonation patterns
  - record and listen to the argument or role play, and evaluate the stress and intonation
  - revise and improve the argument or role play, based on the feedback and self-evaluation
- Some examples of stress and intonation patterns for argumentative skills and role play presentation are:
  - rising intonation at the end of a sentence or clause, to indicate a question, a request, or a suggestion
    - Example: "Do you agree with me?" "Can you please explain your point?" "How about we compromise?"
  - falling intonation at the end of a sentence or clause, to indicate a statement, a command, or a conclusion
    - Example: "I think you are wrong." "You should listen to me." "That's all I have to say."
  - rising-falling intonation at the end of a sentence or clause, to indicate surprise, disbelief, or sarcasm
    - Example: "You really think that?" "Are you kidding me?" "That's very convincing."
  - falling-rising intonation at the end of a sentence or clause, to indicate uncertainty, hesitation, or politeness
    - Example: "I'm not sure about that." "Maybe you have a point." "Thank you for your time."
  - contrastive stress on a word or phrase, to emphasize a difference, a contrast, or a correction
    - Example: "I said NO, not yes." "It's not easy, it's hard." "You are not right, you are wrong."
  - emphatic stress on a word or phrase, to highlight a key point, an argument, or a evidence
    - Example: "This is the MOST important issue." "You MUST consider the facts." "The EVIDENCE is clear."
