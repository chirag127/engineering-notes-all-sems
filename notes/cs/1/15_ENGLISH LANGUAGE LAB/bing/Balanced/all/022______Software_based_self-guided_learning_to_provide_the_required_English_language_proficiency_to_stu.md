# Software based self-guided learning to provide the required English language proficiency to students from an employability and career readiness standpoint.

- Software based self-guided learning is a method of acquiring English language skills through the use of interactive and adaptive software programs that provide feedback, guidance, and assessment to learners.
- The software should align to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), which is a standard for describing the levels of language proficiency in different domains (listening, reading, speaking, and writing).
- The software should deliver a CEFR level – B2 upon completion, which means that the learners can communicate effectively and independently in most situations, express opinions and arguments, and understand the main ideas of complex texts.
- The benefits of software based self-guided learning for English language proficiency are:
  - It allows learners to learn at their own pace, time, and place, and to customize their learning goals and preferences.
  - It provides learners with immediate and personalized feedback, which can enhance their motivation, confidence, and self-regulation.
  - It exposes learners to authentic and diverse language materials, such as texts, audio, video, and games, which can enrich their vocabulary, grammar, and cultural awareness.
  - It tracks and monitors learners' progress and performance, which can help them identify their strengths and weaknesses, and adjust their learning strategies accordingly.
  - It prepares learners for the demands of the globalized and digitalized world, where English is widely used as a lingua franca and a medium of communication and information.
- The challenges of software based self-guided learning for English language proficiency are:
  - It requires learners to have access to reliable and compatible devices, internet connection, and software programs, which may not be available or affordable for everyone.
  - It depends on learners' self-discipline, motivation, and autonomy, which may vary depending on their personality, learning style, and prior knowledge.
  - It may not provide learners with sufficient opportunities to practice their oral and interactive skills, such as speaking and listening, which are essential for effective communication and employability.
  - It may not address learners' individual needs, preferences, and difficulties, which may require human intervention and support, such as teachers, tutors, or peers.
  - It may not reflect the diversity and complexity of the real-world language use, which may involve different accents, dialects, registers, and contexts.
