### 10. Determination of chloride content in water sample.

- Chloride is one of the major anions found in natural waters. It originates from various sources, such as seawater intrusion, industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, and domestic sewage.
- Chloride can affect the taste, corrosion, and biological activity of water. Therefore, it is important to monitor its concentration in drinking water, wastewater, and surface water.
- The most common method for determining chloride content in water sample is the **argentometric method**. It is based on the precipitation of chloride ions with silver ions in the presence of a suitable indicator.
- The procedure for the argentometric method is as follows:

  - Take a known volume of water sample in a conical flask and add a few drops of potassium chromate indicator. The indicator will give a yellow color to the solution.
  - Titrate the solution with standard silver nitrate solution from a burette until a permanent brick-red precipitate of silver chromate appears. This indicates the end point of the titration.
  - Record the volume of silver nitrate solution used and calculate the chloride content in the water sample using the following formula:

    - Chloride content (mg/L) = (Volume of AgNO3 x Normality of AgNO3 x 35.45) / Volume of water sample

- The advantages of the argentometric method are:

  - It is simple, rapid, and accurate.
  - It does not require sophisticated instruments or reagents.
  - It can be applied to a wide range of chloride concentrations.

- The limitations of the argentometric method are:

  - It is not suitable for water samples that contain interfering substances, such as bromide, iodide, cyanide, sulfide, or thiocyanate. These substances can also precipitate with silver ions and cause errors in the results.
  - It is affected by the presence of carbonate and bicarbonate in the water sample. These substances can react with silver ions and form insoluble silver carbonate, which can mask the end point of the titration. To avoid this, the water sample should be acidified with nitric acid before the titration.
  - It requires a careful selection of the indicator. The indicator should have a sharp color change at the end point and should not react with silver ions or chloride ions. Potassium chromate is the most commonly used indicator, but it can also be replaced by other indicators, such as fluorescein, dichlorofluorescein, or ferricyanide.
