# Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the process of combining two or more atomic nuclei into a single, heavier nucleus, releasing a large amount of energy in the process. Nuclear fusion is the source of energy for the Sun and other stars.

Some of the main points to know about nuclear fusion are:

- Nuclear fusion requires high temperatures and pressures to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between positively charged nuclei. The conditions for fusion are achieved in the cores of stars, where the temperature is about 15 million degrees Celsius and the pressure is about 200 billion atmospheres.
- The most common fusion reaction in stars is the proton-proton chain, which converts four hydrogen nuclei (protons) into one helium nucleus (alpha particle), releasing two positrons, two neutrinos, and gamma rays. This reaction converts about 0.7% of the mass of the hydrogen into energy, according to Einstein's famous equation E = mc^2.
- Another fusion reaction that occurs in stars is the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen (CNO) cycle, which uses carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen as catalysts to fuse hydrogen into helium. This reaction is more efficient than the proton-proton chain, but requires higher temperatures and densities to operate. The CNO cycle dominates in stars that are more massive than the Sun.
- Nuclear fusion can also be achieved artificially in devices called fusion reactors, which use magnetic fields or lasers to confine and heat plasma, a state of matter where atoms are ionized and free to move. The most common fusion reaction in fusion reactors is the deuterium-tritium (D-T) reaction, which fuses a deuterium nucleus (one proton and one neutron) with a tritium nucleus (one proton and two neutrons) into a helium nucleus and a neutron, releasing a lot of energy. The D-T reaction is easier to achieve than other fusion reactions, because it has a lower temperature and pressure threshold, and a higher cross-section, which means a higher probability of the nuclei colliding and fusing.
- The main challenges of achieving nuclear fusion on Earth are:

  - Creating and maintaining the high temperatures and pressures needed for fusion, without losing too much energy to the surroundings.
  - Controlling the plasma instabilities and turbulence that can disrupt the fusion process and damage the reactor walls.
  - Handling the high-energy neutrons that are produced by the fusion reactions, which can cause radiation damage and activation of the reactor materials, and also need to be captured and used to generate electricity or breed more tritium fuel.
  - Developing materials and technologies that can withstand the harsh environment of a fusion reactor, and that are safe, reliable, and economical.
  - Solving the engineering and scientific problems that arise from scaling up the fusion reactor from a laboratory experiment to a commercial power plant.

- The main advantages of nuclear fusion as a source of energy are:

  - Nuclear fusion has a very high energy density, which means that a small amount of fuel can produce a large amount of energy. For example, one gram of deuterium-tritium fuel can produce about 90 million kilowatt-hours of energy, which is equivalent to the energy produced by burning 15 million kilograms of coal.
  - Nuclear fusion does not produce greenhouse gases or long-lived radioactive waste, which are major environmental concerns of fossil fuels and nuclear fission. The main byproducts of fusion are helium, which is harmless and useful, and neutrons, which can be used to generate electricity or breed more fuel. The radioactive waste from fusion is mainly the activated materials of the reactor, which have a relatively short half-life and can be recycled or disposed of safely.
  - Nuclear fusion has a virtually unlimited supply of fuel, which can be extracted from seawater or lunar soil. Deuterium is abundant in all water sources, and tritium can be produced from lithium, which is also widely available. Other potential fusion fuels, such as helium-3 or boron-11, are more scarce, but still more abundant than uranium or thorium, which are used for nuclear fission.
  - Nuclear fusion is inherently safe, because the fusion process is self-limiting and cannot run away or explode like a nuclear fission reactor. If the fusion reactor loses its confinement or cooling, the plasma will quickly cool down and stop the fusion reactions. There is no risk of a nuclear meltdown or a nuclear weapon proliferation from fusion.
