# Determination of Cell Conductance of a Solution

- Cell conductance of a solution is the reciprocal of its resistance, i.e. G = 1/R, where G is the conductance and R is the resistance.
- Cell conductance of a solution depends on the concentration, charge, and mobility of the ions present in the solution, as well as the geometry of the cell used to measure it.
- A cell used to measure the conductance of a solution is called a conductance cell. It consists of two electrodes immersed in the solution, connected to a source of alternating current and a device to measure the resistance, such as a Wheatstone bridge.
- The resistance of the solution between the electrodes is given by R = ρl/A, where ρ is the resistivity of the solution, l is the distance between the electrodes, and A is the area of cross-section of the electrodes.
- The cell constant, C, is defined as C = l/A, and it has the units of m^-1. The cell constant depends on the shape and size of the electrodes and the cell, and it can be determined by measuring the resistance of a solution of known conductivity, such as 0.0200 M KCl.
- The conductivity, κ, of a solution is the reciprocal of its resistivity, i.e. κ = 1/ρ, and it has the units of S m^-1 or Ω^-1 m^-1. The conductivity of a solution is proportional to its cell conductance, i.e. κ = GC, where G is the cell conductance and C is the cell constant.
- To determine the cell conductance of a solution, the following steps are followed:
  - Fill the conductance cell with the solution and connect it to the Wheatstone bridge and the alternating current source.
  - Adjust the Wheatstone bridge until the galvanometer shows zero deflection, indicating that the bridge is balanced.
  - Read the value of the resistance, R, from the bridge.
  - Calculate the cell conductance, G, by using the formula G = 1/R.
  - Calculate the conductivity, κ, by using the formula κ = GC, where C is the cell constant.
