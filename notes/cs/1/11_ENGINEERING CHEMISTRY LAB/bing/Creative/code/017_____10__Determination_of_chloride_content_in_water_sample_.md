### 10. Determination of chloride content in water sample

- Chloride is one of the major anions found in water and wastewater. It originates from natural sources such as seawater, salt deposits, and mineral springs, as well as from human activities such as the use of salt for deicing, agricultural runoff, industrial effluents, and domestic sewage.
- Chloride can affect the taste, corrosion, and biological activity of water. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a guideline value of 250 mg/L for chloride in drinking water, based on aesthetic considerations.
- Chloride can be determined by various methods, such as titration, colorimetry, ion chromatography, and ion-selective electrodes. One of the most common and simple methods is the Mohr method, which is a precipitation titration using silver nitrate as the titrant and potassium chromate as the indicator.
- The principle of the Mohr method is that silver nitrate reacts with chloride ions in a 1:1 ratio to form a white precipitate of silver chloride, according to the equation:

    AgNO<sub>3</sub> + Cl<sup>-</sup> → AgCl<sub>(s)</sub> + NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup>

- The endpoint of the titration is detected by the formation of a reddish-brown precipitate of silver chromate, which occurs when all the chloride ions have been consumed and excess silver ions react with chromate ions, according to the equation:

    2AgNO<sub>3</sub> + K<sub>2</sub>CrO<sub>4</sub> → Ag<sub>2</sub>CrO<sub>4</sub><sub>(s)</sub> + 2KNO<sub>3</sub>

- The Mohr method is applicable for water samples with chloride concentrations between 10 and 1000 mg/L. The sample should have a neutral or slightly alkaline pH (7-10) and should not contain interfering substances such as bromide, iodide, cyanide, sulfide, or thiocyanate, which can also precipitate with silver nitrate.

- The procedure for the Mohr method is as follows  :

    1. Take 50 mL of sample (V mL) and dilute to 100 mL with distilled water in a volumetric flask.
    2. If the sample is highly colored, add 3 mL of aluminum hydroxide and shake well, allow to settle, filter, wash and collect filtrate.
    3. Bring the sample pH to 7-8 by adding acid or alkali if necessary, using pH paper or a pH meter to check.
    4. Transfer 50 mL of the sample to a conical flask and add 1 mL of potassium chromate indicator (5% w/v solution).
    5. Titrate the solution against standard silver nitrate solution (0.01 or 0.02 N) until a reddish-brown precipitate is obtained. Record the volume of silver nitrate used (T mL).
    6. Repeat the titration with a blank (distilled water) and a standard (sodium chloride solution of known concentration) to check the accuracy and precision of the method.

- The chloride concentration in the sample can be calculated from the following formula:

    C = (T - B) x N x 35.45 x 1000 / V

    where C is the chloride concentration in mg/L, T is the volume of silver nitrate used for the sample in mL, B is the volume of silver nitrate used for the blank in mL, N is the normality of the silver nitrate solution, and V is the volume of the sample taken for titration in mL.

- The result can be reported as mg/L of chloride or meq/L of chloride by dividing by the equivalent weight of chloride (35.45).

- The sources of error and uncertainty in the Mohr method include:

    - Inaccurate preparation and standardization of the silver nitrate solution
    - Incomplete precipitation or co-precipitation of silver chloride
    - Imprecise measurement of the sample volume and the titrant volume
    - Incorrect pH adjustment of the sample
    - Interference from other anions or cations in the sample
    - Subjective detection of the endpoint color change
