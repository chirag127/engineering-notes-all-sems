# Calibration of Analytical Equipment and Apparatus

- Calibration is the process of verifying the accuracy and precision of an analytical instrument or device by comparing its measurements with a known standard of reference.
- Calibration ensures that the instrument or device is functioning properly and producing reliable and consistent results.
- Calibration is essential for maintaining the quality and validity of analytical data and for complying with regulatory requirements and standards.
- Calibration should be performed periodically, before and after each use, or whenever there is a change in the operating conditions or performance of the instrument or device.
- Calibration should be documented and recorded, and the calibration status should be indicated on the instrument or device.
- Calibration can be classified into two types: external and internal calibration.
  - External calibration involves using a separate device or material that has a known value or property to calibrate the instrument or device. For example, using a thermometer to calibrate a temperature sensor, or using a certified reference material to calibrate a spectrophotometer.
  - Internal calibration involves using a built-in feature or function of the instrument or device to calibrate itself. For example, using a zero-adjustment knob to calibrate a balance, or using a self-test function to calibrate a pH meter.
- Calibration can also be classified into two levels: single-point and multi-point calibration.
  - Single-point calibration involves using only one standard value or property to calibrate the instrument or device. For example, using a pure water sample to calibrate a conductivity meter, or using a blank solution to calibrate a colorimeter.
  - Multi-point calibration involves using two or more standard values or properties to calibrate the instrument or device. For example, using a series of standard solutions to calibrate a titrator, or using a calibration curve to calibrate a chromatograph.
- Calibration can be affected by various factors, such as environmental conditions, human errors, instrument drift, wear and tear, and contamination. Therefore, calibration should be performed under controlled and consistent conditions, following standard operating procedures, and using appropriate and traceable standards and methods.
