### Viscosity
- Viscosity is a measure of the resistance of a fluid to deformation under shear stress. It is also known as the internal friction of a fluid.
- Viscosity depends on the temperature, pressure, and composition of the fluid. In general, viscosity decreases with increasing temperature and decreases with increasing pressure.
- Viscosity also depends on the molecular structure and interactions of the fluid. For example, water has a low viscosity because its molecules are small and have weak intermolecular forces. On the other hand, honey has a high viscosity because its molecules are large and have strong hydrogen bonds.
- Viscosity can be measured by various methods, such as capillary viscometer, rotational viscometer, or falling ball viscometer. The unit of viscosity is Pascal-second (Pa s) or milliPascal-second (mPa s).

### Conductance of solution
- Conductance of solution is a measure of the ability of a solution to conduct electric current. It is also known as the electrical conductivity of a solution.
- Conductance of solution depends on the concentration, temperature, and type of solute and solvent in the solution. In general, conductance increases with increasing concentration and temperature, and decreases with increasing viscosity.
- Conductance of solution also depends on the nature and mobility of the ions in the solution. For example, strong electrolytes, such as sodium chloride, have a high conductance because they dissociate completely into ions that can move freely in the solution. On the other hand, weak electrolytes, such as acetic acid, have a low conductance because they dissociate partially into ions that are less mobile in the solution.
- Conductance of solution can be measured by various methods, such as Wheatstone bridge, conductivity meter, or potentiometer. The unit of conductance is Siemens (S) or microSiemens (µS).

### Chloride and iron content in the water
- Chloride and iron are two common elements that can be found in natural or tap water. They can affect the quality and properties of the water, such as taste, odor, color, corrosion, and hardness.
- Chloride is a negatively charged ion that can originate from various sources, such as seawater, rock salt, road salt, or industrial waste. Chloride can increase the conductance and salinity of the water, and can also cause corrosion of metal pipes and fittings. The recommended limit for chloride in drinking water is 250 mg/L, according to the World Health Organization (WHO).
- Iron is a positively charged ion that can originate from various sources, such as soil, rocks, iron pipes, or groundwater. Iron can decrease the conductance and increase the hardness of the water, and can also cause staining of clothes, fixtures, and appliances. The recommended limit for iron in drinking water is 0.3 mg/L, according to the WHO.
- Chloride and iron content in the water can be measured by various methods, such as titration, colorimetry, spectrophotometry, or ion chromatography. The unit of chloride and iron content is milligram per liter (mg/L) or parts per million (ppm).
