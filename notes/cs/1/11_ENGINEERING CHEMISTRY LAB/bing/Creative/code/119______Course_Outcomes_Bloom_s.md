### Course Outcomes Bloom's Taxonomy

- Course outcomes are brief statements that describe what students will be expected to learn by the end of the course.
- Course outcomes should be aligned with the course content, activities, and assessments, and should reflect the level of cognitive skills that students need to demonstrate.
- Bloom's taxonomy is a model of cognitive skills that can be used to classify educational learning objectives and to design course outcomes .
- Bloom's taxonomy consists of six levels of cognition, from lower to higher: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, and create.
- Each level of cognition has a set of verbs that can be used to write course outcomes that indicate the expected level of learning .
- For example, at the remember level, verbs such as define, list, recall, and identify can be used to write course outcomes that require students to recall factual information .
- At the create level, verbs such as design, construct, produce, and synthesize can be used to write course outcomes that require students to generate new ideas or products .
- Below are some examples of course outcomes that use Bloom's taxonomy:

  - At the end of the course, students will be able to **describe** the colonization of the Americas by the British, French and Spanish (remember level).
  - At the end of the course, students will be able to **explain** the main concepts and theories of social psychology (understand level).
  - At the end of the course, students will be able to **apply** the principles of accounting to prepare financial statements (apply level).
  - At the end of the course, students will be able to **analyze** the outcomes of the Civil War (analyze level).
  - At the end of the course, students will be able to **evaluate** the strengths and weaknesses of different research methods (evaluate level).
  - At the end of the course, students will be able to **create** a portfolio of original artworks that demonstrate their artistic skills and vision (create level).

- Bloom's taxonomy can help instructors design courses that support student learning at multiple levels of cognition, and can help students monitor their own learning progress and goals .
