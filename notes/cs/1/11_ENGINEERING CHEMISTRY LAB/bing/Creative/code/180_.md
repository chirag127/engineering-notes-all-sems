# Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the process of combining two or more atomic nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy in the process. Nuclear fusion is the source of energy for the Sun and other stars.

Some of the main points to know about nuclear fusion are:

- Nuclear fusion requires high temperatures and pressures to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between positively charged nuclei. The conditions for fusion are achieved in the cores of stars, where the temperature is about 15 million degrees Celsius and the pressure is about 250 billion atmospheres.
- The most common fusion reaction in stars is the proton-proton chain, which converts four hydrogen nuclei (protons) into one helium nucleus (alpha particle), releasing two positrons, two neutrinos, and gamma rays. This reaction converts about 0.7% of the mass of the hydrogen into energy, according to Einstein's mass-energy equivalence formula E = mc^2.
- Another fusion reaction that occurs in stars is the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen (CNO) cycle, which uses carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen as catalysts to fuse hydrogen into helium. This reaction is more efficient than the proton-proton chain at higher temperatures and densities, and dominates in stars more massive than the Sun.
- The energy released by fusion reactions in stars is transported to the surface by radiation and convection, creating the luminosity and spectrum of the star. The fusion reactions also produce heavier elements, such as carbon, oxygen, and iron, through a series of nuclear reactions called nucleosynthesis. The heaviest elements, such as gold and uranium, are produced in supernova explosions, when massive stars collapse and explode at the end of their lives.
- Nuclear fusion can also be artificially induced on Earth, using devices such as tokamaks, stellarators, inertial confinement lasers, and Z-pinch machines. These devices aim to create and confine a plasma, a state of matter where the atoms are ionized and the nuclei and electrons are free to move. The plasma must be heated to temperatures of hundreds of millions of degrees Celsius and compressed to high densities to achieve fusion.
- The main challenge of achieving controlled fusion on Earth is to maintain the plasma in a stable and efficient state, without losing too much energy to the surroundings. The plasma must also be confined for a sufficient time to allow enough fusion reactions to occur. The ratio of the fusion power output to the input power is called the Q factor, and a Q factor greater than one means that the fusion device produces more energy than it consumes.
- The most promising fusion fuel for terrestrial applications is a mixture of deuterium and tritium, two isotopes of hydrogen. Deuterium is abundant in seawater, and tritium can be produced from lithium, which is also widely available. The deuterium-tritium fusion reaction produces one helium nucleus, one neutron, and 17.6 MeV of energy. The neutron carries most of the energy, and can be used to heat water and generate electricity, or to breed more tritium from lithium.
- Nuclear fusion has many potential advantages over other energy sources, such as fossil fuels, nuclear fission, and renewables. Fusion does not produce greenhouse gases or long-lived radioactive waste, and has virtually unlimited fuel supply. Fusion is also inherently safe, as any malfunction or disruption would stop the reaction. Fusion could provide clean, abundant, and reliable energy for the future. However, fusion also faces many technical, economic, and social challenges, such as achieving high Q factors, reducing costs, and gaining public acceptance.
