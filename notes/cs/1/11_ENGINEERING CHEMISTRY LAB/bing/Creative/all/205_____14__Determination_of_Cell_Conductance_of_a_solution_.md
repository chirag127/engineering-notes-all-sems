# 14. Determination of Cell Conductance of a solution

- Cell conductance is the measure of how well a solution can conduct electric current between two electrodes.
- Cell conductance depends on the concentration, type and mobility of the ions present in the solution, as well as the geometry and distance of the electrodes.
- Cell conductance is expressed in units of Siemens (S) or mho (℧), which are the reciprocal of ohms (Ω).
- Cell conductance can be determined by applying a known alternating voltage across the electrodes and measuring the resulting alternating current.
- Cell conductance can be calculated by using Ohm's law: G = I / V, where G is the cell conductance, I is the current and V is the voltage.
- Cell conductance can also be expressed as the product of the cell constant and the specific conductance of the solution: G = K * κ, where K is the cell constant and κ is the specific conductance.
- The cell constant is a factor that depends on the geometry and distance of the electrodes, and is usually determined by calibrating the cell with a standard solution of known conductance.
- The specific conductance is a property of the solution that depends on the concentration, type and mobility of the ions, and is usually expressed in units of S/cm or mS/cm.
- The specific conductance can be related to the equivalent conductance, which is the conductance of a solution containing one equivalent of the solute per liter of solution, by using the following formula: κ = Λ / c, where Λ is the equivalent conductance and c is the concentration in equivalents per liter.
- The equivalent conductance can be calculated by using the law of independent migration of ions, which states that the equivalent conductance of a solution is the sum of the equivalent conductances of the cations and anions: Λ = Λ+ + Λ-, where Λ+ is the equivalent conductance of the cations and Λ- is the equivalent conductance of the anions.
