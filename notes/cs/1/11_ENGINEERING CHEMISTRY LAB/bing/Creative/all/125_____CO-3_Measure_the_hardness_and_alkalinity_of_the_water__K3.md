# CO-3 Measure the hardness and alkalinity of the water. K3

- Hardness and alkalinity are two important parameters of water quality that indicate the concentration of dissolved minerals and bases in water respectively.
- Hardness is caused by compounds of calcium and magnesium, and by a variety of other metals, such as iron, manganese, and zinc. Hard water can cause scaling, corrosion, and reduced efficiency of water appliances and equipment.
- Alkalinity measures the total amount of bases present in water that can neutralize acids. Alkalinity is mainly due to the presence of bicarbonate, carbonate, and hydroxide ions in water. Alkalinity can affect the pH, buffering capacity, and biological activity of water.
- Hardness and alkalinity are related through common ions formed in aquatic systems. Specifically, the counter-ions associated with the bicarbonate and carbonate fraction of alkalinity are the principal cations responsible for hardness (usually Ca ++ and Mg ++) (Equations 3 and 4).
- Hardness and alkalinity can be measured by titration methods using standard solutions of acids and indicators. The following steps are involved in the measurement of hardness and alkalinity of water:
  - Collect a water sample in a clean container and measure its volume.
  - Add a few drops of an indicator, such as phenolphthalein or methyl orange, to the water sample and observe the color change.
  - If the water sample turns pink with phenolphthalein, it indicates the presence of carbonate and hydroxide alkalinity. Titrate the water sample with a standard solution of sulfuric acid (H2SO4) until the pink color disappears. Record the volume of acid used. This is the phenolphthalein alkalinity (P-alkalinity).
  - If the water sample turns yellow with methyl orange, it indicates the presence of bicarbonate alkalinity. Titrate the water sample with a standard solution of sulfuric acid (H2SO4) until the yellow color turns orange. Record the volume of acid used. This is the total alkalinity (T-alkalinity).
  - The carbonate alkalinity (C-alkalinity) can be calculated by subtracting the P-alkalinity from the T-alkalinity. The hydroxide alkalinity (OH-alkalinity) can be calculated by subtracting the C-alkalinity from the P-alkalinity.
  - To measure the hardness of water, add a few drops of an indicator, such as eriochrome black T (EBT), to the water sample and observe the color change. If the water sample turns wine red with EBT, it indicates the presence of calcium and magnesium ions. Titrate the water sample with a standard solution of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) until the wine red color turns blue. Record the volume of EDTA used. This is the total hardness (TH) of water.
  - The calcium hardness (CaH) and magnesium hardness (MgH) can be calculated by using separate indicators, such as murexide and calmagite, and titrating with EDTA. Alternatively, the calcium hardness can be calculated by subtracting the magnesium hardness from the total hardness.
  - The hardness and alkalinity of water can be expressed in different units, such as milligrams per liter (mg/L), parts per million (ppm), or grains per gallon (gpg) as calcium carbonate (CaCO3) equivalents. To convert from mg/L to ppm, multiply by 1. To convert from mg/L to gpg, divide by 17.1. To convert from ppm to gpg, divide by 17.1. To convert from gpg to mg/L, multiply by 17.1. To convert from gpg to ppm, multiply by 17.1.
