### The concept of direct manipulation for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction in the subject of Human Computer Interface

- Direct manipulation is an approach to interfaces which involves continuous representation of objects of interest together with rapid, reversible, and incremental actions and feedback.
- Direct manipulation is a style of Human Machine Interaction (HMI) design which features a natural representation of task objects and actions promoting the notion of people performing a task themselves (directly) not through an intermediary like a computer.
- Direct manipulation is one of the central concepts of graphical user interfaces (GUIs) and is sometimes equated with “what you see is what you get” (WYSIWYG). These interfaces combine menu-based interaction with physical actions such as dragging and dropping in order to help the user use the interface with minimal learning.
- Some examples of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - Desktop icons that can be moved, opened, or deleted by clicking or dragging.
  - Sliders, knobs, or dials that can be adjusted by dragging or clicking.
  - Maps or diagrams that can be zoomed, panned, or annotated by gestures or clicks.
  - Virtual reality or augmented reality environments that allow the user to interact with virtual objects using natural movements or devices.
- Some advantages of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - They are easy to learn and use, especially for novice users, as they rely on familiar metaphors and visual cues.
  - They are engaging and satisfying, as they provide immediate and continuous feedback and a sense of control and ownership.
  - They are efficient and expressive, as they allow the user to perform complex tasks with fewer steps and errors.
  - They are adaptable and flexible, as they can accommodate different user preferences, skills, and goals.
- Some disadvantages of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - They may require more screen space and graphical resources than other interfaces, which can affect performance and accessibility.
  - They may not support all types of tasks or domains, especially those that involve abstract or symbolic reasoning, or that require precise or structured input.
  - They may not provide enough guidance or feedback for some users, especially those who are unfamiliar with the interface or the task, or who need more explanation or confirmation.
  - They may not be consistent or compatible with other interfaces or platforms, which can cause confusion or frustration for the user.
