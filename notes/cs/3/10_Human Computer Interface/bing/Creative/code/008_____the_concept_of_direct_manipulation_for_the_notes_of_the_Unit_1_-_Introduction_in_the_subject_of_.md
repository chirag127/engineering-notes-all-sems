### The concept of direct manipulation for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction in the subject of Human Computer Interface

- Direct manipulation is an approach to interfaces which involves continuous representation of objects of interest together with rapid, reversible, and incremental actions and feedback.
- Direct manipulation is a style of Human Machine Interaction (HMI) design which features a natural representation of task objects and actions promoting the notion of people performing a task themselves (directly) not through an intermediary like a computer.
- Direct manipulation is one of the central concepts of graphical user interfaces (GUIs) and is sometimes equated with “what you see is what you get” (WYSIWYG). These interfaces combine menu-based interaction with physical actions such as dragging and dropping in order to help the user use the interface with minimal learning.
- Some examples of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - Desktop metaphor: The user can manipulate files and folders by dragging and dropping them on the screen, as if they were physical objects on a desk.
  - Touchscreen: The user can directly touch and manipulate graphical elements on the screen, such as icons, buttons, sliders, etc.
  - Virtual reality: The user can interact with a simulated environment using gestures, movements, and voice commands, as if they were present in the real world.
- Some advantages of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - They are intuitive and easy to learn, as they rely on natural and familiar actions and feedback.
  - They are engaging and satisfying, as they give the user a sense of control and involvement in the task.
  - They are efficient and flexible, as they allow the user to perform multiple actions in parallel and switch between them easily.
- Some disadvantages of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - They may not be suitable for complex or abstract tasks, as they may require too much screen space or cognitive load to represent all the objects and actions.
  - They may not be accessible or adaptable for users with different abilities, preferences, or contexts, as they may rely on specific input and output modalities that are not available or convenient for everyone.
  - They may not be consistent or compatible with other interfaces, as they may use different metaphors, conventions, or terminology that are not familiar or transferable to other systems.
