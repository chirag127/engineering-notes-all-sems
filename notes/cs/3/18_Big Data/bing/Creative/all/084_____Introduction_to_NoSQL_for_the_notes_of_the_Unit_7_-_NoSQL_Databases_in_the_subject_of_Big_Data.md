# Introduction to NoSQL

- NoSQL stands for **Not only SQL** or **Non-relational** .
- NoSQL is a type of **database management system (DBMS)** that is designed to handle and store large volumes of **unstructured and semi-structured data** .
- NoSQL databases use flexible data models that can adapt to changes in data structures and are capable of performing **lightning fast queries** .
- NoSQL databases have their roots in the **open source community** and have become more popular due to the demands of **Big Data**.
- NoSQL databases provide a variety of benefits including:
  - **Flexible data models**: NoSQL databases do not require a predefined schema to store data, unlike relational databases that use tables and columns. This allows for more flexibility and agility in handling complex and dynamic data.
  - **Horizontal scaling**: NoSQL databases can scale out by adding more nodes or servers to the cluster, rather than scaling up by upgrading the hardware of a single server. This enables NoSQL databases to handle large amounts of data and traffic with lower costs and higher availability.
  - **Lightning fast queries**: NoSQL databases can perform queries on data without the need for complex joins or transactions, as they store data in a way that is optimized for the application's needs. This results in faster and more efficient queries and operations.
  - **Ease of use for developers**: NoSQL databases provide simple and intuitive APIs and interfaces for developers to interact with the data, without the need for complex SQL statements or ORM layers. NoSQL databases also support various programming languages and frameworks, making them more accessible and convenient for developers.
- NoSQL databases come in a variety of types including:
  - **Document databases**: NoSQL databases that store data as documents, which are collections of key-value pairs. Each document can have a different structure and can contain nested data. Document databases are suitable for applications that deal with complex and hierarchical data, such as e-commerce, content management, or social media. Examples of document databases are MongoDB, CouchDB, and Firebase.
  - **Key-value databases**: NoSQL databases that store data as key-value pairs, where the key is a unique identifier and the value can be any type of data. Key-value databases are suitable for applications that need fast and simple access to data, such as caching, session management, or user preferences. Examples of key-value databases are Redis, Memcached, and DynamoDB.
  - **Wide-column stores**: NoSQL databases that store data as columns, which are collections of key-value pairs. Each column can have a different structure and can contain nested data. Wide-column stores are suitable for applications that need to perform analytics and queries on large and sparse data, such as web indexing, recommendation systems, or IoT. Examples of wide-column stores are Cassandra, HBase, and Bigtable.
  - **Graph databases**: NoSQL databases that store data as nodes and edges, which represent entities and relationships. Graph databases are suitable for applications that need to model and analyze complex networks and connections, such as social networks, fraud detection, or knowledge graphs. Examples of graph databases are Neo4j, OrientDB, and Amazon Neptune.
