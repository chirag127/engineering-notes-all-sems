### Internet Addressing

- Internet addressing is a scheme that allows users and applications to identify a specific network or host with which to communicate on the internet.
- An internet address works like a postal address, allowing data to be routed from the source to the destination.
- Each internet host is assigned at least one unique internet address, which is used by the Internet Protocol (IP) and other higher-level protocols.
- Each interface to an internet is assigned its own unique address, which may be different from the host address.
- Internet addresses are used to route packets through the network, which may involve multiple intermediate networks or autonomous systems (AS) .
- Internet addressing differs between IPv4 and IPv6, which are two versions of the IP protocol.
- IPv4 addresses are 32-bit numbers, divided into four octets, and written in dotted-decimal notation, such as 192.168.0.1.
- IPv6 addresses are 128-bit numbers, divided into eight groups of four hexadecimal digits, and written in colon-separated notation, such as 2001:db8::1.
- Each IP address is associated with virtual ports in a computer that act as a doorway that allow web applications or websites to send and receive data or information on your device.
- IP addresses can be either static or dynamic, depending on whether they are assigned permanently or temporarily to a host or interface.
- IP addresses can be either public or private, depending on whether they are globally unique and routable on the internet or reserved for internal use within a network.
- IP addresses can be either unicast, multicast, or broadcast, depending on whether they are used to send data to a single host, a group of hosts, or all hosts on a network.
- IP addresses can be either hierarchical or flat, depending on whether they are structured to reflect the network topology or assigned randomly.
- IP addresses can be either classful or classless, depending on whether they are divided into fixed-size classes or variable-length prefixes.
