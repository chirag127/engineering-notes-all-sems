### Concurrency Control for the notes of the Unit 5 - Real Time Operating Systems and Databases in the subject of Real Time System

- Concurrency control is the process of managing the access and modification of shared data resources by multiple concurrent processes or transactions in a system.
- Concurrency control is essential for ensuring the correctness and consistency of the data, as well as the timeliness and responsiveness of the system.
- Concurrency control is especially important for real-time systems (RTS), which are systems that respond to their environment within specified time constraints.
- RTS are inherently concurrent and typically manage shared data resources, such as sensors, actuators, memory, files, databases, etc.
- RTS require synchronization to ensure both logical and timing correctness. Logical correctness means that the data values and states are consistent with the system specifications and rules. Timing correctness means that the system meets its deadlines and guarantees its quality of service.
- Concurrency control techniques can be classified into two main categories: pessimistic and optimistic.
- Pessimistic concurrency control techniques prevent conflicts from occurring by locking or reserving the data resources before accessing or modifying them. Examples of pessimistic techniques are two-phase locking, timestamp ordering, priority inheritance protocol, etc.
- Optimistic concurrency control techniques allow conflicts to occur and then detect and resolve them after the fact. Examples of optimistic techniques are multiversion concurrency control, validation, compensation, etc.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into centralized and distributed, depending on whether there is a single or multiple authority that coordinates the access and modification of the data resources.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into static and dynamic, depending on whether the access and modification patterns are predetermined or decided at run time.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into serializable and non-serializable, depending on whether they preserve the equivalence of the concurrent execution to some serial execution or not.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into blocking and non-blocking, depending on whether they suspend or abort the processes or transactions that encounter conflicts or not.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into deadlock-free and deadlock-prone, depending on whether they avoid or allow the possibility of deadlock, which is a situation where a set of processes or transactions are waiting for each other indefinitely.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into starvation-free and starvation-prone, depending on whether they ensure or not that every process or transaction eventually gets access to the data resources it needs.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into fair and unfair, depending on whether they treat every process or transaction equally or not.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into simple and complex, depending on the amount of overhead and complexity they introduce to the system.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into general and specific, depending on whether they are applicable to any system or tailored to a particular system or application domain.
- Concurrency control techniques can also be classified into traditional and real-time, depending on whether they consider or not the timing constraints and deadlines of the processes or transactions.
- Concurrency control techniques for real-time systems have to balance the trade-offs between correctness, timeliness, responsiveness, and resource utilization. They have to cope with the challenges of dynamic and unpredictable workloads, heterogeneous and distributed architectures, and diverse and conflicting quality of service requirements.
- Concurrency control techniques for real-time systems have to satisfy the correctness criteria and performance metrics that are relevant and appropriate for the system and application domain. Some of the common correctness criteria are serializability, linearizability, causal consistency, eventual consistency, etc. Some of the common performance metrics are response time, throughput, deadline miss ratio, utilization, etc.
- Concurrency control techniques for real-time systems have to be evaluated and compared using rigorous and realistic methods, such as analytical models, simulation, experimentation, etc. They have to be validated and verified using formal and informal methods, such as proofs, testing, etc. They have to be implemented and integrated using efficient and reliable methods, such as algorithms, protocols, software, hardware, etc.
