### Harappan Technologies

- The Harappan civilization, also known as the Indus Valley Civilization, was a Bronze Age civilization that flourished in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent from about 2500 BCE to 1900 BCE.
- The Harappans developed various technologies and crafts, such as metallurgy, seal carving, pottery, jewelry, weights and measures, urban planning, drainage systems, and writing.
- Some of the notable features of Harappan technologies are:

  - Metallurgy: The Harappans used copper, bronze, lead, and tin to make tools, weapons, vessels, and ornaments. They knew how to alloy copper with tin to make bronze, which is harder and more durable than copper. They also knew how to cast metal using the lost-wax process, which involves making a wax model of the desired object, covering it with clay, heating it to melt the wax, and then pouring molten metal into the hollow mold.
  - Seal carving: The Harappans carved distinctive seals, usually made of steatite, a soft stone, with various motifs and inscriptions. The seals were used to stamp clay on trade goods, to identify property, and possibly to serve as amulets or tokens of authority. The motifs on the seals include animals, such as bulls, elephants, rhinoceroses, and unicorns, as well as human and divine figures. The inscriptions are in a script that has not been deciphered yet.
  - Pottery: The Harappans produced a variety of pottery, ranging from plain and utilitarian to painted and decorative. The pottery was made of fine clay, often mixed with sand or grit to increase its strength. The pottery was shaped by hand or on a wheel, and then fired in kilns. The painted pottery was decorated with geometric patterns, floral motifs, and animal designs, using colors such as black, red, and green.
  - Jewelry: The Harappans made jewelry from materials such as gold, silver, copper, bronze, ivory, shell, bone, and beads of stone, glass, and faience. The jewelry included necklaces, bracelets, earrings, rings, pendants, and brooches. The jewelry was often adorned with intricate designs, such as filigree, granulation, and inlay.
  - Weights and measures: The Harappans developed a system of standardized weights and measures, based on a decimal and binary system. The weights were made of stone or metal, and were shaped like cubes, spheres, or cones. The smallest weight was about 0.8 grams, and the largest was about 12.5 kilograms. The weights were used for trade and taxation purposes. The measures were based on units such as the angula (finger), the hasta (cubit), and the dhanus (bow). The measures were used for surveying, architecture, and engineering.
  - Urban planning: The Harappans built well-planned cities, with grid-like streets, fortified walls, and public and private buildings. The cities had a central citadel, where the administrative and religious structures were located, and a lower town, where the residential and commercial areas were situated. The cities also had sophisticated drainage systems, with covered sewers, wells, and baths. Some of the major cities of the Harappan civilization were Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Lothal, Kalibangan, and Dholavira.
  - Writing: The Harappans developed a system of writing, which is one of the oldest in the world. The writing consisted of about 400 signs, which were written from right to left, and sometimes in a boustrophedon style (alternating direction). The writing was used for inscribing seals, pottery, copper plates, and other objects. The writing has not been deciphered yet, and its language and content are unknown.
