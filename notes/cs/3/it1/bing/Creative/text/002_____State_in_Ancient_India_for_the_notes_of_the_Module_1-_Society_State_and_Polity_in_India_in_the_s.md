### State in Ancient India

- The concept of state in ancient India was not a fixed or uniform one. Different regions and periods had different forms and functions of state.
- The earliest evidence of state formation in India comes from the Harappan civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which had a centralized urban administration, standardized weights and measures, a uniform script, and a well-developed trade network.
- The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) saw the emergence of tribal polities called janapadas, which were ruled by kings (rajas) who performed sacrifices and rituals to maintain their authority and legitimacy. The kings were assisted by priests (brahmanas), warriors (ksatriyas), and commoners (vaisya and sudra).
- The later Vedic period (c. 1000-600 BCE) witnessed the expansion and consolidation of janapadas into larger territorial units called mahajanapadas, which competed for resources and dominance. Some of the prominent mahajanapadas were Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa, Avanti, and Gandhara.
- The sixth century BCE marked the beginning of the second urbanization in India, which was accompanied by the rise of heterodox religious movements such as Buddhism and Jainism, and the development of republican forms of government in some regions, such as the Sakyas, the Vrijis, and the Mallas.
- The Mauryan empire (c. 321-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian state, which was founded by Chandragupta Maurya with the help of his mentor Kautilya, who wrote the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft and polity. The Mauryan state was characterized by a centralized bureaucracy, a large army, a spy network, and a system of taxation and welfare.
- The post-Mauryan period (c. 200 BCE-300 CE) saw the fragmentation of the Mauryan empire into various regional kingdoms, such as the Sungas, the Kanvas, the Satavahanas, the Kushanas, and the Sakas. These kingdoms were influenced by various cultural and religious currents, such as Hellenism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Hinduism.
- The Gupta empire (c. 320-550 CE) is considered the classical age of Indian history, which witnessed the revival of Hinduism, the flourishing of arts, sciences, literature, and trade, and the emergence of the concept of a universal monarch (chakravartin). The Gupta state was based on a decentralized feudal system, where local rulers (samantas) owed allegiance and tribute to the emperor.
- The post-Gupta period (c. 550-1200 CE) was marked by the rise and fall of various regional powers, such as the Vakatakas, the Pallavas, the Chalukyas, the Rashtrakutas, the Pratiharas, the Palas, the Cholas, and the Chahamanas. These kingdoms developed their own distinctive styles of architecture, sculpture, painting, and literature, and also engaged in maritime trade and diplomacy with other regions.
- The medieval period (c. 1200-1700 CE) witnessed the invasion and establishment of Islamic rule in India, which had a profound impact on the political, social, cultural, and religious landscape of the subcontinent. The Delhi Sultanate (c. 1206-1526 CE) and the Mughal empire (c. 1526-1857 CE) were the two major Islamic states in India, which faced challenges from various Hindu and Sikh kingdoms, such as the Vijayanagara empire, the Maratha confederacy, and the Sikh empire.
- The modern period (c. 1700-1947 CE) saw the decline of the Mughal empire and the rise of the British colonial rule, which transformed the Indian state and society in various ways, such as the introduction of the modern education system, the railways, the telegraph, the census, the legal system, and the bureaucracy. The Indian state also witnessed the emergence of various nationalist and anti-colonial movements, such as the Indian National Congress, the Muslim League, the Indian National Army, and the Quit India Movement, which ultimately led to the independence and partition of India in 1947.
