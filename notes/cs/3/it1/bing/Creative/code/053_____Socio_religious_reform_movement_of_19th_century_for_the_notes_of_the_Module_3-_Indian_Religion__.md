### Socio-religious reform movement of 19th century

- The socio-religious reform movement of 19th century was a wave of reforming activities that aimed to modernize and rationalize the Indian society and religion in the light of western education and liberal ideas.
- The movement started in Bengal and soon spread to other parts of India, such as Bombay, Madras, Punjab, and Aligarh.
- The movement was influenced by various factors, such as:
  - The impact of British colonial rule, which exposed the Indians to new political, economic, and cultural challenges, and also created a sense of inferiority and humiliation among them.
  - The contact with western civilization, which introduced the Indians to new concepts of democracy, human rights, nationalism, science, and rationalism, and also exposed the flaws and superstitions of their own traditions and customs.
  - The rise of a new class of educated and enlightened Indians, who were influenced by the western ideas and values, and also felt the need to reform and revitalize their own society and religion.
  - The influence of various religious and social movements in Europe and America, such as the Protestant Reformation, the Enlightenment, the French Revolution, the Transcendentalism, and the Unitarianism, which inspired the Indian reformers to challenge the authority of the orthodox and the priestly class, and to promote individual freedom and social equality.
- The movement had various types and dimensions, such as:
  - The reform of Hinduism, which aimed to purify and simplify the Hindu religion, and to remove the evils and abuses of caste system, idolatry, polytheism, rituals, superstitions, and social oppression, especially of women and lower castes. Some of the prominent reformers and organizations of this type were Raja Ram Mohan Roy and the Brahmo Samaj, Swami Dayanand Saraswati and the Arya Samaj, Swami Vivekananda and the Ramakrishna Mission, and Mahatma Gandhi and the Harijan Sevak Sangh.
  - The reform of Islam, which aimed to reconcile the Islamic faith with the modern values and needs, and to reform the Muslim society and education. Some of the prominent reformers and organizations of this type were Sir Syed Ahmed Khan and the Aligarh Movement, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad and the Khilafat Movement, and Muhammad Iqbal and the Muslim League.
  - The reform of other religions, such as Sikhism, Jainism, Buddhism, and Zoroastrianism, which also faced the challenges of modernity and colonialism, and tried to reform and revive their faiths and communities. Some of the prominent reformers and organizations of this type were Guru Nanak and the Singh Sabha Movement, Mahavira and the Jain Mahasabha, Anagarika Dharmapala and the Maha Bodhi Society, and Dadabhai Naoroji and the Parsi Panchayat.
  - The reform of society, which aimed to improve the social and economic conditions of the Indian people, and to fight against the social evils and injustices, such as child marriage, widow immolation, female infanticide, untouchability, poverty, illiteracy, and exploitation. Some of the prominent reformers and organizations of this type were Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar and the Widow Remarriage Act, Rukhmabai and the Age of Consent Act, Jyotirao Phule and the Satyashodhak Samaj, Narayana Guru and the Sri Narayana Dharma Paripalana Yogam, and B.R. Ambedkar and the All India Scheduled Castes Federation.
- The movement had various impacts and outcomes, such as:
  - The movement contributed to the awakening and modernization of the Indian society and religion, and to the emergence of a new national identity and consciousness among the Indians.
  - The movement also created a new intellectual and cultural milieu, which fostered the growth of literature, art, science, and education in India.
  - The movement also paved the way for the political and social movements of the 20th century, such as the Indian National Congress, the Swadeshi Movement, the Home Rule Movement, the Non-Cooperation Movement, the Civil Disobedience Movement, the Quit India Movement, and the Partition of India.
