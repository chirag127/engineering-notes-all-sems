# Various Philosophical Doctrines

- Indian philosophy refers to the diverse systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent.
- Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought.
- Indian philosophy is characterized by flexibility and the tendency to synthesize different viewpoints and methods.
- Indian philosophy also shares many concepts such as dharma, karma, samsara, dukkha, renunciation, meditation, with almost all of them focusing on the ultimate goal of liberation of the individual from dukkha and samsara through diverse range of spiritual practices (moksha, nirvana).
- Indian philosophy can be broadly divided into two categories: orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika).
- Orthodox schools accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, while unorthodox schools reject or question the Vedas.
- The six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: Samkhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Purva Mimamsa, and Vedanta.
- The main unorthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: Buddhism, Jainism, and Charvaka.

## Samkhya
- Samkhya is the oldest of the orthodox philosophical systems, and it postulates that everything in reality stems from purusha (self or soul or mind) and prakriti (matter, creative agency, energy).
- Purusha is the passive, conscious, and eternal principle, while prakriti is the active, unconscious, and temporal principle.
- Prakriti consists of three qualities or gunas: sattva (purity, harmony, intelligence), rajas (activity, passion, desire), and tamas (inertia, dullness, ignorance).
- Prakriti evolves into various forms of matter and mind, such as the five elements (earth, water, fire, air, and ether), the five senses (sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch), the five organs of action (speech, grasp, movement, excretion, and reproduction), the mind (manas), the intellect (buddhi), and the ego (ahamkara).
- Purusha is the witness of the manifestations of prakriti, but it becomes entangled with prakriti due to ignorance (avidya) and attachment (raga).
- The goal of Samkhya is to discriminate between purusha and prakriti, and to attain liberation (kaivalya) from the cycle of birth and death (samsara) caused by prakriti.

## Yoga
- Yoga is closely related to Samkhya, and it accepts the dualism of purusha and prakriti, but it adds the concept of Ishvara (God) as the supreme purusha or the lord of all souls.
- Yoga is also a practical discipline that prescribes a method of self-control and meditation to achieve the union of the individual soul (jivatma) with the supreme soul (paramatma).
- Yoga is based on the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, which outline the eight limbs or stages of yoga: yama (moral restraints), niyama (observances), asana (postures), pranayama (breath control), pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), and samadhi (absorption).
- Yoga emphasizes the role of the mind in the liberation process, and it uses various techniques to purify and discipline the mind, such as mantra (sound), yantra (diagram), and mudra (gesture).
- Yoga also recognizes the existence of subtle energy channels (nadis) and centers (chakras) in the body, and it aims to awaken the dormant spiritual power (kundalini) at the base of the spine and to raise it to the crown of the head.

## Nyaya
- Nyaya is the school of logic and epistemology, and it analyzes the sources and validity of knowledge.
- Nyaya accepts four means of valid knowledge (pramana): perception (pratyaksha), inference (anumana), comparison (upamana), and testimony (shabda).
- Nyaya also proposes a theory of causation, which states that every effect has a cause, and that the cause must be present, capable, and unimpeded to produce the effect.
- Nyaya defends the existence of God as the efficient cause of the world
