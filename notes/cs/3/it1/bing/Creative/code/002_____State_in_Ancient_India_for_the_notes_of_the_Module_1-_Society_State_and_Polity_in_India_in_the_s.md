### State in Ancient India

- The concept of state in ancient India was not a fixed or uniform one. Different regions and periods had different forms and functions of state.
- The earliest evidence of state formation in India comes from the Harappan civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which had a centralized urban administration, standardized weights and measures, a uniform script, and a well-developed trade network.
- The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) saw the emergence of tribal polities called janapadas, which were ruled by kings (rajas) who performed sacrifices and rituals to maintain their authority and legitimacy. The kings were assisted by priests (brahmins), warriors (kshatriyas), and other officials. The janapadas were often engaged in wars and alliances with each other.
- The later Vedic period (c. 600-500 BCE) witnessed the rise of sixteen mahajanapadas, or great states, which were larger and more complex than the earlier janapadas. Some of these states, such as Magadha, Kosala, and Vatsa, developed into powerful monarchies, while others, such as the Vajji confederation, adopted republican forms of government, where the rulers were elected by an assembly of elders or representatives.
- The Mauryan empire (c. 321-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian state, which unified most of the subcontinent under the rule of Chandragupta Maurya and his successors, especially Ashoka. The Mauryan state was highly centralized and bureaucratic, with a large army, a network of spies, and a system of taxation and administration. The Mauryan state also promoted the spread of Buddhism and other religions, and undertook various welfare and public works projects.
- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE-300 CE) saw the fragmentation of the Mauryan empire and the emergence of various regional states, such as the Sungas, the Kanvas, the Satavahanas, the Kushanas, the Sakas, the Indo-Greeks, and the Nagas. These states were diverse in their political, cultural, and religious aspects, and had varying degrees of contact and interaction with each other and with the outside world.
- The Gupta empire (c. 320-550 CE) was the second pan-Indian state, which reunified most of the northern and central India under the rule of Chandragupta I and his successors, especially Samudragupta and Chandragupta II. The Gupta state was less centralized and more feudal than the Mauryan state, and relied on the loyalty and tribute of local rulers and chiefs. The Gupta state also patronized the revival of Hinduism, especially the worship of Vishnu and Shiva, and fostered a golden age of art, literature, science, and mathematics.
- The post-Gupta period (c. 550-750 CE) saw the decline of the Gupta empire and the rise of various regional states, such as the Vakatakas, the Pallavas, the Chalukyas, the Harshavardhana, and the Pandyas. These states were characterized by political instability, frequent wars, and cultural diversity. They also witnessed the emergence of new religious movements, such as Buddhism, Jainism, and Shaivism, and the development of temple architecture, sculpture, and painting.
