# Harappan Script

- The Harappan script is the writing system used by the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC), also known as the Harappan Civilization, which flourished in the northwest of South Asia from the 3rd to the 2nd millennium BCE.
- The script is not yet deciphered, meaning that the meaning and pronunciation of the symbols are unknown. There are various theories and proposals about the origin, nature, and structure of the script, but none of them are widely accepted.
- The script consists of about 400 signs, most of which are pictographic or ideographic, representing objects, concepts, or sounds. Some signs may have multiple meanings or functions, depending on the context or the position in a sequence.
- The script is written from right to left, in horizontal lines. Sometimes, the signs are arranged in a boustrophedon manner, meaning that the direction of writing alternates between lines. The script is also found on seals, pottery, copper plates, tools, and other artifacts, often accompanied by images of animals, humans, or deities.
- The script is mainly used for short inscriptions, usually consisting of 4 to 5 signs, and rarely exceeding 20 signs. The longest inscription, found on a signboard at Dholavira, has 10 signs on one line and 9 signs on another. The purpose and function of the inscriptions are unclear, but they may have been used for identification, ownership, trade, administration, religion, or magic.
- The script is one of the oldest writing systems in the world, and one of the few that developed independently, without any apparent influence from other scripts. It is also one of the most enigmatic and mysterious, as it remains undeciphered despite many attempts and efforts by scholars and enthusiasts. The script is a valuable source of information and insight into the culture, society, and history of the IVC, and a key to understanding its legacy and influence on later civilizations.
