# Evolutionary Theory

- Evolutionary theory is a perspective that explains the origin and development of human societies and cultures based on the principles of biological evolution.
- Evolutionary theory assumes that human societies and cultures are the products of natural selection, adaptation, and inheritance, and that they change over time in response to environmental and historical factors.
- Evolutionary theory also implies that human societies and cultures can be classified into different stages or types according to their level of complexity, organization, and technology.
- Evolutionary theory was influenced by the works of Charles Darwin, Herbert Spencer, Lewis Henry Morgan, and Edward Burnett Tylor, among others, who applied the concepts of evolution, survival of the fittest, and social progress to the study of human history and culture.
- Evolutionary theory was dominant in the 19th and early 20th centuries, but was criticized and challenged by later anthropologists and sociologists, who pointed out its ethnocentric, deterministic, and teleological biases, and its lack of empirical evidence and historical specificity.
- Evolutionary theory was revived and modified in the second half of the 20th century by scholars such as Julian Steward, Leslie White, Marshall Sahlins, and Elman Service, who developed new approaches such as cultural ecology, neoevolutionism, and cultural materialism, which emphasized the role of environment, technology, and economy in shaping human societies and cultures.
- Evolutionary theory is still relevant and influential in the 21st century, as it provides a framework for understanding the diversity and complexity of human societies and cultures, and for addressing the challenges and opportunities of globalization, development, and sustainability.
