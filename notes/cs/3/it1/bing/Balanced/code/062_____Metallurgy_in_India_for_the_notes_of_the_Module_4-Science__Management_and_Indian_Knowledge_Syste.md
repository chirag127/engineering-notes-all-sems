### Metallurgy in India

Metallurgy is the science and technology of extracting and processing metals from ores. Metallurgy in India has a long and rich history, dating back to the ancient times. Some of the main aspects of metallurgy in India are:

- The earliest evidence of metalworking in India is from the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), where copper, bronze, gold and silver were used for making tools, ornaments, seals and other objects. The Indus people also developed techniques of casting, forging, soldering and alloying metals.
- The Vedic texts (c. 1500-500 BCE) mention various metals and their properties, such as ayas (iron), loha (copper), trapu (zinc), kamsya (bronze), hiranya (gold) and rajata (silver). The Rigveda also describes the process of smelting iron from its ore using a clay furnace and a bellows .
- The Iron Age in India (c. 1200-300 BCE) saw the widespread use of iron for making weapons, tools, agricultural implements and structures. Iron was also exported to other regions, such as the Middle East, China and Southeast Asia. The iron pillar of Delhi, erected by Chandragupta II (c. 375-415 CE), is a remarkable example of the corrosion-resistant iron produced by the Indian metallurgists.
- The Mauryan Empire (c. 322-185 BCE) and the subsequent kingdoms and dynasties in India witnessed the advancement of metallurgy in various fields, such as coinage, sculpture, architecture, jewellery and weaponry. Some of the notable achievements of this period are the Ashoka pillars, the bronze statue of the dancing girl from Mohenjo-daro, the gold coins of the Kushan kings, the iron beams of the Konark temple and the bronze Nataraja statue of the Chola dynasty.
- The medieval period (c. 1200-1700 CE) saw the development of new metals and alloys, such as wootz steel, brass, bidriware and damascene steel. Wootz steel, also known as Indian steel or Damascus steel, was a high-carbon steel with a distinctive pattern of bands and swirls. It was renowned for its hardness, toughness and edge retention, and was used for making swords, daggers and other weapons. Brass, an alloy of copper and zinc, was used for making utensils, lamps, idols and coins. Bidriware, a form of metal inlay art, was invented in the Deccan region, where zinc was mixed with copper, tin and lead to form a black metal, which was then decorated with silver or gold designs. Damascene steel, also known as watered steel or pattern-welded steel, was a type of steel made by forging together layers of different metals, creating a rippled or wavy pattern. It was used for making swords, knives and armour .
- The modern period (c. 1700-present) witnessed the growth of the metallurgical industry in India, especially in the fields of steel and aluminium. The first modern steel plant in India was established by Jamsetji Tata in 1907 at Jamshedpur, which later became the Tata Steel Company. The aluminium industry in India started in 1938 with the establishment of the Aluminium Corporation of India at Alupuram. Today, India is the second largest producer of steel and the third largest producer of aluminium in the world, with a production capacity of 142.3 million tonnes and 3.7 million tonnes respectively in 2020-21 .
- The challenges and opportunities for the metallurgical industry in India include the availability and quality of raw materials, the environmental and social impacts of mining and processing, the innovation and adoption of new technologies, the competitiveness and efficiency of the sector, and the demand and supply of the domestic and global markets .
