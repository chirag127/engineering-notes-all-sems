### Contract Theory

- Contract theory is a branch of political philosophy that studies the origin, nature and legitimacy of the state and its authority over the people.
- Contract theory assumes that the state is the result of a voluntary agreement or contract among rational individuals who consent to give up some of their natural rights and freedoms in exchange for the protection and benefits provided by the state.
- Contract theory also explores the conditions and terms of the contract, the rights and duties of the parties involved, the limits and scope of the state's power, and the possibility of dissolving or revising the contract.
- Contract theory is influenced by the historical and social context of its proponents, who often reflect on the problems and challenges faced by their own societies and times.
- Some of the major contract theorists are Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Immanuel Kant, John Rawls and Robert Nozick.

- Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679) was an English philosopher who wrote Leviathan (1651) in the aftermath of the English Civil War. He argued that in the state of nature, human life was "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short" due to the constant fear of violence and death. He proposed that people entered into a social contract with an absolute sovereign who had the power to enforce the law and maintain peace and order. He believed that the sovereign's authority was derived from the consent of the people, who gave up all their rights except the right to self-preservation. He also claimed that the contract was irrevocable and that the people had no right to rebel against the sovereign.
- John Locke (1632-1704) was an English philosopher who wrote Two Treatises of Government (1689) in response to the Glorious Revolution of 1688. He argued that in the state of nature, people had natural rights to life, liberty and property, which were derived from God and natural law. He proposed that people entered into a social contract with a limited government that had the duty to protect their natural rights and to act according to the consent of the governed. He believed that the government's authority was based on the trust of the people, who retained the right to resist or overthrow the government if it violated their rights or acted tyrannically.
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712-1778) was a French philosopher who wrote The Social Contract (1762) in the context of the Enlightenment and the French Revolution. He argued that in the state of nature, people were free, equal and happy, but they were corrupted by the development of civilization and private property. He proposed that people entered into a social contract with a democratic government that represented the general will of the people, which was the source of legitimacy and sovereignty. He believed that the government's authority was based on the participation and consent of the people, who had the right to revise or dissolve the contract if it failed to express the general will or to promote the common good.
