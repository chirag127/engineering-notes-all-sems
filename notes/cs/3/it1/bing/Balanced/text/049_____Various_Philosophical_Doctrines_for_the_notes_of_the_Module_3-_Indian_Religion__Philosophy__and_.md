### Various Philosophical Doctrines for the notes of the Module 3- Indian Religion, Philosophy, and Practices in the subject of INDIAN TRADITIONS, CULTURAL AND SOCIETY

- Indian philosophy is the reflection and analysis of the diverse worldviews and practices of the Indian subcontinent. It encompasses both orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) systems of thought and practice.  
- Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought. Flexibility and the tendency to synthesize are also characteristic of Indian philosophy. 
- Indian philosophy shares many concepts such as dharma (duty, law, morality), karma (action, cause and effect), samsara (cycle of birth and death), dukkha (suffering), renunciation (detachment, asceticism), meditation (concentration, awareness), and moksha (liberation, nirvana). 
- Indian philosophy can be broadly divided into six schools of orthodox (astika) philosophy and three schools of unorthodox (nastika) philosophy. The six orthodox schools are:  
  - Samkhya: Samkhya is the oldest of the orthodox philosophical systems, and it postulates that everything in reality stems from purusha (self or soul or mind) and prakriti (matter, creative agency, energy). Samkhya is a dualistic and atheistic system that aims to attain liberation by understanding the distinction between purusha and prakriti. 
  - Yoga: Yoga is closely related to Samkhya, but it adds the element of Ishvara (God) as the supreme purusha. Yoga is a practical and experiential system that focuses on the discipline of the body, mind, and senses to achieve union with Ishvara and liberation from samsara. Yoga is also known for its eight limbs or stages of practice, which include ethical rules, physical postures, breath control, sensory withdrawal, concentration, meditation, and absorption. 
  - Nyaya: Nyaya is the school of logic and epistemology, and it investigates the sources and validity of knowledge. Nyaya holds that perception, inference, comparison, and testimony are the four valid means of knowledge, and that error, doubt, and debate can be resolved by applying the rules of logic. Nyaya also defends the existence of God, the self, and the external world, and argues for the liberation of the self from ignorance and bondage. 
  - Vaisheshika: Vaisheshika is the school of atomism and metaphysics, and it analyzes the nature and categories of reality. Vaisheshika holds that reality consists of six types of substances (earth, water, fire, air, ether, and self), seven types of qualities (color, taste, smell, touch, number, dimension, and separateness), and five types of motions (upward, downward, contraction, expansion, and horizontal). Vaisheshika also accepts the concepts of dharma, karma, and moksha, and advocates the ethical and spiritual life. 
  - Purva Mimamsa: Purva Mimamsa is the school of ritual exegesis and hermeneutics, and it interprets the meaning and purpose of the Vedic texts. Purva Mimamsa holds that the Vedas are eternal, authoritative, and self-evident, and that the performance of Vedic rituals is the highest duty and the means of attaining heaven. Purva Mimamsa also discusses the nature and validity of language, the relation between words and meanings, and the methods of textual interpretation. 
  - Vedanta: Vedanta is the school of metaphysics and theology, and it develops the philosophical implications of the Upanishads, the concluding part of the Vedas. Vedanta holds that Brahman (the Absolute Reality) is the source and essence of everything, and that the self (atman) is identical with Brahman. Vedanta also discusses the nature and origin of ignorance (avidya), the cause of bondage and suffering, and the ways of attaining knowledge (jnana) and liberation (moksha).
