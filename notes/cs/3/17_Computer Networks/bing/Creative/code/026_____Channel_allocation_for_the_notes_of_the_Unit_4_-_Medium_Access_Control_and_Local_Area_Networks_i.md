### Channel allocation for the notes of the Unit 4 - Medium Access Control and Local Area Networks in the subject of Computer Networks

Channel allocation is a process in which a single channel is divided and allotted to multiple users in order to carry user specific tasks. There are different techniques or strategies for channel allocation in computer networks, which can be classified into three categories :

- **Static Channel Allocation (SCA)**: In this technique, a fixed portion of the frequency channel is allotted to each user or station, regardless of whether they have data to transmit or not. This technique is suitable for LANs and MANs, where the number of users is fixed and known in advance. The advantages of SCA are simplicity, low overhead, and guaranteed bandwidth. The disadvantages are inefficient utilization of channel, wastage of bandwidth, and poor scalability  .
- **Dynamic Channel Allocation (DCA)**: In this technique, the frequency channel is not permanently assigned to any user or station, but rather allocated on demand, based on the traffic and availability. This technique is suitable for WANs and wireless networks, where the number of users is variable and unknown in advance. The advantages of DCA are efficient utilization of channel, adaptive to traffic variations, and high scalability. The disadvantages are complexity, high overhead, and possible interference  .
- **Hybrid Channel Allocation (HCA)**: In this technique, a combination of SCA and DCA is used, where some portion of the frequency channel is fixed for some users or stations, and the remaining portion is dynamically allocated for others. This technique is suitable for cellular networks, where the number of users is large and distributed over different cells. The advantages of HCA are flexibility, balance between efficiency and overhead, and reduced interference. The disadvantages are moderate complexity and coordination between fixed and dynamic portions.

Some examples of channel allocation algorithms or protocols are:

- **Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA)**: This is a SCA technique, where the frequency channel is divided into several sub-channels, each with a fixed bandwidth, and assigned to different users or stations. Each user or station can transmit data continuously on its assigned sub-channel, without interfering with others. This technique is used in analog cellular networks, such as AMPS  .
- **Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA)**: This is a SCA technique, where the frequency channel is divided into several time slots, each with a fixed duration, and assigned to different users or stations. Each user or station can transmit data only on its assigned time slot, in a round-robin fashion. This technique is used in digital cellular networks, such as GSM  .
- **Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA)**: This is a DCA technique, where the frequency channel is not divided into sub-channels or time slots, but rather shared by all users or stations, using different codes. Each user or station can transmit data at any time, using a unique code that is orthogonal to the codes of others. This technique is used in wireless networks, such as IS-95 and 3G  .
- **Carrier Sense Multiple Access (CSMA)**: This is a DCA technique, where the frequency channel is shared by all users or stations, using a carrier sensing mechanism. Each user or station can transmit data only when it senses that the channel is idle, otherwise it defers its transmission. This technique is used in wired LANs, such as Ethernet  .
- **Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection (CSMA/CD)**: This is a DCA technique, where the frequency channel is shared by all users or stations, using a carrier sensing and collision detection mechanism.
