### Switching techniques and multiplexing

Switching techniques and multiplexing are two important concepts in computer networks that enable efficient and reliable data transmission over shared communication channels.

- Switching techniques are methods of establishing and maintaining a connection between two or more nodes in a network. There are three main types of switching techniques: circuit switching, message switching, and packet switching.
  - Circuit switching is a technique where two nodes communicate with each other over a dedicated communication path. The path is established before the data transmission and remains active until the communication is terminated. Circuit switching is suitable for continuous and real-time data transmission, such as voice and video calls, but it may waste bandwidth if the path is idle.
  - Message switching is a technique where the whole message is treated as a data unit and forwarded from one node to another until it reaches the destination. The message is stored and forwarded at each intermediate node, which may cause delay and congestion. Message switching is suitable for non-real-time and asynchronous data transmission, such as email and file transfer, but it may not guarantee the delivery order of the messages.
  - Packet switching is a technique where the message is broken down into smaller chunks called packets. Each packet has a header that contains the source and destination addresses and other information. The packets are routed independently through the network and reassembled at the destination. Packet switching is suitable for both real-time and non-real-time data transmission, such as web browsing and streaming, but it may cause packet loss and jitter due to network conditions.
- Multiplexing is a technique of combining multiple signals into one signal over a shared medium. Multiplexing allows the efficient utilization of bandwidth and reduces the cost of transmission. There are two main types of multiplexing: frequency division multiplexing (FDM) and time division multiplexing (TDM).
  - Frequency division multiplexing is a technique where the available bandwidth of the medium is divided into several frequency bands. Each signal is modulated with a different carrier frequency and transmitted simultaneously over the medium. The signals are separated at the receiver by using filters. FDM is suitable for analog signals, such as radio and television, but it may cause interference and crosstalk between the signals.
  - Time division multiplexing is a technique where the available time of the medium is divided into several time slots. Each signal is assigned a different time slot and transmitted sequentially over the medium. The signals are synchronized at the receiver by using a clock signal. TDM is suitable for digital signals, such as telephone and data, but it may cause synchronization and framing errors.
- Statistical multiplexing is a variation of TDM that uses variable-length time slots instead of fixed-length time slots. The time slots are allocated dynamically according to the demand and priority of the signals. Statistical multiplexing can increase the efficiency and flexibility of the network, as well as reduce the delay and overhead. Statistical multiplexing is used in packet switching networks, such as the Internet.
