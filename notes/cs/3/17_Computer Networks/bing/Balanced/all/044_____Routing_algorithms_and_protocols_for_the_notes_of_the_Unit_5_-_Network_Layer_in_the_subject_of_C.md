# Routing algorithms and protocols for the notes of the Unit 5 - Network Layer in the subject of Computer Networks

- Routing is the process of finding and selecting the best path for data transmission from source to destination in a computer network.
- Routing algorithms are the software programs that implement the logic of routing, i.e., the criteria and procedure for choosing the optimal path.
- Routing protocols are the set of rules and messages that routers use to communicate with each other and exchange routing information.
- Routing algorithms and protocols can be classified into two main categories: static and dynamic.
  - Static routing algorithms and protocols are fixed and do not change with the network conditions. They are manually configured by the network administrator and require less overhead and complexity. However, they are not scalable, adaptable, or fault-tolerant.
  - Dynamic routing algorithms and protocols are adaptive and change with the network conditions. They are automatically updated by the routers using routing messages and metrics. They require more overhead and complexity, but they are more scalable, flexible, and robust.
- Routing algorithms and protocols can also be classified based on the scope and source of information they use: global and decentralized, proactive and reactive, distance vector and link state.
  - Global routing algorithms and protocols use complete and consistent information about the whole network topology and state. They are also called link state algorithms and protocols. They use a shortest path algorithm, such as Dijkstra's algorithm, to compute the best path for each destination. They require more memory and bandwidth, but they are more accurate and stable. Examples are Open Shortest Path First (OSPF) and Intermediate System to Intermediate System (IS-IS).
  - Decentralized routing algorithms and protocols use partial and local information about the network topology and state. They are also called distance vector algorithms and protocols. They use an iterative process of exchanging routing information with their neighbors to update their routing tables. They require less memory and bandwidth, but they are less accurate and stable. Examples are Routing Information Protocol (RIP) and Border Gateway Protocol (BGP).
  - Proactive routing algorithms and protocols update their routing information periodically, regardless of the traffic demand. They are also called table-driven algorithms and protocols. They have lower latency and higher reliability, but they waste more bandwidth and energy. Examples are Optimized Link State Routing (OLSR) and Destination-Sequenced Distance Vector (DSDV).
  - Reactive routing algorithms and protocols update their routing information on demand, when there is a need to send data to a destination. They are also called on-demand algorithms and protocols. They have lower bandwidth and energy consumption, but they have higher latency and lower reliability. Examples are Ad hoc On-Demand Distance Vector (AODV) and Dynamic Source Routing (DSR).
