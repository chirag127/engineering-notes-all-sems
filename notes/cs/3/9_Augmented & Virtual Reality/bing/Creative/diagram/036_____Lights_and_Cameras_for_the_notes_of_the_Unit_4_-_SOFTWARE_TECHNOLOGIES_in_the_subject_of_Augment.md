### Lights and Cameras for the notes of the Unit 4 - SOFTWARE TECHNOLOGIES in the subject of Augmented & Virtual Reality

- Lights and cameras are essential components of augmented and virtual reality (AR/VR) systems, as they enable the creation and manipulation of realistic 3D scenes and objects that blend with the real world or immerse the user in a virtual environment.
- Lights are used to illuminate the virtual objects and scenes, as well as to estimate the real-world lighting conditions and shadows, in order to achieve a consistent and believable visual experience. Cameras are used to capture the real-world environment and the user's position, orientation, and gestures, as well as to project the virtual content onto the user's field of view.
- Some of the key concepts and technologies related to lights and cameras in AR/VR are:

  - **Lighting estimation**: The process of estimating the intensity, direction, and color of the ambient and directional light sources in the real world, using sensors such as cameras, LiDAR, or photodiodes. This information can be used to adjust the lighting parameters of the virtual objects and scenes, such as brightness, contrast, and color temperature, to match the real-world lighting conditions and create a seamless visual integration.
  - **Shadow mapping**: The technique of creating realistic shadows for the virtual objects and scenes, by rendering them from the perspective of the light sources and storing the depth information in a texture map. This map can be used to determine whether a pixel is in shadow or not, by comparing its depth value with the corresponding value in the map.
  - **LiDAR**: A type of sensor that uses laser pulses to measure the distance and shape of objects and surfaces in the environment. LiDAR can provide high-resolution and accurate depth information, which can be used for occlusion, collision detection, and surface reconstruction in AR/VR applications. LiDAR can also help with lighting estimation, by capturing the reflectance and scattering properties of the materials in the scene .
  - **ToF camera**: A type of camera that uses infrared light to measure the distance and shape of objects and surfaces in the environment. ToF camera can provide low-resolution and noisy depth information, which can be used for occlusion, collision detection, and surface reconstruction in AR/VR applications. ToF camera can also help with lighting estimation, by capturing the ambient light level and color.
  - **Binocular depth sensing**: A technique that uses two cameras to capture the stereo images of the environment, and then computes the depth information by matching the corresponding pixels in the images. Binocular depth sensing can provide medium-resolution and noisy depth information, which can be used for occlusion, collision detection, and surface reconstruction in AR/VR applications. Binocular depth sensing can also help with lighting estimation, by capturing the parallax and perspective cues of the scene.
  - **Structured-light sensor**: A type of sensor that uses a projector and a camera to project and capture a pattern of light onto the environment, and then computes the depth information by analyzing the distortion of the pattern. Structured-light sensor can provide high-resolution and accurate depth information, which can be used for occlusion, collision detection, and surface reconstruction in AR/VR applications. Structured-light sensor can also help with lighting estimation, by capturing the reflectance and scattering properties of the materials in the scene.
  - **Head-mounted display (HMD)**: A type of device that is worn on the user's head and displays the virtual content onto the user's eyes, creating a sense of immersion and presence in the virtual environment. HMDs can be either monocular or binocular, depending on whether they display the same or different images to each eye. HMDs can also be either opaque or transparent, depending on whether they block or allow the user to see the real world through the display.
  - **Projection mapping**: A technique that uses a projector and a camera to project the virtual content onto the real-world surfaces, creating a sense of augmentation and interaction with the physical environment. Projection mapping can be either static or dynamic, depending on whether the projection is fixed or adapts to the changes in the environment. Projection mapping can also be either direct or indirect, depending on whether the projection is visible or invisible to the user.
