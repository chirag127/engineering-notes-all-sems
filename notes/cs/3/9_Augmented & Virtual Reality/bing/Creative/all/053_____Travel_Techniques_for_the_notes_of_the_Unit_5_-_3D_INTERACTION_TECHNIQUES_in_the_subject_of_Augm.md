# Travel Techniques

Travel techniques are 3D interaction techniques that allow the user to move between two locations in a virtual environment, setting the position and orientation of the user's viewpoint. Travel is the motor component of navigation, which also involves wayfinding (the cognitive process of determining and following a route) and cognitive mapping (the mental representation of the environment).

Travel techniques can be classified by the metaphor they use, such as:

- Walking: the user moves by simulating the natural motion of walking, either by using physical devices (such as treadmills, motion platforms, or foot pedals) or by using gestures or buttons to control the speed and direction of movement. Walking techniques are intuitive and can provide a high sense of presence, but they may also cause fatigue, motion sickness, or spatial disorientation. Examples of walking techniques are:

  - Real walking: the user physically walks in the real world, and the virtual viewpoint is updated accordingly. This technique requires a large tracking space and a wireless display system, and it may also suffer from the problem of redirection (how to guide the user to avoid obstacles or boundaries in the real world).
  - Walking-in-place: the user mimics the motion of walking by moving their legs or feet, and the virtual viewpoint is updated based on the detected motion. This technique can reduce the space and hardware requirements of real walking, but it may also reduce the realism and naturalness of the movement. Examples of walking-in-place techniques are:

    - GUD WIP: the user wears a pair of shoes with sensors that detect the heel and toe contact with the ground, and the virtual viewpoint is updated based on the frequency and amplitude of the steps.
    - Gesture WIP: the user performs gestures with their hands or arms to indicate the direction and speed of movement, and the virtual viewpoint is updated based on the gesture recognition. For example, the user can point with their index finger or swing their arms to control the movement.

- Steering: the user moves by controlling a virtual vehicle or proxy that follows the user's input, either by using physical devices (such as joysticks, mice, or steering wheels) or by using gestures or buttons to control the speed and direction of movement. Steering techniques are easy to use and can provide a high degree of control, but they may also cause a low sense of presence, a high cognitive load, or a mismatch between the user's motion and the virtual motion. Examples of steering techniques are:

  - Flying: the user controls a virtual vehicle that can move freely in three dimensions, and the virtual viewpoint is attached to the vehicle. This technique can allow the user to explore large or complex environments, but it may also cause disorientation, nausea, or collision with virtual objects. Examples of flying techniques are:

    - Hand-directed flying: the user holds a physical device (such as a wand or a glove) that acts as a virtual propeller, and the virtual viewpoint is updated based on the orientation and position of the device. For example, the user can tilt the device to change the direction of movement, or move the device closer or farther from the body to change the speed of movement.
    - Gaze-directed flying: the user controls the direction of movement by looking at the desired destination, and the virtual viewpoint is updated based on the user's gaze direction. For example, the user can look at a point in the horizon to fly towards it, or look at the ground to descend.

- Selection-based travel: the user moves by selecting a target location in the virtual environment, and the virtual viewpoint is updated to match the target location. This technique can allow the user to travel quickly and easily, but it may also cause a low sense of presence, a loss of spatial awareness, or a disconnection between the user's motion and the virtual motion. Examples of selection-based travel techniques are:

  - Pointing: the user points at a target location with a physical device (such as a wand or a ray) or a virtual cursor, and the virtual viewpoint is updated to match the target location. This technique can allow the user to travel to distant or occluded locations, but it may also cause occlusion, ambiguity, or inaccuracy in the selection. Examples of pointing techniques are:

    - World in miniature (WIM): the user points at a target location on a scaled-down model of the virtual environment, and the virtual viewpoint is updated to match the target location on the original environment.
