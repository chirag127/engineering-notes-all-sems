### Travel Techniques

Travel techniques are methods for moving the user's viewpoint in a 3D virtual environment. Travel is the motor component of navigation, which also involves wayfinding (the cognitive process of determining and following a route). Travel techniques are essential for exploring large-scale virtual environments, and can affect the user's sense of presence, orientation, and spatial awareness.

Travel techniques can be classified by the metaphor or the input device they use. Some common categories are:

- Walking: These techniques simulate natural walking by mapping the user's physical movements to the virtual environment. Walking techniques can provide high presence and natural interaction, but they require a large tracking space and may cause cybersickness or collisions with real obstacles.
- Steering: These techniques allow the user to control the direction and speed of travel using a device such as a joystick, a mouse, or a gesture. Steering techniques can provide fast and flexible travel, but they may also cause cybersickness or disorientation, and they may not match the user's expectations of natural interaction.
- Selection-based: These techniques allow the user to select a target location or direction in the virtual environment, and then automatically move the user's viewpoint there. Selection-based techniques can reduce cybersickness and disorientation, but they may also reduce presence and natural interaction, and they may not support continuous or precise travel.
- Manipulation-based: These techniques allow the user to manipulate the virtual environment as if it were a physical object, and then move the user's viewpoint accordingly. Manipulation-based techniques can provide intuitive and natural interaction, but they may also cause cybersickness or disorientation, and they may not support large-scale or realistic travel.

Some other aspects of travel techniques are:

- Transition: The way the user's viewpoint changes from the current location to the target location. Transitions can be continuous (smooth and gradual) or discrete (instant or segmented). Continuous transitions can provide more presence and spatial awareness, but they may also cause more cybersickness or disorientation. Discrete transitions can reduce cybersickness and disorientation, but they may also reduce presence and spatial awareness.
- Feedback: The visual, auditory, or haptic cues that inform the user about the state and progress of travel. Feedback can help the user maintain orientation and awareness, but it may also distract or annoy the user. Feedback can be egocentric (based on the user's viewpoint) or exocentric (based on an external viewpoint).
- Constraints: The limitations or rules that govern the travel technique. Constraints can affect the range, speed, direction, or mode of travel. Constraints can help the user avoid errors or conflicts, but they may also reduce the user's freedom and control. Constraints can be physical (based on the real world) or virtual (based on the virtual world).

Travel techniques are an important aspect of 3D interaction, and they should be designed and evaluated according to the goals, tasks, and characteristics of the virtual environment and the user.
