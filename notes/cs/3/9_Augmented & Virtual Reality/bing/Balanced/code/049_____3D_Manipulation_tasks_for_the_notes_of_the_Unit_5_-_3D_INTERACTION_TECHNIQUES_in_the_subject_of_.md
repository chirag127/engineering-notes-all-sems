### 3D Manipulation Tasks

3D manipulation tasks are the tasks of physically handling objects in a 3D environment with one or two hands. They are essential for interacting with virtual objects and creating realistic and immersive experiences. 3D manipulation tasks can be divided into three basic categories: selection, positioning, and rotation. Each category has different techniques and challenges that need to be considered when designing 3D user interfaces.

- Selection: Selection is the task of acquiring or identifying a particular object or subset of objects from the entire set of objects available. Selection is often the first step of any manipulation task, as it determines which object(s) the user wants to interact with. Selection techniques can be classified into two types: ray-casting and virtual hand. Ray-casting techniques use a ray that extends from the user's hand or head to point at and select objects. Virtual hand techniques use a graphical representation of the user's hand to grab and select objects. Both types of techniques have advantages and disadvantages in terms of accuracy, efficiency, and naturalness.

- Positioning: Positioning is the task of changing the 3D position of an object. Positioning is often the second step of any manipulation task, as it allows the user to move the selected object(s) to a desired location. Positioning techniques can be classified into two types: direct and indirect. Direct techniques use the user's hand or a device to directly control the position of the object. Indirect techniques use a device or a gesture to indirectly control the position of the object. Both types of techniques have trade-offs in terms of precision, speed, and range.

- Rotation: Rotation is the task of changing the orientation of an object. Rotation is often the third step of any manipulation task, as it allows the user to align the selected object(s) to a desired direction. Rotation techniques can be classified into two types: direct and indirect. Direct techniques use the user's hand or a device to directly control the orientation of the object. Indirect techniques use a device or a gesture to indirectly control the orientation of the object. Both types of techniques have challenges in terms of degrees of freedom, consistency, and feedback.
