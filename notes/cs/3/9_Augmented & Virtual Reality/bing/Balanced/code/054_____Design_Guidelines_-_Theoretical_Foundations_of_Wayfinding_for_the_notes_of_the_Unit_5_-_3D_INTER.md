### Design Guidelines - Theoretical Foundations of Wayfinding

Wayfinding is the process of navigating and orienting oneself in a physical environment. It involves cognitive, perceptual, and behavioral aspects of finding one's way from a starting point to a destination. Wayfinding is especially important in complex and unfamiliar spaces, such as hospitals, airports, museums, libraries, or virtual environments.

Some of the theoretical foundations of wayfinding are:

- **Spatial cognition**: the mental representation and processing of spatial information, such as landmarks, routes, maps, and spatial relations. Spatial cognition affects how people perceive, remember, and communicate about space.
- **Environmental psychology**: the study of how people interact with and are influenced by their physical surroundings, such as the effects of environmental stressors, preferences, emotions, and behaviors on wayfinding performance and satisfaction.
- **Information design**: the practice of presenting information in a clear, concise, and effective way, using visual, verbal, and auditory cues, such as signs, symbols, colors, sounds, and directions. Information design aims to reduce cognitive load, increase comprehension, and facilitate decision making for wayfinding users.
- **User-centered design**: the process of designing products, services, or systems based on the needs, preferences, and characteristics of the intended users, such as their goals, tasks, abilities, and limitations. User-centered design involves user research, prototyping, testing, and evaluation to ensure usability, accessibility, and satisfaction for wayfinding users.

Some of the design guidelines for wayfinding are:

- **Organize the space**: create a logical and consistent spatial structure, such as zones, regions, or clusters, that reflect the functional and thematic organization of the space. Use landmarks, such as distinctive or memorable features, to anchor and identify the spatial structure.
- **Provide orientation cues**: provide information that helps users to locate themselves in the space, such as maps, directories, or compasses. Use consistent and coherent orientation cues, such as cardinal directions, axes, or grids, to align the space and the information.
- **Provide navigation cues**: provide information that helps users to plan and execute their routes, such as signs, arrows, or paths. Use clear and concise navigation cues, such as symbols, colors, or words, to indicate the destinations, distances, and directions.
- **Provide feedback**: provide information that confirms or corrects the users' actions, such as sounds, lights, or messages. Use positive and negative feedback, such as rewards or warnings, to reinforce or discourage the users' behaviors.
- **Adapt to the users**: provide information that matches the users' needs, preferences, and characteristics, such as their goals, tasks, abilities, and limitations. Use adaptive and personalized information, such as dynamic or interactive displays, to accommodate the users' diversity and variability.
