# Software Requirement Specifications (SRS)

- Software Requirement Specifications (SRS) is a document that describes the features, functions, and constraints of a software system.
- SRS is also known as Software Requirements Document (SRD), Software Requirements Specification (SRS), or Functional Specification (FS).
- SRS is a communication tool between the stakeholders and the developers of the software system.
- SRS helps to ensure that the software system meets the needs and expectations of the customers and users.
- SRS also serves as a basis for software design, testing, validation, verification, maintenance, and evolution.

## Characteristics of a good SRS

- A good SRS should be clear, concise, consistent, complete, correct, traceable, verifiable, modifiable, and unambiguous.
- Clear: The SRS should use simple and precise language that is easy to understand by both technical and non-technical readers.
- Concise: The SRS should avoid unnecessary details, repetitions, and redundancies that may confuse or mislead the readers.
- Consistent: The SRS should use consistent terminology, notation, format, and style throughout the document. The SRS should also avoid conflicting or contradictory requirements.
- Complete: The SRS should cover all the functional and non-functional requirements of the software system, as well as the assumptions, dependencies, constraints, and risks involved.
- Correct: The SRS should reflect the actual needs and expectations of the customers and users, and should be free of errors and mistakes.
- Traceable: The SRS should be able to trace the origin and rationale of each requirement, as well as the impact and dependency of each requirement on other requirements, design, and testing.
- Verifiable: The SRS should be able to verify that each requirement is feasible, testable, and measurable, and that it can be satisfied by the software system.
- Modifiable: The SRS should be able to accommodate changes and updates in the requirements, as well as the feedback and comments from the stakeholders and the developers.
- Unambiguous: The SRS should use precise and unambiguous terms and definitions that avoid confusion and ambiguity among the readers.

## Components of a SRS

- A typical SRS consists of the following components:

  - Introduction: This section provides an overview of the purpose, scope, objectives, and context of the software system, as well as the intended audience and organization of the SRS document.
  - General Description: This section provides a general description of the software system, including its functions, features, users, interfaces, constraints, assumptions, and dependencies.
  - Specific Requirements: This section provides a detailed description of the specific requirements of the software system, including the functional and non-functional requirements, as well as the performance, reliability, security, usability, and maintainability requirements.
  - Appendices: This section provides any additional information that may support or supplement the SRS document, such as glossary, references, diagrams, tables, charts, etc.

## Techniques for eliciting and analyzing requirements

- Requirements elicitation is the process of gathering and understanding the needs and expectations of the customers and users of the software system.
- Requirements analysis is the process of refining and validating the requirements, as well as resolving any conflicts or inconsistencies among the requirements.
- Some of the common techniques for eliciting and analyzing requirements are:

  - Interviews: This technique involves conducting one-on-one or group interviews with the customers, users, and other stakeholders to collect their opinions, preferences, and expectations about the software system.
  - Questionnaires: This technique involves distributing written or online surveys or forms to the customers, users, and other stakeholders to collect their feedback, ratings, and suggestions about the software system.
  - Observation: This technique involves observing and studying the current or similar systems, processes, or tasks that the software system is intended to support or replace, and identifying the problems, issues, and opportunities for improvement.
  - Brainstorming: This technique involves organizing a creative and collaborative session with the customers, users, and other stakeholders to generate and evaluate various ideas and alternatives for the software system.
  - Prototyping: This technique involves creating a mock-up or a simulation of the software system or some of its features, and presenting it to the customers, users, and other stakeholders to obtain their feedback, comments, and approval.
  - Use Cases: This technique involves describing the scenarios or situations in which the software system or some of its features are used by the customers, users, or other actors, and specifying the expected inputs, outputs, and outcomes of each scenario.
