### The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

The ancient Indian political thought conceived the state as a living organism having seven constituent parts or 'limbs' (saptanga):

1. The king: The head of the state. The entire administration was devised to assist the king in performing his duties. The king was expected to be just, virtuous and efficient.

2. Ministers: The ministers aided and advised the king in administering the country. They formed the core of the central government.

3. Territory: The area under the control of the state. The Arthashastra emphasises the importance of the fertility and revenue-yielding capacity of the territory.

4. Forts: Forts or strategically located fortified places defended the state from external aggression and internal disorder.

5. Treasury: The treasury consisting of taxes and revenues as well as other wealth belonging to the state was necessary for the continuity and functioning of the state.

6. Army: The army defended the state from external attacks and maintained internal peace. It was composed of the standing army and auxiliary forces.

7. Allies: The state entered into alliances with other states to strengthen itself militarily or for economic gains. Diplomacy and alliance were emphasised as more desirable than war.

The seven limbs highlight the different constituents of the state that were necessary to ensure its continued existence, unity, strength, and stability. The Arthashastra discusses in detail the structure, functions, and interrelationship between these seven limbs. An efficient and just performance of the functions of each of the limbs was necessary for the well-being of the state as a whole.
