There are many methodologies in software project management, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Some of the most popular ones are:

- Agile: A flexible, iterative process that values collaboration, feedback, and adaptation over rigid plans and documentation. Agile can be combined with other methodologies, such as Scrum, Kanban, or eXtreme Programming, to suit different types of projects and teams.
- Waterfall: A linear, sequential process that follows a predefined set of steps from requirements to design, implementation, testing, and maintenance. Waterfall is suitable for short, predictable projects with clear and fixed requirements and minimal changes.
- Scrum: A subset of Agile that organizes work into short, time-boxed iterations called sprints, each with a specific goal and deliverable. Scrum relies on self-organizing, cross-functional teams that collaborate daily and use artifacts, such as product backlog, sprint backlog, and burndown charts, to track progress and quality.
- Kanban: A subset of Agile that focuses on visualizing and limiting the work in progress (WIP) to improve efficiency and flow. Kanban uses a board with columns representing different stages of the work process, such as to-do, doing, and done, and cards representing individual work items that move across the board as they are completed.
- Scrumban: A hybrid of Scrum and Kanban that combines the structure and roles of Scrum with the flexibility and visualization of Kanban. Scrumban limits the WIP and adds additional processes, such as pull systems, continuous improvement, and prioritization, to optimize the workflow and quality.
- Lean: A philosophy that aims to deliver more value with less waste by eliminating anything that does not add value to the customer or the product. Lean emphasizes continuous improvement, customer feedback, and value stream mapping to identify and eliminate sources of waste, such as defects, overproduction, waiting, inventory, and transportation.
- eXtreme Programming (XP): A subset of Agile that focuses on developing high-quality software through frequent releases, customer involvement, and rigorous testing. XP practices include pair programming, test-driven development, refactoring, collective ownership, and simple design.
- PRINCE2: A structured, process-based methodology that divides the project into manageable stages, each with clear roles, responsibilities, and deliverables. PRINCE2 follows seven principles, such as continued business justification, learn from experience, and manage by exception, and seven themes, such as business case, quality, and risk, to guide the project management.
- PMBOK: A standard, not a methodology, that describes the best practices, processes, and knowledge areas for project management. PMBOK covers five process groups, such as initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing, and 10 knowledge areas, such as integration, scope, time, and cost, that are applicable to any project, regardless of the methodology used.
- Critical Path Method (CPM): A technique that identifies the longest sequence of activities in a project schedule and determines the shortest possible duration of the project. CPM calculates the earliest and latest start and finish times of each activity and identifies the critical path, which is the sequence of activities that cannot be delayed without affecting the project completion date.
- Critical Chain Project Management (CCPM): A technique that addresses the uncertainties and resource constraints in project schedules by using buffers to protect the project completion date and avoid multitasking. CCPM identifies the critical chain, which is the longest sequence of dependent tasks that takes into account the resource availability, and adds buffers to the end of the critical chain and each feeding chain to account for the variability and risks.
