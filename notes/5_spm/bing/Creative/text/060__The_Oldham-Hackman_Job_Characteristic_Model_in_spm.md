### The Oldham-Hackman Job Characteristic Model in spm

The Oldham-Hackman Job Characteristic Model, also known as the Job Characteristics Theory or the Core Characteristics Model, is a framework for designing jobs that are satisfying and motivating for workers. It was developed by organizational psychologists J. Richard Hackman and Greg Oldham in the 1970s, based on previous theories of job enrichment and motivation .

The model proposes that there are five core job dimensions that influence three critical psychological states of workers, which in turn affect their work outcomes. The five core job dimensions are:

- Skill variety: the degree to which a job requires a variety of skills and abilities.
- Task identity: the degree to which a job involves completing a whole and identifiable piece of work.
- Task significance: the degree to which a job has an impact on the lives of other people, within or outside the organization.
- Autonomy: the degree to which a job gives the worker freedom, independence, and discretion in scheduling and performing the work.
- Feedback: the degree to which a job provides the worker with clear and direct information about the effectiveness of his or her performance.

The three critical psychological states are:

- Experienced meaningfulness of the work: the degree to which the worker feels that the work is worthwhile and important.
- Experienced responsibility for the outcomes of the work: the degree to which the worker feels accountable and in control of the results of his or her work.
- Knowledge of the actual results of the work: the degree to which the worker knows and understands how well he or she is doing.

The model suggests that the higher the levels of these core job dimensions, the more likely the workers will experience the three psychological states, which will then lead to positive work outcomes, such as:

- High internal work motivation: the degree to which the worker is intrinsically interested in and committed to the work.
- High-quality work performance: the degree to which the worker performs the work effectively and efficiently.
- High satisfaction with the work: the degree to which the worker is happy and content with the work.
- Low absenteeism and turnover: the degree to which the worker is present and loyal to the organization.

The model also recognizes that individual differences may moderate the effects of the core job dimensions on the psychological states and the work outcomes. For example, some workers may have a higher need for growth and development, which makes them more responsive to enriched jobs, while others may have a lower need for growth and prefer more structured and routine jobs .

The model can be used to assess the motivational potential of a job by calculating a motivational potential score (MPS) based on the following formula:

MPS = (Skill variety + Task identity + Task significance) / 3 x Autonomy x Feedback

The higher the MPS, the more motivating the job is expected to be .

The model can also be used to redesign jobs that are low in motivational potential by increasing one or more of the core job dimensions, such as:

- Combining tasks to increase skill variety, task identity, and task significance.
- Forming natural work units to increase task identity and task significance.
- Establishing client relationships to increase skill variety, autonomy, and feedback.
- Enlarging jobs vertically to increase autonomy and responsibility.
- Opening feedback channels to increase feedback and knowledge of results .

The model has been widely applied and tested in various organizational settings and has received empirical support for its validity and usefulness. However, some limitations and challenges of the model have also been noted, such as:

- The model does not consider other factors that may affect work motivation and satisfaction, such as interpersonal relationships, organizational culture, and leadership.
- The model assumes that all workers prefer enriched jobs, which may not be true for some workers who have different values, preferences, and goals.
- The model may not be applicable to some types of jobs that are highly specialized, standardized, or regulated, where increasing the core job dimensions may not be feasible or desirable .
