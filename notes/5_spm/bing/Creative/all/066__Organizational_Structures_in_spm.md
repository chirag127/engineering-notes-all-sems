### Organizational Structures in spm

Organizational structures in software project management (spm) are the ways of organizing the roles and responsibilities of the project team members and stakeholders. Different organizational structures have different advantages and disadvantages for the project, depending on the project size, complexity, scope, and objectives.

Some of the common organizational structures in spm are:

- **Functional structure**: In this structure, the project team members are grouped by their functional areas, such as engineering, marketing, finance, etc. The project manager has limited authority and control over the project, and has to coordinate with the functional managers of each department. This structure is suitable for projects that are small, simple, and aligned with the existing functions of the organization.

  - Advantages: 
    - Clear division of roles and responsibilities
    - High level of expertise and specialization in each function
    - Minimal disruption to the normal operations of the organization
  - Disadvantages:
    - Poor communication and coordination across functions
    - Slow decision making and conflict resolution
    - Low motivation and commitment of the project team members
  - Example: A software project that involves developing a new feature for an existing product, and requires minimal interaction with other departments.

- **Projectized structure**: In this structure, the project team members are dedicated to the project, and report directly to the project manager. The project manager has full authority and control over the project, and can allocate the resources and assign the tasks as needed. This structure is suitable for projects that are large, complex, and independent of the existing functions of the organization.

  - Advantages:
    - High level of communication and coordination within the project team
    - Fast decision making and conflict resolution
    - High motivation and commitment of the project team members
  - Disadvantages:
    - Lack of continuity and stability for the project team members after the project is completed
    - High cost and risk of losing the expertise and knowledge of the project team members
    - Potential duplication of resources and efforts across projects
  - Example: A software project that involves developing a new product from scratch, and requires a high degree of innovation and creativity.

- **Matrix structure**: In this structure, the project team members are assigned to the project from their functional areas, and report to both the project manager and the functional manager. The project manager has partial authority and control over the project, and has to balance the demands and expectations of both the project and the function. This structure is suitable for projects that are medium-sized, moderately complex, and require some integration with the existing functions of the organization.

  - Advantages:
    - Optimal utilization of resources and skills across projects and functions
    - Enhanced collaboration and knowledge sharing among the project team members
    - Flexibility and adaptability to changing project requirements and priorities
  - Disadvantages:
    - Conflicting roles and responsibilities for the project team members
    - Power struggle and ambiguity between the project manager and the functional manager
    - Increased stress and workload for the project team members
  - Example: A software project that involves developing a new module for an existing product, and requires some coordination with other departments.

A mnemonic to remember the three types of organizational structures in spm is:

- **F**unctional structure: **F**ew project managers, **F**ixed roles, **F**ocused on functions
- **P**rojectized structure: **P**lenty of project managers, **P**roject-based roles, **P**rioritized on projects
- **M**atrix structure: **M**ixed project managers, **M**ultiple roles, **M**oderated by both functions and projects
