## Unit - 3 - Activity Planning and Risk Management for the notes of the course Software Project Management

### Activity Planning

Activity planning is the process of defining and sequencing the tasks, milestones, and deliverables of a software project. It involves identifying the activities that need to be performed, estimating the duration and resources required for each activity, and determining the dependencies and constraints among them. Activity planning helps to create a realistic and feasible schedule for the project, as well as to monitor and control the progress and performance of the project team.

The main steps of activity planning are:

- Define the scope and objectives of the project
- Decompose the project into manageable work packages or tasks
- Estimate the effort, duration, and cost of each task
- Identify the resources and skills needed for each task
- Determine the logical sequence and dependencies of the tasks
- Create a network diagram or a Gantt chart to visualize the project schedule
- Identify the critical path and the slack time of the tasks
- Review and refine the activity plan as the project progresses

Some of the tools and techniques that can be used for activity planning are:

- Work breakdown structure (WBS): A hierarchical decomposition of the project scope into smaller and more manageable work packages or tasks.
- Activity list: A detailed list of all the tasks that need to be performed in the project, along with their attributes such as description, duration, resources, dependencies, etc.
- Activity attributes: Additional information about each task, such as the responsible person, the start and end dates, the status, the priority, the risk level, etc.
- Milestones: Significant events or deliverables that mark the completion of a major phase or a key objective of the project.
- Estimation techniques: Methods for estimating the effort, duration, and cost of each task, such as expert judgment, analogy, parametric, bottom-up, top-down, three-point, etc.
- Resource breakdown structure (RBS): A hierarchical representation of the resources and skills required for the project, such as human, material, equipment, etc.
- Precedence diagramming method (PDM): A technique for creating a network diagram that shows the logical sequence and dependencies of the tasks, using nodes to represent the tasks and arrows to represent the dependencies.
- Gantt chart: A graphical representation of the project schedule that shows the start and end dates, the duration, and the progress of each task, as well as the milestones and the dependencies among the tasks.
- Critical path method (CPM): A technique for identifying the longest path of dependent tasks in the network diagram, which determines the shortest possible duration of the project.
- Slack time: The amount of time that a task can be delayed without affecting the project duration or the start date of the subsequent tasks.

### Risk Management

Risk management is the process of identifying, analyzing, evaluating, treating, and monitoring the potential events or uncertainties that can affect the project, positively or negatively. Risk management helps to reduce the impact and probability of negative risks (threats) and enhance the impact and probability of positive risks (opportunities). Risk management also helps to improve the quality and reliability of the project deliverables, as well as to increase the confidence and satisfaction of the stakeholders.

The main steps of risk management are:

- Identify risks and their triggers: A risk is a potential problem that can impact the project, and a trigger is a sign or a symptom that indicates that a risk is about to occur. Risk identification involves brainstorming, interviewing, surveying, reviewing, and researching the possible sources and causes of risks in the project, such as new, unproven technologies, user and functional requirements, application and system architecture, performance, organizational, financial, strategic, external, etc.
- Analyze risks: Risk analysis involves assessing the impact and probability of each risk, using qualitative or quantitative methods. Qualitative analysis uses descriptive scales, such as high, medium, low, to rank the risks based on their severity and likelihood. Quantitative analysis uses numerical values, such as percentages, probabilities, costs, etc., to measure the risks based on their expected value and variance.
- Evaluate risks: Risk evaluation involves prioritizing the risks based on their impact and probability, using tools such as risk matrix, risk register, risk map, etc. Risk evaluation helps to determine which risks need more attention and resources, and which risks can be ignored or accepted.
