### COCOMO II in spm

COCOMO II is a model that allows one to estimate the cost, effort, and schedule when planning a new software development activity. COCOMO stands for COnstructive COst MOdel. It is the successor of COCOMO 81 and is based on a hierarchy of three increasingly detailed and accurate forms. The forms are:

- **Application Composition Model**: used for prototyping and early design stages, based on object points and code size.
- **Early Design Model**: used for preliminary design stages, based on function points and unadjusted function points.
- **Post-Architecture Model**: used for detailed design stages, based on source lines of code and cost drivers.

Some of the main features of COCOMO II are:

- It supports multiple development modes, such as organic, semi-detached, embedded, reuse, and non-sequential.
- It incorporates scaling factors that reflect the relative economies or diseconomies of scale encountered for software projects of different sizes.
- It considers the effects of various cost drivers, such as product, platform, personnel, and project attributes, on the effort and duration of software development.
- It provides several estimation models, such as effort, schedule, staffing, reliability, and risk, to support different aspects of software project planning and management.
- It allows for calibration and tuning of the model parameters to better match the historical data and experience of the organization.

Some of the advantages of COCOMO II are:

- It is widely used and validated by empirical studies and industry data.
- It is flexible and adaptable to different types of software projects and development processes.
- It is transparent and easy to understand and apply, with well-defined equations and inputs.
- It is supported by various tools and software packages that facilitate the estimation process and analysis.

Some of the disadvantages of COCOMO II are:

- It requires a lot of input data and assumptions, which may not be available or accurate at the early stages of software development.
- It may not account for all the factors and uncertainties that affect the software project outcomes, such as requirements volatility, team dynamics, and environmental changes.
- It may not reflect the specific characteristics and practices of the organization, such as productivity, quality, and reuse levels, unless calibrated and tuned properly.
- It may not be applicable or reliable for some types of software projects, such as web-based, agile, or open-source development.
