### Agriculture in India and Ancient India

Agriculture is the cultivation of plants and animals for food, fiber, fuel, and other purposes. Agriculture is one of the oldest and most important human activities, as it provides the basis for food security, livelihoods, and civilization. India is one of the world's largest and most diverse agricultural regions, with a long and rich history of farming practices and innovations.

Some of the main points to know about agriculture in India and ancient India are:

- **Ancient Indian agriculture** began as early as 9000 BCE in the Indus Valley Civilization, where people cultivated wheat, barley, peas, lentils, and cotton, and domesticated cattle, sheep, goats, and water buffalo. They also developed irrigation systems, plows, and granaries to store their surplus crops .
- **Ancient South Indian agriculture** was equally prosperous, as the Tamil people cultivated a wide range of crops such as rice, sugarcane, millets, black pepper, various grains, coconuts, beans, cotton, plantain, tamarind, and sandalwood. They also grew jackfruit, coconut, palm, areca, and plantain trees, and traded their produce with other regions and countries.
- **The Ganges River valley** became another major agricultural region after about 1500 BCE, as the Vedic people migrated from the northwest and settled in the fertile plains. They grew rice, wheat, barley, millets, pulses, oilseeds, and sugarcane, and raised cattle, horses, elephants, and poultry. They also developed iron tools, plows, and wells to improve their farming efficiency.
- **The Mauryan Empire** (322-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian political entity that unified most of the subcontinent under a centralized administration. The Mauryan rulers promoted agriculture as the main source of revenue and welfare, and implemented various reforms and regulations to improve the productivity and quality of the land and crops. They also built roads, canals, and reservoirs to facilitate trade and irrigation.
- **The Mughal Empire** (1526-1857 CE) was another influential dynasty that ruled over most of India and parts of Afghanistan and Pakistan. The Mughal rulers were patrons of agriculture and introduced many new crops and techniques from Central Asia, Persia, and Europe, such as maize, potatoes, tomatoes, chilies, tobacco, cotton, and silk. They also improved the land revenue system, the irrigation network, and the agricultural markets.
- **Modern Indian agriculture** is the result of centuries of evolution and adaptation to the diverse climatic, geographic, and socio-economic conditions of the subcontinent. India is the world's largest producer of milk, pulses, spices, and jute, and the second-largest producer of rice, wheat, sugarcane, cotton, and fruits and vegetables. India also has a large and varied livestock sector, with the world's largest population of cattle, buffalo, goats, and sheep. India's agriculture sector employs about 60% of the population and contributes about 17% of the GDP.

Some of the main challenges and opportunities for Indian agriculture in the present and future are:

- **Climate change** is a major threat to the sustainability and resilience of Indian agriculture, as it affects the availability and quality of water, soil, and biodiversity, and increases the risks of droughts, floods, pests, and diseases. Indian farmers need to adopt more climate-smart and adaptive practices and technologies, such as crop diversification, conservation agriculture, organic farming, rainwater harvesting, and precision irrigation.
- **Food security and nutrition** are key goals for Indian agriculture, as the country faces the dual burden of hunger and malnutrition on one hand, and obesity and overnutrition on the other. Indian agriculture needs to produce more and better food for its growing and changing population, while ensuring the access and affordability of food for all. Indian agriculture also needs to enhance the nutritional quality and diversity of its crops and products, and reduce the post-harvest losses and food waste.
- **Agricultural innovation and extension** are essential for improving the productivity and profitability of Indian agriculture, as well as its environmental and social impacts.
