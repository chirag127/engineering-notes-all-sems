# Water Management in India and Ancient India

Water management is the planning, development, distribution, and use of water resources for various purposes such as irrigation, drinking, sanitation, industry, energy, and ecology. Water management is essential for the sustainability of human and natural systems, especially in a country like India, which faces diverse and complex water challenges.

Some of the main water challenges in India are:

- Scarcity and uneven distribution of water resources across regions and seasons
- Overexploitation and depletion of groundwater resources
- Pollution and contamination of surface and groundwater sources
- Inadequate and inefficient water infrastructure and services
- Climate change impacts on water availability and quality
- Conflicts and disputes over water sharing and allocation among different users and states
- Lack of awareness and participation of stakeholders in water governance and management

To address these challenges, India needs to adopt an integrated and holistic approach to water management, which considers the social, economic, environmental, and cultural aspects of water. India also needs to learn from its rich and diverse heritage of water management, which dates back to ancient times.

## Water Management in Ancient India

Ancient India had a sophisticated and scientific understanding of hydrology and water resources management, which was reflected in its literature, architecture, engineering, and culture. Some of the salient features of water management in ancient India are:

- The concept of the hydrologic cycle, which describes the movement and exchange of water among the atmosphere, land, and oceans, was well known to the ancient Indians. They recognized the importance of rain, rivers, lakes, springs, and groundwater as sources of water, and devised methods to measure, store, and distribute them.
- The Harappan civilization (3000-1500 BCE), which flourished in the Indus valley, was one of the earliest urban civilizations in the world, and had a remarkable system of water management. The Harappans built elaborate hydraulic structures, such as wells, reservoirs, canals, drains, and baths, to provide water supply and sanitation to their cities. They also had a high standard of hygiene and cleanliness, and used copper and brass vessels to purify water .
- The Vedic period (1500-500 BCE), which marked the emergence of Hinduism and its scriptures, also had a profound influence on water management in ancient India. The Vedas, which are the oldest religious texts in the world, contain many hymns and prayers dedicated to water and its deities, such as Varuna, Indra, and Saraswati. The Vedas also prescribe rules and rituals for the conservation and protection of water, and emphasize the ethical and spiritual values of water.
- The post-Vedic period (500 BCE-500 CE), which witnessed the rise of Buddhism, Jainism, and other schools of thought, also saw the development of various water management techniques and practices in ancient India. Some of the notable examples are:

  - The stepwells, which are unique underground structures that collect and store rainwater, and provide access to groundwater through a series of steps. The stepwells were not only functional, but also artistic and architectural marvels, and served as social and cultural spaces for the communities. The stepwells are found in many parts of India, especially in Gujarat and Rajasthan, and some of them are still in use today.
  - The tanks, which are artificial ponds or lakes that collect and store surface runoff or diverted river water, and provide irrigation and drinking water to the surrounding areas. The tanks were often built by kings, nobles, or religious institutions, and were considered as sacred and auspicious. The tanks also supported the growth of aquatic life and biodiversity, and enhanced the aesthetic and scenic beauty of the landscape. The tanks are found in many regions of India, especially in South India, and some of them are still functional today.
  - The dams, which are barriers that impound or regulate the flow of water in rivers or streams, and create reservoirs or lakes for various purposes. The dams were constructed using different materials, such as stone, brick, earth, or wood, and were designed to withstand floods and earthquakes. The dams also had sluices, gates, and channels to control and distribute the water. The dams are found in many parts of India, especially in North India, and some of them are still operational today.
