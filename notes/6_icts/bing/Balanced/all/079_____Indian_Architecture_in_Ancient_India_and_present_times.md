# Indian Architecture in Ancient India and present times

- Indian architecture is rooted in the history, culture, and religion of India. It reflects the diversity and richness of Indian civilization and its interactions with other cultures and regions.
- Indian architecture can be broadly classified into two main styles: the Nagara style and the Dravidian style, which developed in the northern and southern parts of India respectively. Both styles have many regional variations and sub-styles, and are influenced by various factors such as geography, climate, materials, patronage, and ideology. 
- Some of the best-known examples of ancient Indian architecture are the Hindu temples, which are characterized by their elaborate and ornate designs, sculptures, and carvings. The temples are usually dedicated to a specific deity or aspect of Hinduism, and often serve as the focal point of a sacred complex or a pilgrimage site. Some of the famous temples include the Khajuraho temples, the Konark Sun Temple, the Brihadeeswarar Temple, and the Meenakshi Temple. 
- Another prominent feature of ancient Indian architecture is the Indo-Islamic architecture, which emerged from the fusion of Islamic and Hindu elements. This style was mainly developed by the Muslim rulers of India, especially the Mughals, who built some of the most magnificent monuments and structures in the country. Some of the notable examples of Indo-Islamic architecture are the Qutub Minar, the Red Fort, the Taj Mahal, and the Jama Masjid. 
- In the modern times, Indian architecture has evolved and adapted to the changing needs and aspirations of the society. It has also been influenced by various movements and trends from the West, such as colonialism, modernism, postmodernism, and globalization. Some of the contemporary Indian architects who have made significant contributions to the field are Le Corbusier, Charles Correa, B.V. Doshi, Raj Rewal, and Balkrishna Doshi.
- Indian architecture today is a vibrant and dynamic expression of the country's diversity, creativity, and innovation. It showcases the fusion of tradition and modernity, as well as the integration of local and global influences. It also reflects the social, cultural, and environmental concerns and challenges of the present and the future.
