# Various Philosophical Doctrines in India

- Indian philosophy refers to the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent.
- Indian philosophy is diverse and includes both orthodox and unorthodox schools of thought .
- Orthodox schools are those that accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, while unorthodox schools reject or question the Vedas .
- Three basic concepts form the cornerstone of Indian philosophical thought: the self or soul (atman), works (karma), and liberation (moksha).
- Almost all Indian philosophies focus on the ultimate goal of liberation of the individual from suffering and the cycle of rebirth (samsara) through various spiritual practices (moksha, nirvana).
- Some of the major orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are:
  - Sāṃkhya, a philosophical tradition which regards the universe as consisting of two independent realities: puruṣa (the self or consciousness) and prakṛti (the material nature or energy). Sāṃkhya aims to achieve liberation by discriminating between the two realities and realizing the true nature of the self.
  - Yoga, a school similar to Sāṃkhya (or perhaps even a branch of it) which accepts a personal god and focuses on yogic practices such as meditation, ethical discipline, and physical exercises to attain liberation. Yoga is also associated with the famous text, the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, which outlines the eight limbs of yoga.
  - Nyāya, a school of logic and epistemology that investigates the sources and validity of knowledge. Nyāya employs a method of reasoning based on inference, analogy, testimony, and perception to establish the truth of various propositions. Nyāya also deals with topics such as metaphysics, ethics, and theology.
  - Vaiśeṣika, a school of atomism and naturalism that analyzes the nature of reality into six categories: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence. Vaiśeṣika holds that the ultimate reality is composed of indivisible atoms that combine to form complex entities. Vaiśeṣika also advocates ethical values such as non-violence, truthfulness, and self-control.
  - Mīmāṃsā, a school of exegesis and ritual that interprets the Vedas and their injunctions. Mīmāṃsā argues that the performance of Vedic rituals is the primary duty of human beings and the means to achieve happiness and liberation. Mīmāṃsā also develops a sophisticated theory of language, meaning, and interpretation.
  - Vedānta, a school of metaphysics and theology that expounds the teachings of the Upanishads, the philosophical portions of the Vedas. Vedānta is the most influential and diverse school of Indian philosophy, with various sub-schools such as Advaita (non-dualism), Viśiṣṭādvaita (qualified non-dualism), and Dvaita (dualism). Vedānta explores the nature of Brahman (the supreme reality), Ātman (the individual self), and the relationship between them.
- Some of the major unorthodox schools of Indian philosophy are:
  - Buddhism, a religion and philosophy founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, in the sixth century BCE. Buddhism teaches the four noble truths: the truth of suffering, the truth of the origin of suffering, the truth of the cessation of suffering, and the truth of the path to the cessation of suffering. Buddhism has various branches such as Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana.
