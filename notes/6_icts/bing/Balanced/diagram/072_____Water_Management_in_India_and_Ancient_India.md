### Water Management in Ancient India and India

Water is a vital resource for human existence and civilization. India has a rich and diverse history of water management, dating back to the ancient times. Here are some points to note about water management in ancient India and India:

- The Harappan civilization (3000-1500 BCE) was one of the earliest urban civilizations in the world, and had a sophisticated system of water supply, drainage, irrigation, and sanitation. They built wells, reservoirs, canals, dams, and aqueducts to harness the water from the Indus river and its tributaries. They also had a high standard of hygiene and cleanliness, as evidenced by their public baths, toilets, and sewers  .
- The Vedic period (1500-500 BCE) was a time of cultural and religious development in India, and also a time of scientific and technological advancement. The Vedas, the oldest scriptures of Hinduism, contain many references to hydrological concepts, such as the water cycle, rainfall, evaporation, infiltration, runoff, and groundwater. The Vedas also prescribe rituals and practices for water conservation, purification, and worship.
- The Mauryan empire (321-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian empire, and had a centralized administration and a well-organized economy. The Mauryans built many irrigation works, such as dams, canals, and reservoirs, to support agriculture and trade. They also had a system of water taxation and regulation, and appointed officials to oversee water management. The Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft and economics written by Kautilya, a minister of the Mauryan king Chandragupta, contains detailed instructions on water engineering, law, and policy.
- The Gupta period (320-550 CE) is considered the golden age of Indian culture, science, and art. The Guptas continued the tradition of building and maintaining irrigation works, and also promoted the use of wells and tanks for water storage. They also encouraged the development of water science and technology, and patronized scholars and experts in various fields of hydrology, such as Varahamihira, Aryabhata, and Brahmagupta. They also contributed to the advancement of mathematics, astronomy, and geography, which helped in understanding and predicting water phenomena.
- The medieval period (600-1500 CE) saw the rise and fall of many dynasties and kingdoms in India, and also the arrival of foreign invaders and traders. The medieval rulers built many impressive water structures, such as stepwells, baolis, bawadis, and johads, to collect and store rainwater and groundwater. These structures were not only functional, but also artistic and architectural marvels, often decorated with sculptures, carvings, and paintings. They also served as social and cultural spaces, where people gathered for drinking, bathing, worshiping, and leisure .
- The colonial period (1500-1947 CE) marked the beginning of the British rule in India, and also the introduction of modern water technology and management. The British built many large-scale irrigation projects, such as dams, barrages, and canals, to increase agricultural production and revenue. They also introduced new laws and institutions to regulate water use and ownership, which often conflicted with the traditional and customary rights and practices of the local people. The British also exploited the water resources of India for their own benefit, and neglected the maintenance and conservation of the existing water structures.
- The post-independence period (1947-present) is the current phase of Indian history, and also the phase of rapid population growth, urbanization, industrialization, and globalization. The post-independence governments have continued to build and expand irrigation projects, and also to provide drinking water and sanitation facilities to the people. They have also enacted various policies and programs to address the challenges and issues of water scarcity, pollution, quality, equity, and governance. However, the water situation in India remains critical, as the demand for water exceeds the supply, and the water resources are depleting and deteriorating. The ancient wisdom and practices of water management need to be revived and integrated with the modern technology and management, to ensure the sustainable and equitable use of water in India.
