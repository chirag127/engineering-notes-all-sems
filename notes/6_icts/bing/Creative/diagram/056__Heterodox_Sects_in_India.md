Heterodox sects in India are those religious and philosophical movements that do not accept the authority of the Vedas as the sole and definitive source of knowledge. They are also called nāstika or "atheistic" schools, in contrast to the āstika or "theistic" schools that uphold the Vedas. Some of the major heterodox sects in India are:

- Ājīvika: Founded by Makkhali Gosāla in the 5th century BCE, this sect believed in absolute determinism or fate (niyati), and denied the existence of free will, karma, and liberation (moksha). They also rejected the concept of God, soul, and rebirth. They practiced a strict asceticism and formed discrete communities of renunciates. They were a major rival of early Buddhism and Jainism, and reached the peak of their popularity under the Mauryan emperor Bindusara. They declined after the 4th century BCE, but survived in some parts of South India until the 14th century CE.

- Cārvāka: Also known as Lokāyata, this sect was founded by Bṛhaspati in the 6th century BCE, and was the most radical materialist school of Indian philosophy. They denied the existence of anything beyond the four elements of earth, water, fire, and air, and rejected the notions of God, soul, karma, rebirth, and liberation. They also dismissed the Vedas, the caste system, and the rituals as false and deceptive. They advocated a hedonistic and pragmatic way of life, based on sensory perception and rational inference.

- Buddhism: Founded by Siddhārtha Gautama, also known as the Buddha, in the 6th century BCE, this sect taught the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path as the way to end suffering and attain nirvāṇa, the state of cessation of craving and ignorance. They rejected the authority of the Vedas, the caste system, and the rituals, and emphasized the concepts of karma, rebirth, and dependent origination. They also did not accept the existence of a permanent and independent self or soul (ātman), and instead proposed the doctrine of anātman or no-self. They developed various schools and traditions, such as Theravāda, Mahāyāna, and Vajrayāna, and spread across Asia and beyond.

- Jainism: Founded by Mahāvīra, also known as the Jina, in the 6th century BCE, this sect taught the doctrine of ahiṃsā or non-violence as the supreme principle of conduct. They rejected the authority of the Vedas, the caste system, and the rituals, and emphasized the concepts of karma, rebirth, and liberation. They also affirmed the existence of an eternal and indivisible self or soul (jīva), which is distinct from the non-living matter (ajīva). They practiced a rigorous asceticism and divided into two main sects, the Digambaras and the Śvetāmbaras.
