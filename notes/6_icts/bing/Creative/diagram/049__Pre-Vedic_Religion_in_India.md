Pre-Vedic religion in India refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the people who lived in the Indian subcontinent before the arrival of the Indo-Aryan migrants who brought the Vedic religion with them. The most prominent example of pre-Vedic religion is the Indus Valley Civilization, which flourished from about 2500 BCE to 1500 BCE. The Indus Valley Civilization had a complex urban culture, with sophisticated architecture, writing, trade, and technology. The religion of the Indus Valley people is not well understood, as their script has not been deciphered and their texts have not survived. However, some clues can be gathered from the archaeological evidence, such as seals, figurines, pottery, and ritual objects.

One of the most distinctive features of the Indus Valley religion was the worship of a mother goddess, who may have represented fertility, nature, or creation. She is often depicted as standing on a lotus, holding a plant, or surrounded by animals. Another important deity was a horned male figure, who may have been a god of animals, agriculture, or water. He is sometimes shown sitting in a yogic posture, with two or four horns, and surrounded by animals. Some scholars have identified him with the later Hindu god Shiva, who also has horns and is associated with animals and yoga. Other deities in the Indus Valley religion included a tree god, a snake god, and a bull god. The Indus Valley people also worshipped natural forces, such as the sun, the moon, the stars, the fire, and the water.

The Indus Valley religion had a strong emphasis on ritual purity and cleanliness. The people built elaborate drainage systems, baths, and wells to ensure a constant supply of water. They also practiced ritual bathing, washing, and ablutions, possibly as a way of purifying themselves before worshipping the gods. The Indus Valley religion also involved the use of fire altars, where sacrifices and offerings were made to the gods. The fire altars were usually rectangular or circular, and were made of bricks or stones. The Indus Valley people also buried their dead, sometimes with grave goods, such as pottery, jewelry, and figurines. The burial practices may have reflected a belief in an afterlife or a reverence for the ancestors.
