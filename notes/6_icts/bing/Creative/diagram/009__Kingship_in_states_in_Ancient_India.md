Kingship in ancient India was a complex and dynamic phenomenon that involved both secular and religious aspects. The meaning and significance of kingship changed dramatically between the Vedic and Later Vedic period, and underwent further development under the times of the Jain and Buddhist rulers. The sources of the king's power, his role in the cosmic order, his relationship with his subjects, and his duties and responsibilities were all subjects of debate and reflection in various texts and traditions. The diagram shows the four main varnas or social classes in ancient India, and their respective roles and powers. The Brahmins were the priests and scholars who performed rituals and taught the sacred texts. The Kshatriyas were the warriors and rulers who protected the society and maintained order. The Vaishyas were the merchants and farmers who produced and traded goods and services. The Shudras were the laborers and servants who performed various tasks for the other classes.

The king was the supreme authority in the state, but he was not considered divine or above the law. He was expected to follow the dharma or the moral and religious duty, and to rule with justice and benevolence. He was also dependent on the support and advice of the Brahmins, who instructed him in his duties and guided his spiritual practice. The king was also accountable to the samiti or the governing assembly, which was composed of representatives from the different classes and groups in the society. The samiti had the power to elect, advise, and even depose the king in some cases.

The king's role was also linked to the cosmic order and the gods. He was seen as the representative of the god Vishnu, who incarnated as Rama and Krishna, the ideal kings in the epics. He was also seen as the upholder of the rita or the natural and moral order, and the performer of the royal sacrifices, which ensured the prosperity and harmony of the state and the cosmos. The king was also responsible for the welfare and protection of his subjects, especially the weak and the oppressed. He was expected to provide them with security, justice, education, and charity. He was also expected to patronize the arts, sciences, and religions, and to promote the culture and civilization of his state.
