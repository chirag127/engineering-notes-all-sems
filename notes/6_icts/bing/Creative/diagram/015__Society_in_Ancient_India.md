This diagram shows the four main varnas or classes that were recognized by the ancient Indian texts such as the Vedas and the Manusmriti. The Brahmins were the highest and most respected class, as they performed religious rituals and preserved the sacred knowledge. The Kshatriyas were the second class, as they protected the society and administered justice. The Vaishyas were the third class, as they engaged in trade and agriculture. The Shudras were the lowest class, as they served the other three classes and did menial work. The Dalits were not even considered part of the varna system, as they were seen as impure and polluted by their occupations, such as scavenging, leather work, and cremation. They faced discrimination and oppression from the rest of the society.

The diagram also shows the direction of hierarchy and mobility in the ancient Indian society. The arrows point downwards, indicating that the higher classes had more power and privileges than the lower classes, and that the lower classes had to obey and respect the higher classes. The arrows also indicate that it was very difficult, if not impossible, for a person to move up or down the varna system, as it was determined by birth and karma. The only exception was the Dalits, who could sometimes be accepted into the Shudra class if they converted to a different religion, such as Buddhism or Islam.

The diagram does not show the sub-divisions and variations within each varna, such as the jatis or castes, which were based on occupation, region, and lineage. The diagram also does not show the ashrams or stages of life, which were based on age, gender, and duty. The four ashrams were brahmacharya (student), grihastha (householder), vanaprastha (retired), and sannyasa (renunciant). The diagram also does not show the diversity and complexity of the ancient Indian society, which was influenced by many factors, such as geography, culture, religion, and history. The diagram is a simplified and generalized representation of the idealized social order that was prescribed by the ancient Indian texts, but not necessarily followed by the actual people.
