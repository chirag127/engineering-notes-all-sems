Buddhist literature is a vast and diverse field of study that encompasses the writings of various Buddhist schools and traditions in different languages and genres. One of the main divisions of Buddhist literature is based on the language of the texts: Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit.

Pali is an ancient Indian language that is closely related to the dialects spoken by the Buddha in the 5th century BCE. It is the canonical language of Theravada Buddhism, the dominant form of Buddhism in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Pali literature includes the Pali Canon (Tipitaka), which consists of three collections of scriptures: the Vinaya Pitaka (rules of conduct for monks and nuns), the Sutta Pitaka (discourses of the Buddha and his disciples) and the Abhidhamma Pitaka (systematic analysis of Buddhist doctrine). Pali literature also includes commentaries, sub-commentaries, chronicles, biographies, poems and other works that explain and elaborate on the teachings of the Pali Canon.

Prakrit is a generic term for the vernacular languages that evolved from Sanskrit over time and were spoken by various regions and communities in ancient and medieval India. Prakrit literature includes the texts of the early Buddhist schools that were not part of the Theravada tradition, such as the Sarvastivada, the Dharmaguptaka, the Mahasanghika and the Mahisasaka. These texts are collectively known as the Agamas, which are parallel to the Pali Nikayas in content and structure. Prakrit literature also includes the texts of the Gandharan Buddhist tradition, which are written in the Gandhari language, a Prakrit dialect that was used in the northwestern region of India (now Pakistan and Afghanistan) from the 3rd century BCE to the 4th century CE. The Gandhari texts are mostly fragmentary and include sutras, vinaya, abhidharma and avadana (stories of the Buddha and his previous lives).

Sanskrit is the classical and literary language of ancient and medieval India, which was used by the Brahmanical, Hindu, Jain and Buddhist traditions. Sanskrit literature includes the texts of the Mahayana and Vajrayana schools of Buddhism, which emerged around the 1st century CE and developed new doctrines, practices and scriptures that were different from the early Buddhist schools. Sanskrit literature also includes the texts of the Madhyamaka and Yogacara schools of philosophy, which were influential in shaping the Mahayana and Vajrayana views of reality, emptiness and mind. Sanskrit literature also includes the texts of the Buddhist Tantras, which are esoteric and ritualistic teachings that involve the use of mantras, mudras, mandalas and deities.
