The Harappan script, also known as the Indus script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilization. It is not yet deciphered, and the language it represents is unknown. The script consists of about 400 distinct signs, mostly pictographic or geometric, that were inscribed on seals, pottery, metal objects, and other artifacts. The script is written from right to left, sometimes in a boustrophedon style. The following diagram illustrates the basic architecture of a Harappan script inscription:

Each box represents a sign or a symbol. The signs are arranged in horizontal rows, with a maximum of 17 signs per row. The rows are separated by horizontal lines. The signs are read from right to left, starting from the top row. Sometimes, the direction of writing changes after each row, creating a zigzag pattern. This is called boustrophedon writing. The signs vary in size and shape, and some of them have variants or modifiers. The meaning and function of the signs are not known, but some of them may represent animals, plants, objects, numbers, or sounds. The script may have been used for religious, administrative, or commercial purposes, or for personal identification. The script was used from about 3500 BCE to 1900 BCE, and it was found in various regions of the Indus Valley and beyond, such as Afghanistan, Iran, and Mesopotamia. The script is one of the oldest and most mysterious writing systems in the world.
