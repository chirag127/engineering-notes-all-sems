### Buddhism in India

- Buddhism is an ancient Indian religion that originated in the 6th century BCE in the region of Magadha (now Bihar) and spread throughout northern India and beyond.
- Buddhism is based on the teachings and life experiences of Siddhartha Gautama, also known as the Buddha (the awakened one), who attained enlightenment under a bodhi tree and taught the four noble truths and the eightfold path to end suffering and achieve nirvana.
- Buddhism has three main branches: Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana, each with different doctrines, practices and traditions. Theravada is the oldest and most conservative branch, followed mainly in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Mahayana is the largest and most diverse branch, followed mainly in East Asia and Central Asia. Vajrayana is the smallest and most esoteric branch, followed mainly in Tibet, Nepal and Mongolia.
- Buddhism flourished in India for over a thousand years, from the 3rd century BCE to the 12th century CE, and produced many schools, sects, texts, art, architecture and culture. Buddhism also influenced other Indian religions, such as Hinduism, Jainism and Sikhism, and vice versa.
- Buddhism in India faced many challenges and declines, such as the rise of Hindu revivalism, the invasions of foreign rulers, the corruption and degeneration of monastic institutions, and the loss of patronage and support from the masses. Buddhism eventually disappeared from most parts of India by the 13th century CE, except for some pockets in the Himalayan regions and the south.
- Buddhism in India was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries, due to the efforts of reformers, scholars, missionaries, social movements and political leaders. Some of the notable figures who contributed to the revival of Buddhism in India are Anagarika Dharmapala, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Dalai Lama, Bhikkhu Bodhi and Thich Nhat Hanh.
- Buddhism in India today is a minority religion, with about 8.4 million followers, or 0.7% of the population, according to the 2011 census. Most of the Buddhists in India belong to the Dalit, tribal or Himalayan communities, who converted to Buddhism as a way of emancipation, empowerment and identity. Buddhism in India also attracts many foreign tourists, pilgrims and seekers, who visit the ancient Buddhist sites and learn from the contemporary Buddhist teachers.
