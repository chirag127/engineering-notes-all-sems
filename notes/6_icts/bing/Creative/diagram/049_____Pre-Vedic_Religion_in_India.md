### Pre-Vedic Religion in India

- Pre-Vedic religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the people who lived in the Indus Valley or Harappan civilization, before the arrival of the Indo-Aryan speakers who brought the Vedic religion to India.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was mainly based on the worship of nature, such as trees, animals, rivers, mountains, and celestial bodies. Some of the prominent deities were the Mother Goddess, the Horned God, the Pashupati (Lord of Animals), and the Proto-Shiva.
- The Pre-Vedic religion also involved the use of symbols, such as the swastika, the pipal tree, the bull, and the unicorn, which later became part of the Vedic and Hindu traditions. The Pre-Vedic religion also had a concept of ritual purity, as evidenced by the presence of bathing tanks and drainage systems in the Harappan cities.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was not based on any written scriptures, but on oral traditions and symbols. The Harappan script, which has not been deciphered yet, may have been used for administrative or commercial purposes, rather than for religious communication.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was gradually influenced and assimilated by the Vedic religion, which was brought by the Indo-Aryan speakers who migrated to India from the Central Asian steppes around 1500 BCE. The Vedic religion was based on the worship of many gods, such as Indra, Varuna, Agni, Surya, and Rudra, through the performance of sacrificial rituals, as described in the Vedas, the oldest sacred texts of Hinduism.
- The Vedic religion also incorporated some elements of the Pre-Vedic religion, such as the reverence for the cow, the worship of the fire, and the belief in reincarnation and karma. The Vedic religion also developed a complex system of social classes, known as the varna system, which divided the society into four main groups: the Brahmins (priests), the Kshatriyas (warriors), the Vaishyas (merchants), and the Shudras (servants).
- The Vedic religion underwent many changes and transformations over time, giving rise to various schools of thought and movements, such as the Upanishads, the Sramanas, the Bhakti, and the Tantra, which shaped the diversity and richness of Hinduism as we know it today.
