### Buddhist Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Buddhist literature is the collection of writings that preserve the teachings and traditions of Buddhism.
- Buddhist literature can be divided into two main categories: canonical and non-canonical.
- Canonical literature refers to the scriptures that are considered authoritative and authentic by various Buddhist schools and traditions. Non-canonical literature refers to the writings that are not accepted as canonical by all Buddhists, but may have historical, doctrinal, or literary value.
- The canonical literature of Buddhism is written in various languages, depending on the region and time period of its composition. The most prominent languages are Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit.
- Pali is an ancient language that is closely related to Sanskrit, but has some differences in grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation. It is said that the Buddha delivered his sermons in Pali, or a dialect similar to it, and that his disciples recorded them in the same language. Pali is the canonical language of Theravada Buddhism, the oldest and most conservative branch of Buddhism, which is mainly practiced in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. The Pali canon, also known as the Tipitaka or the Three Baskets, consists of three main collections: the Vinaya Pitaka (the Basket of Discipline), the Sutta Pitaka (the Basket of Discourses), and the Abhidhamma Pitaka (the Basket of Higher Teachings). The Pali canon also includes some non-canonical texts, such as commentaries, sub-commentaries, chronicles, and poems.
- Prakrit is a generic term for any language that is derived from or influenced by Sanskrit, but is not Sanskrit itself. Prakrit languages were spoken by various people in ancient India, especially in the regions of Magadha, Kosala, and Avanti. Prakrit languages are the canonical languages of Mahayana Buddhism, the more liberal and diverse branch of Buddhism, which is mainly practiced in East Asia and Tibet. The Prakrit canon, also known as the Agama or the Four Agamas, consists of four main collections: the Dirgha Agama (the Long Discourses), the Madhyama Agama (the Middle-Length Discourses), the Samyukta Agama (the Connected Discourses), and the Ekottara Agama (the Numerical Discourses). The Prakrit canon also includes some non-canonical texts, such as treatises, stories, and hymns.
- Sanskrit is the classical language of ancient India, which was used for literature, philosophy, religion, and science. Sanskrit is the canonical language of Vajrayana Buddhism, the esoteric and tantric branch of Buddhism, which is mainly practiced in Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan, and Mongolia. The Sanskrit canon, also known as the Kangyur or the Translated Words, consists of two main collections: the Sutra section (the Discourses) and the Tantra section (the Esoteric Teachings). The Sanskrit canon also includes some non-canonical texts, such as commentaries, biographies, and manuals.
