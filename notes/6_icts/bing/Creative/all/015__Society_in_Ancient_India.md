### Society in Ancient India

- The society in ancient India was based on the concept of **varna**, which means "color" or "class". The varna system divided the people into four main categories: **Brahmins** (priests and scholars), **Kshatriyas** (warriors and rulers), **Vaishyas** (merchants and farmers), and **Shudras** (servants and laborers). 
- The varna system was not rigid or hereditary in the early Vedic period (c. 1500-1000 BCE), when the Indo-Aryans migrated to India and composed the Vedas, the oldest scriptures of Hinduism. People could change their varna based on their deeds, skills, and qualities. The Vedas also mention a fifth category of people, called **Adivasis** (original inhabitants) or **Nishadas** (hunters and fishermen), who lived outside the varna system and were considered inferior by the Indo-Aryans. 
- The varna system became more complex and hierarchical in the later Vedic period (c. 1000-600 BCE), when the Indo-Aryans expanded their settlements and came into contact with other cultures and civilizations. The Brahmins and Kshatriyas claimed the highest status and authority, while the Vaishyas and Shudras were subordinated to them. The Adivasis or Nishadas were further marginalized and oppressed, and a new category of people, called **Dalits** (untouchables) or **Chandalas** (outcasts), emerged as a result of inter-varna marriages or social transgressions. The Dalits were assigned the most polluted and menial occupations, such as scavenging, leatherwork, and cremation, and were excluded from the mainstream society. 
- The varna system was influenced by the concept of **karma**, which means "action" or "deed". Karma is the law of cause and effect, which states that one's actions in the present life determine one's destiny in the next life. The varna system was seen as a way of ensuring the proper performance of one's duties (dharma) according to one's karma. The varna system was also linked to the concept of **samsara**, which means "cycle of rebirth". Samsara is the belief that the soul (atman) transmigrates from one body to another until it attains liberation (moksha) from the cycle of birth and death. 
- The society in ancient India was also based on the concept of **ashrama**, which means "stage of life". The ashrama system divided the life of each individual into four main stages: **Brahmacharya** (student), **Grihastha** (householder), **Vanaprastha** (retiree), and **Sannyasa** (renunciant). Each stage had its own duties, responsibilities, and goals, and was meant to help the individual achieve spiritual growth and fulfillment. The ashrama system was mainly applicable to the upper three varnas, especially the Brahmins, who were expected to follow the four stages in order. The lower varnas and the outcastes had limited or no access to the ashrama system. 
- The society in ancient India was characterized by diversity and dynamism, as it witnessed the rise and fall of various kingdoms, empires, and civilizations. Some of the major civilizations that flourished in ancient India were the **Indus Valley Civilization** (c. 2500-1900 BCE), the **Mahajanapada** (c. 600-300 BCE), the **Mauryan Empire** (c. 321-185 BCE), the **Gupta Empire** (c. 320-550 CE), and the **Harsha Empire** (c. 606-647 CE). These civilizations contributed to the development of art, architecture, literature, science, technology, religion, philosophy, and culture in ancient India.
