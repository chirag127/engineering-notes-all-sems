### Evolution of script in India

- A script is a collection of characters used to write one or more languages. A language is a method of communication. Scripts are writing systems that allow the transcription of a language, via alphabet sets.
- The first evidence of a writing system in India can be seen in the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC), which flourished from 2500 BCE to 1900 BCE. The Indus script is a logo-syllabic script, which means it consists of symbols that represent words or syllables. The script is still undeciphered, and its relation to any other language family is unknown .
- The Indus script was used mostly on seals, pottery, and copper tablets. The longest inscription found has 34 symbols, but most inscriptions are very short, with 5 symbols on average. The script was written from right to left, sometimes in a boustrophedonic (alternating direction) style. The number of principal signs is about 400, which is too large for each sign to represent a single sound.
- The Indus script fell out of use after the decline of the IVC around 1900 BCE. The next major script to emerge in India was the Brahmi script, which is the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts. The Brahmi script was developed around the 3rd century BCE, under the influence of Semitic scripts such as Aramaic and Phoenician .
- The Brahmi script was an alphasyllabary, which means it had symbols for consonants and vowels, and each consonant had an inherent vowel that could be modified by diacritics. The Brahmi script was written from left to right, and had about 40 to 50 signs. The script was used to write various languages, such as Prakrit, Sanskrit, Pali, and Tamil .
- The Brahmi script evolved into different regional variants over time, depending on the linguistic and cultural factors. Some of the major scripts that derived from Brahmi are:

  - Devanagari: The script used for Hindi, Marathi, Nepali, and Sanskrit. It developed from the northern variant of Brahmi, called Gupta script, around the 7th century CE. It has a horizontal line at the top of each letter, called shirorekha, and a dot above the letter, called anusvara, to indicate nasalization.
  - Bengali-Assamese: The script used for Bengali, Assamese, and some other languages of eastern India and Bangladesh. It developed from the eastern variant of Brahmi, called Siddhamatrika, around the 10th century CE. It has a distinctive triangular shape for some letters, and a hook at the bottom of each letter, called matra, to indicate vowel length.
  - Gujarati: The script used for Gujarati and some other languages of western India. It developed from the western variant of Brahmi, called Nagari, around the 12th century CE. It has a similar shape to Devanagari, but without the shirorekha. It also has a small circle below the letter, called nukta, to indicate foreign sounds.
  - Gurmukhi: The script used for Punjabi and some other languages of northwestern India and Pakistan. It developed from the Landa script, which was a variant of Sharada, which was a descendant of Brahmi, around the 16th century CE. It has a horizontal line at the bottom of each letter, called kanna, and a dot below the letter, called bindi, to indicate nasalization.
  - Oriya: The script used for Oriya and some other languages of eastern India. It developed from the eastern variant of Brahmi, called Kalinga, around the 10th century CE. It has a curved shape for some letters, and a slash below the letter, called khaṇḍa, to indicate vowel length.
  - Tamil: The script used for Tamil and some other languages of southern India and Sri Lanka. It developed from the southern variant of Brahmi, called Tamil-Brahmi, around the 3rd century BCE. It has a round shape for some letters, and a dot above the letter, called puḷḷi, to indicate the absence of a vowel.
