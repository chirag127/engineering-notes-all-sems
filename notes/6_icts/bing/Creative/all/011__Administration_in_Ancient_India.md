### Administration in Ancient India

- Administration in ancient India was not uniform or centralized, but varied according to different periods, regions, and political systems.
- The earliest evidence of administration in India comes from the Indus Valley civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which had a well-organized urban planning, drainage system, standardized weights and measures, and a writing system. The nature and extent of the authority that governed these cities is not clear, but some scholars suggest that it was a theocratic or oligarchic system.
- The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) saw the emergence of tribal kingdoms and republics, which were governed by assemblies of elders and warriors, such as the sabha and the samiti. The king (raja) was the nominal head of the state, but his power was limited by the customs and laws (dharma) of the tribe. The king was assisted by various officials, such as the purohita (priest), the senani (army chief), and the gramani (village headman).
- The later Vedic period (c. 600-300 BCE) witnessed the rise of large territorial states, known as mahajanapadas, which were either monarchies or oligarchies. The administration became more centralized and hierarchical, with the king having more power and authority. The king was advised by a council of ministers (mantriparishad) and a general assembly (mahasabha). The king also appointed various officials, such as the rajjukas (surveyors), the sannidhatas (treasurers), and the dandapalas (police officers). The state collected taxes from the people, such as the bali (tribute), the bhaga (share), and the shulka (toll).
- The Mauryan empire (c. 321-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian empire, which established a highly centralized and bureaucratic administration. The emperor (samrat) was the supreme ruler, who had absolute power and authority. The emperor was assisted by a council of ministers (mantriparishad) and a chief minister (amatya). The empire was divided into provinces (pradesha), districts (ahara), and villages (grama), each headed by a governor (kumara), a superintendent (sthapati), and a headman (gramika), respectively. The state maintained a large army, a secret service, and a judicial system. The state also collected various taxes, such as the kara (land tax), the bhaga (share), and the pindakara (poll tax). The most famous Mauryan emperor, Ashoka (c. 268-232 BCE), also propagated his moral and religious edicts (dhamma) through inscriptions on pillars and rocks.
- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE-300 CE) saw the fragmentation of the empire and the emergence of various regional kingdoms, such as the Sungas, the Kanvas, the Satavahanas, the Kushanas, and the Guptas. The administration of these kingdoms was largely influenced by the Mauryan model, but with some variations and adaptations. The kings were generally benevolent and tolerant, and patronized various religions and arts. The kings also delegated some of their powers and responsibilities to local bodies, such as the village councils (gramasabha), the guilds (shreni), and the corporations (nigama). The state also collected various taxes, such as the bali (tribute), the bhoga (produce), and the shulka (toll).
- The medieval period (c. 300-1200 CE) witnessed the invasion and settlement of various foreign tribes and dynasties, such as the Huns, the Pallavas, the Chalukyas, the Rashtrakutas, the Pratiharas, the Palas, the Cholas, the Chahamanas, and the Ghaznavids. The administration of these kingdoms was largely feudal and decentralized, with the kings granting lands and privileges to their vassals (samanta) and subordinates (mandaleshvara) in exchange for loyalty and military service. The kings also maintained a large army, a navy, and a spy network. The kings also patronized various religions, arts, and sciences.
