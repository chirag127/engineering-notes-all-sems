### Modern religious practices in India

- India is a secular country with a diverse and pluralistic religious landscape. According to the 2011 census, the major religions practiced in India are Hinduism (79.8%), Islam (14.2%), Christianity (2.3%), Sikhism (1.7%), Buddhism (0.7%) and Jainism (0.4%). 
- Religion plays a significant role in the lives of most Indians, as reflected in various religious practices, rituals, festivals, pilgrimages, and beliefs. Some of the common religious practices in India are:
  - Praying: Most Indians pray daily (60%) and visit a house of worship at least monthly (71%). Praying can be done individually or collectively, at home or in a temple, mosque, church, gurdwara, or other sacred places. Praying can involve reciting mantras, verses, hymns, or names of God, or expressing gratitude, supplication, or devotion.
  - Reading or listening to scripture: Scripture refers to the sacred texts of different religions, such as the Vedas, Upanishads, Bhagavad Gita, Ramayana, and Mahabharata for Hindus; the Quran, Hadith, and Sunnah for Muslims; the Bible for Christians; the Guru Granth Sahib for Sikhs; the Tripitaka for Buddhists; and the Agamas and Tattvartha Sutra for Jains. Reading or listening to scripture can be done for spiritual guidance, moral instruction, or ritual purposes. Most Indians read or listen to scripture on a yearly basis or less often (58%).
  - Performing rites and ceremonies: Rites and ceremonies are religious acts that mark important occasions or transitions in life, such as birth, naming, initiation, marriage, death, and ancestral worship. They can also be done for auspiciousness, purification, protection, or prosperity. Some examples of rites and ceremonies are namkaran (naming ceremony), upanayana (sacred thread ceremony), vivaha (wedding ceremony), antyeshti (funeral rites), shraddha (ancestral rites), puja (worship), havan (fire sacrifice), and zakat (alms-giving).
  - Celebrating festivals: Festivals are religious or cultural events that celebrate or commemorate significant events, personalities, or seasons. They can involve fasting, feasting, exchanging gifts, decorating homes, lighting lamps, bursting crackers, singing, dancing, or taking part in processions. Some examples of festivals are Diwali (festival of lights), Holi (festival of colors), Eid al-Fitr (festival of breaking the fast), Eid al-Adha (festival of sacrifice), Christmas (festival of the birth of Jesus), Vaisakhi (festival of harvest and Sikh new year), Buddha Purnima (festival of the birth, enlightenment, and death of Buddha), and Mahavir Jayanti (festival of the birth of Mahavira).
  - Going on pilgrimages: Pilgrimages are journeys to sacred places or shrines that are associated with a deity, a saint, a guru, or a historical event. Pilgrimages can be done for seeking blessings, fulfilling vows, expressing devotion, or attaining salvation. Some examples of pilgrimage sites are Varanasi (the holy city of Hinduism), Mecca (the birthplace of Prophet Muhammad and the direction of prayer for Muslims), Jerusalem (the holy city of Christianity, Judaism, and Islam), Amritsar (the location of the Golden Temple, the holiest shrine of Sikhism), Bodh Gaya (the place where Buddha attained enlightenment), and Palitana (the place where many Jain Tirthankaras attained liberation).
  - Practicing yoga and meditation: Yoga and meditation are spiritual disciplines that aim to harmonize the body, mind, and soul. Yoga involves physical postures, breathing exercises, and ethical principles. Meditation involves focusing the mind on a single object, thought, or mantra, or observing the thoughts and sensations without judgment. Yoga and meditation can be done for health, well-being, relaxation, or enlightenment. However, not all Indians practice yoga and meditation. In fact, 62% of Indians report never practicing yoga.
