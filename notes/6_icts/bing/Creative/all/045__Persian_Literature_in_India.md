### Persian Literature in India

- Persian literature in India refers to the literary works produced by Persian-speaking writers and poets in the Indian subcontinent, especially during the Delhi Sultanate, the Mughal Empire, and the Sikh Empire.
- Persian was the official language of most Muslim dynasties in the Indian subcontinent, such as the Delhi Sultanate, the Bengal Sultanate, the Bahmani Sultanate, the Mughal Empire and their successor states, and the Sikh Empire. It was also the dominant cultured language of poetry and literature. 
- Persian was introduced to India by various Turkic and Afghan rulers, such as the 11th-century Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi, who patronized Persian poets and scholars in his court. The earliest major Indo-Persian poets were Abu-al-Faraj Runi and Masud Sa'd Salman, who were based in Lahore.
- Persian literature in India reached its height under the Mughal Empire, which was founded by Babur, a Persianized Turkic ruler who spoke Chagatai Turkic as his first language. Babur and his successors, such as Humayun, Akbar, Jahangir, and Shah Jahan, were all well-versed in Persian poetry and literature, and encouraged its development in their courts.
- Some of the most famous Indo-Persian poets and writers were Amir Khusrau, Abdur Rahim Khan-i-Khanan, Faizi, Abul Fazl, Nizamuddin Auliya, Mirza Ghalib, Bedil, and Sirajuddin Ali Khan Arzu. They wrote on various themes, such as love, mysticism, history, ethics, politics, and religion, and used various forms, such as ghazal, masnavi, qasida, rubai, and tazkirah. 
- Persian literature in India also influenced the development of regional languages, such as Urdu, Hindi, Punjabi, Kashmiri, Bengali, and Deccani. Many of these languages borrowed words, phrases, idioms, and literary devices from Persian, and also adopted the Persian script for writing. Some of the regional poets and writers who were influenced by Persian literature were Mir Taqi Mir, Mirza Rafi Sauda, Mir Dard, Wali Deccani, Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, and Rabindranath Tagore. 
- Persian literature in India declined with the gradual deterioration of the Mughal Empire and the rise of British colonialism. English and Hindustani (Hindi-Urdu) overshadowed Persian in importance as the languages of administration, education, and communication. However, Persian literature in India still has a legacy and a relevance in the contemporary world, as it reflects the rich and diverse cultural heritage of the region.
