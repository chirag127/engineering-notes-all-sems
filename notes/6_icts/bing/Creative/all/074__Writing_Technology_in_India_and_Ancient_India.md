### Writing Technology in India and Ancient India

- India has a long history of writing, dating back to the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC) which flourished from 2500 to 1900 BCE. The IVC developed a script that is still undeciphered, but seems to have been logo-syllabic, meaning that it used symbols to represent both words and sounds. The script was written on seals, tablets, pottery, and other objects, and may have been used for trade, administration, or religious purposes.
- After the decline of the IVC, writing disappeared from India until the 3rd century BCE, when the Mauryan emperor Ashoka inscribed his edicts on rocks and pillars across his empire. Ashoka used two different scripts: Kharosthi and Brahmi. Kharosthi was derived from the Aramaic script, which was brought to India by the Persians. Brahmi was an indigenous script, which is considered the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts. Both Kharosthi and Brahmi were syllabic, meaning that they used symbols to represent syllables rather than individual sounds.
- Kharosthi was mainly used in the northwestern regions of India, and did not survive beyond the 4th century CE. Brahmi, on the other hand, spread throughout India and beyond, and gave rise to many regional variants and descendants. Some of the major scripts that evolved from Brahmi are:

  - Devanagari: The most widely used script in India today, used for Hindi, Marathi, Nepali, Sanskrit, and many other languages. Devanagari means "of the city of the gods", and is characterized by a horizontal line on top of the letters and a vertical line on the right side of the letters. Devanagari has 12 vowels and 33 consonants, and uses diacritical marks to modify the sounds of the letters.
  - Gujarati: The script used for Gujarati, the official language of the state of Gujarat. Gujarati is similar to Devanagari, but does not have the vertical line on the right side of the letters. Gujarati has 11 vowels and 31 consonants, and also uses diacritical marks to modify the sounds of the letters.
  - Bengali: The script used for Bengali, the official language of the state of West Bengal and the country of Bangladesh. Bengali is also similar to Devanagari, but has a more rounded shape and a horizontal line below the letters instead of above them. Bengali has 11 vowels and 35 consonants, and also uses diacritical marks to modify the sounds of the letters.
  - Gurmukhi: The script used for Punjabi, the official language of the state of Punjab. Gurmukhi means "from the mouth of the guru", and was developed by the Sikh gurus in the 16th century. Gurmukhi is also similar to Devanagari, but has a more angular shape and a horizontal line above and below the letters. Gurmukhi has 9 vowels and 35 consonants, and also uses diacritical marks to modify the sounds of the letters.
  - Tamil: The script used for Tamil, the official language of the state of Tamil Nadu and the country of Sri Lanka. Tamil is one of the oldest and most distinctive scripts in India, and has a more curvy and looped shape than the other scripts. Tamil has 12 vowels and 18 consonants, and does not use diacritical marks to modify the sounds of the letters. Instead, it uses separate symbols for each vowel-consonant combination.
  - Malayalam: The script used for Malayalam, the official language of the state of Kerala. Malayalam is closely related to Tamil, but has more letters and a more complex shape. Malayalam has 15 vowels and 36 consonants, and also does not use diacritical marks to modify the sounds of the letters. Instead, it uses separate symbols for each vowel-consonant combination.
  - Telugu: The script used for Telugu, the official language of the state of Andhra Pradesh. Telugu is also closely related to Tamil, but has a more rounded and flowing shape.
