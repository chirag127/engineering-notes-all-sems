### Six Systems of Indian Philosophy

The six systems of Indian philosophy are:

- **Nyaya**: The school of logic and epistemology, founded by Akshapada Gautama in the 2nd century BCE. It deals with the methods of acquiring valid knowledge and the criteria of truth. It also discusses topics such as perception, inference, comparison, testimony, causation, self, mind, and God. Nyaya is often combined with Vaisheshika, another school of Indian philosophy, as Nyaya-Vaisheshika.  

- **Vaisheshika**: The school of atomism and naturalism, founded by Kanada Kashyapa in the 6th century BCE. It deals with the classification and analysis of the physical world and its constituents. It postulates that all objects are composed of indivisible atoms (anu) of different types, and that the qualities and actions of objects are determined by the combinations of atoms. Vaisheshika also discusses topics such as substance, quality, action, relation, universality, particularity, and inherence.  

- **Samkhya**: The school of dualism and realism, founded by Kapila in the 6th century BCE. It deals with the distinction and relation between the two fundamental realities: purusha (consciousness or self) and prakriti (matter or nature). It postulates that prakriti consists of three modes or qualities (gunas): sattva (purity or harmony), rajas (activity or passion), and tamas (inertia or ignorance), and that it evolves into various forms of existence, including the mind, the senses, and the elements. Samkhya also discusses topics such as evolution, causation, liberation, and the role of the intellect.  

- **Yoga**: The school of discipline and practice, founded by Patanjali in the 2nd century BCE. It deals with the methods of attaining mental and physical control and liberation from suffering. It is based on the eightfold path (ashtanga) of yoga, which consists of: yama (restraints), niyama (observances), asana (postures), pranayama (breath control), pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), and samadhi (absorption). Yoga also discusses topics such as the nature and functions of the mind, the sources of knowledge, the obstacles to liberation, and the means of overcoming them. Yoga is closely related to Samkhya, but differs in its acceptance of the existence of God.  

- **Purva Mimamsa**: The school of ritual and exegesis, founded by Jaimini in the 3rd century BCE. It deals with the interpretation and application of the Vedic texts, especially the Brahmanas and the Samhitas, which prescribe various rituals and sacrifices. It postulates that the performance of the Vedic duties leads to the attainment of the desired results, both in this life and in the afterlife. Purva Mimamsa also discusses topics such as the nature and authority of the Vedas, the meaning and validity of words, the relation between action and result, and the role of the gods.  

- **Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta**: The school of metaphysics and monism, founded by Badarayana in the 2nd century BCE. It deals with the interpretation and application of the Upanishads, which are the philosophical and mystical portions of the Vedas. It postulates that the ultimate reality is Brahman, the absolute and impersonal essence of all existence, and that the individual self (atman) is identical with Brahman. Vedanta also discusses topics such as the nature and origin of the world, the relation between Brahman and the world, the means and goal of liberation, and the role of grace. Vedanta has several sub-schools, such as Advaita (non-dualism), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism), and Dvaita (dualism), which differ in their views on the nature and relation of Brahman, the self, and the world.
