### Buddhist Literature in Pali,Prakrit And Sanskrit

- Buddhist literature is the collection of texts that preserve the teachings and traditions of Buddhism.
- Buddhist literature can be divided into two main categories: canonical and non-canonical.
- Canonical literature refers to the texts that are considered authoritative and authentic by various Buddhist schools and traditions. These texts are also known as the Tipitaka (Pali) or Tripitaka (Sanskrit), meaning "three baskets", because they consist of three main sections: the Vinaya (monastic discipline), the Sutta (discourses) and the Abhidhamma (higher teachings).
- Non-canonical literature refers to the texts that are not part of the Tipitaka, but are still valued and studied by Buddhists. These texts include commentaries, sub-commentaries, biographies, histories, poetry, stories, and other genres.
- Buddhist literature was originally composed and transmitted orally, and later written down in various languages and scripts. The earliest written Buddhist texts are the Gandhari manuscripts, dating from the 1st century BCE to the 3rd century CE, written in the Gandhari language and the Kharosthi script.
- The most widely studied and influential Buddhist languages are Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit.
- Pali is an ancient Indic language that is closely related to Sanskrit, but has some differences in grammar, vocabulary and pronunciation. Pali is the language of the Theravada school of Buddhism, which is dominant in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Pali is also the language of the oldest and most complete collection of Buddhist canonical texts, the Pali Canon, which contains the four Nikayas (collections) of suttas, the Vinaya Pitaka, and the Abhidhamma Pitaka.
- Prakrit is a generic term for the vernacular languages that evolved from Sanskrit and were spoken in ancient India. Prakrits were the languages of the common people, as opposed to Sanskrit, which was the language of the elite and the sacred. There are many varieties of Prakrits, such as Magadhi, Sauraseni, Maharastri, Gandhari, and others. Prakrits were used by some early Buddhist schools, such as the Sarvastivada, the Mahasanghika, the Mahisasaka, and the Dharmaguptaka, to compose their versions of the Tipitaka, known as the Agamas. Prakrits were also used to write non-canonical Buddhist literature, such as the Jatakas (stories of the Buddha's previous lives), the Avadanas (stories of the Buddha and his disciples), the Asokavadana (the biography of King Asoka), and the Milindapanha (the dialogue between King Milinda and the monk Nagasena).
- Sanskrit is the classical and literary language of ancient India, derived from the Vedic language. Sanskrit is the language of the Brahmanical/Hindu tradition, as well as the language of the Mahayana school of Buddhism, which emerged around the 1st century CE and spread to Central Asia, China, Tibet, Japan, and other regions. Sanskrit is the language of the Mahayana sutras, such as the Prajnaparamita (perfection of wisdom), the Lotus (the teaching of the Buddha as the supreme teacher), the Lankavatara (the teaching of the Buddha as the ultimate reality), and the Vimalakirti (the teaching of the Buddha as the ideal layman). Sanskrit is also the language of the Mahayana Abhidharma, such as the Abhidharmakosa (the treasury of Abhidharma) by Vasubandhu, and the Mahayana commentaries, such as the Madhyamaka (middle way) by Nagarjuna, and the Yogacara (mind-only) by Asanga and Vasubandhu.
