# Evolution of script in India

- A script is a written symbolisation of speech. It consists of a set of characters that represent the sounds of a language.
- India has a long and rich history of writing. It has developed many scripts over time, influenced by various cultures, regions, and languages.
- The earliest known script in India is the Indus script, which was used by the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE). It is still undeciphered and its linguistic affiliation is unknown.
- The next major script in India is the Brahmi script, which was used to write the Prakrit languages, such as Pali and Magadhi, as well as Sanskrit. It is derived from the Aramaic script, which was brought to India by the Persian Empire. It is the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts, as well as some scripts in Sri Lanka, Tibet, and Southeast Asia.
- The Brahmi script evolved into different regional variants over time, such as Kharosthi, Gupta, Nagari, Siddham, and Sharada. These variants differed in their shapes, styles, and diacritical marks. They also adapted to the phonetic features of the languages they were used for, such as vowel length, aspiration, and retroflexion.
- The Brahmi script also gave rise to the cursive scripts, such as Nandinagari, Modi, Kaithi, and Bengali, which were used for faster and informal writing. These scripts were influenced by the writing materials, such as palm leaves, paper, and cloth, and the writing tools, such as pens, brushes, and styluses.
- The scripts in south India, such as Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and Malayalam, became more rounded and looped due to the use of palm leaves, which required smooth strokes to avoid tearing. These scripts also developed their own symbols for numerals, fractions, and punctuation.
- The scripts in north India, such as Devanagari, Gurmukhi, and Gujarati, became more angular and linear due to the use of paper, which allowed sharper strokes. These scripts also adopted the horizontal line above the letters, called the shirorekha, which helped to distinguish them from each other.
- The scripts in east India, such as Oriya, Assamese, and Tibetan, became more curved and ornate due to the use of cloth, which allowed more artistic expression. These scripts also developed their own vowel signs, consonant clusters, and modifiers.
- The scripts in west India, such as Sindhi, Khojki, and Kutchi, became more complex and diverse due to the influence of Arabic, Persian, and Urdu. These scripts also incorporated the dot, called the nukta, to represent sounds that were not native to the Indian languages.
- The scripts in central India, such as Landa, Mahajani, and Gondi, became more simplified and stylized due to the use of wood, stone, and metal, which required less detail and more durability. These scripts also developed their own symbols for currency, weights, and measures.
