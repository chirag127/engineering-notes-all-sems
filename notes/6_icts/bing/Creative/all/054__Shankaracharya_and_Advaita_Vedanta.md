### Shankaracharya and Advaita Vedanta

- Shankaracharya, also known as Shankara or Shankaracarya, was an Indian philosopher and theologian who expounded the doctrine of Advaita Vedanta, a school of Hindu philosophy that emphasizes the non-dual nature of reality.
- He was born in the 8th century CE, possibly in the village of Kaladi in Kerala, and became a renunciant at a young age. He studied under Govinda, a disciple of Gaudapada, who was influenced by Mahayana Buddhism and its concept of emptiness.
- He wrote commentaries on the Brahma Sutras, the principal Upanishads, and the Bhagavad Gita, the foundational texts of Vedanta. He also composed original works, such as the Upadesa Sahasri, the Vivekacudamani, and the Crest-Jewel of Discrimination, which explain his teachings in a more accessible way.
- He traveled across India, engaging in debates with other schools of thought, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Mimamsa, and various sects of Hinduism. He established four monastic centers (mathas) in the four corners of India, which are still active today. He also revived the worship of Shiva and Shakti, and composed hymns in their praise.
- He is regarded as one of the greatest thinkers and reformers of Hinduism, and his influence is felt in almost all aspects of Indian culture and spirituality. He is revered by the followers of Advaita Vedanta as an incarnation of Shiva, and by other Hindus as a saint and a teacher.
- His main teaching is that Brahman, the supreme reality, is the only reality, and that everything else, including the individual self (atman) and the phenomenal world, is an illusion (maya) or a superimposition (adhyasa) on Brahman. He argues that the Upanishads reveal the identity of atman and Brahman, and that the only way to realize this truth is through the direct knowledge (jnana) of one's own self, which is pure consciousness (cit).
- He advocates a threefold method of attaining self-knowledge: hearing (sravana), reflection (manana), and meditation (nididhyasana). He also recognizes the role of ethical conduct (dharma), devotion (bhakti), and renunciation (sannyasa) as preparatory steps for self-knowledge. He maintains that self-knowledge leads to liberation (moksha) from the cycle of birth and death (samsara), which is caused by ignorance (avidya) of one's true nature.
- He uses various logical arguments, scriptural interpretations, and metaphors to support his position and to refute the objections of his opponents. He employs the concept of two levels of reality: the absolute (paramarthika) and the empirical (vyavaharika). He also uses the analogy of the rope and the snake, the clay and the pot, the gold and the ornaments, and the dream and the waking state, to illustrate the relation between Brahman and the world.
- He acknowledges the existence of a personal God (Ishvara), who is the creator, preserver, and destroyer of the world, and who is also the object of devotion for the seekers of liberation. However, he regards Ishvara as a lower aspect of Brahman, who is impersonal, transcendent, and beyond all attributes. He also accepts the validity of the Vedas, the law of karma, and the doctrine of reincarnation, but only as provisional truths that are useful for the ignorant masses.
- He is criticized by some of his opponents for being too influenced by Buddhism, for denying the reality of the world, for undermining the authority of the Vedas, and for being too elitist and rationalistic. However, he is also praised by many for his profound insight, his logical acumen, his poetic eloquence, and his spiritual vision. He is widely regarded as the greatest exponent of Advaita Vedanta, and one of the most influential philosophers of India and the world.
