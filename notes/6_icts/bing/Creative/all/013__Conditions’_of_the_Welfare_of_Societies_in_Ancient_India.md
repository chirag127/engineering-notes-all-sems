### Conditions of the Welfare of Societies in Ancient India

- The concept of social welfare in ancient India was based on the notion of **duty** rather than **rights**. The individual was expected to perform one's duties in accordance with **dharma**, the moral and cosmic order, which would lead to the welfare of both the self and the society.
- The welfare of the society was also the responsibility of the **state** or the **king**, who was supposed to follow the **rajadharma**, the code of conduct for the rulers. The king was expected to protect the people, maintain justice, promote education, culture, trade, agriculture, and public works, and provide for the needs of the poor, the weak, and the oppressed .
- The ancient Indian society was divided into four **varnas** or classes: the Brahmins (priests and scholars), the Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), the Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and the Shudras (servants and laborers). Each varna had its own duties and privileges, and was supposed to cooperate with the other varnas for the common good.
- The ancient Indian society was also divided into four **ashramas** or stages of life: the Brahmacharya (student), the Grihastha (householder), the Vanaprastha (retired), and the Sannyasa (renunciant). Each ashrama had its own rules and obligations, and was supposed to prepare the individual for the next stage of life and ultimately for the liberation from the cycle of birth and death.
- The ancient Indian society also prescribed **sanskaras** or sacraments for the individuals at different stages of life. These were rites of passage that marked the important events and transitions in one's life, such as birth, initiation, marriage, death, etc. These sanskaras were meant to purify, sanctify, and educate the individual and the society.
- The ancient Indian society was based on the institution of **family**, which was the basic unit of social organization. The family was usually patriarchal, joint, and extended, with the eldest male as the head and the authority. The family was responsible for the upbringing, education, and welfare of its members, as well as for the performance of religious and social duties.
- The ancient Indian society also had rules and customs regarding **marriage**, which was considered a sacred and indissoluble bond between a man and a woman. The marriage was usually arranged by the parents or elders of the families, based on the compatibility of the horoscopes, the caste, the profession, and the social status of the partners. The marriage was also a union of two families, and involved various ceremonies and rituals.
- The ancient Indian society also had different views and practices regarding the **position of women**, which varied according to the time, place, and context. Generally, women were respected and honored as mothers, wives, and daughters, and were given education, freedom, and property rights in the early Vedic period. However, in the later periods, women's status declined and they were subjected to various restrictions, inequalities, and oppressions, such as child marriage, sati, purdah, etc .
- The ancient Indian society also had its own **dresses and ornaments**, which reflected the culture, climate, and status of the people. The common dress for men and women was the dhoti (a long cloth wrapped around the waist) and the uttariya (a shawl or scarf worn over the shoulders). The women also wore the sari (a long cloth draped around the body) and the blouse. The people also wore various ornaments made of gold, silver, pearls, gems, etc, such as earrings, necklaces, bracelets, rings, etc.
- The ancient Indian society also had its own **food habits**, which were influenced by the geography, climate, and religion of the people. The staple food was rice, wheat, barley, millets, pulses, vegetables, fruits, milk, ghee, etc. The people also ate meat, fish, eggs, etc, depending on their caste, region, and preference. The people also drank soma (a sacred plant juice), sura (an alcoholic beverage), and water.
