### Puppetry as Cultural Heritage of India

Puppetry is a form of theatre that involves the manipulation of puppets, which are figures that represent humans, animals, or mythical beings. Puppetry is one of the oldest and most diverse forms of artistic expression in India, with a rich and varied history that spans across regions, religions, and cultures. Puppetry is not only a source of entertainment, but also a medium of education, communication, and social commentary. Puppetry reflects the local traditions, values, and beliefs of the people who practice it, and also serves as a vehicle for preserving and transmitting their cultural heritage.

Some of the common features of Indian puppetry are:

- Puppetry is often based on the stories from the epics, such as the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, or from the Puranas, which are ancient Hindu texts that narrate the legends of gods and goddesses. Puppetry also incorporates folk tales, historical events, and contemporary issues.
- Puppetry is usually performed by a group of puppeteers, who belong to a specific caste, community, or family that has inherited the art form from their ancestors. Puppeteers are also skilled in singing, music, and narration, and sometimes use live or recorded music to accompany their shows.
- Puppetry is closely linked to the rituals, festivals, and ceremonies of the local people, and is often performed as a form of worship, offering, or blessing. Puppetry is also used as a means of social awareness, education, and empowerment, especially for the rural and marginalized sections of the society.
- Puppetry is a dynamic and evolving art form that adapts to the changing times and contexts. Puppeteers use various materials, techniques, and styles to create their puppets, such as wood, leather, cloth, paper, clay, metal, etc. Puppeteers also experiment with different forms of puppetry, such as glove, rod, string, shadow, etc., and sometimes combine them to create innovative effects.

Some of the prominent types of puppetry in India are:

- Glove puppetry: This is a form of puppetry where the puppeteer wears the puppet on his or her hand, and manipulates it with the fingers. Glove puppetry is popular in West Bengal, Orissa, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu, where it is known as beni putul, kundhei, pavakoothu, and bommalattam, respectively. Glove puppets are usually made of cloth or leather, and depict human or animal characters. Glove puppetry is often used to convey moral and social messages, and to satirize the political and social issues of the day.
- Rod puppetry: This is a form of puppetry where the puppeteer holds the puppet with a rod attached to its head or back, and moves it with the help of other rods attached to its limbs. Rod puppetry is prevalent in West Bengal, Bihar, and Rajasthan, where it is known as putul nach, yampuri, and kathputli, respectively. Rod puppets are usually made of wood, and depict human or animal characters. Rod puppetry is often used to narrate the stories from the epics, or to showcase the local culture and traditions.
- String puppetry: This is a form of puppetry where the puppeteer controls the puppet with a number of strings attached to its body parts. String puppetry is widespread in India, and is known by different names, such as kathputli in Rajasthan, gombeyatta in Karnataka, bommalattam in Tamil Nadu, bommalakoluvu in Andhra Pradesh, sakhi kandhei in Orissa, and kundhei nach in Assam. String puppets are usually made of wood, cloth, or leather, and depict human or animal characters. String puppetry is often used to enact the stories from the epics, or to display the regional dances and costumes.
- Shadow puppetry: This is a form of puppetry where the puppeteer manipulates the puppet behind a screen, and creates its shadow on the screen with the help of a light source.
