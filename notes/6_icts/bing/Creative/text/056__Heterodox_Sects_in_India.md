### Heterodox Sects in India

Heterodox sects are those that do not accept the authority of the Vedas and the brahmanical tradition. They are also called nāstika (deniers) or śramaṇa (renouncers) schools of Indian philosophy. Some of the major heterodox sects that emerged in ancient India are:

- **Ājīvika**: This sect was founded by Makkhali Gosāla in the 5th century BCE. It was a rival of Buddhism and Jainism. It taught the doctrine of niyati (fate) or determinism, which denied the existence of free will and karma. It also rejected the concepts of ātman (self), rebirth, and liberation. It believed that everything was composed of atoms and that the universe was governed by cosmic laws and forces. The Ājīvikas practiced asceticism and formed discrete communities. They reached the peak of their popularity under the Mauryan emperor Bindusara, but declined afterwards.  

- **Cārvāka**: This sect was also known as Lokāyata (worldly) or Bṛhaspatya (followers of Bṛhaspati). It was a materialist and rationalist school of thought that rejected the supernatural and the metaphysical. It denied the existence of God, soul, afterlife, and morality. It advocated a hedonistic and pragmatic way of life based on sensory perception and empirical evidence. It also criticized the Vedas, the caste system, and the rituals of the brahmanical religion. The Cārvāka school is attributed to Bṛhaspati, but its main exponent was Ajita Kesakambali.  

- **Jainism**: This sect was founded by Mahāvīra, the 24th and the last Tīrthaṅkara (ford-maker) in the 6th century BCE. He was a contemporary of the Buddha and a member of the Jñātrika clan. He preached the doctrine of anekāntavāda (many-sidedness) or non-absolutism, which recognized the validity of multiple perspectives and the relativity of truth. He also taught the principles of ahiṃsā (non-violence), satya (truth), asteya (non-stealing), brahmacarya (celibacy), and aparigraha (non-attachment) as the means to attain liberation from the cycle of birth and death. He emphasized the existence of jīva (soul) and ajīva (non-soul) as the two fundamental realities, and the role of karma and saṃsāra (transmigration) in binding the soul to the material world. The Jains practiced rigorous asceticism and followed the five vows (vratas) and the three jewels (ratnas) of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct. They also divided themselves into two major sects: the Digambaras (sky-clad) and the Śvetāmbaras (white-clad).  

- **Buddhism**: This sect was founded by Siddhārtha Gautama, also known as the Buddha (the awakened one) in the 6th century BCE. He was a prince of the Śākya clan who renounced his worldly life and attained enlightenment under the Bodhi tree. He taught the four noble truths (ārya-satyas) of suffering, its cause, its cessation, and the path leading to its cessation. He also taught the eightfold path (aṣṭāṅga-mārga) of right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration as the way to achieve nirvāṇa (extinction) or liberation from the cycle of birth and death. He rejected the concepts of ātman, brahman, and God, and advocated the doctrine of anātman (no-self) or dependent origination (pratītya-samutpāda).
