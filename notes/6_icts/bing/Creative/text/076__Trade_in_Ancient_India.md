### Trade in Ancient India

- Trade in ancient India was an important source of commerce and cultural exchange between different regions and civilizations.
- India has a long history of trade, dating back to the Harappan civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which had extensive contacts with Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Central Asia. The Harappans traded mainly in gold, copper, ivory, beads, textiles, and agricultural products. They used seals, weights, and measures to regulate their trade transactions. 
- After the decline of the Harappan civilization, trade continued in various forms and directions. The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) saw the emergence of new kingdoms and tribes, some of which engaged in trade and commerce with each other and with foreign lands. The Vedas mention trade in horses, cattle, grains, metals, gems, and spices. The Vedic people also used coins, called nishka, as a medium of exchange. 
- The later Vedic period (c. 600-300 BCE) witnessed the rise of urbanization and the development of trade networks across the subcontinent. The Mahajanapadas, or the sixteen great kingdoms, competed and cooperated with each other in trade and politics. Some of the important trade centers of this period were Rajagriha, Vaishali, Sravasti, Kosambi, Varanasi, Ujjain, Taxila, and Champa. Trade also expanded to the regions of South India, such as the Tamil kingdoms of Chola, Chera, and Pandya, which had maritime contacts with Southeast Asia and the Mediterranean. 
- The Mauryan Empire (c. 321-185 BCE) consolidated and centralized the trade and administration of most of India. The Mauryan rulers, especially Ashoka, promoted trade and commerce within and outside the empire. They built roads, bridges, rest houses, and wells to facilitate trade and travel. They also issued standardized coins, called karshapana, and maintained a network of spies and officials to regulate trade and collect taxes. The Mauryan trade was mainly in agricultural products, textiles, metals, gems, ivory, and spices. The Mauryans also traded with foreign countries, such as China, Persia, Greece, and Rome. The famous Greek ambassador, Megasthenes, visited the Mauryan capital of Pataliputra and wrote an account of its wealth and splendor. 
- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE-300 CE) saw the fragmentation of the empire and the emergence of new regional powers, such as the Shungas, the Satavahanas, the Kushanas, and the Guptas. Trade and commerce continued to flourish in this period, with new innovations and developments. The use of silver and gold coins increased, and new types of coins, such as the punch-marked coins, the die-struck coins, and the portrait coins, were introduced. The trade routes also diversified and extended, reaching as far as Central Asia, China, Southeast Asia, Africa, and Europe. The Silk Road, the Spice Route, and the Indian Ocean trade were some of the major trade routes of this period. The trade goods included silk, cotton, wool, indigo, pepper, cinnamon, ginger, cardamom, turmeric, pearls, coral, diamonds, rubies, sapphires, and lapis lazuli. The trade also facilitated the spread of ideas, religions, art, and literature across different regions and cultures. Buddhism, Jainism, Hinduism, and Christianity were some of the religions that benefited from trade and commerce.   
- Trade and commerce in ancient India reached its peak during the Gupta period (c. 320-550 CE), which is considered the golden age of Indian culture. The Gupta rulers, especially Chandragupta II, patronized trade and commerce and issued a variety of coins, such as the dinara, the suvarna, and the rupaka. The Gupta trade was mainly in agricultural products, textiles, metals, gems, and spices. The Guptas also traded with foreign countries, such as China, Persia, and Rome. The famous Chinese pilgrim, Faxian, visited India during the Gupta period and wrote an account of its prosperity and culture.
