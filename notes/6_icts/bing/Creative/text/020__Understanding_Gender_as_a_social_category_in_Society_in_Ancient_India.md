#### Understanding Gender as a social category in Society in Ancient India

- Gender as a social category refers to the social construction of differences and inequalities between men and women based on their biological sex, roles, expectations and norms.
- Gender equality means that the different behaviour, aspirations and needs of women and men are considered, valued and favoured equally, and that they have equal access to resources and opportunities.
- In ancient India, the status and position of women varied across different periods and regions, depending on the cultural, religious and political factors that influenced the society.
- In general, women in ancient India enjoyed a high degree of respect and dignity, especially in the Indus Valley Civilization, the early Vedic period, and the Buddhist and Jain traditions.
- However, women also faced discrimination and oppression in some aspects of their lives, such as marriage, inheritance, education, and religious rights, especially in the later Vedic period, the epic period, and the period of Dharmashastra and Purana.
- Some of the main points that illustrate the gender dynamics in ancient India are:

  - In the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500-1500 BCE), women had a respectable position in society and participated in various economic and religious activities. They wore elaborate jewellery and cosmetics, and some of them may have been priestesses or rulers.
  - In the early Vedic period (c. 1500-1000 BCE), women were revered as goddesses and mothers, and had access to education and religious ceremonies. They could attend public assemblies and voice their opinions. They had the freedom to choose their husbands and could remarry if widowed. There were many women sages, poets and scholars, such as Gargi, Maitreyi, Lopamudra and Ghosha .
  - In the later Vedic period (c. 1000-600 BCE), the position of women gradually deteriorated as the society became more patriarchal and hierarchical. Women lost their political rights and were confined to the domestic sphere. They were subjected to the authority of their fathers, husbands and sons, and had to follow the norms of patrilineal and patrilocal kinship. They were denied education and religious rites, and were expected to be obedient, faithful and chaste. The practices of child marriage, sati (self-immolation of widows), and dowry emerged in this period .
  - In the epic period (c. 600 BCE-300 CE), the two major epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, depicted the ideal and the real roles of women in society. The ideal role was that of a devoted wife and mother, who followed the virtues of pativrata (loyalty to husband) and matrivata (love for children). The real role was that of a victim of war, violence, abduction, rape, and humiliation. The epics also showed the courage, intelligence, and leadership of some women characters, such as Sita, Draupadi, Kunti, and Kaikeyi .
  - In the period of Dharmashastra and Purana (c. 300 BCE-1200 CE), the legal and religious texts codified the norms and duties of women in society. The Dharmashastra prescribed the four stages of life (ashrama) for men, but only two for women: brahmacharya (student) and grihastha (householder). Women were excluded from the other two stages: vanaprastha (retired) and sannyasa (renounced). The Dharmashastra also laid down the rules of inheritance, marriage, divorce, and widowhood, which favoured men over women. The Purana narrated the myths and legends of gods and goddesses, and reinforced the stereotypes of women as either virtuous or wicked, depending on their adherence to the patriarchal norms .
  - In the Buddhist and Jain traditions (c. 600 BCE-1200 CE), women were accorded respect and their rightful place in society. Both religions rejected the caste system and the ritual sacrifices of the Vedic religion, and advocated non-violence, compassion, and equality. Women were allowed to join the monastic orders and attain spiritual liberation. They also contributed to the spread of the teachings and the literature of these religions.
