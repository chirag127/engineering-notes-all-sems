### Writing Technology in India and Ancient India

- India has a long history of writing, dating back to the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC) which flourished from 2500 to 1900 BCE. The IVC developed a script that is still undeciphered, but seems to have been logo-syllabic, meaning that it used symbols to represent both words and syllables. The script was used to write on seals, pottery, copper tablets, and other objects. The script fell out of use after the decline of the IVC.
- The earliest deciphered scripts in India are the Brahmi and Kharosthi scripts, which emerged around the 3rd century BCE during the Mauryan Empire. Brahmi was a semi-alphabetic script that used symbols to represent consonants and diacritical marks to indicate vowels. Kharosthi was a syllabic script that used symbols to represent consonant-vowel combinations. Both scripts were used to write Prakrit, an ancient Indo-Aryan language, as well as other languages such as Sanskrit, Pali, and Gandhari.
- Brahmi and Kharosthi gave rise to many other scripts in India and Southeast Asia over the centuries. Brahmi evolved into various regional scripts, such as Gupta, Nagari, Grantha, Siddham, and Sharada, which in turn developed into modern scripts such as Devanagari, Bengali, Tamil, Malayalam, Telugu, Kannada, Sinhala, Khmer, Mon, Thai, Lao, Burmese, and others. Kharosthi influenced the scripts of Central Asia, such as Sogdian, Khotanese, and Tibetan.
- The scripts of India are generally classified into two main groups: the northern or Indic scripts, which are derived from Brahmi and are mostly written from left to right, and the southern or Dravidian scripts, which are derived from Grantha and are mostly written from right to left. The scripts of India are also characterized by their use of conjuncts, which are ligatures or combinations of two or more consonants, and their use of head strokes, which are horizontal lines that connect the letters in a word.
- The scripts of India have been influenced by various factors, such as religion, culture, politics, and technology. For example, the development of Buddhism and Jainism in ancient India led to the spread of writing and literacy among the masses, as well as the creation of new scripts and languages, such as Pali and Ardhamagadhi. The Islamic invasions and rule in medieval India introduced the Arabic and Persian scripts and languages, which influenced the scripts and languages of northern India, such as Urdu, Hindi, and Punjabi. The colonial rule of the British and other European powers introduced the Roman script and English language, which became the lingua franca of modern India and influenced the transliteration and standardization of Indian scripts and languages.
- The scripts of India have also adapted to the changes brought by modernity and technology. The invention of printing and typography led to the simplification and regularization of some scripts, such as Devanagari and Bengali, as well as the creation of new fonts and styles. The digitalization and globalization of communication and information led to the development of Unicode, which is a universal standard for encoding and displaying scripts on computers and the internet. The proliferation of Roman-alphabet keyboards and transliteration software enabled the users to type and publish online in Indian scripts more easily and widely. The increased use of Indian scripts on social media, advertisements, and entertainment also reflected the growing literacy and diversity of the Indian population.
