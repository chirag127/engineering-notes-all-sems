### Buddhist Literature in Pali,Prakrit And Sanskrit

- Buddhist literature is the collection of texts that preserve the teachings and traditions of Buddhism. It includes various genres, such as discourses, monastic rules, verses, stories, and philosophical treatises.
- Buddhist literature can be divided into two main categories: canonical and non-canonical. Canonical literature refers to the texts that are considered authoritative and authentic by the different Buddhist schools and traditions. Non-canonical literature refers to the texts that are not accepted as authoritative by all Buddhists, but may still have historical, cultural, or doctrinal value.
- The canonical literature of Buddhism is also known as the Tripitaka (Three Baskets), which consists of three main sections: the Sutra Pitaka (Basket of Discourses), the Vinaya Pitaka (Basket of Discipline), and the Abhidharma Pitaka (Basket of Higher Teaching).
- The canonical literature of Buddhism was originally composed and transmitted orally in various languages and dialects of ancient India, such as Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit. Pali and Prakrit are derived from Sanskrit, which was the classical language of Indian culture and literature. Pali is a form of Prakrit that was used as the main language of early Buddhism, especially in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Prakrit is a generic term for any language that differs from Sanskrit, and it includes several regional and literary dialects. Sanskrit was later adopted as the language of Buddhist literature by some schools and traditions, especially in Central and East Asia.
- The canonical literature of Buddhism was written down in different scripts and preserved in different regions and countries, such as India, Sri Lanka, China, Tibet, Nepal, and Japan. The oldest surviving manuscripts of Buddhist literature date back to the first centuries CE, and they are mostly written in Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit. Some of the earliest manuscripts of Buddhist literature are written in the Gandhari language and the Kharosthi script, which were used in the northwest of India and Central Asia. Other manuscripts of Buddhist literature are written in the Brahmi script, which was the common script of ancient India, and its derivatives, such as the Sinhala script, the Tibetan script, and the Devanagari script.
- The canonical literature of Buddhism is not identical in all traditions and schools, as different collections and editions were developed over time. The most widely studied and influential collections of Buddhist literature are the Pali Canon of the Theravada school, the Chinese Canon of the Mahayana and Vajrayana schools, and the Tibetan Canon of the Vajrayana school. These collections contain parallel texts that are shared by the early Buddhist schools, as well as texts that are unique to each tradition and school. The Pali Canon is the only complete collection of the early Buddhist literature that survives in its original language. The Chinese Canon and the Tibetan Canon are translations of the Buddhist literature from various languages, such as Pali, Prakrit, Sanskrit, and Gandhari, into Chinese and Tibetan, respectively.
- The canonical literature of Buddhism is the primary source of information and inspiration for Buddhist doctrine, practice, and history. It contains the teachings of the Buddha and his disciples, the rules and regulations of the monastic community, the analysis and classification of the phenomena of existence, the stories and legends of the Buddha and his previous lives, and the commentaries and interpretations of the Buddhist masters. The canonical literature of Buddhism is also a valuable resource for the study of Indian culture, language, and literature, as well as the history and development of Buddhism in Asia and beyond.
