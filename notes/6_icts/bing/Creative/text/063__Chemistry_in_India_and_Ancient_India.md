### Chemistry in India and Ancient India

Chemistry is the science of matter and its interactions. Chemistry in ancient India was called "Rasayana" in Sanskrit, which means "the art of obtaining the essence" of substances. Ancient Indian chemistry was not only a theoretical discipline, but also a practical one that involved various applications in medicine, metallurgy, cosmetics, alchemy, agriculture, and warfare. Here are some points to note about chemistry in India and ancient India:

- The earliest evidence of chemical techniques in India can be traced back to the Indus Valley Civilization (around 2500 BCE to 1500 BCE), which was skilled in pottery, faience, cement, dyeing, and metallurgy. The Harappans used various natural substances such as clay, limestone, gypsum, mica, quartz, copper oxide, iron oxide, and manganese oxide to produce different colors and textures of pottery and faience. They also knew how to fire pottery under different conditions of oxidation and reduction to achieve different effects. They also experimented with various mortars and cements to build their structures and seal their drains.   

- The Vedic period (around 1500 BCE to 700 CE) saw the development of various fermented drinks and methods of fermentation, such as soma, madhu, and sura. Soma was a sacred drink made from the juice of the soma plant, which was believed to have divine and healing properties. Madhu was a honey-based drink, and sura was a barley-based drink. The Vedas also mention various metals such as gold, silver, copper, iron, tin, lead, and mercury, and their uses in rituals, ornaments, weapons, and tools. The Vedas also describe the process of purifying metals by heating them with charcoal and blowing air through them.   

- The Ayurvedic period (around 700 BCE to 700 CE) was marked by the emergence of Ayurveda, the ancient system of medicine that is based on the balance of the five elements (earth, water, fire, air, and ether) and the three humors (vata, pitta, and kapha) in the body. Ayurveda used various plant and animal products, minerals, metals, and salts to prepare medicines and cosmetics. Ayurveda also developed various methods of extraction, distillation, sublimation, calcination, and incineration to obtain the essence of substances. Some of the important texts of Ayurveda are Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, and Ashtanga Hridaya.   

- The transitional period (around 700 CE to 1100 CE) was a time of interaction and exchange of ideas between Indian and foreign scholars, especially from the Arab world. The Arabs translated many Sanskrit texts on chemistry, medicine, astronomy, mathematics, and other sciences into Arabic, and also introduced some new concepts and techniques. Some of the notable Arab scholars who contributed to chemistry were Jabir ibn Hayyan (also known as Geber), al-Razi (also known as Rhazes), and al-Biruni. Some of the notable Indian scholars who contributed to chemistry were Nagarjuna, Sankara, and Vagbhata.  

- The Tantric period (around 700 CE to 1300 CE) was a period of experimentation and innovation in chemistry, especially in the field of alchemy. Alchemy was the art of transforming base metals into gold or silver, or creating the elixir of life that could bestow immortality. Alchemy was also a spiritual pursuit that aimed at the purification of the soul and the attainment of enlightenment. Alchemy involved various processes such as melting, dissolving, coagulating, filtering, crystallizing, and amalgamating. Some of the important texts of alchemy are Rasaratnakara, Rasarnava, and Rasendra Chintamani. Some of the important alchemists were Nagarjuna, Nityanatha, and Bhaskaracharya.
