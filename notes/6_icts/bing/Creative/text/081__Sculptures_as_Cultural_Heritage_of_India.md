### Sculptures as Cultural Heritage of India

- Sculpture is one of the oldest and most enduring forms of artistic expression in the Indian subcontinent, reflecting the diversity and richness of its culture, religions, and history.
- Sculpture in India can be broadly classified into three categories: stone, metal, and terracotta. Stone sculpture is the most prevalent and monumental, often adorning temples, stupas, pillars, and caves. Metal sculpture is mainly used for icons, votive images, and ritual objects, often made of bronze, copper, or brass. Terracotta sculpture is the most ancient and widespread, consisting of small figurines of humans and animals, often with painted or glazed surfaces.
- The earliest known sculpture in India is from the Indus Valley civilization (c. 2500-1800 BCE), which produced small terracotta figurines of female deities, animals, and toys, as well as seals with carved animal motifs. The Indus Valley civilization also had a sophisticated urban planning and drainage system, but its script and culture remain largely mysterious.
- The Mauryan period (c. 322-185 BCE) saw the emergence of large stone sculpture, especially the pillars of Ashoka, which were erected throughout the empire and bore edicts and symbols of the emperor's Buddhist faith. The pillars were topped by animal capitals, such as the famous four lions at Sarnath, which became the emblem of modern India. The Mauryan period also produced the first examples of rock-cut architecture, such as the Barabar Caves, which were carved out of solid rock and decorated with polished surfaces and inscriptions.
- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE-320 CE) witnessed the development of regional styles and schools of sculpture, influenced by various cultural and religious currents. The most prominent were the Gandhara and Mathura schools, which flourished in the northwestern and northern parts of India respectively. The Gandhara school was influenced by the Greco-Roman art and culture that came with the invasion of Alexander the Great and his successors. The Gandhara sculptures depict the Buddha and other Buddhist figures in a realistic and humanistic manner, often wearing Greek-style robes and hairstyles. The Mathura school, on the other hand, was more indigenous and eclectic, drawing from various sources such as Hinduism, Jainism, and Buddhism. The Mathura sculptures portray the Buddha and other deities in a more abstract and idealized manner, often with round faces, almond-shaped eyes, and curly hair. The Mathura school also produced the first images of Hindu gods and goddesses, such as Vishnu, Shiva, and Durga, as well as the Jina images of Jainism.
- The Gupta period (c. 320-550 CE) is considered the golden age of Indian sculpture, as well as other arts and sciences. The Gupta sculptures are characterized by a refined and graceful style, with smooth and polished surfaces, elegant proportions, and expressive gestures. The Gupta sculptures depict a variety of themes, such as the life and teachings of the Buddha, the legends and myths of Hinduism, and the scenes of courtly and daily life. The Gupta period also saw the expansion and embellishment of rock-cut architecture, such as the Ajanta and Ellora caves, which are famous for their exquisite paintings and sculptures.
- The medieval period (c. 550-1200 CE) saw the rise and fall of various dynasties and kingdoms, which left their mark on the sculpture of India. The most notable were the Pallava, Chola, Rashtrakuta, Hoysala, and Pala dynasties, which ruled over different regions of southern and eastern India. The Pallava and Chola dynasties are renowned for their bronze sculptures, especially the Nataraja or the dancing Shiva, which symbolizes the cosmic dance of creation and destruction. The Rashtrakuta and Hoysala dynasties are known for their rock-cut and freestanding temples, which are adorned with intricate and elaborate sculptures of Hindu and Jain themes. The Pala dynasty is famous for its terracotta and metal sculptures, which reflect the influence of Buddhism and Tantrism. The Pala sculptures depict the Buddha and other Buddhist deities in a serene and compassionate manner, often with elaborate crowns, jewels, and halos.
- The medieval period also witnessed the arrival and spread of Islam in India, which brought a new dimension to the sculpture of India. The Islamic rulers and patrons generally discouraged the representation of living beings, especially in religious contexts, and favored geometric and floral motifs instead.
