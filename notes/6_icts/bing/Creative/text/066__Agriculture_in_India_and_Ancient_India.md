### Agriculture in India and Ancient India

Agriculture is one of the oldest and most important activities in the Indian subcontinent. It has a long history of development, innovation and adaptation to different environmental and cultural conditions. Here are some points about agriculture in India and ancient India:

- Agriculture in India began around 9000 BCE in the north-western region, with the cultivation of plants and domestication of animals, such as barley, wheat, cattle, sheep and goat.  
- The Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1900 BCE) was one of the earliest urban civilizations in the world, and had a sophisticated system of irrigation, drainage, crop storage and trade. Barley and wheat were the main crops, along with dates, sesame, peas, lentils and cotton.  
- The Vedic period (1500-500 BCE) saw the expansion of agriculture to other parts of the subcontinent, and the introduction of new crops, such as rice, millets, beans, sugarcane and spices. The Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, contain many references to agriculture and its rituals, such as ploughing, sowing, harvesting and offering.  
- The Mauryan Empire (321-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian empire, and promoted agriculture as a source of revenue and welfare. The Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft and economics, written by Kautilya, the prime minister of Chandragupta Maurya, the founder of the empire, contains detailed instructions on various aspects of agriculture, such as land measurement, taxation, irrigation, crop rotation, soil fertility, pest control and animal husbandry.  
- The Gupta Empire (320-550 CE) is considered the golden age of ancient India, and witnessed a flourishing of art, literature, science and culture. Agriculture was also prosperous, and supported a large population and urbanization. Rice, wheat, millets, pulses, oilseeds, fruits, vegetables, spices and flowers were grown in abundance. The iron plough, the rotary tiller, the canal system and the artificial reservoirs were some of the innovations in agriculture during this period.  
- The medieval period (600-1500 CE) saw the emergence of various regional kingdoms and dynasties, such as the Cholas, the Rashtrakutas, the Palas, the Chalukyas, the Pratiharas, the Rajputs, the Delhi Sultanate and the Vijayanagara Empire. Agriculture was the mainstay of the economy, and was influenced by the local geography, climate and culture. Rice was the dominant crop in the south and east, while wheat was the main crop in the north and west. Other crops included millets, sorghum, maize, cotton, sugarcane, indigo, saffron, pepper, ginger, turmeric, cardamom, clove, nutmeg and coconut.  
- The Mughal Empire (1526-1857 CE) was the last major imperial power in India, and brought many changes in the political, social and cultural spheres. Agriculture was also affected by the Mughal policies and practices, such as the land revenue system, the expansion of irrigation, the introduction of new crops and techniques, and the promotion of trade and commerce. The Mughals introduced crops such as tobacco, potato, tomato, chilli, pineapple, papaya, guava and cashew from the New World, and improved the cultivation of rice, wheat, cotton, sugarcane, indigo and opium.  
- The colonial period (1757-1947 CE) marked the beginning of the British rule in India, and had a profound impact on the economy, society and culture of the subcontinent. Agriculture was also transformed by the colonial policies and interventions, such as the Permanent Settlement, the Ryotwari System, the Zamindari System, the commercialization of agriculture, the introduction of railways, roads and canals, the famines and the peasant movements. The British encouraged the cultivation of cash crops, such as cotton, jute, tea, coffee, rubber and opium, for the export market, and discouraged the cultivation of food crops, such as rice and wheat, for the domestic consumption.
