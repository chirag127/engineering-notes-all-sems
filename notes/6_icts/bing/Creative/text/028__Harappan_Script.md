#### Harappan Script

- Harappan script, also known as Indus script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilization, which flourished in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent from c. 3500 to 1900 BCE. 
- The script has not been deciphered yet, despite many attempts, and the language or languages it represents are unknown. 
- The script is mainly found on stamp seals, seal impressions, pottery, bronze and copper plates, tools, and weapons.  
- The script consists of over 400 distinct signs, some of which may be variants or modifiers of other signs.  
- The script is written from right to left, sometimes in a boustrophedon style (alternating direction). 
- The script shows some regional variations, suggesting that it may have been used for different purposes or by different communities. 
- The script is mostly composed of short inscriptions, usually consisting of four or five signs. The longest inscription has 26 signs.  
- The script has no known bilingual inscription to help decipher it, and no significant changes over time that could indicate its evolution or development.  
- The script may have some connection with the Brahmi script, which emerged in India around the 3rd century BCE, or the megalithic culture graffiti symbols of southern and central India, which date from the 2nd millennium BCE to the 1st millennium CE.  
- The script may have some relation to a Dravidian language, as suggested by some linguists based on the analysis of the sign order, the phonetic values, and the semantic features of the script.
