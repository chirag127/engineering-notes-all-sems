### Administration in Ancient India

- Administration in ancient India was a complex and diverse phenomenon that evolved over time and varied across regions and kingdoms.
- The earliest evidence of administration in India can be traced to the Indus Valley civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which had a systematic and centralized government that controlled urban planning, trade, taxation, and public works.
- The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) saw the emergence of tribal republics and monarchies, which had different forms of administration based on kinship, council, and assembly. The king was assisted by various officials, such as the purohita (priest), senani (army chief), and gramani (village headman). The king was also bound by the dharma (duty) and the rita (cosmic order) that regulated the social and moral life of the people.
- The later Vedic period (c. 600-300 BCE) witnessed the rise of large territorial states, known as mahajanapadas, which had more elaborate and hierarchical administrative systems. The king was the supreme authority, who exercised executive, legislative, and judicial powers. He was aided by a council of ministers, a bureaucracy, and a spy network. The kingdom was divided into provinces, districts, and villages, each with its own officials and functionaries. The king also collected taxes and maintained a standing army.
- The Mauryan empire (c. 321-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian state that established a highly centralized and efficient administration. The emperor was the head of the state, who delegated his powers to various departments, such as revenue, justice, foreign affairs, and military. The empire was divided into four provinces, each headed by a governor, and further subdivided into smaller units. The Mauryan administration also implemented a system of law and order, welfare schemes, and public works, such as roads, irrigation, and forests. The Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft attributed to Kautilya, provides a detailed account of the Mauryan administration.
- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE-300 CE) saw the fragmentation of the empire and the emergence of regional powers, such as the Sungas, the Kanvas, the Satavahanas, the Kushanas, and the Guptas. The administration of these kingdoms was largely influenced by the Mauryan model, but with some variations and adaptations. The kings were more dependent on the support of the feudal lords, the local assemblies, and the religious institutions. The administration also became more decentralized and flexible, allowing for regional diversity and autonomy.
- The Gupta empire (c. 320-550 CE) is considered the golden age of ancient India, as it witnessed political stability, economic prosperity, cultural flourishing, and administrative efficiency. The Gupta kings were benevolent and tolerant rulers, who patronized art, literature, science, and religion. The administration of the empire was based on the principle of subsidiarity, which meant that the lower levels of administration had more autonomy and responsibility. The empire was divided into provinces, which were further divided into districts, towns, and villages. The administration was also characterized by a balance between the central and the local authorities, the secular and the religious institutions, and the royal and the feudal interests.
- The post-Gupta period (c. 550-1200 CE) was marked by political fragmentation and foreign invasions, which led to the decline of the centralized administration and the rise of regional kingdoms, such as the Pallavas, the Chalukyas, the Rashtrakutas, the Pratiharas, the Pala, and the Cholas. The administration of these kingdoms was influenced by the local traditions, the geographical factors, and the cultural interactions. The kings were often assisted by a council of ministers, a bureaucracy, and a feudal hierarchy. The administration also relied on the role of the temples, the guilds, and the village councils, which performed various functions, such as collecting taxes, providing welfare, and resolving disputes. The inscriptions on the temple walls provide valuable information on the administration of these kingdoms.
- The administration in ancient India was not a static or uniform phenomenon, but a dynamic and diverse one, that reflected the changing political, social, and cultural realities of the subcontinent.
