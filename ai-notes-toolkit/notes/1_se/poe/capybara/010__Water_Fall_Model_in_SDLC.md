### Water Fall Model in SDLC

The Waterfall model is a linear sequential approach to software development. It is also known as the traditional approach to software development. Let's take a closer look at the different stages involved in the Waterfall Model:

1. Requirements Gathering: The first stage in the Waterfall Model is gathering requirements. The requirements are gathered from the customer or client. The requirements are then documented in a document called the Software Requirement Specification (SRS) document.

2. Design: Once the requirements are gathered, the design phase starts. The design is the blueprint of the software. In this stage, the software architects, system analysts, and designers come together to design the software.

3. Implementation: In this stage, the actual coding of the software is done. The implementation stage involves writing code, testing the code, and fixing bugs.

4. Testing: Once the implementation is complete, the software is tested. The testing is done to ensure that the software meets the requirements mentioned in the SRS document.

5. Deployment: Once the testing is complete, the software is deployed. The deployment stage involves installing the software on the customer's computer systems.

6. Maintenance: The final stage in the Waterfall Model is maintenance. In this stage, the software is maintained and updated regularly.

Advantages of the Waterfall Model:

- The Waterfall Model is easy to understand and follow.
- The Waterfall Model is best suited for small projects with well-defined requirements.
- The Waterfall Model ensures that the software meets the customer's requirements.

Disadvantages of the Waterfall Model:

- The Waterfall Model is not suitable for large and complex projects.
- The Waterfall Model does not allow for changes to be made easily once the project is in the testing phase.
- The Waterfall Model does not involve customer feedback until the testing phase.

Conclusion:

The Waterfall Model is a linear sequential approach to software development. It is easy to understand and follow. It is best suited for small projects with well-defined requirements. However, it is not suitable for large and complex projects, and it does not allow for changes to be made easily once the project is in the testing phase.
