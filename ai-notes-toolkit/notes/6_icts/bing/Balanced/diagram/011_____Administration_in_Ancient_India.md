### Administration in Ancient India

- Administration in ancient India refers to the structures, procedures and behaviors that the ruling dynasties or emperors used to govern their respective territories and people.
- The earliest evidence of administration in ancient India can be traced to the Indus Valley civilization (2500-1700 BCE), which had a systematic and organized government in the cities of Mohenjodaro and Harappa. The Indus Valley civilization had a centralized authority, a uniform script, standardized weights and measures, urban planning, drainage system, public baths and granaries.
- After the decline of the Indus Valley civilization, the Vedic period (1500-600 BCE) witnessed the emergence of various tribal kingdoms and republics, which had different forms of administration. The most common form of government was monarchy, where the king was the supreme authority and was assisted by a council of ministers and advisers. The king performed various functions such as maintaining law and order, protecting the people, performing sacrifices, collecting taxes and leading the army. The king was also bound by the dharma (duty) and the rita (cosmic order) and was expected to rule with justice and benevolence.
- The Vedic period also saw the development of the concept of saptanga or the seven limbs of the state, which consisted of the king, the minister, the territory, the fort, the treasury, the army and the ally. These seven elements were considered essential for the stability and prosperity of the state.
- The later Vedic period (600-300 BCE) witnessed the rise of the Mahajanapadas or the sixteen great kingdoms, which competed with each other for supremacy. Some of these kingdoms were monarchical, such as Magadha, Kosala and Avanti, while some were republican, such as the Vajji, the Malla and the Shakya. The republican states had a more democratic form of administration, where the people elected their representatives and participated in the decision-making process. The republican states also had a federation of clans or tribes, which formed a confederacy for mutual defense and cooperation.
- The Mauryan Empire (321-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian empire, which unified most of the Indian subcontinent under one rule. The Mauryan administration was highly centralized and efficient, with a well-defined hierarchy of officials and departments. The emperor was the head of the state and the supreme commander of the army. He was assisted by a chief minister, who was in charge of the civil administration, and a council of ministers, who advised him on various matters. The empire was divided into provinces, districts, sub-districts and villages, each with its own governor, magistrate, collector and other officials. The Mauryan administration also had a secret service, a postal system, a judicial system, a revenue system and a welfare system. The Mauryan administration was guided by the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft and governance, written by Kautilya, the chief minister of Chandragupta Maurya.
- The post-Mauryan period (185 BCE-320 CE) saw the fragmentation of the Indian subcontinent into various regional kingdoms, such as the Sunga, the Satavahana, the Kushana, the Shaka, the Gupta and the Harsha. These kingdoms had different forms of administration, but they generally followed the Mauryan model of centralization and bureaucracy. The kings were the supreme authorities and were assisted by ministers, governors, generals and other officials. The kings also patronized various religions, arts, sciences and literature. The Gupta Empire (320-550 CE) is considered the golden age of ancient India, as it witnessed the peak of political, economic, cultural and intellectual achievements.
- The medieval period (550-1200 CE) saw the emergence of various regional powers, such as the Pallava, the Chalukya, the Rashtrakuta, the Pala, the Pratihara, the Chola, the Chahamana and the Hoysala. These kingdoms had different forms of administration, but they generally followed the feudal system, where the king granted lands and privileges to his nobles, vassals and subordinates in exchange for their loyalty, service and tribute. The kings also maintained a large army, a strong navy, a complex revenue system and a codified law.
