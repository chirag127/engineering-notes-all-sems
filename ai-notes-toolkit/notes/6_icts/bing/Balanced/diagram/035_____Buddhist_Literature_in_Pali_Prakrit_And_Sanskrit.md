### Buddhist Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Buddhist literature is the collection of writings that preserve the teachings and traditions of Buddhism.
- Buddhist literature can be divided into two main categories: canonical and non-canonical.
- Canonical literature refers to the scriptures that are considered authoritative and authentic by various Buddhist schools and traditions. Non-canonical literature includes commentaries, biographies, stories, poems, and other works that are not part of the official canon, but are still valued for their historical, doctrinal, or literary significance.
- The canonical literature of Buddhism is written in various languages, depending on the region and time period of its composition. The most prominent languages are Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit.
- Pali is an ancient language that is closely related to Sanskrit, but has some differences in grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation. It is said that the Buddha delivered his sermons in Pali, or a dialect similar to it, and that his disciples recorded them in the same language. Pali is the canonical language of Theravada Buddhism, the oldest and most conservative branch of Buddhism, which is mainly practiced in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. The Pali canon, also known as the Tipitaka or the Three Baskets, consists of three main collections: the Vinaya Pitaka (the Basket of Discipline), which contains the rules and regulations for the monastic community; the Sutta Pitaka (the Basket of Discourses), which contains the dialogues and discourses of the Buddha and his disciples; and the Abhidhamma Pitaka (the Basket of Higher Teaching), which contains the systematic analysis and exposition of the Buddhist doctrine and psychology.
- Prakrit is a generic term for any language that is derived from or influenced by Sanskrit, but is not Sanskrit itself. Prakrit languages are usually simpler and more colloquial than Sanskrit, and reflect the regional and social variations of the speakers. Prakrit languages were widely used in ancient and medieval India for literature, administration, and communication. Some of the Prakrit languages that are relevant for Buddhist literature are Sauraseni, Maharastri, Magadhi, and Gandhari. Prakrit is the canonical language of some of the early Buddhist schools, such as the Mahasanghikas, the Sarvastivadins, and the Dharmaguptakas, which are collectively known as the Hinayana or the Lesser Vehicle. The Prakrit canon, also known as the Agama or the Collection, consists of four main collections: the Dirgha Agama (the Long Collection), which contains long discourses of the Buddha; the Madhyama Agama (the Middle Collection), which contains medium-length discourses of the Buddha; the Samyukta Agama (the Connected Collection), which contains short discourses of the Buddha grouped by topic; and the Ekottara Agama (the Numerical Collection), which contains discourses of the Buddha arranged by numbers.
- Sanskrit is an ancient and classical language that is considered the language of the gods and the scholars in Hinduism. It is a highly refined and sophisticated language that has a complex grammar, a rich vocabulary, and a poetic beauty. Sanskrit is the canonical language of Mahayana Buddhism, the later and more liberal branch of Buddhism, which is mainly practiced in Tibet, China, Japan, and Korea. The Sanskrit canon, also known as the Mahayana Sutras or the Great Vehicle Sutras, consists of various texts that expound the Mahayana teachings and practices, such as the Prajnaparamita Sutras (the Perfection of Wisdom Sutras), which emphasize the concept of emptiness; the Lotus Sutra, which reveals the universal Buddhahood of all beings; the Avatamsaka Sutra (the Flower Garland Sutra), which describes the interpenetration of all phenomena; the Pure Land Sutras, which extol the virtues and merits of the Buddha Amitabha and his paradise; and the Vajrayana Sutras (the Diamond Vehicle Sutras), which introduce the esoteric and tantric methods of achieving enlightenment.
