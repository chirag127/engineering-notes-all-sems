# Agriculture in India and Ancient India

Agriculture is the cultivation of plants and animals for food, fiber, fuel, and other purposes. Agriculture is one of the oldest and most important human activities, as it provides the basis for food security, livelihoods, and civilization. Agriculture in India and ancient India has a long and rich history, with evidence of domestication and cultivation dating back to the Neolithic period.

Some of the main points about agriculture in India and ancient India are:

- **Ancient Indian agriculture** began around 9000 BCE in the Indus Valley region, where the earliest evidence of wheat, barley, peas, sesame, and dates cultivation has been found . The Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1900 BCE) developed a sophisticated urban culture, with irrigation systems, granaries, and trade networks. The Indus Valley people also domesticated animals such as cattle, sheep, goats, and elephants.
- **Ancient South Indian agriculture** was also well-developed, with the Tamil people cultivating a wide range of crops such as rice, sugarcane, millets, black pepper, various grains, coconuts, beans, cotton, plantain, tamarind, and sandalwood, among others. The Sangam literature (300 BCE-300 CE) describes the agricultural practices, seasons, festivals, and markets of the ancient Tamils.
- **Ancient Indian agriculture** was influenced by various factors, such as climate, geography, soil, water, religion, and culture. The ancient Indians developed various techniques and tools for agriculture, such as ploughs, sickles, axes, hoes, wells, canals, tanks, and dams. They also used organic fertilizers, such as cow dung and ashes, and practiced crop rotation, mixed cropping, and intercropping. The ancient Indians also had a good knowledge of plant and animal breeding, pest control, and veterinary medicine.
- **Agriculture in the Indian subcontinent** underwent significant changes after the arrival of the Aryans (1500-500 BCE), who introduced new crops such as rice, millets, sorghum, and lentils, and new animals such as horses, camels, and chickens. The Vedic literature (1500-500 BCE) contains many references to agriculture, such as the Rigveda, which mentions various crops, animals, and agricultural rituals. The later Vedic period (1000-500 BCE) saw the emergence of iron technology, which improved the agricultural productivity and expansion.
- **Agriculture in the Indian subcontinent** continued to evolve and diversify in the subsequent periods, such as the Mauryan Empire (321-185 BCE), which established a centralized administration and taxation system for agriculture, and promoted irrigation, forestry, and animal husbandry. The Gupta Empire (320-550 CE) is considered the golden age of Indian agriculture, as it witnessed a flourishing of trade, commerce, arts, and sciences, and the development of new crops, such as cotton, sugarcane, and spices. The medieval period (600-1500 CE) saw the introduction of new crops, such as wheat, rice, and pulses, from West and Central Asia, and the expansion of irrigation and land reclamation projects by various dynasties, such as the Cholas, the Delhi Sultanate, and the Vijayanagara Empire. The Mughal Empire (1526-1857 CE) also contributed to the agricultural development, by introducing new crops, such as maize, potatoes, tomatoes, and chilies, from the Americas, and by implementing land reforms, revenue systems, and public works.
- **Agriculture in India** underwent a major transformation during the colonial period (1757-1947), as the British introduced new policies, institutions, and technologies that affected the agricultural production, distribution, and consumption. The British imposed various taxes, such as the zamindari system, the ryotwari system, and the mahalwari system, which increased the burden on the farmers and led to famines, poverty, and indebtedness. The British also introduced new crops, such as tea, coffee, indigo, opium, and jute, which were grown for export, and new technologies, such as railways, canals, and plantations, which increased the commercialization and market integration of agriculture.
