#### Understanding Gender as a Social Category in Society in Ancient India

- Gender is a social construct that defines the roles, expectations and norms associated with different sexes in a given society.
- In ancient India, gender roles were not fixed or rigid, but varied according to the time period, region, caste, class and religion.
- Generally, women in ancient India were respected and valued for their contributions to the family, society and culture. They had access to education, religious rites, property rights and political participation in some cases.
- However, women also faced discrimination, oppression and violence in various forms, such as child marriage, sati, dowry, polygamy, widowhood and domestic abuse. These practices were influenced by patriarchal norms, laws and customs that restricted women's autonomy and agency.
- Some of the factors that shaped gender relations in ancient India were:

  - The Vedic period (1500-600 BCE): This was the earliest phase of Indian history, marked by the composition of the Vedas, the sacred texts of Hinduism. Women in this period enjoyed a high status and participated in religious ceremonies, intellectual debates and social activities. They could become rishis (sages) and compose hymns. Some of the famous women rishis were Gargi, Maitreyi, Lopamudra and Ghosha  .
  - The post-Vedic period (600-300 BCE): This was the period of transition from tribal to urban society, marked by the emergence of Buddhism, Jainism and other heterodox sects. Women in this period faced a decline in their status and freedom, as they were increasingly confined to the domestic sphere and subjected to the authority of their fathers, husbands and sons. They were denied access to education, property and public life. However, some women found refuge and liberation in the new religious movements, such as Buddhism and Jainism, which offered them equal opportunities for spiritual attainment and social service. Some of the famous women followers of these religions were Mahaprajapati Gautami, the foster mother of Buddha; Yasodhara, the wife of Buddha; Mallika, the queen of King Pasenadi; and Chandana, the disciple of Mahavira  .
  - The Mauryan period (321-185 BCE): This was the period of the first pan-Indian empire, founded by Chandragupta Maurya and expanded by Ashoka. Women in this period enjoyed some political and economic rights, as they could serve as ministers, ambassadors, spies, courtesans and traders. They also benefited from the welfare policies and moral teachings of Ashoka, who promoted non-violence, tolerance and compassion. Some of the famous women in this period were Chanakya, the chief minister of Chandragupta; Durdhara, the wife of Chandragupta; Kaurwaki, the wife of Ashoka; and Sanghamitra, the daughter of Ashoka who propagated Buddhism in Sri Lanka  .
  - The post-Mauryan period (185 BCE-320 CE): This was the period of political fragmentation and cultural diversity, marked by the rise and fall of various regional kingdoms, such as the Shungas, the Kushans, the Satavahanas and the Guptas. Women in this period experienced a mixed situation, as they were influenced by the local customs, laws and traditions of their respective regions. Some regions, such as the south and the northwest, were more liberal and progressive, while others, such as the north and the east, were more conservative and regressive. Some of the positive aspects of this period for women were the patronage of art, literature and education; the recognition of women's rights in inheritance, divorce and remarriage; and the emergence of women poets, artists and scholars, such as Amrapali, the courtesan of Vaishali; Nagarjuna, the philosopher of Madhyamika Buddhism; and Kalidasa, the poet of Abhijnanashakuntala. Some of the negative aspects of this period for women were the prevalence of child marriage, sati, dowry and polygamy; the subordination of women to the varnashrama dharma, the social and moral code of Hinduism; and the exclusion of women from public and religious life  .
