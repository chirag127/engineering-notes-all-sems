# Pyrotechnics in India and Ancient India

Pyrotechnics are the art and science of creating and using fireworks for entertainment, military, or ceremonial purposes. Pyrotechnics have a long and fascinating history in India and ancient India, as well as in other parts of the world. Here are some points to know about pyrotechnics in India and ancient India:

- One of the earliest notes of pyrotechnical shows in India is made by Abdur Razzaq, the ambassador of the Timurid Sultan Shahrukh to the court of the Vijayanagar king Devaraya II in 1443. He wrote that he witnessed a grand display of fireworks at the king's palace, which lasted for an hour and filled the sky with various colors and shapes.
- Fireworks and pyrotechnic shows existed as a form of royal entertainment in many medieval Indian kingdoms during festivals, events and special occasions like weddings. Manufacturing formulas for fireworks describing pyrotechnic mixtures are found within Kautukachintamani, a Sanskrit volume by Gajapati Prataparudradeva (1497-1539), a reputed king of Orissa .
- Gunpowder, an essential ingredient of fire crackers, was unknown in ancient India. It was introduced to India by the Mongols and the Arabs in the 13th century. The Indian name for gunpowder was agni churana, meaning fire powder. The earliest reference to gunpowder in India is found in the 13th century Sanskrit text Sukraniti, which mentions the use of fire arrows and fire balls in warfare .
- The Mughal emperors were fond of fireworks and used them to celebrate their victories, birthdays, and festivals. Akbar, the greatest of the Mughal rulers, is said to have spent a fortune on fireworks and employed a special department of pyrotechnists to design and execute spectacular displays. His son Jahangir and grandson Shah Jahan also patronized the art of pyrotechnics and commissioned elaborate shows for their courts and subjects.
- The British colonial rulers also adopted the practice of fireworks and used them to mark important occasions such as the coronation of the king or queen, the arrival of a new governor-general, or the anniversary of the East India Company. They also introduced new types of fireworks such as rockets, shells, and Roman candles, which were more powerful and colorful than the traditional Indian ones.
- The popularity of fireworks in India increased with the advent of democracy and independence in the 20th century. Fireworks became a symbol of national pride and joy, and were used to celebrate various festivals such as Diwali, Holi, Dussehra, Eid, and Christmas. Fireworks also became a part of the cultural and religious identity of different communities and regions in India, such as the Tamils, the Bengalis, the Punjabis, and the Gujaratis.
- Today, India is one of the largest producers and consumers of fireworks in the world. The fireworks industry in India is mainly concentrated in the town of Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu, which accounts for about 90% of the domestic production and 80% of the exports. The industry employs about 250,000 people and generates an annual revenue of about Rs. 2,000 crore. The industry is also facing challenges such as environmental pollution, safety hazards, child labor, and competition from China.
