# Buddhism in India

- Buddhism is an ancient Indian religion that originated in the 6th century BCE in the region of Magadha (now Bihar) and spread throughout northern India and beyond.
- Buddhism is based on the teachings and life experiences of Siddhartha Gautama, also known as the Buddha (the awakened one), who renounced his princely life and attained enlightenment under a bodhi tree.
- Buddhism teaches the four noble truths: the truth of suffering, the truth of the cause of suffering, the truth of the cessation of suffering, and the truth of the path to the cessation of suffering.
- Buddhism also teaches the eightfold path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration, which are the means to achieve nirvana, the ultimate goal of liberation from the cycle of rebirth (samsara).
- Buddhism has two main branches: Theravada and Mahayana. Theravada is the oldest and most conservative form of Buddhism, which follows the Pali canon of scriptures and emphasizes the practice of meditation and morality. Mahayana is a more diverse and expansive form of Buddhism, which follows various sutras and doctrines and emphasizes the role of bodhisattvas, compassionate beings who postpone their own enlightenment to help others.
- Buddhism flourished in India for over a thousand years, from the 3rd century BCE to the 12th century CE, and produced many schools, sects, and traditions, such as the Madhyamaka, Yogacara, Pure Land, Zen, and Vajrayana. Buddhism also influenced Indian art, architecture, literature, philosophy, and culture.
- Buddhism spread from India to other parts of Asia, such as Central Asia, China, Tibet, Japan, Korea, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and beyond, through various routes of trade, pilgrimage, and missionary activity. Buddhism adapted to different cultures and contexts and developed into various regional and local forms.
- Buddhism declined in India from the 8th century CE onwards, due to various factors, such as the rise of Hinduism, the invasions of Muslim rulers, the corruption and degeneration of Buddhist monasteries, and the loss of patronage and support from the laity. Buddhism survived in some pockets of India, such as Ladakh, Sikkim, and Arunachal Pradesh, and was revived in the 20th century by the efforts of reformers, such as B.R. Ambedkar, who converted millions of Dalits (untouchables) to Buddhism.
