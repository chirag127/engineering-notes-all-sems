#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

- Purusārtha (Sanskrit: पुरुषार्थ) means "object (ive) of human pursuit" or "human goal".
- It is a key concept in Hinduism that refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. These are:
  - Dharma (righteousness, moral values)
  - Artha (prosperity, economic values)
  - Kama (pleasure, love, psychological values)
  - Moksha (liberation, spiritual values)
- The concept of Purusārtha is a fundamental principle of social ethics in ancient India. It is meant to set an ideal objective and also to convey the right means to achieve them.
- The four Purusārthas are not mutually exclusive, but rather interdependent and complementary. They are also flexible and adaptable according to the individual's stage of life, social class, and personal inclination.
- The concept of Purusārtha is based on the belief that human life is a sacred journey of the soul, and that the ultimate goal is to attain moksha, or liberation from the cycle of birth and death. Moksha can be achieved by following one's dharma, or duty, according to one's varna (social class) and ashrama (stage of life).
- Dharma is the first and foremost Purusārtha, as it provides the ethical foundation for the other three. Dharma is the moral law that governs the individual and the society, and it is based on the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. Dharma also means the inherent nature or essence of a thing, and the duty or obligation that arises from it. Dharma varies according to one's varna and ashrama, and also according to the context and circumstances. Dharma is the means to achieve artha, kama, and moksha, and also the end in itself. 
- Artha is the second Purusārtha, and it means wealth, prosperity, and material well-being. Artha is necessary for the survival and comfort of the individual and the society, and it also enables the fulfillment of one's dharma and kama. Artha should be acquired and used in accordance with dharma, and not for selfish or immoral purposes. Artha is also a means to achieve moksha, as it can be used for charity, service, and spiritual pursuits. 
- Kama is the third Purusārtha, and it means desire, pleasure, and enjoyment. Kama includes both sensual and aesthetic pleasures, as well as love, affection, and emotional satisfaction. Kama is a natural and legitimate human goal, and it is also a source of happiness and motivation. Kama should be pursued in moderation and in harmony with dharma and artha, and not at the expense of them. Kama is also a means to achieve moksha, as it can lead to detachment, renunciation, and devotion. 
- Moksha is the fourth and final Purusārtha, and it means liberation, freedom, and salvation. Moksha is the ultimate goal of human life, and it is the state of being free from ignorance, attachment, and suffering. Moksha is achieved by realizing one's true self, which is identical with Brahman, the supreme reality. Moksha can be attained by various paths, such as knowledge, action, devotion, and meditation. Moksha is the end of all Purusārthas, and it is also the source of all bliss and peace. 
- The concept of Purusārtha reflects the holistic and dynamic vision of human life in ancient India. It provides a framework for understanding the purpose and meaning of life, and also for guiding the individual and the society towards the highest good. The concept of Purusārtha also shows the diversity and unity of Hinduism, as it accommodates different values, goals, and paths within a common worldview.
