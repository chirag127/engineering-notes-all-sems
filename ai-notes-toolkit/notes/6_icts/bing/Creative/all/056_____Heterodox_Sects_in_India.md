# Heterodox Sects in India

Heterodox sects are those that do not accept the authority of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. They emerged in the 6th century BCE as a reaction to the ritualistic and hierarchical Vedic religion. They were also influenced by the social and economic changes that occurred in India at that time, such as urbanization, trade, and the rise of new classes and professions. Some of the heterodox sects that developed and survived are:

- Jainism: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara or spiritual teacher, Jainism teaches the doctrine of ahimsa or non-violence towards all living beings. It also emphasizes the concepts of anekantavada or multiple perspectives, syadvada or conditional truth, and aparigraha or non-attachment. Jains practice asceticism, vegetarianism, and follow the five vows of non-violence, truth, non-stealing, celibacy, and non-possession. Jainism is divided into two main sects: Digambara or sky-clad, and Svetambara or white-clad, based on their differences in doctrine and practice.
- Buddhism: Founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha or the enlightened one, Buddhism teaches the four noble truths: the truth of suffering, the truth of the cause of suffering, the truth of the end of suffering, and the truth of the path to the end of suffering. The path to the end of suffering is the eightfold path, which consists of right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. Buddhists also believe in the concepts of karma, rebirth, and nirvana, the state of liberation from the cycle of suffering. Buddhism is divided into many schools and traditions, such as Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana, based on their differences in interpretation and practice.
- Ajivika: Founded by Makkhali Gosala, Ajivika is a materialist and fatalist school of thought that denies the existence of free will, moral responsibility, and the efficacy of human action. It holds that everything is predetermined by the cosmic force of niyati or destiny, and that the soul is composed of atoms that are subject to the laws of nature. Ajivikas practice extreme asceticism, such as fasting, nudity, and self-mortification, in order to exhaust their karma and attain liberation. Ajivika was a major rival of Jainism and Buddhism, but declined and disappeared by the medieval period.
