# Indian Cinema as a Performing Art in India

- Indian cinema is a form of performing art that combines storytelling, acting, music, dance, and visual effects to create a powerful medium of expression and entertainment.
- Indian cinema has a rich and diverse history that spans over a century, from the silent era to the present day, reflecting the social, cultural, and political changes in the country.
- Indian cinema is influenced by various traditions of Indian performing arts, such as theater, folk, classical, and modern dance, music, and literature, as well as by foreign influences, such as Hollywood, European, and Asian cinema.
- Indian cinema is also a source of inspiration and influence for other forms of performing arts, such as theater, music, and television, as many artists and personalities have emerged from the film industry.
- Indian cinema can be studied as a performing art from various perspectives, such as the following:

  - The Natyasastra and the Rasa Theory: The Natyasastra is an ancient treatise on dramaturgy and aesthetics, written by Bharata Muni, that lays down the principles and techniques of theater and dance. The Rasa Theory is a key concept of the Natyasastra, which states that the aim of performing arts is to evoke the nine basic emotions (rasas) in the audience, such as love, anger, fear, joy, etc. The Rasa Theory has been applied to Indian cinema by various scholars and filmmakers, who have analyzed the use of music, dialogue, gestures, costumes, and cinematography to create the desired emotional effects.
  - The Indian People's Theatre Association (IPTA) and the Social Realism: The IPTA was a progressive cultural movement that emerged in the 1940s, in the context of the Indian independence struggle and the social and economic problems of the masses. The IPTA aimed to use theater and other performing arts as a medium of social awareness and change, by depicting the realities and struggles of the oppressed classes, such as the peasants, workers, women, and minorities. The IPTA also influenced the Indian cinema, as many of its members became filmmakers, actors, writers, and composers, who made films that addressed the issues of poverty, inequality, communalism, corruption, and nationalism.
  - The Parallel Cinema and the Art Cinema: The Parallel Cinema was a movement of alternative and experimental films that emerged in the 1950s and 1960s, in contrast to the mainstream commercial cinema, which was dominated by formulaic and escapist genres, such as romance, action, and comedy. The Parallel Cinema aimed to explore the artistic and aesthetic aspects of cinema, by using realistic, complex, and nuanced narratives, characters, and themes, that reflected the social, political, and cultural realities of the contemporary India. The Parallel Cinema also influenced the Art Cinema, which was a term used to describe the films that were made with artistic and creative intentions, rather than commercial and entertainment purposes, and that received critical acclaim and recognition at national and international film festivals.
