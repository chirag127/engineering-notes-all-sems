### Interrupts

- An interrupt is a signal to the processor emitted by hardware or software indicating an event that needs immediate attention .
- An interrupt causes the processor to suspend its current execution and service the interrupt by executing the corresponding interrupt service routine (ISR) .
- Interrupts are useful for handling events that are asynchronous, unpredictable, or urgent, such as input/output, timers, exceptions, or errors .
- Interrupts can be classified into two main types: hardware interrupts and software interrupts.
  - Hardware interrupts are generated by external devices, such as keyboards, mice, printers, disks, or network cards, that request the processor's attention .
  - Software interrupts are generated by instructions executed by the processor, such as system calls, traps, or exceptions, that request the processor to perform a specific service or handle an error .
- Interrupts can also be classified into two modes: vectored and non-vectored.
  - Vectored interrupts are those where the interrupting device or instruction provides the address of the ISR to the processor.
  - Non-vectored interrupts are those where the interrupting device or instruction does not provide the address of the ISR, and the processor has to use a fixed address or a table to find the ISR.
- Interrupts can also be classified into two levels: maskable and non-maskable.
  - Maskable interrupts are those that can be ignored or disabled by the processor, depending on the priority or status of the interrupt.
  - Non-maskable interrupts are those that cannot be ignored or disabled by the processor, and must be serviced immediately, such as power failure or system reset.
- Interrupts can also be classified into two methods: polling and interrupt-driven.
  - Polling is a method where the processor periodically checks the status of each device or instruction to see if an interrupt has occurred.
  - Interrupt-driven is a method where the processor is notified by the device or instruction when an interrupt occurs, using a dedicated interrupt request line or a shared bus signal.
- Interrupts require the processor to perform the following steps :
  - Save the current state of the processor, such as the program counter, the status register, and the general-purpose registers, in a stack or a special register .
  - Identify the source and type of the interrupt, using a vector, a table, or a priority scheme .
  - Transfer the control to the ISR, using a jump or a call instruction .
  - Execute the ISR, which may involve reading or writing data from or to the device or memory, or performing some computation or service .
  - Restore the state of the processor, using the saved values from the stack or the special register .
  - Return the control to the interrupted program, using a return or a resume instruction .
- Interrupts can improve the performance, responsiveness, and functionality of the system, by allowing the processor to handle multiple tasks concurrently, without wasting cycles on polling or waiting .
