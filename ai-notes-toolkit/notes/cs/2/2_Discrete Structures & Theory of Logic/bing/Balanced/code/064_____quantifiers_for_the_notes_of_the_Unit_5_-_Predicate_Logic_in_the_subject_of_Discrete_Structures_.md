### Quantifiers

- Quantifiers are symbols that express the quantity of a predicate or a statement.
- There are two main types of quantifiers: universal and existential.
- The universal quantifier, denoted by ∀, means "for all" or "for every". It is used to express that a predicate or a statement is true for every element in a given domain.
- The existential quantifier, denoted by ∃, means "there exists" or "there is". It is used to express that a predicate or a statement is true for at least one element in a given domain.
- Quantifiers can be combined with logical connectives, such as negation, conjunction, disjunction, implication, and equivalence, to form complex statements.
- The order and scope of quantifiers are important for determining the meaning of a statement. The scope of a quantifier is the part of the statement that it affects. The order of quantifiers determines which quantifier has the highest priority and which one has the lowest priority.
- The rules of inference for quantifiers are similar to the rules of inference for propositional logic, with some modifications. For example, the universal instantiation rule allows us to replace a universally quantified variable with a specific element from the domain, while the existential generalization rule allows us to introduce an existentially quantified variable from a specific element in the domain.
