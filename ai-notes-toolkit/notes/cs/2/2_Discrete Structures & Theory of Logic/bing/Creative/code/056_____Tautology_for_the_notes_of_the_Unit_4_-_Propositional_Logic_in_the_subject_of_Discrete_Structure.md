### Tautology for the notes of the Unit 4 - Propositional Logic in the subject of Discrete Structures & Theory of Logic

- A tautology is a propositional formula that is true under any possible Boolean valuation of its propositional variables.
- A tautology is an assertion of propositional logic that is true in all situations; that is, it is true for all possible values of its variables.
- A tautology can be recognized by using a truth table, a logical equivalence, or a proof system.
- Examples of tautologies are:
  - p ∨ ¬p (law of excluded middle)
  - p → p (law of identity)
  - (p → q) ∨ (q → p) (law of material implication)
  - (p ∧ q) → p (law of simplification)
  - (p ∧ (p ∨ q)) ↔ p (law of absorption)
- A tautology can be used as a rule of replacement in a logical proof to eliminate redundancy in disjunctions and conjunctions.
- The principle of idempotency of disjunction states that p ∨ p is logically equivalent to p.
- The principle of idempotency of conjunction states that p ∧ p is logically equivalent to p.
- A tautology can also be used as a premise or a conclusion in a logical argument, as it is always valid.
- A tautology is different from a contradiction, which is a propositional formula that is false under any possible Boolean valuation of its propositional variables .
- A tautology is also different from a contingency, which is a propositional formula that is neither a tautology nor a contradiction, and has some true and some false valuations.
