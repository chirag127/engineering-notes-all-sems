### Proof by contradiction

- A proof by contradiction is a method of proving a statement by assuming that it is false and deriving a contradiction from that assumption.
- The contradiction can be either a logical inconsistency or a violation of a known fact or theorem.
- The basic principle of proof by contradiction is that if a statement is true, then its negation is false, and vice versa.
- To prove a statement P by contradiction, we follow these steps:

  1. Assume that P is false, i.e., assume that ¬P is true.
  2. Use logical reasoning and known facts or theorems to deduce a contradiction from ¬P.
  3. Conclude that ¬P is false, and therefore P is true.

- An example of a proof by contradiction is the proof that √2 is irrational.

  - To prove that √2 is irrational, we assume that it is rational, i.e., that it can be written as a fraction of two integers a and b, where b ≠ 0 and a and b have no common factors.
  - Then we have √2 = a/b, and squaring both sides gives 2 = a²/b², or 2b² = a².
  - This implies that a² is even, and therefore a is even, i.e., a = 2k for some integer k.
  - Substituting a = 2k into 2b² = a² gives 2b² = 4k², or b² = 2k².
  - This implies that b² is even, and therefore b is even, i.e., b = 2l for some integer l.
  - But then we have a = 2k and b = 2l, which means that a and b have a common factor of 2, contradicting the assumption that they have no common factors.
  - Therefore, the assumption that √2 is rational is false, and hence √2 is irrational.
