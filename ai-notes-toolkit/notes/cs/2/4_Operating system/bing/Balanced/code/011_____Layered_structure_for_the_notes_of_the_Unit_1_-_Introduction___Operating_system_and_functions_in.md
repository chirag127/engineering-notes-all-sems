### Layered structure

- A layered structure is a way of organizing the components of an operating system into different levels or layers, each with a specific functionality and responsibility.
- The main advantages of a layered structure are:
  - It simplifies the design and implementation of the operating system, as each layer can be developed and tested independently.
  - It enhances the modularity and extensibility of the operating system, as new features or services can be added or modified by changing or replacing a layer.
  - It increases the security and reliability of the operating system, as errors or faults in one layer are isolated from the rest of the system.
- The main disadvantages of a layered structure are:
  - It may introduce some overhead and inefficiency, as each layer has to communicate with the adjacent layers through well-defined interfaces.
  - It may not reflect the actual dependencies and interactions among the components of the operating system, as some layers may need to bypass or access lower or higher layers directly.
  - It may be difficult to define the optimal number and functionality of the layers, as there is no clear-cut criterion or standard for doing so.
- An example of a layered structure is the ISO/OSI model, which defines seven layers for the communication system: physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, and application.
- Another example of a layered structure is the UNIX operating system, which consists of four layers: hardware, kernel, shell, and user applications.
