### Origin of Partial Differential Equations

- Partial differential equations (PDEs) are equations that involve partial derivatives of multivariable functions.
- PDEs are used to model various phenomena in physics, engineering, biology, and other disciplines.
- The study of PDEs started in the 18th century with the work of Euler, d'Alembert, Lagrange, and Laplace, who used them to describe the mechanics of continua, such as fluids, solids, and gases.
- Some of the classical PDEs that arose from physical problems are the wave equation, the heat equation, the Laplace equation, and the Poisson equation.
- The wave equation models the propagation of waves, such as sound, light, or water waves.
- The heat equation models the diffusion of heat in a medium, such as a metal rod or a cake.
- The Laplace equation models the potential field of a static distribution of charges, masses, or sources, such as the electric potential or the gravitational potential.
- The Poisson equation models the potential field of a non-static distribution of charges, masses, or sources, such as the electric current or the fluid flow.
- The solution of a PDE is a function that satisfies the equation and some boundary and initial conditions, which specify the values or the behavior of the function on the boundary or at the initial time of the domain of interest.
- The methods of solving PDEs depend on the type, order, and complexity of the equation, as well as the domain, boundary, and initial conditions.
- Some of the common methods are separation of variables, Fourier series, Fourier transform, Laplace transform, Green's functions, and numerical methods.
- The theory of PDEs has been developed and enriched by many mathematicians in the 19th and 20th centuries, such as Cauchy, Riemann, Fourier, Dirichlet, Neumann, Gauss, Stokes, Helmholtz, Maxwell, Legendre, Bessel, Hermite, Laguerre, Chebyshev, Poincaré, Hilbert, Sobolev, Fredholm, Volterra, Picard, Lax, Schwartz, Sobolev, and many others.
- The theory of PDEs involves various branches of mathematics, such as analysis, algebra, geometry, topology, and probability.
- Some of the important concepts and results in the theory of PDEs are the existence, uniqueness, and regularity of solutions, the maximum principle, the energy method, the weak formulation, the variational principle, the Sobolev spaces, the elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic classifications, the characteristics, the conservation laws, the well-posedness, the ill-posedness, the stability, the convergence, the error analysis, the inverse problems, the nonlinear phenomena, the bifurcations, the chaos, the solitons, the shocks, the singularities, the boundary layers, the asymptotic analysis, the homogenization, the perturbation, the renormalization, the symmetries, the integrability, the Hamiltonian systems, the dynamical systems, the control theory, the optimization, the calculus of variations, the functional analysis, the spectral theory, the eigenvalue problems, the Sturm-Liouville theory, the Fredholm theory, the integral equations, the distributions, the generalized functions, the Fourier analysis, the harmonic analysis, the complex analysis, the analytic functions, the Cauchy-Riemann equations, the residue theorem, the conformal mapping, the Riemann surfaces, the differential geometry, the manifolds, the tensors, the curvature, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, the Riemannian geometry, the Lorentzian geometry, the general relativity, the Einstein equations, the differential topology, the degree theory, the index theory, the de Rham cohomology, the Hodge theory, the Poincaré lemma, the Stokes theorem, the probability theory, the stochastic processes, the Brownian motion, the Wiener process, the Ito calculus, the stochastic differential equations, the Kolmogorov equations, the Fokker-Planck equations, the Feynman-Kac formula, the martingales, the Markov processes, the Monte Carlo methods, and many others.
