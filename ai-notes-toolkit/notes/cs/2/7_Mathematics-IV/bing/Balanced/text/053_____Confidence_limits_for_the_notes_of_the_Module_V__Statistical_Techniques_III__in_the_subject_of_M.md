### Confidence limits

- Confidence limits are the numbers at the upper and lower end of a confidence interval.
- A confidence interval is a range of values that contains the true parameter of a population with a given probability.
- The probability is called the confidence level and is usually expressed as a percentage (e.g., 95%).
- The confidence level indicates how often the confidence interval would contain the true parameter if the same experiment were repeated many times.
- Confidence limits are calculated from the sample statistic (e.g., mean, proportion, difference) and the standard error of the sample statistic.
- The standard error measures the variability of the sample statistic due to sampling error.
- The confidence limits depend on the sample size, the confidence level, and the distribution of the population.
- The larger the sample size, the narrower the confidence interval and the more precise the estimate.
- The higher the confidence level, the wider the confidence interval and the more conservative the estimate.
- The distribution of the population affects the shape and the formula of the confidence interval.
- For example, if the population is normally distributed, the confidence interval for the mean is given by:

$$\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

where $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean, $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the critical value of the standard normal distribution for a given confidence level, $s$ is the sample standard deviation, and $n$ is the sample size.

- If the population is not normally distributed, other methods such as bootstrapping or transformation can be used to construct confidence intervals.
- Confidence intervals are useful for estimating unknown population parameters and assessing the uncertainty of the estimates.
- They can also be used to compare two or more groups, test hypotheses, or construct prediction intervals.
