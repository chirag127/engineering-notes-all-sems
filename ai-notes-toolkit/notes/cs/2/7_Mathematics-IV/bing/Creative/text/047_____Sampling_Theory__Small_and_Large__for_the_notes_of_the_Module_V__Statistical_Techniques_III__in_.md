### Sampling Theory (Small and Large)

- Sampling theory is the study of how to select a subset of a population (called a sample) that can represent the characteristics of the whole population.
- Sampling is useful when the population is too large or difficult to measure or observe directly.
- Sampling can reduce the cost and time of data collection and analysis, and can increase the accuracy and precision of the results.
- Sampling methods can be classified into two types: probability sampling and non-probability sampling.
  - Probability sampling is when every element in the population has a known and non-zero chance of being selected in the sample. This ensures that the sample is representative and unbiased, and that the sampling error can be estimated.
  - Non-probability sampling is when the elements in the sample are selected based on convenience, availability, or judgment. This may introduce bias and limit the generalizability of the results, and the sampling error cannot be measured.
- Sampling theory can be studied under two aspects: sampling of attributes and sampling of variables.
  - Sampling of attributes is when the elements in the population are classified into two or more categories based on a certain attribute (such as gender, opinion, or disease status). The sample is used to estimate the proportion or frequency of each category in the population.
  - Sampling of variables is when the elements in the population have a numerical value for a certain variable (such as height, weight, or income). The sample is used to estimate the mean, variance, or other statistics of the variable in the population.
- Sampling theory can also be studied in the context of large and small samples.
  - A large sample is one that includes more than 30 elements, whereas a small sample is one that includes 30 or fewer elements.
  - For large samples, the sampling distribution of the sample mean or proportion is approximately normal, regardless of the shape of the population distribution. This is due to the central limit theorem, which states that the sum or average of a large number of independent and identically distributed random variables tends to follow a normal distribution.
  - For small samples, the sampling distribution of the sample mean or proportion may not be normal, especially if the population distribution is skewed or has outliers. In this case, the sample mean or proportion may not be a good estimator of the population mean or proportion, and the sampling error may be large. For small samples, the sampling distributions of the t, F, and chi-square statistics are often used instead of the normal distribution. These distributions are based on the degrees of freedom, which depend on the sample size and the number of parameters estimated.
