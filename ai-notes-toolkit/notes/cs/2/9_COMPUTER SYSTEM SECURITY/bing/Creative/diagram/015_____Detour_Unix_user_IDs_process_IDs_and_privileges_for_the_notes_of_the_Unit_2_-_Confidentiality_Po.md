### Detour Unix user IDs process IDs and privileges

- Unix is a multi-user operating system that allows multiple users to access the same system and share its resources.
- Each user of a Unix system has a unique identifier called a user ID (UID). The UID is an integer number that is assigned to the user when the account is created. The UID is used to identify the owner of files and processes, and to determine the permissions and privileges of the user.
- The UID 0 is reserved for the superuser (also known as root or system administrator), who has full access and control over the system. The superuser can create, modify, or delete any file or process, regardless of the owner or permissions.
- A user account can belong to multiple groups, each of which has a unique identifier called a group ID (GID). The GID is also an integer number that is assigned to the group when it is created. The GID is used to identify the members of the group and to determine the permissions and privileges of the group.
- A process is an instance of a running program that performs some task on the system. A process is associated with a UID and a GID that determine its owner and its access rights to files and resources. A process inherits the UID and GID of the user who created it, unless the process executes a special program that changes its UID or GID.
- A process can have up to three UIDs and three GIDs associated with it: the real UID (RUID) and GID (RGID), the effective UID (EUID) and GID (EGID), and the saved UID (SUID) and GID (SGID).
  - The RUID and RGID are the original UID and GID of the user who created the process. They are used to identify the user who is responsible for the process and to check the ownership of files and resources.
  - The EUID and EGID are the current UID and GID of the process. They are used to evaluate the permissions and privileges of the process to perform a particular action, such as reading, writing, or executing a file or accessing a resource. The EUID and EGID can be different from the RUID and RGID if the process executes a program that has the setuid or setgid bit set, which allows the process to run with the UID or GID of the owner of the program, rather than the UID or GID of the user who executed it.
  - The SUID and SGID are the UID and GID of the program that has the setuid or setgid bit set. They are used to store the privileged UID or GID that the process can temporarily drop or restore. The SUID and SGID are only meaningful if the EUID or EGID is different from the RUID or RGID.
- A process can change its EUID and EGID as long as the new ID is the same as either the RUID or the SUID, or the RGID or the SGID, respectively. This allows the process to drop or restore its privilege as needed. For example, a process that executes a setuid program can drop its privilege permanently by setting its EUID, RUID, and SUID to the same value, or temporarily by setting its EUID to the RUID and keeping the SUID as the privileged ID, which can be restored later by setting the EUID to the SUID. Similarly, a process can change its EGID and SGID as long as the new ID is the same as either the RGID or the SGID.
