### Derivations for the notes of the Unit 3 - Regular and Non-Regular Grammars in the subject of Theory of Automata and Formal Languages

- A **regular grammar** is a formal grammar that can generate only regular languages, which are a subset of context-free languages. Regular languages can be recognized by finite automata.
- A regular grammar can be either **right-regular** or **left-regular**, depending on the position of the non-terminal symbol in the production rules. A right-regular grammar has the non-terminal symbol on the right-hand side of the production, while a left-regular grammar has it on the left-hand side.
- A regular grammar can be represented by a **regular expression**, which is a concise way of describing the patterns of strings in a regular language. A regular expression can be constructed using the following operations: concatenation, union, and Kleene star.
- A **non-regular grammar** is a formal grammar that can generate languages that are not regular, such as context-free languages, context-sensitive languages, or recursively enumerable languages. Non-regular languages cannot be recognized by finite automata, but require more powerful machines, such as pushdown automata, linear bounded automata, or Turing machines.
- A non-regular grammar can have production rules that are more complex than those of a regular grammar, such as having more than one non-terminal symbol on either side of the production, or having a non-terminal symbol in the middle of the production. A non-regular grammar cannot be represented by a regular expression, but may require a context-free grammar, a context-sensitive grammar, or a recursively enumerable grammar.
