# Humour for the Unit 3 - Technical Presentation

Humour is the quality of being amusing or comic, especially as expressed in literature or speech. Humour can be used in technical presentations to enhance the effectiveness and appeal of the message, as well as to create a rapport with the audience. However, humour should be used carefully and appropriately, as it can also backfire or offend the listeners if done poorly. Here are some points to consider when using humour in technical presentations:

- **Know your audience and purpose.** Different types of humour may suit different audiences and contexts. For example, a technical presentation for a professional conference may not be a suitable place for jokes, but the audience may appreciate some light-hearted anecdotes or examples that relate to the topic. On the other hand, a technical presentation for a general public may benefit from more humour to capture their attention and interest. The purpose of the presentation should also guide the use of humour. For instance, if the presentation aims to persuade or inform, humour should support the main argument or message, not distract from it.
- **Use humour sparingly and strategically.** Too much humour can undermine the credibility and seriousness of the presentation, as well as bore or annoy the audience. Humour should be used as a spice, not as the main dish. It should be relevant, timely, and appropriate to the topic and tone of the presentation. Humour should also be placed strategically in the presentation, such as at the beginning to grab the audience's attention, in the middle to break the monotony or complexity, or at the end to leave a positive impression.
- **Use humour that is self-deprecating, observational, or situational.** These are some of the humour techniques that are more likely to be effective and acceptable in technical presentations, as they do not target or offend anyone, and they can show the speaker's humility, honesty, or insight. Self-deprecating humour is when the speaker makes fun of himself or herself, such as admitting a mistake or weakness. Observational humour is when the speaker comments on something that is common or familiar to the audience, such as a trend or a stereotype. Situational humour is when the speaker uses something that happens in the moment, such as a technical glitch or a coincidence, to make a joke.
- **Avoid humour that is offensive, controversial, or clichéd.** These are some of the humour techniques that are more likely to be ineffective and inappropriate in technical presentations, as they can alienate or anger the audience, and they can damage the speaker's reputation or image. Offensive humour is when the speaker insults or mocks someone or something, such as a person, a group, a culture, or a belief. Controversial humour is when the speaker jokes about something that is sensitive or divisive, such as politics, religion, or sex. Clichéd humour is when the speaker uses something that is overused or predictable, such as a pun, a riddle, or a meme.
- **Practice and test your humour.** Humour is not easy to master, and it requires skill, timing, and delivery. The speaker should practice and test his or her humour before the presentation, such as by rehearsing in front of a mirror, a friend, or a colleague, and by getting feedback and suggestions. The speaker should also be prepared to adapt or abandon his or her humour if it does not work or if it causes a negative reaction from the audience. The speaker should not force or fake his or her humour, as it can be perceived as unnatural or insincere. The speaker should also not apologize or explain his or her humour, as it can ruin the effect or imply a lack of confidence.
