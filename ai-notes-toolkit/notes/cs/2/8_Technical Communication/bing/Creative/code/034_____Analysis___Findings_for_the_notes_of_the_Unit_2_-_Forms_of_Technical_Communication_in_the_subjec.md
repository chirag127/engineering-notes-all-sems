### Analysis & Findings

- Analysis is the process of examining, interpreting, and evaluating data, information, or evidence to draw conclusions, make recommendations, or support decisions.
- Findings are the results or outcomes of the analysis, usually presented in the form of facts, figures, graphs, tables, charts, or diagrams.
- Analysis and findings are important components of technical communication, as they help the audience understand the purpose, scope, methods, results, and implications of a technical document or project.
- Some common types of analysis and findings in technical communication are:

  - **Statistical analysis**: The use of mathematical techniques to summarize, compare, or infer patterns or relationships from numerical data. Examples: mean, median, mode, standard deviation, correlation, regression, hypothesis testing, etc.
  - **Qualitative analysis**: The use of non-numerical methods to explore, describe, or explain phenomena or experiences from textual, visual, or audio data. Examples: thematic analysis, content analysis, discourse analysis, narrative analysis, etc.
  - **Comparative analysis**: The use of criteria or standards to evaluate, contrast, or rank two or more alternatives, options, or solutions. Examples: cost-benefit analysis, SWOT analysis, feasibility analysis, etc.
  - **Causal analysis**: The use of logic or evidence to identify, explain, or predict the causes or effects of a problem, issue, or event. Examples: root cause analysis, fault tree analysis, fishbone diagram, etc.
  - **Impact analysis**: The use of methods or tools to assess the potential or actual consequences or implications of a change, action, or decision on various stakeholders, factors, or systems. Examples: environmental impact assessment, risk analysis, stakeholder analysis, etc.

- Some general guidelines for presenting analysis and findings in technical communication are:

  - **Define the purpose and scope of the analysis**: Explain why the analysis was conducted, what questions or objectives it aimed to address, and what limitations or assumptions it involved.
  - **Describe the methods and data sources of the analysis**: Provide details on how the data was collected, selected, processed, and analyzed, and what tools or techniques were used. Cite and acknowledge the sources of the data and the methods, and ensure their reliability and validity.
  - **Organize and visualize the findings**: Arrange the findings in a logical and coherent order, and use appropriate headings, subheadings, and transitions to guide the reader. Use tables, graphs, charts, or diagrams to display the findings clearly and accurately, and label them with titles, captions, and legends. Refer to the visuals in the text and explain their main points or implications.
  - **Interpret and discuss the findings**: Analyze the findings and explain what they mean, how they relate to the purpose and objectives of the analysis, and how they support or contradict the existing literature or knowledge. Highlight the key findings, trends, patterns, or relationships, and point out any limitations, uncertainties, or anomalies. Provide recommendations, conclusions, or implications based on the findings, and suggest directions for future research or action.
