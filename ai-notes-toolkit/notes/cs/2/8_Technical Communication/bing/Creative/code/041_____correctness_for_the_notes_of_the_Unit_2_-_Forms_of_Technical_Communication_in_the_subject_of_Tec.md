# Correctness for the notes of the Unit 2 - Forms of Technical Communication

Correctness is one of the essential qualities of effective technical communication. It means that the information conveyed is accurate, precise, and free of errors. Correctness also implies that the communication is appropriate for the context, purpose, and audience of the document. Some of the aspects of correctness in technical communication are:

- **Grammatical correctness**: This refers to the proper use of grammar, spelling, punctuation, and syntax in writing. Grammatical errors can undermine the credibility and clarity of the message, and cause confusion or misunderstanding among the readers. To achieve grammatical correctness, technical writers should follow the rules and conventions of standard English, and use tools such as dictionaries, style guides, and proofreading software to check and correct their writing  .

- **Passive voice**: The passive voice is a verb structure that places the object of the action before the subject, and often omits the agent of the action. For example, "The report was written by John" is a passive sentence, while "John wrote the report" is an active sentence. The passive voice is often discouraged in business writing because it is less direct, concise, and clear than the active voice. However, the passive voice can be useful in some situations in technical communication, such as when the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or obvious, or when the focus is on the process or result rather than the actor. For example, "The samples were tested for purity" is a passive sentence that emphasizes the testing process rather than the tester.

- **Commands and directions**: Technical communication often contains a lot of commands and directions, such as instructions, procedures, guidelines, and recommendations. These are usually written in the imperative mood, which expresses a request, order, or advice. For example, "Turn off the power supply before opening the case" is an imperative sentence that gives a direction. To write effective commands and directions, technical writers should use clear, specific, and polite language, and avoid ambiguity, jargon, or slang. They should also use parallel structure, transitional words, and numbering or bulleting to organize the commands and directions in a logical and easy-to-follow way.

- **Bullets and lists**: Technical communication often relies heavily on bulleted or numbered lists to present information in a concise, organized, and visually appealing way. Lists can help the readers to scan, understand, and remember the main points, steps, or features of the document. To write effective lists, technical writers should use consistent formatting, punctuation, and capitalization, and ensure that the items in the list are parallel, coherent, and relevant. They should also use appropriate list markers, such as bullets, numbers, letters, or symbols, depending on the type and purpose of the list.

- **Contextual correctness**: This refers to the suitability and appropriateness of the communication for the specific situation, purpose, and audience. Contextual correctness involves considering the tone, style, format, and level of detail of the document, and adapting them to the needs and expectations of the readers. For example, a technical report for a scientific journal may require a formal tone, a standard format, and a high level of detail, while a user manual for a software product may require a friendly tone, a customized format, and a moderate level of detail. To achieve contextual correctness, technical writers should analyze the communication context, and use strategies such as audience analysis, document design, and feedback to tailor their writing to the situation.
