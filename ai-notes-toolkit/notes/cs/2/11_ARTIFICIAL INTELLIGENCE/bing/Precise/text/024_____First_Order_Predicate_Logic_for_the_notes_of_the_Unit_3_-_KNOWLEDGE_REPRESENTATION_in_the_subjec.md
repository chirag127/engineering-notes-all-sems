### First Order Predicate Logic

First-order predicate logic is a formal system used in mathematics, philosophy, linguistics, and computer science. It is also known as first-order logic, predicate calculus, or quantification theory. It is used to represent and reason about statements that involve objects, properties, and relations.

Here are some key points to remember about first-order predicate logic:

1. First-order predicate logic extends propositional logic by introducing quantifiers, which allow us to make statements about collections of objects.
2. The two most common quantifiers are the universal quantifier, denoted by the symbol ∀, and the existential quantifier, denoted by the symbol ∃.
3. The universal quantifier is used to make statements that are true for all objects in a given domain, while the existential quantifier is used to make statements that are true for at least one object in the domain.
4. First-order predicate logic also introduces the concept of predicates, which are used to represent properties of objects or relations between objects.
5. Predicates are usually denoted by capital letters, and can take one or more arguments, which represent the objects that the predicate applies to.
6. In first-order predicate logic, statements are formed by combining predicates and quantifiers using logical connectives such as and, or, and not.
7. First-order predicate logic is more expressive than propositional logic, as it allows us to represent and reason about statements that involve objects and their properties and relations.
