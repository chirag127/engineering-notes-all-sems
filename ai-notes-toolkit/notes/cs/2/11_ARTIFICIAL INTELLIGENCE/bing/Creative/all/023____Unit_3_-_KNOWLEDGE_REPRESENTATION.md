## Unit 3 - KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATION

- Knowledge representation is the process of encoding information about a domain in a form that can be used by an intelligent agent to perform reasoning and problem-solving tasks.
- Knowledge representation involves choosing a suitable representation language, defining the syntax and semantics of the language, and designing methods for acquiring, manipulating, and using the knowledge in the language.
- Some of the goals of knowledge representation are:
  - Expressiveness: the ability to represent complex and diverse concepts and relations in the domain.
  - Efficiency: the ability to perform reasoning and problem-solving tasks with minimal computational resources.
  - Adequacy: the ability to capture the relevant and essential aspects of the domain and the agent's goals and beliefs.
  - Learnability: the ability to acquire new knowledge from various sources and update existing knowledge based on new evidence and feedback.
- Some of the challenges of knowledge representation are:
  - Uncertainty: the presence of incomplete, inconsistent, or noisy information in the domain or the agent's observations.
  - Incompleteness: the impossibility of representing all the possible facts and rules in the domain or the agent's knowledge base.
  - Intractability: the difficulty of finding efficient algorithms for reasoning and problem-solving tasks in some representation languages.
  - Trade-offs: the need to balance between different criteria and objectives when choosing a representation language and a reasoning method.
- Some of the common types of knowledge representation languages are:
  - Logic: a formal language that uses symbols and rules to represent facts and inferences in the domain. Logic can be divided into propositional logic, predicate logic, modal logic, temporal logic, etc. depending on the expressive power and the types of concepts and relations that can be represented.
  - Semantic networks: a graphical language that uses nodes and links to represent concepts and relations in the domain. Semantic networks can be divided into hierarchical networks, associative networks, causal networks, etc. depending on the types of links and the semantics of the nodes.
  - Frames: a language that uses structures to represent concepts and attributes in the domain. Frames can be divided into slots and fillers, scripts, schemas, etc. depending on the types of attributes and the organization of the structures.
  - Production rules: a language that uses conditional statements to represent actions and effects in the domain. Production rules can be divided into forward chaining, backward chaining, etc. depending on the direction and the order of applying the rules.
  - Ontologies: a language that uses terms and definitions to represent categories and properties in the domain. Ontologies can be divided into taxonomies, thesauri, conceptual graphs, etc. depending on the level of abstraction and the granularity of the terms.
