### Knowledge Representation
- Knowledge representation is the process of encoding information about a domain in a form that can be used by an intelligent agent to reason and act.
- Knowledge representation is a key component of artificial intelligence, as it enables the agent to store, manipulate, and infer knowledge from data and facts.
- Knowledge representation involves two aspects: syntax and semantics.
  - Syntax is the set of rules that define how symbols and expressions can be combined to form valid sentences or statements in a language.
  - Semantics is the meaning or interpretation of the sentences or statements in a language, given a context or a model of the domain.
- Knowledge representation also requires a trade-off between expressiveness and tractability.
  - Expressiveness is the ability of a language to capture complex and nuanced concepts and relations in a domain.
  - Tractability is the ease and efficiency of performing reasoning and computation with a language.
  - Generally, the more expressive a language is, the less tractable it is, and vice versa.
- There are different types of knowledge representation languages, each with its own advantages and disadvantages, depending on the domain and the task.
  - Some of the common types are:
    - Logic-based languages: use formal logic to represent facts and rules, and use logical inference to derive new knowledge. Examples are propositional logic, first-order logic, modal logic, etc.
    - Semantic networks: use nodes and links to represent concepts and relations, and use graph algorithms to perform reasoning and search. Examples are conceptual graphs, frames, ontologies, etc.
    - Production systems: use condition-action rules to represent knowledge, and use forward or backward chaining to apply the rules. Examples are expert systems, rule-based systems, etc.
    - Probabilistic languages: use probabilities and statistics to represent uncertainty and variability in knowledge, and use probabilistic inference to compute the likelihood of events and hypotheses. Examples are Bayesian networks, Markov models, etc.
