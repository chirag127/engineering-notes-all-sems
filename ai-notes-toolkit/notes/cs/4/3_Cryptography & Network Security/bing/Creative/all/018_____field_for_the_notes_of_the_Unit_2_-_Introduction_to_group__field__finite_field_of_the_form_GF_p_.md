- A **group** is a set of elements with a binary operation that satisfies four properties: closure, associativity, identity, and inverse. A group is **abelian** if it also satisfies commutativity. Examples of groups are the integers with addition, the nonzero rational numbers with multiplication, and the set of permutations with composition.
- A **field** is a set of elements with two binary operations, usually called addition and multiplication, that satisfy the properties of a group for both operations, as well as distributivity, commutativity, and identity for both operations. Examples of fields are the rational numbers, the real numbers, and the complex numbers with the usual addition and multiplication.
- A **finite field** is a field that has a finite number of elements. A finite field of order p, where p is a prime number, is denoted by GF(p) and is defined as the set of integers {0, 1, ..., p-1} with arithmetic operations modulo p. For example, GF(2) is the set {0, 1} with addition and multiplication modulo 2. A finite field of order p^n, where n is a positive integer, is denoted by GF(p^n) and is defined as the set of polynomials of degree less than n with coefficients in GF(p) and arithmetic operations modulo a primitive polynomial of degree n. For example, GF(2^3) is the set of polynomials of degree less than 3 with coefficients in {0, 1} and arithmetic operations modulo x^3 + x + 1, which is a primitive polynomial of degree 3.
- **Modular arithmetic** is a system of arithmetic for integers, where numbers "wrap around" after reaching a certain value, called the modulus. For example, in modulo 12 arithmetic, 15 is equivalent to 3, because 15 - 3 is divisible by 12. Modular arithmetic is useful for cryptography because it allows for operations that are easy to perform but hard to reverse, such as exponentiation and multiplication.
- A **prime number** is a natural number greater than 1 that has no positive divisors other than 1 and itself. For example, 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, and 13 are prime numbers. A **composite number** is a natural number greater than 1 that is not prime. For example, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, and 12 are composite numbers.
- Two natural numbers are **relatively prime** or **coprime** if they have no common positive divisors other than 1. For example, 8 and 15 are relatively prime, because their only common divisor is 1. Two natural numbers are **divisible** or **congruent** modulo n if their difference is divisible by n. For example, 15 and 27 are congruent modulo 12, because 15 - 27 = -12 is divisible by 12. The notation for congruence modulo n is a ≡ b (mod n), where a and b are the numbers and n is the modulus. For example, 15 ≡ 27 (mod 12).
- The **Extended Euclidean Algorithm** is an algorithm that computes the greatest common divisor (gcd) of two natural numbers, as well as the coefficients of a linear combination of the two numbers that equals the gcd. For example, the gcd of 30 and 18 is 6, and the Extended Euclidean Algorithm can find integers x and y such that 30x + 18y = 6. The algorithm works by applying repeated divisions with remainder until the remainder is zero, and then backtracking to find the coefficients. The algorithm is useful for cryptography because it can be used to find the multiplicative inverse of a number modulo n, which is the number x such that ax ≡ 1 (mod n), where a is the given number and n is the modulus. For example, the multiplicative inverse of 7 modulo 26 is 15, because 7 * 15 ≡ 1 (mod 26).
