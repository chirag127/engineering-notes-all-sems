# Public Distribution System: Social Welfare Systems

- The Public Distribution System (PDS) is a scheme that provides subsidized food grains and other essential commodities to the poor and vulnerable sections of the society through a network of fair price shops (FPS).
- The PDS is one of the largest social welfare programs in the world, covering nearly 800 million people in India, or about two-thirds of the population.
- The PDS is implemented by the central and state governments in a cooperative manner. The central government is responsible for procurement, allocation, and transportation of food grains, while the state governments are responsible for identification of beneficiaries, distribution of food grains, and monitoring of FPS.
- The PDS is financed by the central and state governments through budgetary allocations, food subsidies, and levies on food grains. In 2017, the central government spent about $16 billion on the PDS, or about 1 percent of GDP.
- The PDS is regulated by the National Food Security Act (NFSA) of 2013, which entitles 75 percent of the rural population and 50 percent of the urban population to receive 5 kg of food grains per person per month at subsidized prices of Rs. 3, 2, and 1 per kg for rice, wheat, and coarse grains, respectively.
- The PDS is also linked to other social welfare schemes, such as the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), which provides 100 days of guaranteed wage employment to rural households, and the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS), which provides supplementary nutrition, health, and education services to children under six years and pregnant and lactating women.
- The PDS has been credited with improving food security, reducing poverty, and enhancing human development in India. However, it also faces several challenges, such as leakages, diversion, corruption, inefficiency, and exclusion of the needy.
- The PDS has been undergoing various reforms, such as computerization, digitization, biometric authentication, direct benefit transfer, and end-to-end automation, to improve its transparency, accountability, and effectiveness. The PDS is also being integrated with the Aadhaar system, which is a unique identification number for every resident of India, to eliminate duplicate and fake beneficiaries and ensure targeted delivery of benefits.
- The PDS is an example of a distributive social welfare policy, which aims to benefit everyone equally, with aid dependent on taxes. Distributive policies are different from redistributive policies, which aim to transfer resources from the rich to the poor, or from the majority to the minority, to reduce inequality and promote social justice.
- Distributive policies are also different from regulatory policies, which aim to control or limit the behavior of individuals or groups, such as environmental protection, consumer safety, or public health.
- Social welfare policies are a fundamental determinant of population health and health inequalities, in high and low-income countries alike. They account for a substantial share of public expenditure and have a direct impact on the living conditions, income, and access to services of the people.
- Social welfare policies are influenced by various factors, such as political ideology, economic development, social movements, public opinion, and international organizations. They also reflect the values, norms, and preferences of the society.
- Social welfare policies are evaluated based on their efficiency, effectiveness, equity, and sustainability. Efficiency refers to the optimal use of resources to achieve the desired outcomes. Effectiveness refers to the extent to which the policy goals are achieved. Equity refers to the fairness and justice of the policy outcomes and impacts. Sustainability refers to the long-term viability and durability of the policy.
