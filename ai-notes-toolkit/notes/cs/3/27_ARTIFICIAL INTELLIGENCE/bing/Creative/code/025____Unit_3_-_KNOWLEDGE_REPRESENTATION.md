# Unit 3 - KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATION

- Knowledge representation is the process of encoding information about a domain in a form that can be used by an intelligent agent to reason and act.
- Knowledge representation involves choosing a language or a data structure to represent the relevant aspects of the domain, such as objects, properties, relations, events, actions, goals, etc.
- Knowledge representation also involves designing inference mechanisms or algorithms that can manipulate the representations to derive new knowledge, answer queries, solve problems, or plan actions.
- Knowledge representation is a key component of artificial intelligence, as it enables agents to model the world, reason about it, and communicate with other agents or humans.
- Some of the criteria for evaluating knowledge representation languages or systems are:
  - Expressiveness: the ability to capture the complexity and diversity of the domain.
  - Efficiency: the ease and speed of performing inference and computation on the representations.
  - Understandability: the clarity and simplicity of the representations for human users or developers.
  - Extensibility: the possibility of adding new knowledge or modifying existing knowledge without affecting the consistency or validity of the representations.
- Some of the common types of knowledge representation languages or systems are:
  - Logic: a formal system of symbols and rules that can express propositions, facts, rules, and queries, and support deductive reasoning and proof. Examples are propositional logic, first-order logic, modal logic, etc.
  - Semantic networks: a graph-based structure that represents concepts as nodes and relations as links, and supports associative and hierarchical reasoning. Examples are WordNet, ConceptNet, etc.
  - Frames: a structure that represents a concept or an entity as a collection of slots and values, and supports inheritance and default reasoning. Examples are CYC, Ontolingua, etc.
  - Production rules: a structure that represents a condition-action pair, and supports forward and backward chaining reasoning. Examples are CLIPS, Jess, etc.
  - Bayesian networks: a graphical model that represents probabilistic dependencies among variables, and supports probabilistic reasoning and inference. Examples are HUGIN, BNT, etc.
