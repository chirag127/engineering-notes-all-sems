### Architecture for Intelligent Agents

- An **intelligent agent** is a system that perceives its environment via sensors and acts rationally upon that environment with its effectors.
- The **architecture** of an agent describes its modules and capabilities, and how these operate together.
- The architecture of an agent determines how it processes the information from its sensors and how it selects the actions to perform with its effectors.
- There are different types of architectures for intelligent agents, depending on their level of complexity, rationality, and interaction.
- Some of the common types of architectures are:

  - **Reactive agents**: Reactive agents are the simplest type of AI agent. They do not have any internal state or memory, and they act only based on the current percept. They do not have any goals or plans, and they do not reason about the consequences of their actions. Reactive agents are fast and robust, but they are limited in their capabilities and adaptability.
  - **Model-based reflex agents**: Model-based reflex agents are an improvement over reactive agents. They have an internal state that represents some aspects of the environment that are not directly observable by the sensors. They use this state to update their knowledge of the environment and to infer the effects of their actions. Model-based reflex agents are more flexible and rational than reactive agents, but they still lack any explicit goals or plans.
  - **Goal-based agents**: Goal-based agents are more sophisticated than model-based reflex agents. They have an internal state that represents not only the environment, but also their own goals. They use this state to generate and evaluate possible actions that can achieve their goals. Goal-based agents are more intelligent and proactive than model-based reflex agents, but they still do not consider the utility or preferences of their actions.
  - **Utility-based agents**: Utility-based agents are an extension of goal-based agents. They have an internal state that represents not only the environment and their goals, but also their own preferences or utility function. They use this state to compare and rank the possible actions that can achieve their goals, and to select the one that maximizes their utility. Utility-based agents are more rational and optimal than goal-based agents, but they still do not interact with other agents.
  - **Interacting agents**: Interacting agents are the most advanced type of AI agent. They have an internal state that represents not only the environment, their goals, and their utility, but also the other agents in the environment. They use this state to communicate, cooperate, coordinate, negotiate, or compete with other agents. Interacting agents are more social and adaptive than utility-based agents, but they also face more challenges and complexities.

- Some examples of architectures for intelligent agents are:

  - **DALI**: DALI is a logic-based architecture for defining intelligent agents that can reason, learn, plan, and communicate. DALI agents are based on a dialectical logic programming language that allows them to represent and update their beliefs, goals, and plans, and to perform abductive, inductive, and deductive reasoning. DALI agents can also communicate with other DALI agents or with humans using speech acts and natural language.
  - **BDI**: BDI is a popular architecture for designing intelligent agents that can act in dynamic and uncertain environments. BDI agents are based on the notion of beliefs, desires, and intentions, which are the mental attitudes that guide their behavior. Beliefs are the agent's knowledge of the environment, desires are the agent's goals or preferences, and intentions are the agent's commitments or plans to achieve some desires. BDI agents can deliberate and select the best intentions based on their beliefs and desires, and can execute and monitor their intentions using reactive rules.
  - **SOAR**: SOAR is a cognitive architecture for developing intelligent agents that can perform a wide range of tasks and domains. SOAR agents are based on the idea of problem spaces, which are the representations of the agent's knowledge and goals in a specific task or domain. SOAR agents can solve problems by applying operators to transform the problem space from an initial state to a goal state, and can learn from their experience by creating and using rules that generalize their operators. SOAR agents can also interact with other SOAR agents or with humans using natural language or graphical interfaces.
