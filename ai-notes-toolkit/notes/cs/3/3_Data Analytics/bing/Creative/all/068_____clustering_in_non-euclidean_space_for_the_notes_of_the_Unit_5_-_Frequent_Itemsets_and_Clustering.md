# Clustering in Non-Euclidean Space

Clustering is the process of grouping data points into meaningful and coherent subsets based on some similarity or distance measure. Clustering can be useful for exploratory data analysis, data compression, data visualization, and data mining.

Non-Euclidean space is a space that does not follow the rules of Euclidean geometry, such as the Pythagorean theorem or the parallel postulate. Non-Euclidean spaces can have different shapes, such as spherical, hyperbolic, or elliptic. Non-Euclidean spaces can also have different metrics, such as the Manhattan distance, the Minkowski distance, or the Mahalanobis distance.

Clustering in non-Euclidean space can be challenging, because some of the assumptions and properties of Euclidean space do not hold. For example, the concept of a cluster center may not be well-defined, the distance between two points may not be symmetric or satisfy the triangle inequality, and the number of dimensions may not be fixed or finite.

Some of the methods and techniques for clustering in non-Euclidean space are:

- Non-Euclidean c-means clustering algorithms: These algorithms rely on weighted norms to measure the distance between the feature vectors and the prototypes that represent the clusters. The proposed algorithms are developed by solving a constrained minimization problem in an iterative fashion.
- Ward method of hierarchical clustering for non-Euclidean similarity: This method is a variant of the Ward linkage method in agglomerative hierarchical clustering, which minimizes the within-cluster sum of squares. This method can be used for non-Euclidean similarity, i.e., non-positive definite matrix of similarity, by transforming the similarity matrix into a dissimilarity matrix and applying the Ward method on the dissimilarity matrix.
- Medoid-based clustering: This method is a generalization of the k-means clustering algorithm, which uses the medoid as the cluster center instead of the mean. The medoid is the most representative point of a cluster, i.e., the point that has the smallest average distance to all other points in the cluster. This method can work with arbitrary metrics, but it may not be unique or stable for small clusters.
- Spectral clustering: This method is based on the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the similarity matrix of the data points. The similarity matrix can be constructed using any kernel function that measures the affinity between two points. The method then applies a dimensionality reduction technique, such as principal component analysis or multidimensional scaling, to project the data points into a lower-dimensional space, where the clusters can be identified using a simple clustering algorithm, such as k-means .
- Density-based clustering: This method is based on the idea that clusters are dense regions of data points that are separated by sparse regions. The method does not require a predefined number of clusters or a distance metric, but instead uses a density threshold and a neighborhood radius to determine the core points, the border points, and the noise points of each cluster. The method can handle clusters of arbitrary shapes and sizes, as well as outliers and noise.
