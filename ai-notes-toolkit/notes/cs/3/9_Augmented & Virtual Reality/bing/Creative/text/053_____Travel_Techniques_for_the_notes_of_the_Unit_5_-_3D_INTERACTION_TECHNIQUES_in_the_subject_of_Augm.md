### Travel Techniques

Travel techniques are methods for moving the user's viewpoint in a 3D virtual environment. Travel is the motor component of navigation, which also involves wayfinding (the cognitive process of determining and following a route). Travel is a common and essential task in many 3D applications, especially when the virtual environment is larger than the user's physical space or display.

Travel techniques can be classified by the metaphor they use, such as:

- Walking: the user moves the viewpoint by simulating the natural motion of walking, either by using physical devices (such as treadmills, motion platforms, or foot pedals) or by using gestures or buttons to control the speed and direction of movement. Walking techniques aim to provide a high sense of presence and spatial awareness, but they may also cause fatigue, motion sickness, or collisions with physical obstacles.
- Steering: the user moves the viewpoint by pointing or orienting a device (such as a mouse, a joystick, a wand, or a head tracker) in the desired direction of movement. Steering techniques are simple and intuitive, but they may also cause disorientation, vection (the illusion of self-motion), or loss of accuracy.
- Selection-based travel: the user moves the viewpoint by selecting a target location or object in the virtual environment, either by using a cursor, a ray, or a gesture. Selection-based travel techniques are fast and precise, but they may also cause spatial discontinuity, cognitive overload, or lack of feedback.
- Manipulation-based travel: the user moves the viewpoint by manipulating the virtual environment, either by scaling, rotating, or translating it. Manipulation-based travel techniques are flexible and powerful, but they may also cause distortion, inconsistency, or loss of realism.

Some travel techniques may also incorporate different perspectives of multiple cameras, such as through-the-lens, transitioning to remote camera feeds, or snapshots of augmented scenes. These techniques may provide additional information or guidance, but they may also cause confusion, distraction, or loss of immersion.

Travel techniques should be designed and evaluated according to several criteria, such as:

- Effectiveness: how well the technique allows the user to reach the desired destination or explore the virtual environment.
- Efficiency: how fast and easy the technique is to use, and how much physical or mental effort it requires.
- Satisfaction: how enjoyable and comfortable the technique is for the user, and how much it supports the user's sense of presence and spatial awareness.
- Compatibility: how well the technique matches the application's goals, tasks, and context, and how well it integrates with other interaction techniques and devices.
