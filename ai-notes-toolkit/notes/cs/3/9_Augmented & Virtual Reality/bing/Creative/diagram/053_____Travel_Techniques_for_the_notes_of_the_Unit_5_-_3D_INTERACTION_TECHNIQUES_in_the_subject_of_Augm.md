# Travel Techniques

Travel techniques are methods for moving the user's viewpoint in a 3D virtual environment. Travel is the motor component of navigation, which also involves wayfinding (the cognitive process of determining and following a route). Travel is one of the most basic and common 3D interaction techniques, used in almost any large-scale virtual environment.

Travel techniques can be classified by the metaphor they use, such as:

- Walking: the user moves the viewpoint by simulating the natural motion of walking, either by using physical input devices (such as treadmills, pedals, or motion sensors) or by using non-physical input devices (such as keyboards, mice, or joysticks). Walking techniques aim to provide a high level of immersion and naturalness, but they may also cause fatigue, motion sickness, or spatial disorientation.
- Steering: the user moves the viewpoint by controlling the direction and speed of a virtual vehicle, such as a car, a plane, or a spaceship. Steering techniques use non-physical input devices, such as keyboards, mice, joysticks, or steering wheels. Steering techniques offer a high level of control and feedback, but they may also require a high level of skill and attention, and they may not match the user's expectations or preferences.
- Selection-based travel: the user moves the viewpoint by selecting a target location or direction in the virtual environment, either by pointing, clicking, or gazing. Selection-based travel techniques use non-physical input devices, such as mice, touchscreens, or eye trackers. Selection-based travel techniques are simple and efficient, but they may also cause a loss of spatial awareness, a lack of continuity, or a sense of teleportation.
- Manipulation-based travel: the user moves the viewpoint by manipulating the virtual environment, such as by grabbing, dragging, or scaling. Manipulation-based travel techniques use non-physical input devices, such as mice, touchscreens, or gesture recognition. Manipulation-based travel techniques are intuitive and flexible, but they may also cause a loss of realism, a violation of physical constraints, or a distortion of perspective.

Travel techniques can also be classified by other aspects, such as:

- Input devices: the physical or non-physical devices that the user uses to control the travel technique, such as keyboards, mice, joysticks, steering wheels, treadmills, pedals, motion sensors, touchscreens, eye trackers, or gesture recognition.
- Output devices: the devices that display the virtual environment to the user, such as monitors, projectors, head-mounted displays, or augmented reality glasses.
- Feedback: the information that the user receives from the travel technique, such as visual, auditory, or haptic cues, that indicate the current position, orientation, speed, or direction of the viewpoint.
- Constraints: the limitations or rules that the travel technique imposes on the user's movement, such as collision detection, gravity, inertia, or boundaries.
- Perspectives: the different views of the virtual environment that the user can switch between, such as first-person, third-person, bird's-eye, or through-the-lens.
