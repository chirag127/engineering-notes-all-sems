### Visual Displays

Visual displays are output devices that present visual information to the user in 3D user interfaces. They can vary in size, shape, resolution, field of view, field of regard, and degree of immersion. They can also support different types of stereoscopic rendering, such as active or passive, to create a sense of depth and realism.

The main types of visual displays used in 3D user interfaces are:

- **Monitors**: These are flat-screen displays that show 2D or 3D images on a desktop or wall. They are the most common and affordable type of display, but they have limited field of view and immersion. They can be used for windowed or full-screen applications, and can support stereoscopic rendering with special glasses or filters.
- **Surround-screen displays**: These are large-scale displays that surround the user with multiple screens or projectors. They provide a wider field of view and a higher sense of presence than monitors, but they are more expensive and require more space. They can be used for immersive or semi-immersive applications, and can support stereoscopic rendering with special glasses or filters.
- **Workbenches**: These are table-top displays that show 3D images on a horizontal surface. They allow the user to interact with the images from above or from the sides, and can support multiple users and viewpoints. They are suitable for collaborative or manipulative tasks, and can support stereoscopic rendering with special glasses or filters.
- **Hemispherical displays**: These are dome-shaped displays that cover the upper half of the user's field of view. They provide a high degree of immersion and a natural perspective, but they are costly and require a dedicated room. They can be used for panoramic or environmental applications, and can support stereoscopic rendering with special glasses or filters.
- **Head-mounted displays (HMDs)**: These are wearable displays that attach to the user's head and show 3D images in front of the eyes. They provide a very high degree of immersion and a personal viewpoint, but they are heavy and uncomfortable, and can cause motion sickness or eye strain. They can be used for fully immersive or augmented reality applications, and can support stereoscopic rendering with built-in lenses or screens.
- **Arm-mounted displays**: These are wearable displays that attach to the user's arm and show 3D images on a small screen. They provide a portable and convenient way of accessing 3D information, but they have low resolution and limited interaction. They can be used for mobile or wearable applications, and can support stereoscopic rendering with special glasses or filters.
- **Autostereoscopic displays**: These are displays that show 3D images without the need for special glasses or filters. They use various techniques, such as lenticular lenses, parallax barriers, or holograms, to create different views for each eye. They provide a natural and comfortable way of viewing 3D information, but they have low resolution and narrow viewing angles. They can be used for public or personal applications, and can support monoscopic or stereoscopic rendering.

Some common visual display characteristics that affect the quality and performance of 3D user interfaces are:

- **Brightness**: This is the amount of light emitted by the display. It affects the visibility and contrast of the images, and can influence the user's mood and attention. Brightness can be measured in candelas per square meter (cd/m2) or nits, and can be adjusted by the display settings or the ambient lighting.
- **Color contrast**: This is the difference in color and brightness between the foreground and the background of the images. It affects the clarity and legibility of the images, and can influence the user's perception and cognition. Color contrast can be measured by the ratio of the luminance of the brightest and the darkest colors, and can be adjusted by the display settings or the color scheme.
- **Gamma correction**: This is the process of adjusting the brightness and contrast of the images to match the characteristics of the display and the human eye. It affects the accuracy and realism of the images, and can influence the user's satisfaction and preference. Gamma correction can be measured by the exponent of the power function that relates the input and output values, and can be adjusted by the display settings or the image processing.
