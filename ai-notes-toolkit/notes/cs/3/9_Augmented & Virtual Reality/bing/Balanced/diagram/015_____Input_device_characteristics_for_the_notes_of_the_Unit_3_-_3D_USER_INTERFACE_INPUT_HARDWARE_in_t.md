### Input device characteristics

- Input devices are hardware that allow the user to communicate with the system by providing input in 3D space  .
- Input devices can be classified according to the following characteristics :
  - Degrees of freedom (DOFs): the number and type of independent parameters that define the position and orientation of the device or the user's body part in 3D space. For example, a 6-DOF device can report three translational and three rotational parameters.
  - DOF composition: the way the DOFs are grouped or separated in the device. For example, a device can have integral DOFs, where all the DOFs are controlled by a single physical component, or separable DOFs, where different DOFs are controlled by different components.
  - Type of electronics: the way the device reports its state to the system. For example, a device can be digital, where it reports discrete values (e.g., on/off, 0/1), or analog, where it reports continuous values (e.g., voltage, position).
  - Range of reported values: the set of possible values that the device can report for each DOF. For example, a device can have discrete, continuous, or hybrid range of values. A discrete range is a finite set of values, such as the keys on a keyboard. A continuous range is an infinite set of values, such as the position of a mouse. A hybrid range is a combination of discrete and continuous values, such as the buttons and joystick on a gamepad.
  - Sensing technology: the method or principle that the device uses to measure its state or the user's input. For example, a device can use mechanical, optical, magnetic, acoustic, inertial, or other types of sensors to detect motion, orientation, force, or touch.
  - Device form factor: the physical shape, size, weight, and ergonomics of the device. For example, a device can be handheld, wearable, desktop, or room-scale. The form factor affects the user's comfort, mobility, and naturalness of interaction.
  - Device feedback: the type and amount of information that the device provides to the user through its physical properties or behavior. For example, a device can provide tactile, force, or auditory feedback to the user, such as vibration, resistance, or sound. The feedback can enhance the user's perception, awareness, and performance of the interaction.

- Input devices can be categorized into the following types :
  - Traditional input devices: devices that are commonly used for 2D interaction, such as keyboards, mice, trackballs, touchpads, or touchscreens. These devices can also be used for 3D interaction, but they have some limitations, such as low DOFs, low naturalness, or high cognitive load.
  - Spatial input devices: devices that are designed for 3D interaction, such as 3D mice, joysticks, trackers, gloves, or wands. These devices can report the position and orientation of the device or the user's body part in 3D space, and allow the user to perform direct manipulation, navigation, or gesture-based interaction.
  - Active and passive sensing devices: devices that use active or passive methods to measure the state or input of the device or the user. Active sensing devices emit some form of energy, such as light, sound, or magnetic field, and measure the reflection or interference of the energy to determine the position or orientation of the device or the user. Passive sensing devices rely on external sources of energy, such as natural light, gravity, or earth's magnetic field, and measure the intensity or direction of the energy to determine the state or input of the device or the user.
  - Complementary input devices: devices that provide additional or alternative input modalities to the user, such as speech, eye gaze, facial expression, or brain activity. These devices can enhance the user's expressiveness, accessibility, or immersion of the interaction, but they also have some challenges, such as accuracy, reliability, or privacy.
  - Special purpose input devices: devices that are designed for specific tasks or applications, such as steering wheels, flight sticks, haptic devices, or musical instruments. These devices can provide high fidelity, realism, or naturalness of the interaction, but they also have some drawbacks, such as cost, complexity, or portability.
