# User Centered Wayfinding Support

- Wayfinding is the process of navigating and orienting oneself in an unfamiliar environment.
- Wayfinding support is the provision of cues, aids, or tools that facilitate wayfinding.
- User centered wayfinding support is the design of wayfinding support that considers the needs, preferences, and characteristics of the users.
- User centered wayfinding support can improve the usability, accessibility, and satisfaction of 3D user interfaces.
- User centered wayfinding support can also enhance the spatial knowledge, presence, and immersion of the users in 3D environments.

## User Centered Wayfinding Support Techniques

- User centered wayfinding support techniques can be classified into three categories: environmental, cognitive, and behavioral.
- Environmental techniques are those that modify the 3D environment to provide wayfinding cues, such as landmarks, signs, maps, or trails.
- Cognitive techniques are those that augment the user's cognitive abilities to process wayfinding information, such as auditory feedback, haptic feedback, or head-up displays.
- Behavioral techniques are those that influence the user's behavior to facilitate wayfinding, such as guidance, steering, or teleportation.

## User Centered Wayfinding Support Design Principles

- User centered wayfinding support design principles are guidelines that can help designers to create effective and efficient wayfinding support for 3D user interfaces.
- Some of the design principles are:
  - Provide multiple and consistent cues: use different types of cues (e.g., visual, auditory, haptic) and ensure they are consistent across the 3D environment.
  - Provide global and local information: use cues that provide both an overview of the 3D environment and details of the current location and direction.
  - Provide adaptive and personalized information: use cues that adapt to the user's needs, preferences, and characteristics, such as skill level, spatial ability, or language.
  - Provide feedback and confirmation: use cues that inform the user of their progress and confirm their actions, such as sound effects, animations, or messages.
  - Provide clear and simple information: use cues that are easy to understand and follow, such as symbols, icons, or colors.
  - Provide minimal and unobtrusive information: use cues that are sufficient but not excessive, and that do not interfere with the user's tasks or immersion, such as transparency, fading, or hiding.
