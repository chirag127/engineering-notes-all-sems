### Common Approaches to Real Time Scheduling

- Real time scheduling is the process of assigning execution time to tasks that have timing constraints, such as deadlines or periodicity.
- Real time scheduling aims to ensure that tasks meet their timing requirements, while maximizing system performance and resource utilization.
- There are different approaches to real time scheduling, depending on the characteristics of the tasks, the system, and the environment.
- Some of the common approaches are:

  - **Clock-driven approach**: This approach is also known as time-driven or table-driven approach. It is mainly used for hard real time systems, where all the properties of the tasks, such as arrival time, execution time, deadline, and period, are known at design time. In this approach, a static schedule is computed offline, based on the worst-case execution time of the tasks, and stored in a table. The table specifies the start time and the duration of each task in each cycle. A timer interrupts the system at regular intervals, and triggers the execution of the tasks according to the table. This approach guarantees that all tasks will meet their deadlines, as long as the system does not deviate from the assumptions made during the offline analysis. However, this approach is not flexible or adaptable to dynamic changes in the system or the environment, such as task arrivals, variations in execution time, or resource failures. It also may waste CPU time if the tasks finish earlier than their worst-case execution time  .

  - **Round-robin approach**: This approach is also known as cyclic executive or time-slice approach. It is a simple and fair scheduling algorithm, commonly used in time-shared systems. In this approach, tasks are scheduled in a circular order, based on a fixed time slice allocated to each task. The scheduler switches from one task to another after the expiration of the time slice, regardless of the task's state or priority. This approach ensures that all tasks get a chance to execute, and avoids starvation. However, this approach does not consider the timing requirements of the tasks, such as deadlines or periods. It also may introduce unnecessary context switches, which increase the overhead and reduce the system performance .

  - **Weighted round-robin approach**: This approach is a variation of the round-robin approach, where tasks are assigned different weights, based on their importance or priority. The weight of a task determines the length of the time slice allocated to it, such that higher-weight tasks get longer time slices than lower-weight tasks. This approach tries to balance the fairness and the performance of the system, by giving more CPU time to more critical tasks, while still allowing lower-priority tasks to execute. However, this approach still does not consider the timing requirements of the tasks, such as deadlines or periods. It also may introduce unnecessary context switches, which increase the overhead and reduce the system performance .

  - **Priority-driven approach**: This approach is also known as preemptive or event-driven approach. It is widely used for soft and hard real time systems, where tasks have dynamic and unpredictable properties, such as arrival time, execution time, deadline, and period. In this approach, tasks are assigned priorities, based on their timing requirements or other criteria. The scheduler always selects the highest-priority task that is ready to execute, and preempts the currently running task if a higher-priority task arrives. This approach ensures that the most urgent tasks are executed first, and increases the chances of meeting the deadlines. However, this approach may suffer from priority inversion, where a low-priority task blocks a high-priority task from executing, due to resource contention or synchronization. It also may suffer from deadline misses, if the system is overloaded or the tasks have unpredictable execution times   .

  - **Dynamic versus static systems**: This is a classification of real time systems, based on the degree of predictability and variability of the tasks and the environment. A static system is one where all the properties of the tasks, such as arrival time, execution time, deadline, and period, are known at design time, and do not change during the system execution. A dynamic system is one where some or all of the properties of the tasks are unknown at design time, and may change during the system execution, due to external events or internal factors.
