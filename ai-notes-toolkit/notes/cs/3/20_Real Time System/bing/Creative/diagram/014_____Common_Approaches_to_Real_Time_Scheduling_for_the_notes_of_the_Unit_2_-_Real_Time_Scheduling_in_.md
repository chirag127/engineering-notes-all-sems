### Common Approaches to Real Time Scheduling

- Real time scheduling is the process of assigning tasks to processors or resources in a way that meets their timing constraints, such as deadlines, periods, or release times.
- Real time scheduling can be classified into two main categories: clock-driven and priority-driven.
- Clock-driven scheduling, also known as time-driven or cyclical scheduling, is a technique where scheduling decisions are made at specific time instants that are predetermined offline. A table or a program segment is used to specify which task should execute at each time instant. Clock-driven scheduling is suitable for hard real time systems where all the properties of the tasks are known in advance and do not change at runtime. Clock-driven scheduling can guarantee the schedulability of the tasks and avoid runtime overheads, but it is inflexible and cannot handle dynamic or unpredictable events.
- Priority-driven scheduling, also known as event-driven or preemptive scheduling, is a technique where scheduling decisions are made based on the priorities of the tasks. A task with a higher priority can preempt a task with a lower priority. Priority-driven scheduling is suitable for soft or firm real time systems where the properties of the tasks may vary or be unknown at design time. Priority-driven scheduling can handle dynamic or unpredictable events, but it may incur runtime overheads and may not guarantee the schedulability of the tasks.
- There are different ways to assign priorities to the tasks, such as static or dynamic, fixed or variable, or based on different criteria, such as deadlines, periods, or utilization. Some examples of priority-driven scheduling algorithms are:
  - Round-robin scheduling, where tasks are scheduled in a circular order based on a fixed time slice. This algorithm is simple and fair, but it does not consider the timing constraints of the tasks.
  - Weighted round-robin scheduling, where tasks are scheduled in a circular order based on a variable time slice that depends on their weights. This algorithm can differentiate the importance of the tasks, but it still does not consider the timing constraints of the tasks.
  - Rate-monotonic scheduling, where tasks are assigned static priorities based on their periods. The shorter the period, the higher the priority. This algorithm is optimal for periodic tasks with implicit deadlines, meaning that the deadline is equal to the period.
  - Deadline-monotonic scheduling, where tasks are assigned static priorities based on their deadlines. The shorter the deadline, the higher the priority. This algorithm is optimal for periodic or sporadic tasks with constrained deadlines, meaning that the deadline is less than or equal to the period.
  - Earliest-deadline-first scheduling, where tasks are assigned dynamic priorities based on their deadlines. The closer the deadline, the higher the priority. This algorithm is optimal for periodic, sporadic, or aperiodic tasks with arbitrary deadlines, meaning that the deadline can be any value.
  - Least-slack-time-first scheduling, where tasks are assigned dynamic priorities based on their slack time. The slack time is the difference between the deadline and the remaining execution time. The smaller the slack time, the higher the priority. This algorithm is optimal for periodic, sporadic, or aperiodic tasks with arbitrary deadlines, but it requires accurate estimation of the execution time of the tasks.
