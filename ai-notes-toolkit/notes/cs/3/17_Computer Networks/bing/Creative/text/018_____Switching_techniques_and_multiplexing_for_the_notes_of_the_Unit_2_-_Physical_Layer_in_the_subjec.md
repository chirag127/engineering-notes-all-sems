### Switching techniques and multiplexing

Switching techniques and multiplexing are two important concepts in computer networks that enable efficient and reliable data transmission over shared communication links.

- Switching techniques are methods of establishing and maintaining a connection between two or more nodes in a network. There are three main types of switching techniques: circuit switching, message switching, and packet switching .
  - Circuit switching is a switching technique where two nodes communicate with each other over a dedicated communication path. The path is established before the data transmission and remains active until the communication is terminated. Circuit switching is suitable for real-time and continuous data transmission, such as voice and video calls. However, it is inefficient in terms of bandwidth utilization, as the dedicated path cannot be used by other nodes even if there is no data to transmit.
  - Message switching is a switching technique where the whole message is treated as a data unit. The message is stored and forwarded by intermediate nodes until it reaches the destination node. Message switching does not require a dedicated path, but it introduces delays and overheads due to the storage and forwarding process. Message switching is suitable for non-real-time and intermittent data transmission, such as email and file transfer. However, it is not scalable for large messages or high traffic, as it requires a lot of buffer space and processing power at the intermediate nodes.
  - Packet switching is a switching technique where the message is broken down into smaller chunks called packets. The packets are routed independently by intermediate nodes based on the destination address and network conditions. Packet switching does not require a dedicated path, but it introduces fragmentation and reassembly issues due to the packetization process. Packet switching is suitable for both real-time and non-real-time data transmission, as it can adapt to the dynamic network conditions and optimize the bandwidth utilization. However, it is prone to packet loss, delay, and jitter, as the packets may take different paths and arrive out of order at the destination node.

- Multiplexing is a technique of combining multiple signals into one signal over a shared medium. Multiplexing allows the efficient and simultaneous transmission of multiple signals across a single data link. There are four main types of multiplexing techniques: frequency division multiplexing (FDM), time division multiplexing (TDM), wavelength division multiplexing (WDM), and statistical multiplexing .
  - Frequency division multiplexing (FDM) is a multiplexing technique where the frequency spectrum of the shared medium is divided into several non-overlapping frequency bands. Each signal is modulated by a different carrier frequency and transmitted over a different frequency band. FDM is suitable for analog signals, such as radio and television broadcasts. However, it is inefficient in terms of bandwidth utilization, as the frequency bands have to be separated by guard bands to prevent interference.
  - Time division multiplexing (TDM) is a multiplexing technique where the time domain of the shared medium is divided into several time slots. Each signal is assigned a different time slot and transmitted over the shared medium in a round-robin fashion. TDM is suitable for digital signals, such as telephone and computer networks. However, it is inefficient in terms of bandwidth utilization, as the time slots have to be synchronized and padded to prevent overlap and underflow.
  - Wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) is a multiplexing technique where the wavelength spectrum of the shared medium is divided into several non-overlapping wavelength bands. Each signal is modulated by a different light wavelength and transmitted over a different wavelength band. WDM is suitable for optical signals, such as fiber optic networks. WDM can increase the transmission capacity and distance of the optical medium by using multiple light sources and detectors. However, it is expensive and complex to implement, as it requires high-precision optical components and filters.
  - Statistical multiplexing is a multiplexing technique where the shared medium is dynamically allocated to the signals based on their demand and priority. Statistical multiplexing does not divide the medium into fixed frequency bands or time slots, but rather uses a buffer and a scheduler to multiplex the signals. Statistical multiplexing is suitable for packet switching networks, as it can adapt to the variable and bursty nature of the packet traffic. Statistical multiplexing can increase the efficiency and utilization of the network, as it can allocate more bandwidth to the signals that need it more. However, it is unpredictable and probabilistic, as it depends on the network conditions and traffic patterns.
