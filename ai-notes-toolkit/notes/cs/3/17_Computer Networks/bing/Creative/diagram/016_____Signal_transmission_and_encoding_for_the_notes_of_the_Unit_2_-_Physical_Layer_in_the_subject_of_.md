### Signal transmission and encoding

- Signal transmission is the process of sending and receiving data over a physical medium, such as a wire, a cable, or a wireless channel.
- Encoding is the process of converting data into a format that can be transmitted and recognized by the sender and the receiver.
- Encoding can be classified into two types: digital-to-digital encoding and analog-to-digital encoding.
- Digital-to-digital encoding is the representation of digital information (bits) by a digital signal, such as voltage levels or current pulses.
- Analog-to-digital encoding is the representation of analog information (sound, light, etc.) by a digital signal, such as binary codes or pulse code modulation (PCM).
- Encoding techniques can be categorized into four groups: unipolar, polar, bipolar, and Manchester.
- Unipolar encoding uses only one voltage level to represent bits. For example, 0 volts for 0 and 5 volts for 1. Unipolar encoding is simple but prone to errors and synchronization problems.
- Polar encoding uses two voltage levels to represent bits. For example, -5 volts for 0 and 5 volts for 1. Polar encoding is more reliable than unipolar encoding but requires more bandwidth and power.
- Bipolar encoding uses three voltage levels to represent bits. For example, -5 volts for 0, 0 volts for no change, and 5 volts for 1. Bipolar encoding is more efficient than polar encoding but requires more complex circuitry and synchronization.
- Manchester encoding uses a transition in the middle of each bit period to represent bits. For example, a low-to-high transition for 0 and a high-to-low transition for 1. Manchester encoding is self-synchronizing and immune to noise but requires twice the bandwidth of polar encoding.
