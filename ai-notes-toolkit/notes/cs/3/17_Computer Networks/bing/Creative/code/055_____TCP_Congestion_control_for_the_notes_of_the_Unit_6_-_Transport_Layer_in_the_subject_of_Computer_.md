### TCP Congestion Control

TCP congestion control is a mechanism that aims to regulate the amount of data that a sender can inject into the network, based on the network capacity and the feedback from the receiver. TCP congestion control prevents the network from being overloaded and reduces the packet loss and delay. TCP congestion control consists of three main phases: slow start, congestion avoidance, and congestion detection  .

- **Slow start**: In this phase, the sender starts with a small congestion window (cwnd) of one or two segments, and increases it by one segment for every acknowledgment (ACK) received. This results in an exponential growth of the cwnd until it reaches a threshold value (ssthresh), which is estimated based on the network conditions. The slow start phase allows the sender to probe the network for its available bandwidth and avoid sending too much data too fast   .

- **Congestion avoidance**: In this phase, the sender has reached the ssthresh and switches to a more conservative mode of increasing the cwnd. Instead of increasing it by one segment for every ACK, the sender increases it by one segment for every round-trip time (RTT), which is the time it takes for a segment to travel from the sender to the receiver and back. This results in a linear growth of the cwnd, which is more stable and less aggressive than the exponential growth in the slow start phase. The congestion avoidance phase aims to keep the network operating at its optimal point, where the throughput is high and the delay is low   .

- **Congestion detection**: In this phase, the sender detects that the network is congested, either by receiving duplicate ACKs or by experiencing a timeout. A duplicate ACK is an indication that a segment has been lost or reordered in the network, and a timeout is an indication that the sender has not received any ACK for a long time. Both events trigger the sender to reduce the cwnd and the ssthresh, and enter either the slow start phase or the congestion avoidance phase, depending on the severity of the congestion. The congestion detection phase allows the sender to react to the network congestion and avoid further packet loss and delay   .

There are different variants of TCP congestion control algorithms that implement different strategies for adjusting the cwnd and the ssthresh in response to the network feedback. Some of the most common TCP congestion control algorithms are:

- **TCP Reno**: This is the standard TCP congestion control algorithm that follows the three phases described above. It uses a fast retransmit mechanism to detect packet loss based on duplicate ACKs, and a fast recovery mechanism to reduce the cwnd by half and enter the congestion avoidance phase. However, TCP Reno cannot handle multiple packet losses within a single RTT, and suffers from low throughput and high delay in such scenarios .

- **TCP NewReno**: This is an improvement over TCP Reno that can handle multiple packet losses within a single RTT. It uses a partial ACK mechanism to detect how many segments have been successfully received by the receiver, and retransmits only the lost segments. It also maintains the cwnd at the reduced value until all the lost segments are recovered, and then enters the congestion avoidance phase. TCP NewReno can achieve higher throughput and lower delay than TCP Reno in networks with high packet loss rates .

- **TCP Vegas**: This is a TCP congestion control algorithm that uses a proactive approach to avoid congestion, rather than a reactive approach to detect congestion. It measures the actual RTT of the segments and compares it with the expected RTT, which is the RTT when the network is not congested. If the difference between the actual RTT and the expected RTT exceeds a certain threshold, it indicates that the network is approaching congestion, and the sender reduces the cwnd accordingly. TCP Vegas can achieve higher throughput and lower delay than TCP Reno and TCP NewReno in networks with low to moderate packet loss rates, but it may suffer from unfairness and underutilization in networks with high packet loss rates or with competing TCP Reno flows .
