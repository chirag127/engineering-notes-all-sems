# Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

- State formation is the process of emergence and development of a centralized political organization that exercises authority and control over a territory and its people.
- State formation in ancient India was influenced by various factors, such as geography, ecology, economy, society, culture, religion, and ideology.
- The stages of state formation in ancient India can be broadly divided into four phases:

## Phase 1: Pre-state Societies (Before 6th century BCE)

- In this phase, there were no states or kingdoms, but only tribal communities or clans (jana or vish) that lived in villages or pastoral settlements.
- These communities were governed by their elders, chiefs, or councils, who maintained law and order, settled disputes, and performed rituals.
- The main sources of livelihood were agriculture, animal husbandry, hunting, and gathering. Trade and commerce were limited and mainly barter-based.
- The main social divisions were based on kinship, occupation, and varna (the four-fold division of society into Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras).
- The main religious beliefs and practices were based on the worship of nature, ancestors, and various gods and goddesses. The Vedas, the oldest scriptures of Hinduism, were composed and transmitted orally in this phase.

## Phase 2: Early State Societies (6th century BCE to 4th century CE)

- In this phase, the first states or kingdoms (janapadas or mahajanapadas) emerged and expanded in the Indo-Gangetic plains, especially along the river valleys of the Ganges and the Yamuna.
- These states were ruled by kings (raja or maharaja) who claimed divine or heroic origin and exercised absolute power over their subjects. They were assisted by ministers, officials, and priests, who administered the state, collected taxes, and performed rituals.
- The main sources of livelihood were agriculture, animal husbandry, trade, and commerce. Urbanization, coinage, and writing developed in this phase. Trade and commerce flourished within and outside India, especially with the Persian, Greek, and Roman empires.
- The main social divisions were based on varna, jati (sub-castes), and ashrama (stages of life). The caste system became more rigid and hierarchical, with Brahmins and Kshatriyas dominating the social order. The status of women declined and they were subjected to various restrictions and inequalities.
- The main religious beliefs and practices were based on the Vedas, the Upanishads, and the Dharmashastras. The Vedic religion was challenged by new religious movements, such as Buddhism, Jainism, and Ajivikism, which preached non-violence, renunciation, and salvation. The Mauryan Empire (4th-2nd century BCE) was the first pan-Indian state that patronized Buddhism and promoted the ideals of dhamma (righteousness) and ahimsa (non-violence).

## Phase 3: Classical State Societies (4th century CE to 12th century CE)

- In this phase, the second wave of state formation and expansion occurred in different regions of India, such as the Deccan, the South, and the East.
- These states were ruled by kings (raja or maharaja) who claimed divine or heroic origin and exercised absolute or limited power over their subjects. They were assisted by ministers, officials, and priests, who administered the state, collected taxes, and performed rituals.
- The main sources of livelihood were agriculture, animal husbandry, trade, and commerce. Urbanization, coinage, and writing continued to develop in this phase. Trade and commerce flourished within and outside India, especially with the Southeast Asian, Arab, and Chinese empires.
- The main social divisions were based on varna, jati, and ashrama. The caste system became more complex and diverse, with the emergence of new castes and sub-castes. The status of women varied across regions and classes, but generally remained subordinate and dependent on men.
- The main religious beliefs and practices were based on the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Dharmashastras, the Puranas, and the Agamas. The Vedic religion was revived and reformed by various sects, such as the Vaishnavism, Shaivism, and Shaktism, which worshiped Vishnu, Shiva, and Shakti as the supreme deities.
