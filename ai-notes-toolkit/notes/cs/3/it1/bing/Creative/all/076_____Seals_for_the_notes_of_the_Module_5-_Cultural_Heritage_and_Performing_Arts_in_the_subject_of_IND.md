# Seals for the notes of the Module 5- Cultural Heritage and Performing Arts in the subject of INDIAN TRADITIONS, CULTURAL AND SOCIETY

- Seals are small objects that have an engraved design on one side and are used to make an impression on a soft material, such as clay or wax.
- Seals have been used in various cultures and civilizations for different purposes, such as trade, identification, authentication, communication, and ritual.
- In India, seals have a long history and reflect the diversity and richness of its cultural heritage and performing arts.
- Some of the types and examples of seals in Indian culture are:

  - **Indus Valley seals**: These are the oldest known seals in India, dating back to the third millennium BCE. They are mostly made of steatite, a soft stone that can be easily carved. They are rectangular, circular, or cylindrical in shape and have an animal motif and a script on one side. The animal motif may represent a deity, a clan symbol, or a trade emblem. The script is still undeciphered and may have been used for identification or communication. The seals were used to seal clay containers or documents and were carried by merchants and officials. Some of the animals depicted on the seals are buffaloes, rhinoceroses, elephants, unicorns, and composite creatures  .

  - **Mauryan seals**: These are the seals that were used during the Mauryan Empire (4th-2nd century BCE), one of the largest and most powerful empires in ancient India. They are made of terracotta, copper, silver, or ivory and have a circular or oval shape. They have a royal emblem, such as the lion capital or the bull capital, and a legend in Brahmi script on one side. The emblem may symbolize the authority, power, or faith of the emperor. The legend may mention the name, title, or rank of the seal owner or the place of issue. The seals were used to mark official documents, coins, or goods and were issued by the central or provincial administration.

  - **Gupta seals**: These are the seals that were used during the Gupta Empire (4th-6th century CE), considered the golden age of Indian culture and art. They are made of clay, copper, gold, or silver and have a square or rectangular shape. They have a religious image, such as a Buddha, a Bodhisattva, or a Hindu deity, and an inscription in Brahmi or Sanskrit script on one side. The image may reflect the personal devotion, patronage, or affiliation of the seal owner. The inscription may mention the name, title, or lineage of the seal owner or the purpose or occasion of the seal. The seals were used to seal clay tablets, manuscripts, or donations and were issued by the royal family, nobility, clergy, or merchants.

  - **Medieval seals**: These are the seals that were used during the medieval period (6th-16th century CE), characterized by the emergence and expansion of various regional kingdoms and dynasties in India. They are made of metal, stone, or wood and have a variety of shapes, such as circular, octagonal, hexagonal, or triangular. They have a floral, geometric, or calligraphic design and an inscription in various scripts, such as Arabic, Persian, Nagari, or Tamil, on one side. The design may represent the artistic style, cultural influence, or religious preference of the seal owner. The inscription may mention the name, title, or motto of the seal owner or the date or place of the seal. The seals were used to seal letters, documents, or edicts and were issued by the rulers, nobles, officials, or religious leaders.

  - **Modern seals**: These are the seals that are used in the modern period (17th century CE onwards), influenced by the colonial and post-colonial developments in India.
