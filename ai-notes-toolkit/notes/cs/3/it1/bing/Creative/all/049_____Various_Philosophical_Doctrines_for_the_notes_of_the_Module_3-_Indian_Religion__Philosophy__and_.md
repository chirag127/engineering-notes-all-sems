# Various Philosophical Doctrines

- Indian philosophy refers to the diverse systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. 
- Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought. 
- Indian philosophy is characterized by flexibility and the tendency to synthesize, as well as by the common themes of karma, rebirth, liberation, meditation, and renunciation .
- Indian philosophy can be broadly divided into two categories: orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika). 
- Orthodox schools accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, while unorthodox schools reject or question the Vedas. 
- The six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: 
  - Samkhya: Samkhya is the oldest of the orthodox philosophical systems, and it postulates that everything in reality stems from purusha (self or soul or mind) and prakriti (matter, creative agency, energy). Purusha is pure consciousness, while prakriti is the source of all material and mental phenomena. The goal of Samkhya is to attain the discrimination of purusha from prakriti, and thus achieve liberation from the cycle of rebirth .
  - Yoga: Yoga is closely related to Samkhya, and it adopts its metaphysical dualism of purusha and prakriti. However, Yoga emphasizes the practical aspects of achieving liberation, such as ethical discipline, physical exercises, breath control, and meditation. Yoga is also associated with the famous text, the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, which outlines the eight limbs of Yoga as a path to liberation .
  - Nyaya: Nyaya is the school of logic and epistemology, and it investigates the sources and validity of knowledge. Nyaya holds that there are four valid means of knowledge: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Nyaya also develops a sophisticated system of logic, based on the analysis of propositions, terms, and relations. Nyaya defends the existence of God, the self, and the external world, and argues for the liberation of the self from ignorance and bondage .
  - Vaisheshika: Vaisheshika is the school of atomism and naturalism, and it studies the nature and classification of reality. Vaisheshika holds that there are six categories of reality: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence. Substance is further divided into nine types: earth, water, fire, air, ether, time, space, self, and mind. Vaisheshika also proposes that all physical substances are composed of indivisible atoms, and that the self can attain liberation by acquiring true knowledge of reality .
  - Purva Mimamsa: Purva Mimamsa is the school of ritual exegesis and hermeneutics, and it focuses on the interpretation and application of the Vedas, especially the ritual and sacrificial portions. Purva Mimamsa holds that the Vedas are eternal, authorless, and infallible, and that they prescribe the means to achieve the highest good, which is the attainment of heaven. Purva Mimamsa also develops a theory of language, meaning, and inference, and defends the reality of the self, the gods, and the law of karma .
  - Vedanta: Vedanta is the school of metaphysics and spirituality, and it deals with the philosophical and theological aspects of the Vedas, especially the Upanishads, which are the mystical and philosophical portions. Vedanta is the most influential and diverse of the orthodox schools, and it has many sub-schools, such as Advaita (non-dualism), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism), and Dvaita (dualism). Vedanta explores the nature and relation of Brahman (the supreme reality), Atman (the individual self), and the world, and the means to attain the realization of Brahman as the ultimate goal of life .
