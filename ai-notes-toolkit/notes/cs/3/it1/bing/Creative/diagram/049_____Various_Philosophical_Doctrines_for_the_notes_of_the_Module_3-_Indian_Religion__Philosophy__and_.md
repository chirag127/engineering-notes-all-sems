### Various Philosophical Doctrines

- Indian philosophy refers to the diverse systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. 
- Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought. 
- Indian philosophy is characterized by flexibility and the tendency to synthesize different viewpoints and traditions. 
- Indian philosophy shares many concepts such as dharma (duty, law, morality), karma (action, cause and effect), samsara (cycle of rebirth), dukkha (suffering), renunciation, meditation, and moksha (liberation). 
- Indian philosophy can be broadly classified into two categories: orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika). 
- Orthodox schools accept the authority of the Vedas (ancient scriptures) as the source of knowledge and revelation. 
- Unorthodox schools reject the Vedas and rely on other sources of knowledge such as perception, inference, and experience. 
- The six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: 
  - Samkhya: Samkhya is the oldest of the orthodox philosophical systems, and it postulates that everything in reality stems from purusha (self or soul or mind) and prakriti (matter, creative agency, energy). Samkhya is a dualistic and atheistic system that explains the evolution of the universe and the diversity of life forms. Samkhya also provides a theory of knowledge and a path of liberation through discrimination and detachment. 
  - Yoga: Yoga is closely related to Samkhya, but it adds the concept of Ishvara (God) as the supreme purusha and the source of all knowledge. Yoga is a practical and ethical system that prescribes a discipline of physical, mental, and spiritual exercises to achieve self-realization and union with Ishvara. Yoga also develops a psychology of the mind and its functions, and a theory of perception and cognition. 
  - Nyaya: Nyaya is a system of logic and epistemology that analyzes the sources and methods of valid knowledge. Nyaya accepts four means of knowledge: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Nyaya also provides a realistic and pluralistic ontology that recognizes the existence of substances, qualities, actions, relations, universals, and God. Nyaya defends theism and the law of karma through logical arguments and debates. 
  - Vaisheshika: Vaisheshika is a system of atomism and naturalism that explains the nature and composition of physical reality. Vaisheshika accepts six categories of reality: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence. Vaisheshika postulates that all substances are composed of indivisible atoms that combine and recombine to form complex entities. Vaisheshika also accepts the existence of God as the efficient cause of the creation and dissolution of the universe. 
  - Purva Mimamsa: Purva Mimamsa is a system of exegesis and ritualism that interprets and defends the authority and validity of the Vedas. Purva Mimamsa focuses on the analysis of the language and meaning of the Vedic texts, and the performance of the Vedic sacrifices and ceremonies. Purva Mimamsa argues that the Vedas are eternal, self-evident, and infallible, and that the fulfillment of one's duties and obligations according to the Vedic injunctions leads to happiness and liberation. 
  - Vedanta: Vedanta is a system of metaphysics and spirituality that derives its teachings from the Upanishads (the philosophical portions of the Vedas). Vedanta affirms the identity of the individual self (atman) and the supreme self (brahman), and the illusory nature of the phenomenal world (maya). Vedanta also provides various methods of realizing the ultimate truth and attaining liberation, such as knowledge, devotion, service, and meditation. Vedanta has several sub-schools, such as Advaita (non-dualism), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism), and Dvaita (dualism).
