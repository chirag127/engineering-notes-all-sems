### Sculptures

Sculptures are three-dimensional art forms that are created by carving, modeling, casting, or assembling various materials. Sculptures can be realistic, abstract, or symbolic, and can convey different meanings and messages. Sculptures are an important part of the cultural heritage and performing arts of India, as they reflect the diverse religious, historical, and artistic influences that shaped the Indian subcontinent.

Some of the main points to note about sculptures in Indian culture are:

- The earliest known sculptures in India are from the Indus Valley civilization (3300–1700 BCE), which include the famous small bronze Dancing Girl, a symbol of grace and vitality. Other sculptures from this period include stone seals, pottery figurines, and terracotta animals and deities.
- The Mauryan period (322–185 BCE) saw the emergence of large-scale stone sculptures, such as the Ashoka Pillar at Sarnath, which has four lions on top and represents the Buddhist concept of dharma. The Mauryan sculptures also show the influence of Persian and Greek art, as seen in the polished surfaces and realistic details.
- The post-Mauryan period (185 BCE–320 CE) witnessed the development of various regional styles of sculpture, such as the Gandhara school, which blended Indian and Greco-Roman elements, and the Mathura school, which focused on the depiction of Buddha and other Hindu and Jain deities. The sculptures of this period are characterized by a sense of dynamism, expression, and emotion.
- The Gupta period (320–550 CE) is considered the golden age of Indian art, as the sculptures of this period achieved a high degree of refinement, elegance, and harmony. The Gupta sculptures are known for their idealized and graceful forms, serene faces, and smooth contours. The most famous example of Gupta sculpture is the Buddha statue at Sarnath, which shows the Buddha in a meditative pose and a gesture of teaching.
- The medieval period (550–1200 CE) saw the proliferation of temple architecture and sculpture in India, as different dynasties and regions developed their own distinctive styles and motifs. Some of the prominent examples of temple sculpture are the rock-cut caves of Ajanta and Ellora, which depict scenes from Buddhism, Hinduism, and Jainism; the Khajuraho temples, which are famous for their erotic sculptures; the Pallava and Chola temples of South India, which are adorned with elaborate carvings of gods and goddesses; and the Hoysala temples of Karnataka, which are known for their intricate and ornate designs.
- The modern period (1200–present) witnessed the influence of Islamic, European, and other foreign cultures on Indian sculpture, as well as the emergence of new forms and mediums of expression. Some of the notable modern Indian sculptors are Ramkinkar Baij, who experimented with folk and tribal art; Amrita Sher-Gil, who combined realism and symbolism; Nandalal Bose, who revived the classical and folk traditions; and M.F. Husain, who blended cubism and expressionism.
