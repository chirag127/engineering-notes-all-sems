### Drama

Drama is a form of performing arts, where stories are enacted by the utilisation of dialogue, music, signal and dance. Drama has a long and rich history in the Indian culture, dating back to the Vedic period. Drama is also closely linked to the religious and ritual aspects of the Indian society, as well as the various forms of folk theatre that exist in different regions.

Some of the main points to note about drama in the Indian culture are:

- The earliest evidence of drama in the Indian subcontinent can be traced back to the Rigveda, which contains hymns in the form of dialogues, scenes and fables.
- The classical stage of Indian drama begins with the composition of the Nātyaśāstra, a treatise on the theory and practice of drama, attributed to Bharata Muni in the 3rd-4th century BCE. The Nātyaśāstra lays down the principles of dramaturgy, aesthetics, stagecraft, genres, styles, emotions, gestures, music and dance.
- The Nātyaśāstra also defines the two main types of classical drama: nāṭaka and prakaraṇa. Nāṭaka is based on the epics and the purāṇas, and deals with the heroic deeds of gods, kings and sages. Prakaraṇa is based on fictional stories, and deals with the romantic and social aspects of human life.
- Some of the famous playwrights of the classical period are Kālidāsa, Bhāsa, Aśvaghoṣa, Śūdraka, Viśākhadatta and Harṣa. Their works cover a wide range of themes, such as love, war, politics, comedy, tragedy and satire.
- The classical drama was performed in specially designed theatres, called raṅgaśālās, which followed the specifications given in the Nātyaśāstra. The stage was divided into three parts: the aprākṛta (the backstage), the prākṛta (the main stage) and the nirvahaṇikā (the side wings). The audience was seated in a semi-circular arrangement, facing the stage.
- The classical drama was accompanied by music and dance, which were integral parts of the expression and communication. The music was based on the rāgas and the tālas, and the dance was based on the mudrās and the abhinayas, as prescribed in the Nātyaśāstra. The actors and the musicians were trained in the various aspects of the art, and were expected to follow the rules of the śāstra.
- The classical drama declined after the 10th century CE, due to the political and social upheavals in the Indian subcontinent. However, the tradition of drama continued in the form of various regional and folk theatres, which adapted and modified the classical elements to suit their local contexts and audiences.
- Some of the famous forms of traditional and folk theatres in India are: Yatra/Jatra (Bengal and Odisha), Ramleela and Raasleela (Uttar Pradesh), Swang and Nautanki (Haryana and Punjab), Dashavatar (Maharashtra and Goa), Kariyala and Bhagat (Himachal Pradesh), Khyal and Tamasha (Rajasthan and Maharashtra), Bhavai and Rasdhari (Gujarat), Yakshagana and Bayalata (Karnataka), Koodiyattam and Kathakali (Kerala), Therukoothu and Kattaikkuttu (Tamil Nadu), Bhaona and Ankiya Nat (Assam), Chhau and Jhumur (Jharkhand), and Bhand Pather and Ladishah (Jammu and Kashmir).
- The modern period of Indian drama began in the 19th century, with the influence of the Western theatre and the social reform movements. The Parsi theatre, which emerged in Bombay, was one of the first forms of modern theatre, which combined the elements of the classical, folk and Western theatre. The Parsi theatre was popular for its melodramatic and musical plays, which dealt with historical, mythological and contemporary themes.
