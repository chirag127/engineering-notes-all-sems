### Buddhist and Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Pali and Prakrit are two languages that were used to write the religious literature of Buddhism and Jainism, respectively. Pali is an old form of Prakrit that combines several dialects, while Prakrit is a term for any language other than Sanskrit.
- The earliest Buddhist works were written in Pali, which was spoken in Magadha and South Bihar, where the Buddha lived and taught. The Pali canon, also known as the Tipitaka or the Three Baskets, is the collection of the Buddha's teachings and the rules of the monastic order. It consists of three sections: the Vinaya Pitaka (the Basket of Discipline), the Sutta Pitaka (the Basket of Discourses), and the Abhidhamma Pitaka (the Basket of Higher Teachings).
- The Pali canon contains various genres of literature, such as dialogues, stories, poems, hymns, parables, similes, and ethical instructions. Some of the most famous works in the Pali canon are the Dhammapada (the Path of Truth), a collection of verses on the Buddha's teachings; the Jataka (the Birth Stories), a collection of stories about the Buddha's previous lives; and the Milindapanha (the Questions of King Milinda), a dialogue between a Greek king and a Buddhist monk.
- The Buddhist literature in Sanskrit and Prakrit developed later than the Pali literature, and reflects the diversity of the Buddhist schools and sects that emerged after the Buddha's death. The Sanskrit and Prakrit texts include the Mahayana sutras, which expound the doctrines of the Great Vehicle, such as the emptiness of all phenomena, the bodhisattva ideal, and the Buddha-nature; the Tantras, which contain esoteric teachings and practices of the Vajrayana or the Diamond Vehicle; and the commentaries, treatises, and biographies of various Buddhist masters and scholars.
- The Jain literature is written in various Prakrit languages, such as Ardhamagadhi, Sauraseni, and Maharashtri, as well as in Sanskrit. The Jain canon, also known as the Agama or the Siddhanta, is the collection of the teachings of the Tirthankaras or the Ford-makers, the enlightened beings who established the Jain path. The Jain canon consists of two main sections: the Anga (the Limbs) and the Angabahya (the Outside the Limbs). The Anga contains twelve texts that deal with the Jain doctrine, ethics, cosmology, and history, while the Angabahya contains thirty-four texts that deal with various topics, such as logic, grammar, mathematics, and art.
- The Jain literature also contains various genres of literature, such as stories, legends, fables, poems, hymns, and biographies. Some of the most famous works in the Jain literature are the Kalpasutra (the Book of Rituals), a text that describes the lives of the Tirthankaras and the rules of the monastic order; the Uttaradhyayana (the Final Teaching), a text that contains the sermons of Mahavira, the last Tirthankara; and the Trishashtishalakapurusha (the Sixty-three Illustrious Men), a text that narrates the stories of the exemplary figures in the Jain tradition.
