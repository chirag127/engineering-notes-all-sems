### Various Philosophical Doctrines

- Indian philosophy refers to the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. It encompasses both orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) schools of thought, as well as various traditions of spirituality and mysticism .
- Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought. Flexibility and the tendency to synthesize are also characteristic of Indian philosophy. Indian schools generally embody the doctrines of karma and rebirth; man must be perfected before he can achieve salvation.
- The main orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are:
  - Samkhya: Samkhya is the oldest of the orthodox philosophical systems, and it postulates that everything in reality stems from purusha (self or soul or mind) and prakriti (matter, creative agency, energy). Samkhya is a dualistic and atheistic system that aims at the liberation of the purusha from the bondage of prakriti through knowledge and discrimination .
  - Yoga: Yoga is closely related to Samkhya, but it adds the element of theism and the practice of various disciplines to achieve the union of the individual soul with the supreme soul or God. Yoga is also a practical system of ethics and meditation that aims at the control of the mind and the attainment of bliss .
  - Nyaya: Nyaya is the school of logic and epistemology, and it analyzes the sources and validity of knowledge. Nyaya is a realistic and pluralistic system that accepts four means of valid knowledge: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Nyaya also deals with the topics of metaphysics, ethics, and soteriology .
  - Vaisheshika: Vaisheshika is the school of atomism and naturalism, and it explains the nature and composition of reality. Vaisheshika is a realistic and pluralistic system that accepts six categories of being: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence. Vaisheshika also accepts the existence of God, soul, and karma, but they are not essential for its ontology .
  - Purva Mimamsa: Purva Mimamsa is the school of ritual exegesis and hermeneutics, and it focuses on the interpretation and application of the Vedic texts. Purva Mimamsa is a conservative and ritualistic system that upholds the authority and infallibility of the Vedas and the performance of sacrifices as the means of attaining heaven and liberation .
  - Vedanta: Vedanta is the school of metaphysics and theology, and it develops the philosophical implications of the Upanishads, the concluding part of the Vedas. Vedanta is a diverse and complex system that has many sub-schools and interpretations, but the common theme is the identity of the individual soul and the supreme soul or Brahman. Vedanta also explores the concepts of maya (illusion), avidya (ignorance), and moksha (liberation) .
- The main unorthodox schools of Indian philosophy are:
  - Buddhism: Buddhism is the system of thought and practice founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, in the sixth century BCE. Buddhism rejects the authority of the Vedas and the existence of a permanent soul or God. Buddhism teaches the four noble truths: the truth of suffering, the truth of the origin of suffering, the truth of the cessation of suffering, and the truth of the path to the cessation of suffering. Buddhism also advocates the eightfold path of right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration as the way to achieve nirvana, the state of freedom from suffering and rebirth .
  - Jainism: Jainism is the system of thought and practice founded by Mahavira, the Jina, in the sixth century BCE. Jainism rejects the authority of the Vedas and the existence of a creator God. Jainism teaches the doctrine of anekantavada (many-sidedness), which holds that reality is complex and multifaceted, and that different viewpoints can be valid.
