### Water Management in India

Water management is the planning, development, allocation, utilization, and conservation of water resources for various purposes such as drinking, irrigation, industry, energy, and ecology. Water management is essential for ensuring water security, health, and economic growth in India, which faces multiple challenges such as water scarcity, pollution, overexploitation, floods, and droughts. Some of the key aspects of water management in India are:

- Providing drinking water to rural areas: The government has been responsible for providing drinking water to 833 million rural people in some 640,000 villages across the country. However, many rural areas still lack access to safe and adequate water supply due to poor infrastructure, quality, and sustainability. The World Bank has supported several projects to improve rural water supply and sanitation, such as the National Rural Drinking Water Program, the Swachh Bharat Mission, and the Jal Jeevan Mission.
- Managing groundwater: Groundwater is the main source of water for domestic, agricultural, and industrial use in India. However, over the past few decades, groundwater extraction has risen exponentially, leading to depletion, salinization, and contamination of aquifers. India is now the world's largest user of groundwater, accounting for about 25% of the global total. The World Bank has supported initiatives to promote sustainable groundwater management, such as the Atal Bhujal Yojana, which is India's largest community-led groundwater management program, covering seven states with the highest rates of groundwater depletion.
- Improving access to water on dry rainfed lands: About 60% of India's net sown area is rainfed, which means it depends on rainfall for irrigation. Rainfed agriculture contributes to 44% of the country's food production and supports the livelihoods of millions of small and marginal farmers. However, rainfed areas face low and erratic rainfall, soil erosion, and low productivity. The World Bank has supported projects to enhance water availability and efficiency on rainfed lands, such as the Watershed Development Project, the National Hydrology Project, and the Climate Smart Agriculture Project.
- Cleaning the Ganga: The Ganga is India's most sacred and longest river, which supports the lives and livelihoods of about 500 million people. However, the river is severely polluted by domestic, industrial, and agricultural waste, affecting its water quality, biodiversity, and cultural significance. The World Bank has supported the National Ganga River Basin Project, which aims to improve the management and restoration of the Ganga basin through institutional strengthening, wastewater treatment, solid waste management, and riverfront development.
- Managing floods and droughts: India is prone to frequent and severe floods and droughts, which cause loss of lives, livelihoods, and assets. Climate change is expected to exacerbate the frequency and intensity of these extreme events. The World Bank has supported projects to improve the resilience and preparedness of communities and institutions to cope with floods and droughts, such as the National Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project, the Dam Rehabilitation and Improvement Project, and the National Disaster Risk Management Project.
- Enhancing dam safety in India: India has about 5,200 large dams, which provide irrigation, water supply, hydropower, and flood control benefits. However, many of these dams are aging and require urgent repair and maintenance to ensure their safety and functionality. The World Bank has supported the Dam Rehabilitation and Improvement Project, which aims to improve the safety and operational performance of selected dams across India.
