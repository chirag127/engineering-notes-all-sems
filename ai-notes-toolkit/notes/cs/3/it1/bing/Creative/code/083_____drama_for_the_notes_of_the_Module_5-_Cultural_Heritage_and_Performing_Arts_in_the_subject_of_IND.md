### Drama

Drama is a form of performing arts, where stories are enacted by the utilisation of dialogue, music, signal and dance. Drama has a long and rich history in the Indian culture, dating back to the Vedic period. Drama is also closely linked to the religious and ritual aspects of the Indian society, as well as the aesthetic and literary traditions.

Some of the main points to note about drama in the Indian culture are:

- The earliest evidence of drama in the Indian subcontinent can be traced back to the Rigveda (1200-1500 BCE), which contains a number of hymns in the form of dialogues, or even scenes, as well as hymns that make use of other literary forms such as animal fables.
- The classical stage of Indian drama begins with the composition of the Nātyaśāstra (lit. "Science of Drama") by Bharata Muni in the 3rd-4th century BCE. The Nātyaśāstra is a comprehensive treatise on the theory and practice of drama, covering aspects such as the origin and purpose of drama, the types and genres of drama, the structure and plot of drama, the characters and roles of drama, the language and style of drama, the music and dance of drama, the costumes and makeup of drama, the stage and props of drama, and the audience and appreciation of drama.
- The Nātyaśāstra also defines the two main types of classical Indian drama: nāṭaka and prakaraṇa. Nāṭaka is a heroic drama based on the epics and purāṇas, such as the Rāmāyaṇa and the Mahābhārata, or on the lives and deeds of kings and sages. Prakaraṇa is a realistic drama based on contemporary life and society, dealing with themes such as love, marriage, family, and politics.
- The Nātyaśāstra also classifies the classical Indian drama into ten subtypes, according to the dominant sentiment (rasa) expressed in the drama. The ten rasas are: śṛṅgāra (love), hāsya (humor), karuṇa (compassion), raudra (fury), vīra (heroism), bhayānaka (fear), bībhatsa (disgust), adbhuta (wonder), śānta (peace), and vātsalya (parental affection).
- Some of the most famous examples of classical Indian drama are: Mṛcchakaṭika (The Little Clay Cart) by Śūdraka, a prakaraṇa about the love story of a merchant and a courtesan; Abhijñānaśākuntala (The Recognition of Śakuntalā) by Kālidāsa, a nāṭaka about the romance and separation of King Duṣyanta and Śakuntalā, the daughter of a sage; Mālavikāgnimitra (Mālavikā and Agnimitra) by Kālidāsa, a prakaraṇa about the love affair of King Agnimitra and Mālavikā, a maid in his palace; and Uttararāmacarita (The Later Deeds of Rāma) by Bhavabhūti, a nāṭaka about the exile and reunion of Rāma and Sītā, the protagonists of the Rāmāyaṇa.
- The classical Indian drama also influenced the development of drama in other Asian cultures, such as China, Japan, Tibet, Thailand, Cambodia, Indonesia, and Sri Lanka. The Indian drama was transmitted to these regions through trade, pilgrimage, and cultural exchange, and was adapted and modified according to the local languages, religions, and customs.
- The medieval period of Indian drama saw the emergence of various regional and vernacular forms of drama, such as the Sanskrit drama, the Tamil drama, the Kannada drama, the Telugu drama, the Malayalam drama, the Bengali drama, the Marathi drama, the Gujarati drama, the Hindi drama, and the Urdu drama.
