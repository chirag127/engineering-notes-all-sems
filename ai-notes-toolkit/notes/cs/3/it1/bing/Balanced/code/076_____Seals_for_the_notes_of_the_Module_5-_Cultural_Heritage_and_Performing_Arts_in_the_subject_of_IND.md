# Seals for the notes of the Module 5- Cultural Heritage and Performing Arts in the subject of INDIAN TRADITIONS, CULTURAL AND SOCIETY

- Seals are engraved objects that are used to imprint a mark or a symbol on another surface, such as clay, wax, or paper.
- Seals have been used in various cultures and civilizations for different purposes, such as trade, identification, authentication, communication, and ritual.
- In India, seals have a long history and a rich cultural significance, dating back to the Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1900 BCE).
- Some of the main features and functions of seals in Indian culture are:

  - Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization: These are the earliest known seals in India, and they are mostly made of steatite, a soft stone that can be easily carved. They are rectangular, circular, or cylindrical in shape, and they have a hole on one end for attaching a string or a stick. They depict various animals, such as buffaloes, rhinoceroses, elephants, and unicorns, as well as trees and composite creatures. They also have a script that has not been deciphered yet. These seals were used for trade and commerce, as well as for religious and symbolic purposes. They reflect the sophisticated urban culture and the cosmology of the Indus people  .
  - Seals of the Mauryan Empire (322-185 BCE): These are the first seals that have inscriptions in Brahmi, the oldest Indian script. They are made of terracotta, copper, or ivory, and they have a circular or oval shape. They depict various symbols, such as the lion capital, the bull, the elephant, the lotus, and the sun. They also have the names and titles of the rulers, such as Ashoka, Bindusara, and Chandragupta. These seals were used for administrative and political purposes, as well as for propagating the Buddhist teachings of Ashoka .
  - Seals of the Gupta Empire (320-550 CE): These are the seals that show the artistic and cultural achievements of the Gupta period, which is considered the golden age of Indian civilization. They are made of gold, silver, copper, or clay, and they have a square or rectangular shape. They depict various deities, such as Vishnu, Shiva, Ganesha, and Lakshmi, as well as scenes from Hindu mythology and literature. They also have inscriptions in Sanskrit, the classical language of India. These seals were used for religious and literary purposes, as well as for expressing the royal authority and patronage of the Gupta kings .
  - Seals of the Mughal Empire (1526-1857): These are the seals that reflect the Islamic influence and the cultural synthesis of the Mughal period, which is known for its architectural and artistic achievements. They are made of metal, stone, or wood, and they have a circular or oval shape. They depict various calligraphic styles, such as Naskh, Nastaliq, and Thuluth, as well as floral and geometric motifs. They also have the names and titles of the emperors, such as Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan, and Aurangzeb. These seals were used for official and personal purposes, as well as for expressing the artistic and aesthetic tastes of the Mughal rulers .
  - Seals of the British Raj (1858-1947): These are the seals that represent the colonial and imperial domination of the British in India, as well as the resistance and nationalism of the Indian people. They are made of metal, wax, or paper, and they have a circular or rectangular shape. They depict various symbols, such as the crown, the lion, the star, and the lotus, as well as the coat of arms and the motto of the British monarchy. They also have the names and titles of the governors, viceroys, and kings, such as Lord Curzon, Lord Mountbatten, and George V. These seals were used for administrative and legal purposes, as well as for asserting the sovereignty and legitimacy of the British rule .
