### Various Philosophical Doctrines for the notes of the Module 3- Indian Religion, Philosophy, and Practices in the subject of INDIAN TRADITIONS, CULTURAL AND SOCIETY

- Indian philosophy is the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent.
- Indian philosophy can be divided into two broad categories: orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) systems.
- Orthodox systems are those that accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, as the source of knowledge and truth.
- Unorthodox systems are those that reject the Vedas and rely on other sources of knowledge, such as reason, experience, or intuition.
- The six orthodox systems of Indian philosophy are:
  - Nyaya: a school of logic and epistemology that analyzes the sources and validity of knowledge.
  - Vaisheshika: a school of metaphysics and natural philosophy that classifies reality into six categories: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence.
  - Samkhya: a school of dualism and cosmology that posits two fundamental principles: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter).
  - Yoga: a school of psychology and ethics that prescribes a discipline of physical and mental practices to attain liberation from the bondage of prakriti.
  - Purva-Mimamsa (or Mimamsa): a school of hermeneutics and ritual that interprets the Vedic texts and prescribes the performance of sacrifices and duties to attain the highest good.
  - Vedanta: a school of metaphysics and theology that expounds the doctrine of Brahman (the absolute reality) and Atman (the individual soul) and their identity or difference.
- The three unorthodox systems of Indian philosophy are:
  - Buddhism: a religion and philosophy founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha) that teaches the four noble truths, the eightfold path, and the doctrine of no-self (anatman) and dependent origination (pratityasamutpada).
  - Jainism: a religion and philosophy founded by Mahavira (the Jina) that teaches the principles of non-violence (ahimsa), non-absolutism (anekantavada), and non-possessiveness (aparigraha) and the path of renunciation and asceticism to attain liberation from the cycle of rebirth (samsara).
  - Lokayata (or Charvaka): a school of materialism and skepticism that denies the existence of anything beyond the four elements (earth, water, fire, and air) and the senses and rejects the notions of soul, karma, and rebirth.
- Indian philosophy has been concerned with various philosophical problems, such as the nature of the world (cosmology), the nature of reality (metaphysics), logic, the nature of knowledge (epistemology), ethics, and the philosophy of religion.
- Indian philosophy has also influenced and been influenced by other traditions, such as Greek, Persian, Islamic, Chinese, and Western philosophy.
