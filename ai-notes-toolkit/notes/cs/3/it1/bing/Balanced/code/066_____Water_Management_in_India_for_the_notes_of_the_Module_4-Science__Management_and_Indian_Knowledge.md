### Water Management in India

Water management is the planning, development, allocation, utilization, and conservation of water resources for various purposes such as drinking, irrigation, industry, energy, and ecology. Water management is essential for ensuring water security, health, and economic growth in India, which faces multiple challenges such as water scarcity, pollution, overexploitation, floods, and droughts. Some of the key aspects of water management in India are:

- Providing drinking water to rural areas: The government has been responsible for providing drinking water to 833 million rural people in some 640,000 villages across the country. However, many rural areas still lack access to safe and adequate water supply due to poor infrastructure, quality, and sustainability. The World Bank has supported several projects to improve rural water supply and sanitation, such as the Swajal Project, the National Rural Drinking Water Program, and the Jal Jeevan Mission.

- Groundwater management: Over the past few decades, groundwater extraction has risen exponentially across India. India is now the world's largest user of groundwater, accounting for about 25% of the global total. Groundwater is the main source of irrigation and drinking water for most of the population, but it is also depleting rapidly due to overuse, lack of regulation, and climate change. The World Bank has supported the Atal Bhujal Yojana, India's largest community-led groundwater management program, which aims to improve rural livelihoods and build resilience in seven Indian states that have the highest rates of groundwater depletion .

- Improving access to water on dry rainfed lands: About 60% of India's net sown area is rainfed, which means it depends on rainfall for irrigation. Rainfed agriculture is vulnerable to climate variability and droughts, and often suffers from low productivity and income. The World Bank has supported the National Watershed Development Project for Rainfed Areas, which aims to enhance the productive potential of rainfed lands through watershed management, soil and water conservation, crop diversification, and livelihood enhancement.

- Cleaning the Ganga: The Ganga is India's most sacred and longest river, which supports the livelihoods and well-being of more than 500 million people. However, the river is also severely polluted by domestic, industrial, and agricultural waste, which poses a threat to public health, biodiversity, and cultural heritage. The World Bank has supported the National Ganga River Basin Project, which aims to improve the water quality and ecological health of the Ganga and its tributaries through institutional strengthening, wastewater management, and riverfront development.

- Managing floods and droughts: India is prone to frequent and severe floods and droughts, which cause loss of lives, livelihoods, and assets. Floods affect about 30 million people and 8 million hectares of land every year, while droughts affect about 50 million people and 40 million hectares of land every year. The World Bank has supported several projects to enhance the resilience and preparedness of the communities and institutions to cope with floods and droughts, such as the National Hydrology Project, the Dam Rehabilitation and Improvement Project, and the Climate Change Adaptation Project.

- Enhancing dam safety in India: India has about 5,200 large dams, which provide irrigation, water supply, hydropower, and flood control benefits. However, many of these dams are aging and require urgent repairs and maintenance to ensure their safety and functionality. The World Bank has supported the Dam Safety Project, which aims to improve the safety and performance of selected existing dams and associated appurtenances in India through the implementation of dam safety guidelines, standards, and procedures.
