### Agriculture in India

- Agriculture is the primary source of livelihood for about 58% of India's population.
- India is the world's largest producer of spices, pulses, milk, tea, cashew and jute; and the second largest producer of wheat, rice, fruits and vegetables, sugarcane, cotton and oilseeds.
- India has a diverse agro-climatic zone, ranging from tropical to temperate, arid to humid, and high rainfall to drought-prone areas.
- India's agriculture is largely dependent on the monsoon, which accounts for about 75% of the annual rainfall. The variability and unpredictability of the monsoon poses challenges for crop production and water management.
- India's agriculture faces several issues such as low productivity, soil degradation, water scarcity, climate change, pests and diseases, market fluctuations, and socio-economic factors.
- India's agriculture also has several strengths such as a large and diverse genetic resource base, a rich tradition of indigenous knowledge and practices, a vibrant agricultural research and extension system, and a huge domestic and export market potential.
- India's agriculture has undergone several changes over the years, such as the Green Revolution, the White Revolution, the Blue Revolution, and the Evergreen Revolution. These have contributed to increasing the production and productivity of various crops and commodities, enhancing food security, and improving the livelihoods of farmers.
- India's agriculture also faces new opportunities and challenges in the 21st century, such as the adoption of new technologies, the integration of global markets, the promotion of organic and sustainable farming, the diversification of crops and value addition, the empowerment of women and youth, and the conservation of natural resources and biodiversity.
- India's agriculture is influenced by and influences the science, management and Indian knowledge system. Science provides the scientific basis and tools for improving agricultural practices and innovations. Management involves the planning, organization, coordination, and control of agricultural resources and activities. Indian knowledge system encompasses the indigenous, traditional, and local knowledge and wisdom of the farmers and communities, which have been developed and transmitted over generations. These three aspects are interrelated and interdependent, and together they shape the present and future of Indian agriculture.
