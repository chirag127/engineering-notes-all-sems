### Society in Ancient India

- The society in ancient India was based on the concept of **varna**, which was a four-fold division of people into four classes: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers). 
- The society was also organized into **ashramas**, which were four stages of life that each individual was expected to follow: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (retired), and Sannyasa (renunciant).
- The society was governed by the rules of **dharma**, which was the moral and religious duty of each person according to their varna and ashrama. Dharma also included the performance of **samskaras**, which were rites of passage that marked the important events in one's life, such as birth, initiation, marriage, and death.
- The society was structured around the institution of **family**, which was the basic unit of social life. The family was usually patriarchal, with the father or the eldest male as the head. The family was also patrilineal, with the inheritance and succession passing through the male line. The family was also extended, with several generations living together under one roof.
- The society had different norms and practices regarding **marriage**, which was considered a sacred and lifelong bond between a man and a woman. The marriage was usually arranged by the parents or elders, based on the compatibility of the horoscopes, caste, and social status of the bride and groom. The marriage was also monogamous, with polygamy being allowed only for the upper classes and under certain circumstances. The marriage was also endogamous, with inter-caste and inter-religious marriages being prohibited or discouraged.
- The society had a varied and changing position of **women**, who enjoyed different degrees of freedom and rights depending on the period, region, and class. In general, women were subordinate to men in the patriarchal and patrilineal society, and had to follow the norms of **pativrata**, which was the ideal of a faithful and devoted wife. Women were also expected to observe the customs of **sati**, which was the self-immolation of a widow on her husband's funeral pyre, and **purdah**, which was the seclusion and veiling of women from public view. However, women also had some opportunities for education, religious participation, artistic expression, and social service, especially in the early and medieval periods.
