### Drama

Drama is a form of performing arts, where stories are enacted by the utilisation of dialogue, music, signal and dance. Drama has a long and rich history in the Indian culture, dating back to the Vedic period. Drama is also closely linked to the religious and ritual aspects of the Indian society, as well as the various regional and linguistic traditions.

Some of the main points to note about drama in the Indian culture are:

- The earliest evidence of drama in the Indian subcontinent can be traced back to the Rigveda (1200-1500 BCE), which contains a number of hymns in the form of dialogues, or even scenes, as well as hymns that make use of other literary forms such as animal fables.
- The classical stage of Indian drama begins with the composition of the Nātyaśāstra (lit. "Science of Drama") by Bharata Muni in the 3rd-4th century BCE. The Nātyaśāstra is a comprehensive treatise on the theory and practice of drama, covering aspects such as the origin and purpose of drama, the types and genres of drama, the structure and plot of drama, the elements and techniques of drama, the rules and conventions of drama, the aesthetics and emotions of drama, and the performance and audience of drama.
- The Nātyaśāstra also defines the two main types of classical Indian drama: nāṭaka and prakaraṇa. Nāṭaka is a heroic drama based on the epics and purāṇas, such as the Rāmāyaṇa and the Mahābhārata, or on the lives and deeds of kings and sages. Prakaraṇa is a realistic drama based on the contemporary society and its problems, such as love, marriage, and politics.
- The Nātyaśāstra also classifies the classical Indian drama into ten subtypes, according to the number of acts, the nature of the hero, the plot, and the sentiment. These are: nāṭikā, bhāṇa, prahasana, ḍima, vyāyoga, samavakāra, īhāmṛga, anka, vīthi, and utsṛṣṭikāṅka.
- The classical Indian drama reached its peak during the Gupta period (4th-6th century CE), when some of the most renowned playwrights such as Kālidāsa, Bhāsa, Śūdraka, Viśākhadatta, and Harṣa composed their masterpieces. Some of the famous examples of classical Indian drama are: Abhijñānaśākuntalam (The Recognition of Śakuntalā), Mālavikāgnimitram (Mālavikā and Agnimitra), Mṛcchakaṭikā (The Little Clay Cart), Mudrārākṣasa (The Signet of the Minister), and Nāgānanda (The Joy of the Serpents).
- The classical Indian drama also influenced the development of drama in other Asian countries, such as China, Japan, Tibet, Thailand, Cambodia, Indonesia, and Sri Lanka, through the spread of Buddhism and Hinduism.
- The medieval period (7th-16th century CE) saw the decline of the classical Indian drama, due to the political and social upheavals, the Islamic invasions, and the loss of patronage. However, some regional and folk forms of drama continued to flourish, such as the Yātra or Jātra in eastern India, the Rāmleela and Rāsleela in northern India, the Swang and Nautanki in western India, the Daśāvatāra and Khyāl in central India, and the Kariyala and Bhānd in Kashmir.
- The modern period (17th-20th century CE) witnessed the revival and adaptation of the classical Indian drama, as well as the emergence of new forms of drama, influenced by the Western and Persian cultures.
