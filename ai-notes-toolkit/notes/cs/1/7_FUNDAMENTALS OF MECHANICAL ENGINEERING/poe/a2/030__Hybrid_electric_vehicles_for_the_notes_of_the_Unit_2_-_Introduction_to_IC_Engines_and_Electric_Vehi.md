### Hybrid electric vehicles

Hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs) have both an internal combustion engine and an electric motor. They do not need to be plugged in to charge the battery. The key advantages of HEVs are:

- Improved fuel economy. The electric motor can provide extra power assist during acceleration, reducing the load on the engine and improving fuel efficiency.
- Reduced emissions. The engine can be turned off at idle, during stops, and at low speeds, reducing emissions.
- Extended range. The presence of both an engine and electric motor provides an extended range over battery electric vehicles.

The main disadvantages are the added cost and complexity of the powertrain. There are several types of HEVs:

- Series hybrids have only an electric motor that is powered by a battery pack and/or fuel cell. The engine drives a generator to charge the battery pack.
- Parallel hybrids have both an engine and electric motor that can separately or together power the wheels. The Toyota Prius is a popular example of a parallel HEV.
- Power-split (or series-parallel) hybrids have a planetary gear set that allows for power splitting between the engine and motor.

The choice of HEV configuration depends on the requirements for power, efficiency, and cost. HEVs show significant promise to reduce emissions and oil usage from the transportation sector. However, challenges around cost reduction, battery life, and consumer adoption still remain for widespread adoption.
