### Newtonian and Non-Newtonian fluid

- A fluid is a substance that can flow and take the shape of the container that holds it.
- A fluid can be classified as either Newtonian or non-Newtonian, depending on how its viscosity (resistance to flow) changes with the applied shear stress (force per unit area).
- A Newtonian fluid is a fluid whose viscosity is constant and does not depend on the shear stress. Examples of Newtonian fluids are water, air, oil, and honey (at low temperatures).
- A non-Newtonian fluid is a fluid whose viscosity changes with the shear stress. Examples of non-Newtonian fluids are blood, ketchup, toothpaste, and slime.
- Non-Newtonian fluids can be further classified into different types, such as:
  - Shear-thinning fluids: These are fluids whose viscosity decreases with increasing shear stress. They are also called pseudoplastic fluids. Examples are ketchup, paint, and shampoo.
  - Shear-thickening fluids: These are fluids whose viscosity increases with increasing shear stress. They are also called dilatant fluids. Examples are cornstarch and water mixture, quicksand, and silly putty.
  - Bingham fluids: These are fluids that behave like solids until a certain shear stress is applied, and then flow like liquids. They are also called yield stress fluids. Examples are toothpaste, mayonnaise, and mud.
  - Thixotropic fluids: These are fluids that become less viscous over time when subjected to a constant shear stress. They are also called time-dependent fluids. Examples are yogurt, jelly, and blood.
  - Rheopectic fluids: These are fluids that become more viscous over time when subjected to a constant shear stress. They are also called anti-thixotropic fluids. Examples are cream, whipped cream, and some types of clay.
- The behavior of non-Newtonian fluids can be explained by the molecular structure and interactions of the fluid. For example, shear-thinning fluids have long and flexible molecules that tend to align in the direction of the flow, reducing the viscosity. Shear-thickening fluids have solid particles suspended in a liquid that tend to jam and form clusters under high shear stress, increasing the viscosity. Bingham fluids have a network of bonds between the molecules that need to be broken before the fluid can flow. Thixotropic fluids have a reversible structure that breaks down under shear stress and reforms when the stress is removed. Rheopectic fluids have a structure that builds up under shear stress and persists when the stress is removed.
- The study of non-Newtonian fluids is important for many applications in engineering, medicine, biology, and everyday life. For example, non-Newtonian fluids can be used to design protective materials, such as bulletproof vests and helmets, that can absorb impact and resist penetration. Non-Newtonian fluids can also be used to model the behavior of biological fluids, such as blood, saliva, and mucus, that have complex and dynamic properties. Non-Newtonian fluids can also be found in many household products, such as cosmetics, food, and cleaning agents, that have desirable characteristics, such as ease of spreading, stability, and texture.
