### Fluids properties

- A fluid is a substance that can flow and conform to the shape of its container. Fluids include liquids and gases, as well as some solids that can deform under stress, such as sand or clay.
- Fluids have some common properties that are important for understanding their behavior and applications in engineering. These properties include density, pressure, temperature, viscosity, surface tension, and compressibility.
- Density is the mass per unit volume of a fluid. It is usually denoted by the Greek letter rho (ρ) and measured in kilograms per cubic meter (kg/m3) or grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm3). Density can vary with temperature, pressure, and composition of the fluid. For example, water has a density of about 1000 kg/m3 at 4°C and atmospheric pressure, but it decreases slightly as the temperature increases or the pressure decreases.
- Pressure is the force per unit area exerted by a fluid on a surface. It is usually denoted by the letter p and measured in pascals (Pa) or newtons per square meter (N/m2). Pressure can vary with depth, elevation, and motion of the fluid. For example, the atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 101.3 kPa, but it decreases as the altitude increases. The pressure in a fluid also increases as the depth increases, according to the hydrostatic equation: p = p0 + ρgh, where p0 is the pressure at the surface, ρ is the density of the fluid, g is the gravitational acceleration, and h is the depth below the surface.
- Temperature is the measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules in a fluid. It is usually denoted by the letter T and measured in degrees Celsius (°C) or kelvins (K). Temperature can affect the density, pressure, viscosity, and compressibility of the fluid. For example, the viscosity of water decreases as the temperature increases, making it easier to flow.
- Viscosity is the measure of the resistance of a fluid to flow or deformation. It is usually denoted by the Greek letter mu (μ) and measured in pascal-seconds (Pa·s) or newton-seconds per square meter (N·s/m2). Viscosity can vary with temperature, pressure, and composition of the fluid. For example, honey has a higher viscosity than water, making it harder to flow. Viscosity can be classified into two types: dynamic viscosity and kinematic viscosity. Dynamic viscosity is the ratio of the shear stress to the shear rate in a fluid, while kinematic viscosity is the ratio of the dynamic viscosity to the density of the fluid.
- Surface tension is the measure of the tendency of a fluid to minimize its surface area. It is usually denoted by the Greek letter sigma (σ) and measured in newtons per meter (N/m) or joules per square meter (J/m2). Surface tension can vary with temperature, pressure, and composition of the fluid. For example, water has a higher surface tension than alcohol, making it form droplets or bubbles more easily. Surface tension can be explained by the cohesive forces between the molecules in the fluid, which are stronger at the surface than in the bulk.
- Compressibility is the measure of the change in volume of a fluid due to a change in pressure. It is usually denoted by the Greek letter beta (β) and measured in inverse pascals (Pa-1) or inverse newtons per square meter (N-1/m2). Compressibility can vary with temperature, pressure, and composition of the fluid. For example, gases are more compressible than liquids, meaning they can be squeezed into a smaller volume by applying pressure. Compressibility can be related to the bulk modulus of elasticity of the fluid, which is the ratio of the change in pressure to the fractional change in volume.
