### Free Body Diagram

A free body diagram is a graphical tool that helps us analyze the forces acting on an object in a given situation. It is a simplified representation of the object and the forces, without showing the details of the surrounding environment or the internal structure of the object. A free body diagram can help us solve problems involving Newton's laws of motion, equilibrium, friction, and other topics in mechanics.

To draw a free body diagram, we follow these steps:

- Identify the object of interest and isolate it from the rest of the system. Draw a simple shape (such as a dot, a circle, or a box) to represent the object.
- Choose a coordinate system and label the axes. Usually, we use a Cartesian coordinate system with the x-axis horizontal and the y-axis vertical, but sometimes we may need to use a different orientation or a polar coordinate system.
- Draw and label all the forces acting on the object as vectors. The vectors should start from the center of mass of the object and point in the direction of the force. The length of the vector should indicate the magnitude of the force. The label should indicate the type and source of the force. For example, F_g for the gravitational force, F_N for the normal force, F_f for the frictional force, etc.
- If the object is in equilibrium, the net force acting on it is zero. This means that the vector sum of all the forces is zero. We can use this condition to find unknown forces or to check the consistency of our diagram.
- If the object is not in equilibrium, the net force acting on it is equal to its mass times its acceleration. This means that the vector sum of all the forces is equal to m*a. We can use this condition to find unknown forces, accelerations, or masses.

Here are some examples of free body diagrams for different situations:

- A book resting on a table. The book is in equilibrium, so the net force is zero. There are two forces acting on the book: the gravitational force F_g downward and the normal force F_N upward. The normal force is equal and opposite to the gravitational force, so F_N = -F_g.

- A car moving at a constant speed on a flat road. The car is not in equilibrium, but it has a constant velocity, so the net force is zero. There are four forces acting on the car: the gravitational force F_g downward, the normal force F_N upward, the frictional force F_f backward, and the driving force F_D forward. The driving force is equal and opposite to the frictional force, so F_D = -F_f.

![car on road](https://www.physicsclassroom.com/Class/newtlaws/u2l2a2.gif)

- A block sliding down an inclined plane. The block is not in equilibrium, and it has a non-zero acceleration, so the net force is not zero. There are three forces acting on the block: the gravitational force F_g downward, the normal force F_N perpendicular to the plane, and the frictional force F_f parallel to the plane and opposite to the motion. The net force is the vector sum of these forces, and it points along the plane and downward. We can use trigonometry to find the components of the gravitational force along and perpendicular to the plane, and use Newton's second law to find the acceleration of the block.
