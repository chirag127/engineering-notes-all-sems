### Skew-Hermitian Matrix

- A square matrix of complex numbers is said to be a skew-Hermitian matrix if that matrix is equal to the negative of its conjugate transpose, also called tranjugate    .
- Mathematically, the definition of the skew-Hermitian matrix is as follows: A square matrix A = [a ij] n × n is such that A* = −A, that is, for every a ij ∈ A, a ij = −a ji*.
- The conjugate transpose of a matrix A, denoted by A*, is obtained by taking the transpose of A and then replacing each element by its complex conjugate    .
- For example, if A = [ i 1 + i 2 − 3 i − 1 + i 2 i 1 − 2 − 3 i − 1 0], then A* = [ −i −1 − i −2 + 3i 1 − i −2 − i −1 + 2 + 3i 1 0] and −A = [ −i −1 − i −2 + 3i 1 − i −2 − i −1 + 2 + 3i 1 0], so A is a skew-Hermitian matrix.

- Properties of the skew-Hermitian matrix    :
  - The diagonal elements of a skew-Hermitian matrix are always equal to zero or purely imaginary    .
  - The sum and difference of two skew-Hermitian matrices are also skew-Hermitian    .
  - The product of two skew-Hermitian matrices is Hermitian if they commute, and skew-Hermitian if they anti-commute .
  - The scalar multiple of a skew-Hermitian matrix is skew-Hermitian if the scalar is real, and Hermitian if the scalar is purely imaginary .
  - The inverse of a nonsingular skew-Hermitian matrix is also skew-Hermitian .
  - The eigenvalues of a skew-Hermitian matrix are all purely imaginary or zero  .
  - The eigenvectors of a skew-Hermitian matrix for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal  .
  - Every skew-Hermitian matrix is an example of a normal matrix, and hence it is diagonalizable  .
  - The determinant of a skew-Hermitian matrix is a non-negative real number.
  - The trace of a skew-Hermitian matrix is always zero or purely imaginary.
