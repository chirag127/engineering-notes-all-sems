### Energy production mechanism

- Energy is the capacity to do work or cause change in a system.
- Biological organisms are open systems that exchange energy and matter with their surroundings.
- Energy comes to living systems through electrons occupying high energy states, either from food (respiration) or from light (photosynthesis).
- Energy is stored in the chemical bonds of organic molecules, such as carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids, which are used as fuel and building material by the cells.
- Energy is released from organic molecules by breaking down their bonds through a series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions, called metabolic pathways.
- Metabolic pathways can be classified into two types: catabolic and anabolic.
- Catabolic pathways break down complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy and reducing power (electrons) in the process. Examples of catabolic pathways are glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.
- Anabolic pathways synthesize complex molecules from simpler ones, consuming energy and reducing power in the process. Examples of anabolic pathways are gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and fatty acid synthesis.
- The energy released from catabolic pathways is used to drive anabolic pathways, as well as other cellular processes, such as transport, movement, and signaling.
- The energy currency of the cell is adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which is composed of a nitrogenous base (adenine), a sugar (ribose), and three phosphate groups.
- ATP is synthesized from adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate (Pi) by adding a phosphate group, a process that requires energy input.
- ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP and Pi by removing a phosphate group, a process that releases energy output.
- The energy released from ATP hydrolysis is used to power various cellular activities, such as muscle contraction, protein synthesis, and membrane transport.
- The energy balance of the cell is maintained by coupling endergonic reactions (those that require energy) with exergonic reactions (those that release energy), often through the intermediacy of ATP.
- The efficiency of energy conversion in biological systems is limited by the second law of thermodynamics, which states that entropy (disorder) of the universe always increases.
- The energy flow in biological systems is unidirectional, from high to low energy states, and ultimately dissipated as heat to the environment.
