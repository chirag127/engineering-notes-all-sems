# Relationship of carbohydrate metabolism to other compounds

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the process of breaking down and synthesizing carbohydrates, which are organic molecules composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms.
- Carbohydrates can be classified into simple sugars (monosaccharides and disaccharides) and complex sugars (polysaccharides), such as starch, glycogen, and cellulose.
- Carbohydrate metabolism is connected to the metabolism of other compounds, such as proteins and lipids, through common intermediates and pathways  .
- The main pathways of carbohydrate metabolism are glycolysis, the formation of acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA), the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle), and the electron transport system and oxidative phosphorylation.
- Glycolysis is the first step of glucose catabolism, which is the breakdown of glucose to produce energy in the form of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Glycolysis converts one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, which can then enter the mitochondrial matrix for further oxidation.
- The formation of acetyl CoA is the link between glycolysis and the citric acid cycle. Pyruvate is oxidized and decarboxylated to form acetyl CoA, which is then transferred to the citric acid cycle.
- The citric acid cycle is a series of reactions that occur in the mitochondrial matrix and generate energy by oxidizing acetyl CoA to carbon dioxide and water. The citric acid cycle also produces reduced coenzymes, such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH2), which carry electrons to the electron transport system.
- The electron transport system and oxidative phosphorylation are the final steps of glucose catabolism, which occur in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The electron transport system consists of a series of protein complexes that transfer electrons from NADH and FADH2 to oxygen, forming water and releasing energy. The energy is used to pump protons across the membrane, creating a proton gradient that drives the synthesis of ATP by the enzyme ATP synthase.
- Carbohydrate metabolism is connected to the metabolism of other compounds through the following ways  :
  - The simple sugars that can also feed into glucose catabolism include galactose, fructose, glycogen, and pentose. These are catabolized during glycolysis or converted into glucose by various enzymes .
  - The amino acids from proteins can enter glucose catabolism through pyruvate, acetyl CoA, or components of the citric acid cycle, depending on their structure and degradation pathway  . Some amino acids can also be synthesized from intermediates of glucose catabolism, such as oxaloacetate, alpha-ketoglutarate, and pyruvate.
  - The fatty acids from lipids can enter glucose catabolism through acetyl CoA, which is the product of beta-oxidation of fatty acids in the mitochondrial matrix . Fatty acids can also be synthesized from acetyl CoA by the process of lipogenesis in the cytosol.
  - The glycerol from lipids can enter glucose catabolism through glycolysis, after being converted into dihydroxyacetone phosphate by the enzyme glycerol kinase. Glycerol can also be synthesized from dihydroxyacetone phosphate by the reverse reaction.
  - The nucleotides from nucleic acids can enter glucose catabolism through the pentose phosphate pathway, which is an alternative pathway of glucose oxidation that produces ribose-5-phosphate, a precursor for nucleotide synthesis. Nucleotides can also be degraded into components that can enter glycolysis or the citric acid cycle, such as ribose, pyrimidines, and purines.
