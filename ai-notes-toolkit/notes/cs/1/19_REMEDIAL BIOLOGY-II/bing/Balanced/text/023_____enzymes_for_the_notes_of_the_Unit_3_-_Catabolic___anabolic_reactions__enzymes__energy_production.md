### Enzymes

- Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up the rate of biochemical reactions by lowering the activation energy required for the reaction to occur.
- Enzymes are highly specific for their substrates, which are the molecules that bind to the enzyme's active site and undergo a chemical change.
- Enzymes can be classified into two main types based on the direction of the reaction they catalyze: anabolic enzymes and catabolic enzymes.

### Anabolic Enzymes

- Anabolic enzymes are involved in the synthesis of larger, more complex molecules from smaller units. They consume energy in the form of ATP or NADPH to drive the reaction forward.
- Anabolic enzymes are essential for the growth, repair, and maintenance of cells and tissues. They also play a role in the storage of energy and information in the form of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.
- An example of an anabolic reaction is photosynthesis, where plants use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. The enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) is the key enzyme that catalyzes the first step of the Calvin cycle, which is the anabolic pathway that produces glucose from carbon dioxide.

### Catabolic Enzymes

- Catabolic enzymes are involved in the breakdown of larger, more complex molecules into smaller units. They release energy in the form of ATP or NADH that can be used for other cellular processes.
- Catabolic enzymes are essential for the degradation of waste products, toxins, and foreign substances. They also play a role in the generation of energy and building blocks for anabolic reactions.
- An example of a catabolic reaction is cellular respiration, where cells use oxygen to oxidize glucose and produce carbon dioxide, water, and ATP. The enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase is the key enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate into acetyl-CoA, which is the entry point for the citric acid cycle, which is the catabolic pathway that produces ATP from glucose.
