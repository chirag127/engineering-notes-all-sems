### Relationship of carbohydrate metabolism to other compounds

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the process of breaking down and synthesizing carbohydrates, which are organic molecules composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms.
- Carbohydrates can be classified into simple sugars (monosaccharides and disaccharides) and complex sugars (polysaccharides), such as starch, glycogen, and cellulose.
- Carbohydrate metabolism is connected to the metabolism of other compounds, such as proteins and lipids, through common intermediates and pathways  .
- Some of the connections are:

  - The simple sugars, such as glucose, fructose, and galactose, can be catabolized during glycolysis, which is the first step of glucose catabolism  .
  - Glycolysis produces pyruvate, which can be converted to acetyl CoA, the entry point of the citric acid cycle, which is the second step of glucose catabolism  .
  - The citric acid cycle produces NADH and FADH2, which are electron carriers that feed into the electron transport chain, which is the third and final step of glucose catabolism  .
  - The electron transport chain produces ATP, which is the main energy currency of the cell, and water  .
  - The fatty acids from lipids can be broken down by beta-oxidation, which also produces acetyl CoA, which can enter the citric acid cycle .
  - The glycerol from lipids can be converted to dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), which is an intermediate of glycolysis, and can be further metabolized to pyruvate or glucose .
  - The amino acids from proteins can be deaminated, which removes the amino group and produces ammonia and a carbon skeleton  .
  - The carbon skeleton of the amino acids can enter the glucose catabolism pathways at different points, depending on their structure  .
  - Some amino acids can be converted to pyruvate, some to acetyl CoA, and some to intermediates of the citric acid cycle, such as oxaloacetate, alpha-ketoglutarate, succinyl CoA, fumarate, and malate  .
  - The ammonia from the deamination of amino acids can be converted to urea, which is the main nitrogenous waste product in mammals, or to other compounds, depending on the organism  .

- These connections allow the cell to regulate the balance between the synthesis and breakdown of carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids, depending on the availability of substrates and the energy demand  .
- These connections also allow the cell to adapt to different environmental and physiological conditions, such as starvation, exercise, and stress, by switching between different metabolic pathways  .
