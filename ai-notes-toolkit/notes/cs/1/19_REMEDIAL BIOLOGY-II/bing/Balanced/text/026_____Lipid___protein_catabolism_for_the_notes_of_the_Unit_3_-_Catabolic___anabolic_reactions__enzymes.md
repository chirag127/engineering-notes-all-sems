### Lipid and Protein Catabolism

- Lipids and proteins are important macromolecules that can be used as energy sources by cells.
- Lipids are composed of glycerol and fatty acids, while proteins are composed of amino acids.
- Lipids and proteins are broken down by specific enzymes into smaller molecules that can enter the central metabolic pathways of glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.

#### Lipid Catabolism

- Lipids are hydrolyzed by lipases into glycerol and free fatty acids.
- Glycerol can be phosphorylated and converted into dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), an intermediate of glycolysis.
- Fatty acids are activated by coenzyme A (CoA) and transported into the mitochondria by carnitine.
- Fatty acids undergo beta-oxidation, a series of reactions that cleave two-carbon units from the acyl-CoA chain and produce acetyl-CoA, NADH, and FADH2.
- Acetyl-CoA can enter the citric acid cycle and generate more NADH, FADH2, and ATP.
- NADH and FADH2 can donate electrons to the electron transport chain and drive the synthesis of ATP by oxidative phosphorylation.

#### Protein Catabolism

- Proteins are hydrolyzed by proteases into smaller peptides and amino acids .
- Peptides and amino acids are transported into the cells by specific transporters.
- Amino acids are deaminated, meaning they lose their amino group, which is converted into ammonia and then urea for excretion.
- The remaining carbon skeletons of amino acids are converted into various intermediates of glycolysis or the citric acid cycle, depending on their structure.
- These intermediates can be oxidized to generate acetyl-CoA, NADH, FADH2, and ATP.
- Acetyl-CoA, NADH, and FADH2 can enter the same pathways as in lipid catabolism and produce more ATP.
