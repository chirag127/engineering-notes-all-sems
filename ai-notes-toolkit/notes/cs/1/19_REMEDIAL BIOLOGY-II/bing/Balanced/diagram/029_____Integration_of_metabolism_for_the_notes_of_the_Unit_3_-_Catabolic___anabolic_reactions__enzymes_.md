### Integration of metabolism

- Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions that occur in a living cell to maintain its structure and function.
- Metabolism can be divided into two major types: catabolism and anabolism.
  - Catabolism is the breakdown of complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy and reducing power.
  - Anabolism is the synthesis of complex molecules from simpler ones, requiring energy and reducing power.
- Metabolism is regulated by various factors, such as enzymes, hormones, allosteric effectors, and feedback mechanisms.
- Metabolism is integrated in different tissues and organs to coordinate the energy and biosynthetic needs of the whole organism.
- Metabolism is also adapted to different physiological states, such as fasting, feeding, exercise, and stress.

#### Catabolic and anabolic reactions: enzymes, energy production and carbohydrate metabolism

- Catabolic and anabolic reactions are linked by common intermediates and cofactors, such as ATP, NADH, NADPH, FADH2, and acetyl-CoA.
- Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up the rate of metabolic reactions by lowering the activation energy. Enzymes are specific for their substrates and can be regulated by various mechanisms, such as allosteric modulation, covalent modification, and gene expression.
- Energy production is the process of converting the chemical energy stored in nutrients into a usable form, such as ATP. The major pathways of energy production are glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.
- Carbohydrate metabolism is the breakdown and synthesis of sugars and polysaccharides. The major pathways of carbohydrate metabolism are glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, glycogenesis, and the pentose phosphate pathway.

#### Lipid and protein catabolism, Energy production mechanism, metabolic diversity and pathways of energy use

- Lipid catabolism is the breakdown of fatty acids and glycerol into acetyl-CoA and other intermediates. The major pathways of lipid catabolism are beta-oxidation, ketogenesis, and lipolysis.
- Protein catabolism is the breakdown of amino acids into keto acids and ammonia. The major pathways of protein catabolism are transamination, deamination, and the urea cycle.
- Energy production mechanism is the process of coupling the electron transport chain and the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane to the synthesis of ATP by ATP synthase. This process is also known as chemiosmosis or oxidative phosphorylation.
- Metabolic diversity is the ability of different organisms to use different substrates and pathways for energy production and biosynthesis. For example, some organisms can use oxygen as the final electron acceptor, while others can use nitrate, sulfate, or carbon dioxide. Some organisms can also use light as the energy source, while others can use inorganic compounds, such as hydrogen, iron, or sulfur.
- Pathways of energy use are the processes that consume ATP and other energy-rich molecules for various cellular functions, such as biosynthesis, transport, movement, signaling, and maintenance.

#### Integration of metabolism

- Integration of metabolism is the coordination and regulation of different metabolic pathways and processes to achieve homeostasis and adaptation in the organism.
- Integration of metabolism is achieved by various mechanisms, such as hormonal control, tissue-specific expression, substrate availability, and feedback inhibition.
- Three hormones—insulin, glucagon, and epinephrine—play a critical role in integrating metabolism, especially energy metabolism, in different tissues. Insulin stimulates the uptake and utilization of glucose and inhibits gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis. Glucagon stimulates gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis and inhibits glycolysis and glycogenesis. Epinephrine stimulates glycolysis and glycogenolysis and inhibits glycogenesis and lipogenesis.
- Different tissues and organs have different metabolic roles and capacities, depending on their functions and energy demands. For example, the liver is the major site of gluconeogenesis, ketogenesis, and ureagenesis, while the muscle is the major site of glycogen storage and utilization. The brain relies mainly on glucose and ketone bodies as the energy sources, while the heart prefers fatty acids and lactate.
