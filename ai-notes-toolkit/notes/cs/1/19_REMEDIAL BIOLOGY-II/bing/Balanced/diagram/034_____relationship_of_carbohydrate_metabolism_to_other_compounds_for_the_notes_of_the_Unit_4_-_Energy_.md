### Relationship of carbohydrate metabolism to other compounds

- Carbohydrate metabolism is the process of breaking down and synthesizing carbohydrates, which are organic molecules composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms.
- Carbohydrates can be classified into simple sugars (such as glucose and fructose) and complex sugars (such as starch, glycogen, and cellulose).
- Carbohydrate metabolism is connected to the metabolism of proteins and lipids, which are other types of organic molecules that provide energy and structural components for living cells.
- The breakdown and synthesis of carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids connect with the pathways of glucose catabolism, which is the process of converting glucose into energy and carbon dioxide  .
- Glucose catabolism involves three main stages: glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and the electron transport chain.
- Glycolysis is the first stage of glucose catabolism, where glucose is split into two molecules of pyruvate, producing a net gain of two molecules of ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and two molecules of NADH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) per glucose molecule.
- Pyruvate is then converted into acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA), which is the link between glycolysis and the citric acid cycle.
- The citric acid cycle, also known as the Krebs cycle, is the second stage of glucose catabolism, where acetyl CoA is oxidized into carbon dioxide, producing more ATP, NADH, and FADH2 (flavin adenine dinucleotide) per acetyl CoA molecule.
- The electron transport chain, also known as the oxidative phosphorylation, is the third and final stage of glucose catabolism, where the electrons from NADH and FADH2 are transferred to oxygen, forming water and generating a large amount of ATP through a process called chemiosmosis.
- The carbohydrates that can also feed into glucose catabolism include galactose, fructose, glycogen, and pentose. These are catabolized during glycolysis.
- The amino acids from proteins connect with glucose catabolism through pyruvate, acetyl CoA, and components of the citric acid cycle. Some amino acids can be converted into pyruvate or acetyl CoA, while others can enter the citric acid cycle directly or indirectly .
- The fatty acids from lipids connect with glucose catabolism through acetyl CoA. Fatty acids can be broken down into acetyl CoA through a process called beta-oxidation .
- The synthesis of carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids also depends on the intermediates and products of glucose catabolism. For example, glucose can be synthesized from pyruvate, lactate, glycerol, or some amino acids through a process called gluconeogenesis. Amino acids can be synthesized from pyruvate, acetyl CoA, or citric acid cycle intermediates through a process called transamination. Fatty acids can be synthesized from acetyl CoA through a process called fatty acid synthesis.
- The regulation of carbohydrate, protein, and lipid metabolism is influenced by various factors, such as hormones, enzymes, energy demand, and oxygen availability. For example, insulin and glucagon are hormones that regulate blood glucose levels by stimulating or inhibiting glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, glycogen synthesis, and glycogen breakdown. Enzymes are proteins that catalyze or speed up the chemical reactions of metabolism. Energy demand and oxygen availability affect the rate and efficiency of glucose catabolism and ATP production.
