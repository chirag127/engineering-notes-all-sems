# Viral Multiplication

Viral multiplication is the process of producing new viruses using the host cell's machinery and resources. Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites, which means they can only replicate inside living cells. Viruses have different types of genomes, such as DNA or RNA, single-stranded or double-stranded, linear or circular, etc. The type of genome determines the mode of replication and the enzymes required for viral multiplication.

The general steps of viral multiplication are:

- **Attachment**: The virus binds to a specific receptor on the surface of the host cell. The receptor is usually a protein or a carbohydrate that is involved in normal cellular functions. The attachment is mediated by viral proteins called attachment proteins or spikes, which recognize and bind to the receptor. The attachment determines the host range and tissue tropism of the virus, i.e., the types of cells and organisms that the virus can infect.
- **Penetration**: The virus enters the host cell by one of the following mechanisms: endocytosis, fusion, or injection. Endocytosis is the process of engulfing the virus by the cell membrane and forming a vesicle called an endosome. Fusion is the process of merging the viral envelope with the cell membrane and releasing the viral capsid into the cytoplasm. Injection is the process of injecting the viral genome into the cell through a tail-like structure, as in some bacteriophages.
- **Uncoating**: The virus releases its genome from the capsid and makes it available for replication and transcription. Uncoating can occur in the endosome, in the cytoplasm, or in the nucleus, depending on the type of virus. Uncoating may require the action of viral or cellular enzymes, or changes in pH or temperature.
- **Biosynthesis**: The virus synthesizes new copies of its genome and viral proteins using the host cell's machinery and resources. The biosynthesis can be divided into two phases: early and late. The early phase involves the expression of viral genes that encode enzymes and regulatory proteins that are essential for viral replication and transcription. The late phase involves the expression of viral genes that encode structural proteins that form the viral capsid and envelope. The biosynthesis depends on the type of viral genome and the location of replication and transcription. For example, DNA viruses usually replicate and transcribe in the nucleus, while RNA viruses usually replicate and transcribe in the cytoplasm. Some viruses use reverse transcriptase to convert their RNA genome into DNA, such as retroviruses and hepadnaviruses.
- **Assembly**: The virus assembles new virions by putting together the viral genome and the viral proteins. The assembly can occur in the cytoplasm, in the nucleus, or at the cell membrane, depending on the type of virus. The assembly may require the help of viral or cellular proteins, or self-assembly of viral components.
- **Release**: The virus exits the host cell by one of the following mechanisms: lysis, budding, or exocytosis. Lysis is the process of breaking the cell membrane and releasing the virions into the extracellular environment. Lysis usually results in the death of the host cell. Budding is the process of pinching off a portion of the cell membrane and wrapping it around the virion, forming a viral envelope. Budding may or may not result in the death of the host cell. Exocytosis is the process of fusing the vesicle containing the virion with the cell membrane and releasing it into the extracellular environment. Exocytosis usually does not result in the death of the host cell.

The viral multiplication can be classified into two types based on the life cycle, i.e., lytic cycle or lysogenic cycle. The lytic cycle is the process of producing new virions and destroying the host cell. The lysogenic cycle is the process of integrating the viral genome into the host genome and remaining dormant until a trigger activates the lytic cycle. Some viruses can undergo both cycles, such as bacteriophages and herpesviruses. Some viruses can only undergo the lytic cycle, such as polioviruses and influenza viruses. Some viruses can only undergo the lysogenic cycle, such as retroviruses and proviruses.
