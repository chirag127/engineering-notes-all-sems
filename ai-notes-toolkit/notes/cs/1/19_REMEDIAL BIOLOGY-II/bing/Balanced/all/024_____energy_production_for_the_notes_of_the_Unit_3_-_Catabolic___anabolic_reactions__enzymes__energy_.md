## Catabolic and anabolic reactions

- Catabolic reactions are those that break down large molecules into smaller ones and release energy. An example of a catabolic reaction is the process of food digestion, where different enzymes break down food particles so they can be absorbed by the small intestine.
- Anabolic reactions are those that build up large molecules from smaller ones and require energy. An example of an anabolic reaction is photosynthesis, where plants make glucose molecules from different raw materials.
- Catabolic and anabolic reactions are connected by the common currency of energy, which is ATP. ATP is produced by catabolic reactions and used by anabolic reactions.
- Catabolic and anabolic reactions are also regulated by enzymes, which are proteins that catalyze or speed up chemical reactions. Enzymes lower the activation energy of a reaction, which is the amount of energy needed to start the reaction.
- Enzymes have specific shapes and active sites that bind to specific substrates or reactants. The enzyme-substrate complex undergoes a chemical change and produces products that are released from the enzyme. The enzyme can then bind to another substrate and repeat the process.
- Enzymes can be affected by factors such as temperature, pH, concentration, inhibitors, and activators. These factors can change the shape or activity of the enzyme and affect the rate of the reaction.

## Energy production and carbohydrate metabolism

- Carbohydrates are organic molecules that consist of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms. They are the main source of energy for living organisms and can be classified into monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides based on the number of sugar units they contain.
- Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates and include glucose, fructose, and galactose. They can be joined together by glycosidic bonds to form disaccharides, such as sucrose, lactose, and maltose. Disaccharides can be further joined to form polysaccharides, such as starch, glycogen, cellulose, and chitin.
- Carbohydrate metabolism is the process of breaking down or synthesizing carbohydrates to produce or store energy. The most common pathway of carbohydrate metabolism is glycolysis, which is the catabolic breakdown of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm of the cell and produces a net gain of two ATP and two NADH molecules per glucose molecule.
- Pyruvate can then enter different pathways depending on the availability of oxygen and the type of organism. In the presence of oxygen, pyruvate is transported into the mitochondria and converted into acetyl-CoA, which enters the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle). The citric acid cycle is a series of reactions that produces two ATP, six NADH, and two FADH2 molecules per acetyl-CoA molecule. The NADH and FADH2 molecules then transfer their electrons to the electron transport chain, which is a series of protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The electron transport chain generates a proton gradient across the membrane, which drives the synthesis of ATP by the enzyme ATP synthase. This process is called oxidative phosphorylation and produces about 28 ATP molecules per glucose molecule.
- In the absence of oxygen, pyruvate undergoes fermentation, which is the anaerobic conversion of pyruvate into other molecules that can regenerate NAD+ for glycolysis. There are different types of fermentation, such as lactic acid fermentation and alcoholic fermentation. Lactic acid fermentation occurs in some animal cells and some bacteria, and converts pyruvate into lactate. Alcoholic fermentation occurs in some plants and fungi, and converts pyruvate into ethanol and carbon dioxide.
