# 14. Determination of Cell Conductance of a Solution

- Cell conductance is a measure of how well a solution can conduct electric current between two electrodes.
- Cell conductance depends on the concentration, type and mobility of the ions in the solution, as well as the geometry and material of the electrodes and the cell.
- Cell conductance can be determined experimentally by applying a known alternating voltage across the electrodes and measuring the resulting alternating current.
- Cell conductance can be calculated using the formula:

    `G = I / V`

    where G is the cell conductance, I is the current and V is the voltage.

- Cell conductance can also be expressed as the product of the cell constant and the specific conductance of the solution:

    `G = k * C`

    where k is the cell constant, which depends on the geometry and material of the electrodes and the cell, and C is the specific conductance of the solution, which depends on the concentration, type and mobility of the ions.

- The cell constant can be determined by measuring the cell conductance of a solution with a known specific conductance, such as a standard potassium chloride solution, and then using the formula:

    `k = G / C`

- The specific conductance of an unknown solution can then be determined by measuring its cell conductance and dividing it by the cell constant:

    `C = G / k`

- The specific conductance of a solution is related to its molar conductivity, which is the conductance of a solution containing one mole of electrolyte per unit volume, by the formula:

    `C = L * c`

    where L is the molar conductivity and c is the molar concentration of the electrolyte.

- The molar conductivity of a solution depends on the degree of dissociation of the electrolyte and the mobility of the ions. For strong electrolytes, such as salts, acids and bases, the molar conductivity decreases with increasing concentration, due to the increased interionic interactions and reduced mobility of the ions. For weak electrolytes, such as acetic acid and ammonia, the molar conductivity increases with increasing concentration, due to the increased degree of dissociation of the electrolyte and the increased number of ions.

- The molar conductivity of a solution at infinite dilution, where the interionic interactions are negligible and the degree of dissociation is complete, is denoted by L0 and can be calculated using the Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions, which states that the molar conductivity of a solution at infinite dilution is equal to the sum of the molar conductivities of the individual ions at infinite dilution, multiplied by their stoichiometric coefficients:

    `L0 = v+ * L0+ + v- * L0-`

    where v+ and v- are the stoichiometric coefficients of the cation and the anion, respectively, and L0+ and L0- are the molar conductivities of the cation and the anion at infinite dilution, respectively.

- The molar conductivities of the individual ions at infinite dilution can be determined experimentally by measuring the molar conductivities of different strong electrolytes containing the same ion at infinite dilution, and then using a system of linear equations to solve for the unknown values.

- The degree of dissociation of a weak electrolyte can be calculated using the formula:

    `α = L / L0`

    where α is the degree of dissociation, L is the molar conductivity of the weak electrolyte at a given concentration, and L0 is the molar conductivity of the weak electrolyte at infinite dilution.

- The dissociation constant of a weak electrolyte can be calculated using the formula:

    `K = α^2 * c / (1 - α)`

    where K is the dissociation constant, α is the degree of dissociation, and c is the molar concentration of the weak electrolyte.
