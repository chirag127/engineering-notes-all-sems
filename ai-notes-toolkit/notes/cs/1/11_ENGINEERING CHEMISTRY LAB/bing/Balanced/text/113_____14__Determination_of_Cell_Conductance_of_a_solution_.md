### 14. Determination of Cell Conductance of a Solution

- The conductance of a solution is the reciprocal of its resistance. It is a measure of how well the solution can carry an electric current.
- The conductance of a solution depends on the concentration, number of charges, and mobility of the ions it contains.
- The conductance of a solution can be measured by using a special type of cell known as a conductivity cell and a Wheatstone bridge.
- A conductivity cell consists of two electrodes of known area and distance, immersed in the solution whose conductance is to be measured.
- The cell constant, C, is the ratio of the distance between the electrodes, l, and the area of cross-section of the electrodes, a. It has the units of m^-1^. C = l/a 
- The cell constant can be determined by measuring the resistance of a cell filled with a solution of known conductivity, such as 0.0200 M KCl.
- The conductivity of any solution can be calculated from the experimental resistance by using the formula: k = C/R, where k is the conductivity and R is the resistance.
- The conductivity of a solution is proportional to the molar conductivity of the electrolyte, which is the conductance of a solution containing one mole of the electrolyte per unit volume.
- The molar conductivity of an electrolyte varies with its concentration and temperature.
- The molar conductivity of a strong electrolyte approaches a limiting value as the concentration approaches zero. This is known as the Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions.
- The molar conductivity of a weak electrolyte can be calculated from the molar conductivity of its strong electrolyte components by using the Ostwald's dilution law.
