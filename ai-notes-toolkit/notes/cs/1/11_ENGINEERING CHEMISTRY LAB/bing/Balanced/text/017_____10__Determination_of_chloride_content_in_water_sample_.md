### 10. Determination of chloride content in water sample.

- Chloride is one of the major anions found in natural waters. It originates from various sources, such as seawater intrusion, industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, and domestic sewage.
- Chloride can affect the taste, corrosion, and biological activity of water. Therefore, it is important to monitor and control its concentration in drinking water, wastewater, and environmental samples.
- The most common method for determining chloride content in water sample is the **argentometric method**. This method is based on the precipitation of chloride ions as silver chloride (AgCl) by adding a known excess of silver nitrate (AgNO3) solution.
- The end point of the precipitation reaction is detected by using a suitable indicator, such as potassium chromate (K2CrO4), which forms a reddish-brown precipitate of silver chromate (Ag2CrO4) when all the chloride ions have been precipitated.
- The amount of silver nitrate used can be calculated by subtracting the amount of excess silver nitrate from the initial amount. The amount of chloride in the sample can be calculated by using the stoichiometric ratio of 1:1 between silver and chloride ions.
- The procedure for the argentometric method is as follows:

  - Pipette a known volume of the water sample into a conical flask.
  - Add a few drops of potassium chromate indicator to the flask.
  - Titrate the sample with standard silver nitrate solution from a burette until a permanent reddish-brown color appears.
  - Record the final burette reading and calculate the volume of silver nitrate used.
  - Repeat the titration with a blank solution (distilled water) and a standard solution (known concentration of chloride) to obtain the correction factor and the standardization factor, respectively.
  - Calculate the chloride content in the water sample by using the following formula:

    `Cl- (mg/L) = (V1 - V2) x N x F x 35.45 / V`

    where V1 is the volume of silver nitrate used for the sample (mL), V2 is the volume of silver nitrate used for the blank (mL), N is the normality of silver nitrate solution, F is the standardization factor, and V is the volume of the water sample (mL).

- The advantages of the argentometric method are:

  - It is simple, rapid, and accurate.
  - It does not require sophisticated instruments or reagents.
  - It can be applied to a wide range of chloride concentrations.

- The limitations of the argentometric method are:

  - It is susceptible to interferences from other anions, such as bromide, iodide, cyanide, sulfide, and thiocyanate, which can also precipitate with silver nitrate.
  - It requires a clear and colorless sample, as turbidity and color can obscure the end point.
  - It is affected by the temperature and pH of the sample, as these factors can influence the solubility and color of the precipitates.
