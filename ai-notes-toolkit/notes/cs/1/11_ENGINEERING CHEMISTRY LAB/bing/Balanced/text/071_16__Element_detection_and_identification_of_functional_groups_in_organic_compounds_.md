# Element detection and identification of functional groups in organic compounds

- Organic compounds are those that contain carbon atoms, usually bonded to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, or halogens.
- Functional groups are specific groups of atoms or bonds within organic molecules that are responsible for their chemical properties and reactions.
- Element detection is the process of identifying the presence of certain elements in an organic compound, such as carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulfur, halogens, etc.
- Identification of functional groups is the process of determining the type and location of functional groups in an organic compound, such as alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, amines, etc.
- Element detection and identification of functional groups are important steps in the qualitative analysis of organic compounds, which helps to determine their structure and properties.
- Element detection and identification of functional groups can be done by various chemical tests, such as:

  - Carbon and hydrogen detection: A small amount of the organic compound is heated with copper oxide in a test tube. If carbon and hydrogen are present, carbon dioxide and water are formed, which can be detected by passing the gas through lime water (turns milky) and anhydrous copper sulfate (turns blue).
  - Nitrogen detection: A small amount of the organic compound is heated with sodium metal in a test tube. If nitrogen is present, ammonia gas is evolved, which can be detected by its pungent smell and by turning moist red litmus paper blue.
  - Sulfur detection: A small amount of the organic compound is heated with sodium metal in a test tube. If sulfur is present, hydrogen sulfide gas is evolved, which can be detected by its rotten egg smell and by turning moist lead acetate paper black.
  - Halogen detection: A small amount of the organic compound is heated with sodium metal in a test tube. If halogens are present, sodium halides are formed, which can be detected by adding dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate solution. A white precipitate indicates chloride, a cream precipitate indicates bromide, and a yellow precipitate indicates iodide.
  - Alcoholic -OH group detection: A small amount of the organic compound is added to a solution of ceric ammonium nitrate. If an alcoholic -OH group is present, the solution turns red.
  - Carbonyl group (aldehydes and ketones) detection: A small amount of the organic compound is added to a solution of 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine. If a carbonyl group is present, a yellow, orange, or red precipitate of the corresponding hydrazone is formed.
  - Aldehyde group detection: A small amount of the organic compound is added to a solution of Fehling's reagent (a mixture of copper sulfate, sodium carbonate, and potassium sodium tartrate). If an aldehyde group is present, a red precipitate of copper oxide is formed. Alternatively, a small amount of the organic compound is added to a solution of Tollens' reagent (a mixture of silver nitrate and ammonia). If an aldehyde group is present, a silver mirror is formed on the inner surface of the test tube.
  - Carboxylic acid group detection: A small amount of the organic compound is added to a solution of sodium bicarbonate. If a carboxylic acid group is present, carbon dioxide gas is evolved, which can be detected by its effervescence and by turning lime water milky.
  - Amino group detection: A small amount of the organic compound is added to a solution of ninhydrin. If a primary or secondary amino group is present, a purple color is produced. Alternatively, a small amount of the organic compound is added to a solution of nitrous acid. If a primary amino group is present, nitrogen gas is evolved, which can be detected by its bubbling and by displacing water in an inverted test tube. If a secondary amino group is present, a yellow oily liquid of nitrosamine is formed.
