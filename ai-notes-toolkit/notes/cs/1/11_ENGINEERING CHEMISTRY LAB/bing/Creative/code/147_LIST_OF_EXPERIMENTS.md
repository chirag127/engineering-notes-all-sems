# LIST OF EXPERIMENTS

- An experiment is a scientific procedure that aims to test a hypothesis, answer a question, or demonstrate a known fact.
- Experiments usually involve manipulating one or more variables, measuring their effects, and comparing them with a control group.
- Experiments can be classified into different types based on their purpose, design, and outcome.

## Types of experiments

- **Descriptive experiments** describe the characteristics or behavior of a phenomenon or a group of subjects. For example, observing the color of a flower or the height of a plant.
- **Relational experiments** examine the relationship between two or more variables. For example, testing the correlation between smoking and lung cancer or between temperature and pressure.
- **Causal experiments** test the cause-and-effect relationship between an independent variable (the cause) and a dependent variable (the effect). For example, testing the effect of fertilizer on plant growth or of a drug on blood pressure.
- **Quasi-experiments** are similar to causal experiments, but they lack random assignment of subjects to groups or conditions. For example, comparing the academic performance of students in different schools or of patients in different hospitals.
- **Natural experiments** exploit natural variations in the independent variable to study its effect on the dependent variable. For example, studying the impact of a natural disaster, a policy change, or a historical event on a population or an outcome.

## Design of experiments

- **Experimental design** is the process of planning and conducting an experiment to ensure its validity, reliability, and efficiency.
- Experimental design involves choosing the appropriate type of experiment, selecting the variables, defining the hypothesis, determining the sample size, assigning the subjects to groups or conditions, controlling the confounding factors, applying the treatments, measuring the outcomes, and analyzing the data.
- Some common experimental designs are:

  - **Randomized controlled trial (RCT)**: A causal experiment that randomly assigns subjects to either a treatment group or a control group, and compares their outcomes. RCTs are considered the gold standard for testing the efficacy of interventions in medicine, education, and social sciences.
  - **Factorial design**: An experiment that tests the effect of two or more independent variables (factors) and their interactions on a dependent variable. For example, testing the effect of temperature and humidity on plant growth.
  - **Repeated measures design**: An experiment that measures the same subjects under different conditions or at different times. For example, testing the effect of caffeine on alertness before and after drinking coffee.
  - **Crossover design**: A type of repeated measures design that switches the subjects between the treatment and control groups. For example, testing the effect of two different drugs on pain relief by giving each subject both drugs in a random order.
  - **Matched pairs design**: An experiment that pairs subjects with similar characteristics and assigns them to different groups or conditions. For example, testing the effect of a diet on weight loss by matching subjects based on their age, gender, and body mass index.

## Outcome of experiments

- The outcome of an experiment is the result or the conclusion that can be drawn from the data collected and analyzed.
- The outcome of an experiment can be expressed in different ways, such as:

  - **Statistical significance**: The probability that the observed difference or effect is due to chance rather than to the independent variable. A low p-value (usually less than 0.05) indicates that the difference or effect is statistically significant, meaning that it is unlikely to occur by chance.
  - **Effect size**: The magnitude or the strength of the difference or effect. A large effect size indicates that the difference or effect is meaningful or important, regardless of the statistical significance.
  - **Confidence interval**: The range of values that contains the true value of the difference or effect with a certain level of confidence (usually 95%). A narrow confidence interval indicates that the estimate is precise and reliable.
  - **Practical significance**: The relevance or the usefulness of the difference or effect for the real-world situation or problem. A practically significant difference or effect is one that has a noticeable or beneficial impact on the outcome of interest.
