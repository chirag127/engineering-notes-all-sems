# 14. Determination of Cell Conductance of a Solution

- The conductance of a solution is the reciprocal of its resistance. It is a measure of how well the solution can carry an electric current.
- The conductance of a solution depends on the concentration, charge, and mobility of the ions it contains. The mobility of an ion is its ability to move through the solution under an electric potential gradient.
- The unit of conductance is siemens (S) or ohm^-1^. The unit of conductance per unit length is siemens per meter (S/m) or ohm^-1^ m^-1^.
- The conductance of a solution can be measured by using a special type of cell known as a conductivity cell and a Wheatstone bridge. A conductivity cell consists of two electrodes of known area and distance, immersed in the solution. A Wheatstone bridge is a circuit that can measure the resistance of an unknown resistor by comparing it with a known resistor.
- The resistance of the solution in the conductivity cell is given by:

  R = ρl/a

  where R is the resistance, ρ is the resistivity, l is the distance between the electrodes, and a is the area of the electrodes.

- The conductivity of the solution is the reciprocal of the resistivity, and is given by:

  κ = 1/ρ = a/Rl

  where κ is the conductivity.

- The cell constant, C, is the ratio of the distance and the area of the electrodes, and is given by:

  C = l/a

  The cell constant has the unit of m^-1^, and is a constant for a particular cell.

- The conductivity of the solution can be calculated from the cell constant and the resistance by using the formula:

  κ = C/R

  where R is the resistance measured by the Wheatstone bridge.

- The cell constant can be determined by measuring the resistance of a cell filled with a solution of known conductivity, such as 0.0200 M KCl. The conductivity of KCl at different temperatures can be found in standard tables.

- Once the cell constant is determined, the conductivity of any solution can be calculated from the experimental resistance by using the formula:

  κ = C/R

- The conductivity of a solution is related to the concentration of the ions by the equation:

  κ = Σciλi

  where ci is the concentration of the ith ion, and λi is the molar conductivity of the ith ion. The molar conductivity is the conductivity of a solution containing one mole of the ion per unit volume.

- The molar conductivity of an ion depends on its charge and mobility. The molar conductivity of an ion at infinite dilution, λi^0^, is the maximum value of the molar conductivity when the ion is completely dissociated and free from any interaction with other ions. The molar conductivity of an ion at any concentration, λi, can be calculated from the molar conductivity at infinite dilution by using the equation:

  λi = λi^0^ - k√ci

  where k is a constant that depends on the ion and the solvent.

- The molar conductivity of a solution at infinite dilution, Λ^0^, is the sum of the molar conductivities of the ions at infinite dilution, and is given by:

  Λ^0^ = Σνiλi^0^

  where νi is the stoichiometric coefficient of the ith ion in the electrolyte.

- The molar conductivity of a solution at any concentration, Λ, is the ratio of the conductivity and the concentration of the electrolyte, and is given by:

  Λ = κ/c

  where c is the concentration of the electrolyte.

- The molar conductivity of a solution at any concentration, Λ, can be calculated from the molar conductivity at infinite dilution, Λ^0^, by using the equation:

  Λ = Λ^0^ - K√c

  where K is a constant that depends on the electrolyte and the solvent.

- The degree of dissociation, α, of a weak electrolyte is the fraction of the electrolyte that is dissociated into ions in the solution.
