# Viscosity, Conductance of Solution, Chloride and Iron Content in the Water

## Viscosity

- Viscosity is the property of a fluid that measures its resistance to flow or deformation.
- Viscosity is related to the internal friction or cohesion of the fluid molecules, as well as the external forces acting on them.
- Viscosity can be expressed as the ratio of the shear stress (force per unit area) to the shear rate (velocity gradient) of the fluid.
- Viscosity has the SI unit of pascal-second (Pa·s) or the equivalent unit of newton-second per square meter (N·s/m2).
- Viscosity can also be expressed in terms of the kinematic viscosity, which is the ratio of the dynamic viscosity to the density of the fluid.
- Kinematic viscosity has the SI unit of square meter per second (m2/s) or the equivalent unit of stokes (St).
- Viscosity depends on the temperature, pressure, and composition of the fluid, as well as the presence of impurities or additives.
- Generally, viscosity decreases with increasing temperature and increases with increasing pressure.
- Liquids tend to have higher viscosities than gases, and solids tend to have higher viscosities than liquids.
- Some examples of fluids with high viscosities are honey, molasses, glycerin, and oil, while some examples of fluids with low viscosities are water, air, and alcohol.

## Conductance of Solution

- Conductance of solution is the measure of the ability of a solution to conduct electric current.
- Conductance of solution is related to the concentration, type, and mobility of the ions present in the solution, as well as the temperature and viscosity of the solution.
- Conductance of solution can be expressed as the ratio of the electric current (I) to the potential difference (V) applied across the solution.
- Conductance of solution has the SI unit of siemens (S) or the equivalent unit of ohm-1 (Ω-1).
- Conductance of solution can also be expressed in terms of the specific conductance or conductivity, which is the ratio of the conductance to the length (L) and cross-sectional area (A) of the solution.
- Specific conductance or conductivity has the SI unit of siemens per meter (S/m) or the equivalent unit of ohm-1 meter-1 (Ω-1 m-1).
- Conductance of solution depends on the nature and concentration of the electrolyte, the temperature and viscosity of the solution, and the presence of impurities or additives.
- Generally, conductance of solution increases with increasing concentration and temperature, and decreases with increasing viscosity.
- Strong electrolytes, such as sodium chloride and sulfuric acid, tend to have higher conductances than weak electrolytes, such as acetic acid and ammonia, while non-electrolytes, such as sugar and ethanol, tend to have negligible conductances.

## Chloride and Iron Content in the Water

- Chloride and iron are two common elements that can be found in natural or tap water, and they can affect the quality and suitability of the water for various purposes.
- Chloride is a negatively charged ion (Cl-) that can originate from various sources, such as seawater intrusion, rock weathering, industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, or road salt.
- Chloride can affect the taste, odor, and corrosivity of the water, as well as the health of aquatic organisms and plants.
- The recommended limit for chloride in drinking water is 250 mg/L, according to the World Health Organization (WHO).
- Iron is a positively charged ion (Fe2+ or Fe3+) that can originate from various sources, such as soil erosion, groundwater leaching, mining activities, or corrosion of pipes and fittings.
- Iron can affect the color, turbidity, taste, odor, and staining of the water, as well as the growth of bacteria and algae.
- The recommended limit for iron in drinking water is 0.3 mg/L, according to the WHO.
