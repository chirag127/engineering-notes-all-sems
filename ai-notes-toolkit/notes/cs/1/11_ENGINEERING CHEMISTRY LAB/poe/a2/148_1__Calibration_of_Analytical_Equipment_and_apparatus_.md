# Calibration of Analytical Equipment and apparatus

1. Calibration is a process that establishes a relationship between the output signals of an instrument and the corresponding input values by applying known input or standard values and recording the corresponding output values. It is carried out periodically to ensure accuracy of the instrument.
2. For analytical instruments, calibration involves preparing a series of standard solutions of known concentrations and measuring their responses to plot a calibration curve. The calibration curve is then used to determine unknown concentrations by comparing their responses to that of the standards.
3. Common calibration standards:
- Pure solvents: Used for calibrating instruments like refractometers.
- Single element solutions: Used for AAS, ICP-AES. e.g. Standard solutions of metals like Cu, Fe, Ca.
- Multielement solutions: Mixture of elements in a single solution. Convenient but matrix effects can influence calibration.
- Certified reference materials: Provide matrix matching and more accurate calibration.
4. Calibration frequency depends on the stability and precision of the instrument. More precise instruments need more frequent calibration. Environmental conditions can also affect frequency. Typically, calibration is done daily, weekly or monthly based on need.
5. Keeping calibration records is important to keep track of instrument performance over time and to maintain high quality data. Records should include parameters for calibration, correlation statistics and other relevant details.
