### High temperature superconductors

- High temperature superconductors (HTS) are materials that behave as superconductors at temperatures above 77 K (−196.2 °C; −321.1 °F), the boiling point of liquid nitrogen.
- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and can expel magnetic fields when cooled below a critical temperature (Tc).
- HTS have higher Tc than conventional superconductors, which usually have Tc below 30 K (−243.2 °C; −405.7 °F).
- HTS have potential applications in power transmission, magnetic levitation, quantum computing, and other fields that require high efficiency and low loss of energy.
- The main class of HTS is copper oxides combined with other metals, especially the rare-earth barium copper oxides (REBCOs) such as yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO).
- The second class of HTS is the iron-based compounds, such as iron pnictides and iron chalcogenides.
- The mechanism of HTS is still not fully understood, but it is believed to involve the formation of Cooper pairs, which are pairs of electrons that are bound together by a quantum phenomenon called superexchange .
- The Cooper pairs can move through the lattice of atoms without resistance, as they are in a quantum state that is unaffected by the thermal vibrations of the atoms.
- The Cooper pairs are also affected by the magnetic fields, which can break them apart and destroy the superconductivity. This is why superconductors have a critical magnetic field (Hc) as well as a critical temperature.
- HTS have higher Hc than conventional superconductors, which means they can withstand stronger magnetic fields and generate higher magnetic fields themselves.
- HTS are usually fabricated as thin films, wires, or tapes, which can be used to make coils, cables, or devices.
- HTS are still challenging to produce, as they require precise control of the composition, structure, and doping of the materials.
- HTS are also sensitive to defects, impurities, and strain, which can affect their properties and performance.
- HTS are still far from being widely used, as they require expensive and complex cooling systems, and face competition from other technologies such as low temperature superconductors, permanent magnets, and semiconductors .
- HTS are still an active area of research, as scientists are trying to discover new materials, improve the existing ones, and understand the underlying physics of superconductivity.
