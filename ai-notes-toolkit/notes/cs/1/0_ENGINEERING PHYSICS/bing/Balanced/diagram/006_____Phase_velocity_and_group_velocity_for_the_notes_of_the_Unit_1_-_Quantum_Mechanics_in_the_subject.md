- Phase velocity and group velocity are two important and related concepts in wave mechanics. They arise in quantum mechanics in the time development of the state function for the continuous case, i.e. wave packets.
- The phase velocity of a wave is the rate at which the wave propagates in any medium. This is the velocity at which the phase of any one frequency component of the wave travels.
- The group velocity of a wave is the velocity with which the overall envelope shape of the wave's amplitudes—known as the modulation or envelope of the wave—propagates through space.
- Phase velocity and group velocity are related through Rayleigh's formula, which is:

$$v_g = v_p + \lambda \frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$$

where $v_g$ is the group velocity, $v_p$ is the phase velocity, $\lambda$ is the wavelength, and $\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$ is the derivative of the phase velocity with respect to the wavelength.

- If the derivative term is zero, group velocity equals phase velocity. In this case, there is no dispersion, which means that the wave does not change its shape as it propagates.
- In quantum mechanics, the phase velocity and the group velocity of a wave have different meanings for the associated particle. The phase velocity is related to the total energy of the particle, while the group velocity is related to the kinetic energy of the particle.
- Using the de Broglie postulates, which are:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$
$$\omega = \frac{E}{\hbar}$$

where $h$ is the Planck constant, $p$ is the momentum, $E$ is the energy, and $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, we can show that the group velocity of a free particle is twice the phase velocity, as follows:

$$v_p = \frac{\omega}{\lambda} = \frac{E}{h} \frac{p}{h} = \frac{p}{m}$$
$$v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{d}{dk} \frac{E}{\hbar} = \frac{d}{dk} \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m} = \frac{\hbar k}{m} = \frac{p}{m}$$

where $k$ is the wave number, and $m$ is the mass of the particle. Therefore,

$$v_g = 2 v_p$$
