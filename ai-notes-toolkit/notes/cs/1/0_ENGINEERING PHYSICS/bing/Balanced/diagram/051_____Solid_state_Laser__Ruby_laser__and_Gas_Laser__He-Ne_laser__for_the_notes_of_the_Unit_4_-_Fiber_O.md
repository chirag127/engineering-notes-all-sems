### Solid state Laser (Ruby laser) and Gas Laser (He-Ne laser)

#### Solid state Laser

- A solid state laser is a laser that uses a solid material as the gain medium, where the light is amplified.
- The most common solid state laser is the ruby laser, which was the first laser ever invented.
- Other examples of solid state lasers are neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) laser, titanium-sapphire (Ti:sapphire) laser, and erbium-doped fiber laser (EDFL).

#### Ruby laser

- A ruby laser is a solid state laser that uses a synthetic ruby crystal as the gain medium.
- The ruby crystal consists of a cylindrical rod with polished ends, doped with chromium ions that provide the lasing action.
- The ruby rod is placed between two mirrors, forming an optical cavity. One of the mirrors is partially transparent, allowing some light to escape as the laser output.
- The ruby laser produces pulses of coherent visible light at a wavelength of 694.3 nm, which is a deep red color, with a very narrow linewidth of 0.53 nm.
- The ruby laser is a three level laser, meaning that it requires a high pumping energy to achieve population inversion and lasing.
- The ruby laser is pumped by a flash lamp, which emits a broad spectrum of light that excites the chromium ions from the ground state to a higher energy level.
- The excited chromium ions then decay to a metastable state, where they can stay for a long time without emitting radiation.
- When the population of the metastable state exceeds that of the ground state, population inversion is achieved and lasing can occur.
- The lasing process involves the stimulated emission of photons from the metastable state to the ground state, creating a coherent beam of red light.
- The ruby laser can only produce short pulses of light, typically lasting a few milliseconds, because the population inversion is quickly depleted by the lasing action and the spontaneous emission of photons from the metastable state.
- The ruby laser has applications in holography, spectroscopy, and medicine.

#### Gas Laser

- A gas laser is a laser that uses a gas as the gain medium, where the light is amplified.
- The most common gas laser is the helium-neon (He-Ne) laser, which was the first continuous wave laser ever invented.
- Other examples of gas lasers are carbon dioxide (CO2) laser, argon-ion (Ar+) laser, and nitrogen (N2) laser.

#### He-Ne laser

- A He-Ne laser is a gas laser that uses a mixture of helium and neon gases as the gain medium.
- The He-Ne laser consists of a glass tube filled with the gas mixture, with electrodes at both ends, and mirrors at the ends of the tube, forming an optical cavity. One of the mirrors is partially transparent, allowing some light to escape as the laser output.
- The He-Ne laser produces continuous wave coherent light at a wavelength of 632.8 nm, which is a red-orange color, with a very narrow linewidth of 0.0015 nm.
- The He-Ne laser is a four level laser, meaning that it requires a low pumping energy to achieve population inversion and lasing.
- The He-Ne laser is pumped by an electric discharge, which ionizes the gas mixture and creates a plasma.
- The plasma consists of electrons, helium ions, neon ions, and neutral atoms of both gases.
- The electrons collide with the helium atoms, transferring energy to them and exciting them to higher energy levels.
- The excited helium atoms then collide with the neon atoms, transferring energy to them and exciting them to higher energy levels.
- The excited neon atoms then decay to lower energy levels, emitting photons of various wavelengths, some of which match the lasing wavelength of 632.8 nm.
- The photons of the lasing wavelength are amplified by the stimulated emission of more photons from the excited neon atoms, creating a coherent beam of red-orange light.
- The He-Ne laser can produce a continuous beam of light, because the population inversion is maintained by the constant pumping action and the fast decay of the neon atoms to the ground state.
- The He-Ne laser has applications in metrology, alignment, scanning, and communication.
