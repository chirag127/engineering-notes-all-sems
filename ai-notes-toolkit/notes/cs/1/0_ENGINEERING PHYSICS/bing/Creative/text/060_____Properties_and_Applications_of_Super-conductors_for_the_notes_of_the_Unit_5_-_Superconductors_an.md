### Properties and Applications of Superconductors

- A superconductor is a material that has zero electrical resistance when cooled below a certain critical temperature.
- Superconductors also exhibit the Meissner effect, which means they expel magnetic fields from their interior and create a perfect diamagnetism.
- The critical temperature and the critical magnetic field of a superconductor depend on the type and structure of the material. There are two types of superconductors: conventional and unconventional.
- Conventional superconductors are usually metallic alloys or compounds that have critical temperatures below 30 K. They can be explained by the BCS theory, which states that the electrons in the material form Cooper pairs that are bound by phonon-mediated attraction.
- Unconventional superconductors are usually ceramic or organic materials that have critical temperatures above 30 K. They cannot be explained by the BCS theory, and their exact mechanism is still unknown. Some examples are cuprates, iron-based superconductors, and carbon nanotubes.
- Superconductors have many applications in various fields, such as:
  - Particle accelerators, generators, and electric motors, where superconductors can create powerful electromagnets with low energy loss.
  - Transportation, where superconductors can enable high-speed and efficient maglev trains that levitate above the tracks using magnetic repulsion.
  - Computing, where superconductors can enable faster and more reliable data processing and storage using superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) and Josephson junctions.
  - Medical, where superconductors can enable high-resolution and non-invasive imaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetoencephalography (MEG).
  - Power transmission, where superconductors can enable long-distance and low-loss transmission of electricity using superconducting cables.
