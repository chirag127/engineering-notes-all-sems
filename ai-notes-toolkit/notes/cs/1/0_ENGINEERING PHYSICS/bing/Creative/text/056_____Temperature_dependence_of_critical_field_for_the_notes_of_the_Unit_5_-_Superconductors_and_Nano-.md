### Temperature dependence of critical field for superconductors

- The critical field is the maximum magnetic field that a superconductor can withstand without losing its superconductivity.
- The critical field depends on the temperature of the superconductor, and generally decreases as the temperature increases.
- The temperature dependence of the critical field can be described approximately by the empirical formula:

$$
B_c(T) = B_c(0) \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^2 \right]
$$

where $B_c(0)$ is the critical field at absolute zero temperature, and $T_c$ is the critical temperature of the superconductor.

- The critical field can also be divided into two components: the lower critical field $H_{c1}$ and the upper critical field $H_{c2}$.
- The lower critical field is the minimum magnetic field that can penetrate the superconductor and create vortices of supercurrents. Below this field, the superconductor behaves as a perfect diamagnet and expels all the magnetic flux (Meissner effect).
- The upper critical field is the maximum magnetic field that the superconductor can sustain without destroying the superconducting state. Above this field, the superconductor becomes a normal conductor.
- The lower and upper critical fields also depend on the temperature of the superconductor, and can be expressed by the following formulas:

$$
H_{c1}(T) = H_{c1}(0) \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^2 \right]
$$

$$
H_{c2}(T) = H_{c2}(0) \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right) \right]
$$

where $H_{c1}(0)$ and $H_{c2}(0)$ are the lower and upper critical fields at absolute zero temperature, respectively.

- The temperature dependence of the critical fields can vary for different types of superconductors, such as conventional (low-temperature) superconductors, high-temperature superconductors, and unconventional superconductors.
- For example, some high-temperature superconductors show an anomalous temperature dependence of the upper critical field, with a negative curvature at any temperature, which can be explained by the influence of localization effects in two-dimensional or quasi-two-dimensional systems .
- Some unconventional superconductors, such as heavy-fermion compounds, show a non-monotonic temperature dependence of the lower critical field, with a minimum at a certain temperature, which can be attributed to the presence of multiple superconducting bands or a spin-triplet component in the superconducting wavefunction.
