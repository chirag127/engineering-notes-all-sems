# High temperature superconductors

- High temperature superconductors (HTS) are materials that behave as superconductors at temperatures above 77 K (−196.2 °C; −321.1 °F), the boiling point of liquid nitrogen.
- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and can expel magnetic fields when cooled below a critical temperature (Tc).
- HTS have much higher Tc than conventional superconductors, which are mostly metallic alloys that require cooling with liquid helium to temperatures below 30 K (−243.2 °C; −405.7 °F).
- HTS have many potential applications in fields such as power transmission, energy storage, magnetic levitation, and quantum computing, due to their high efficiency and strong magnetic properties.
- The main class of HTS is copper oxides combined with other metals, especially the rare-earth barium copper oxides (REBCOs) such as yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO).
- The second class of HTS is the iron-based compounds, such as iron pnictides and iron chalcogenides, which have lower Tc but are easier to synthesize and manipulate.
- The mechanism of HTS is still not fully understood, but it is believed to involve the formation of Cooper pairs, which are pairs of electrons that are bound together by an attractive interaction mediated by lattice vibrations or other mechanisms .
- The Cooper pairs can move through the material without scattering, resulting in zero resistance and perfect diamagnetism (the expulsion of magnetic fields).
- HTS are usually characterized by their Tc, their critical current density (Jc), which is the maximum current they can carry without losing superconductivity, and their critical magnetic field (Hc), which is the maximum magnetic field they can withstand without losing superconductivity.
- HTS are also classified by their symmetry, which determines how the Cooper pairs are arranged in momentum space and how they respond to external perturbations. The symmetry can be either s-wave, d-wave, or p-wave, depending on the angular dependence of the pairing function.
- HTS are challenging to fabricate and manipulate, as they are often brittle, sensitive to oxygen content, and require precise doping and annealing conditions. They also suffer from flux pinning, which is the trapping of magnetic vortices in the material that can reduce the current flow and cause dissipation.
- HTS are the subject of intense research and development, as scientists and engineers seek to improve their properties, understand their physics, and explore their applications. Some of the recent breakthroughs and controversies in HTS include:

  - The discovery of superconductivity in hydrogen-rich materials at pressures above 150 GPa and temperatures above 200 K, which may be the highest Tc ever achieved.
  - The observation of a quantum spin liquid state in a copper oxide material, which may be a precursor to HTS and may reveal the origin of the pairing mechanism.
  - The development of high-performance REBCO tapes and wires, which can carry high currents and withstand high magnetic fields, and are suitable for large-scale applications such as power cables and magnets.
  - The debate over the validity and reproducibility of the claimed observation of superconductivity in a carbonaceous sulfur hydride material at ambient pressure and 287 K, which would be a revolutionary finding if confirmed.
