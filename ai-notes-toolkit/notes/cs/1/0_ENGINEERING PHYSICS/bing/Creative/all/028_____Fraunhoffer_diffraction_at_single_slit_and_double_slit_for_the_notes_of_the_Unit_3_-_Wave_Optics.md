# Fraunhofer Diffraction at Single Slit and Double Slit

- Fraunhofer diffraction is a type of diffraction that occurs when plane waves are incident on a diffracting object, and the diffraction pattern is viewed at a sufficiently long distance from the object (in the far-field region), or at the focal plane of a converging lens.
- Fraunhofer diffraction can be analyzed using the Huygens-Fresnel principle, which states that every point of the wavefront in the plane of the diffracting object acts as a secondary source and sends secondary waves in all directions .
- Fraunhofer diffraction can be used to study the shape and size of the diffracting object, such as a slit, a grating, a circular aperture, etc.

## Fraunhofer Diffraction at a Single Slit

- Let a parallel beam of monochromatic light of wavelength `λ` be incident on a slit of width `d` as shown in the figure below. The slit is perpendicular to the plane of the paper and is placed at the origin of the coordinate system.

![Single slit diffraction](https://gkscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Fraunhofer-Diffraction-at-a-Single-Slit.jpg)

- According to the Huygens-Fresnel principle, every point of the slit acts as a secondary source and sends secondary waves in all directions. The secondary waves interfere with each other and form a diffraction pattern on a screen placed at a distance `D` from the slit, where `D` is much larger than `d` and `λ`.
- The intensity of the diffraction pattern at any point on the screen depends on the phase difference between the secondary waves reaching that point. The phase difference in turn depends on the path difference between the secondary waves.
- To find the path difference, we consider a point `P` on the screen at an angle `θ` from the central axis of the slit. We divide the slit into two equal halves, and consider two points `A` and `B` at the center of each half. The path difference between the secondary waves from `A` and `B` is given by `AB sin θ`, where `AB = d/2`.
- Similarly, we can divide each half of the slit into two equal parts, and consider two points `C` and `D` at the center of each part. The path difference between the secondary waves from `C` and `D` is given by `CD sin θ`, where `CD = d/4`.
- We can continue this process of dividing the slit into smaller and smaller parts, and find the path difference between the secondary waves from any two adjacent points on the slit. The path difference is always given by `ds sin θ`, where `ds` is the width of each part.
- The phase difference between the secondary waves from any two adjacent points on the slit is given by `φ = 2π/λ ds sin θ`. The phase difference between the secondary waves from the two ends of the slit is given by `Φ = 2π/λ d sin θ`.
- The amplitude of the resultant wave at `P` is given by the superposition of all the secondary waves from the slit. Using the principle of superposition, we can write the amplitude as `A = A0 sin(Φ/2) / (Φ/2)`, where `A0` is the amplitude of each secondary wave.
- The intensity of the resultant wave at `P` is proportional to the square of the amplitude, i.e., `I = I0 (sin(Φ/2) / (Φ/2))^2`, where `I0` is the intensity of each secondary wave.
- The intensity of the diffraction pattern at `P` depends on the value of `Φ`, which in turn depends on the angle `θ`. The intensity is maximum when `Φ` is zero or a multiple of `2π`, i.e., when `sin θ` is zero or a multiple of `λ/d`. The intensity is minimum when `Φ` is an odd multiple of `π`, i.e., when `sin θ` is an odd multiple of `λ/2d`.
- The central maximum of the diffraction pattern is the brightest and the widest, and the intensity and the width of the maxima decrease as we move away from the center. The minima are equally spaced
