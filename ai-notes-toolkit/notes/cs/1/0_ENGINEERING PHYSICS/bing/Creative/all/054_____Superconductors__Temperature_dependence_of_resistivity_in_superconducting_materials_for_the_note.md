# Superconductors: Temperature dependence of resistivity in superconducting materials

- Superconductivity is a phenomenon in certain metals and ceramics where the resistivity of the material drops to zero below a certain critical temperature known as Tc .
- The resistivity of materials depends on the temperature. The equation ρt = ρ0 [1 + α (T – T0) shows the relation between the temperature and the resistivity for any material .
- In the equation, ρ0 is the resistivity at a standard temperature, ρt is the resistivity at t 0 C, T0 is the reference temperature and α is the temperature coefficient of resistivity.
- For normal metals, α is positive, which means that the resistivity increases with increasing temperature. This is because the thermal vibrations of the atoms in the lattice increase, which scatter the electrons more and reduce their mobility.
- For superconductors, α is negative, which means that the resistivity decreases with increasing temperature until it reaches zero at Tc. This is because the electrons form pairs called Cooper pairs, which can move through the lattice without resistance due to quantum effects.
- The value of Tc depends on the type and structure of the superconducting material. For most industrial superconductors, Tc is below 12 K, which requires cooling with liquid helium. For some ceramic materials, such as cuprates, Tc can reach up to 135 K, which allows cooling with liquid nitrogen .
- The temperature dependence of resistivity in superconducting materials can be plotted as a curve that shows a sharp drop at Tc. The curve can be approximated by the equation ρt = ρn (1 - (T/Tc)^4), where ρn is the normal resistivity above Tc.
