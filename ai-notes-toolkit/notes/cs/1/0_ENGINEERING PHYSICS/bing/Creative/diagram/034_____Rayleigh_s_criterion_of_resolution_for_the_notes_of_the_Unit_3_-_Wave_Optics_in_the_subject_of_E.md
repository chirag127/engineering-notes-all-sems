### Rayleigh's criterion of resolution

- Rayleigh's criterion of resolution is a measure of the ability of an optical system to distinguish two adjacent point sources of light, such as stars or distant objects.
- According to Rayleigh's criterion, two point sources are just resolvable when the central maximum of the diffraction pattern of one source coincides with the first minimum of the diffraction pattern of the other source. See Figure 1.
- Rayleigh's criterion can be applied to circular apertures, such as lenses or telescopes, or to single slits, such as gratings or spectroscopes.
- For circular apertures, the minimum angular separation θ between two resolvable point sources is given by:

  θ = 1.22 λ / D

  where λ is the wavelength of light and D is the diameter of the aperture. This is also called the angular resolution of the optical system.
- For single slits, the minimum angular separation θ between two resolvable point sources is given by:

  θ = λ / b

  where λ is the wavelength of light and b is the width of the slit. This is also called the resolving power of the optical system.
- Rayleigh's criterion is based on the assumption that the human eye can detect a contrast of 26% between the central maximum and the first minimum of the diffraction pattern. However, this may vary depending on the brightness and color of the sources, the background illumination, and the visual acuity of the observer.
- Rayleigh's criterion is a useful approximation for the limit of resolution, but it does not account for other factors that may affect the image quality, such as aberrations, scattering, noise, or atmospheric turbulence.
