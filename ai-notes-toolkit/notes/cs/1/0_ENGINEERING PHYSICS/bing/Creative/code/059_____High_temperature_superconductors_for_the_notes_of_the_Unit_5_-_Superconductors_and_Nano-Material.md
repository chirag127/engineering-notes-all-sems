### High temperature superconductors

- High temperature superconductors (HTS) are materials that behave as superconductors at temperatures above 77 K (-196.2 °C; -321.1 °F), the boiling point of liquid nitrogen.
- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and can expel magnetic fields when cooled below a critical temperature (Tc).
- HTS have higher Tc than conventional superconductors, which usually have Tc below 30 K (-243.2 °C; -405.7 °F).
- HTS have potential applications in power transmission, magnetic levitation, quantum computing, and other fields that require high efficiency and low loss of energy.
- The main class of HTS is copper oxides combined with other metals, especially the rare-earth barium copper oxides (REBCOs) such as yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO).
- The second class of HTS is the iron-based compounds, such as iron pnictides and iron chalcogenides.
- The mechanism of HTS is still not fully understood, but it is believed to involve the formation of Cooper pairs, which are pairs of electrons that are bound together by a quantum phenomenon called the exchange of virtual phonons .
- The Cooper pairs can move through the lattice of the material without scattering, resulting in zero resistance and superconductivity.
- HTS are usually fabricated by thin film deposition, powder-in-tube, or melt-textured growth methods.
- HTS are usually characterized by measuring their Tc, critical current density (Jc), critical magnetic field (Hc), and critical current (Ic).
- HTS are also studied by using spectroscopic techniques, such as scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), and Raman spectroscopy.
- HTS are still facing challenges such as low Jc, high anisotropy, low mechanical strength, and high cost of fabrication and cooling.
- HTS are also subject to controversy, such as the recent claim of room-temperature superconductivity in a hydrogen-rich material, which has not been independently verified or reproduced.
