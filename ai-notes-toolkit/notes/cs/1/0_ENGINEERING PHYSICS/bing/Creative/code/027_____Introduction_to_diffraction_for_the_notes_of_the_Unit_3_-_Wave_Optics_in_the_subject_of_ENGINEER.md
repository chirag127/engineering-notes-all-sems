### Introduction to diffraction

- Diffraction is the process by which a beam of light or other system of waves is spread out as a result of passing through a narrow aperture or across an edge, typically accompanied by interference between the wave forms produced  .
- Diffraction occurs when the size of the obstacle or the slit is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength of the incident wave.
- Diffraction is a manifestation of the wave nature of light and other electromagnetic radiation, as well as sound, X-rays, gamma rays, and particles such as atoms, neutrons, and electrons .
- Diffraction can produce fringes of parallel light and dark or colored bands, which are the result of constructive and destructive interference of the diffracted waves .
- Diffraction can be classified into two types: Fresnel diffraction and Fraunhofer diffraction, depending on the distance between the source, the obstacle or slit, and the screen where the pattern is observed.
- Fresnel diffraction occurs when the source and the screen are at a finite distance from the obstacle or slit, and the wavefronts are spherical or curved.
- Fraunhofer diffraction occurs when the source and the screen are at an infinite distance from the obstacle or slit, and the wavefronts are plane or parallel.
- Diffraction can be analyzed using the Huygens-Fresnel principle, which states that every point on a wavefront can be considered as a source of secondary spherical wavelets, and the resultant wave at any point is the superposition of these wavelets.
- Diffraction can also be explained using the wave equation, which describes the propagation of waves in a medium, and the boundary conditions, which specify the behavior of waves at the edges of the medium or the obstacle.
- Diffraction is an important phenomenon in many fields of science and engineering, such as optics, acoustics, astronomy, microscopy, spectroscopy, crystallography, and quantum mechanics.
