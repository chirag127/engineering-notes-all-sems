### Interference in Uniform and Wedge Shaped Thin Films

Interference is a phenomenon in which two or more waves superimpose to form a resultant wave of greater, lower, or the same amplitude. Interference usually refers to the interaction of waves that are correlated or coherent with each other, either because they come from the same source or because they have the same or nearly the same frequency.

In the context of uniform and wedge shaped thin films, interference occurs when light waves reflect off the top and bottom surfaces of a thin film. The waves that reflect off the bottom surface travel a slightly longer distance than the waves that reflect off the top surface, causing a phase difference between the two waves. This phase difference can result in constructive or destructive interference, depending on the thickness of the film and the wavelength of the light.

In a uniform thin film, the thickness of the film is constant, resulting in a constant phase difference between the two waves. This can result in a series of bright and dark bands, known as interference fringes, when the film is viewed under monochromatic light.

In a wedge shaped thin film, the thickness of the film varies, resulting in a varying phase difference between the two waves. This can result in a more complex interference pattern, with the fringes becoming closer together as the thickness of the film decreases.

Interference in thin films has many practical applications, including the creation of anti-reflective coatings, the measurement of film thickness, and the production of optical filters.

- Interference occurs when light waves reflect off the top and bottom surfaces of a thin film.
