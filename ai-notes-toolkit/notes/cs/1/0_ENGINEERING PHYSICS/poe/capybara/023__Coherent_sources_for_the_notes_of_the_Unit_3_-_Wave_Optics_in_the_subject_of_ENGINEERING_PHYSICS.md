### Coherent sources for the notes of the Unit 3 - Wave Optics in the subject of ENGINEERING PHYSICS

Here are some important points to keep in mind about coherent sources in the context of Wave Optics:

- Coherent sources are a type of source that produce light waves with a constant phase difference between them. This means that the waves maintain a fixed relationship to each other as they propagate through space.

- In Wave Optics, coherent sources are used to study interference and diffraction phenomena. These phenomena arise due to the interaction of waves from coherent sources.

- Some common examples of coherent sources include lasers, LEDs, and sodium vapor lamps. These sources produce light waves that are in phase with each other and have a fixed relationship as they propagate through space.

- Coherent sources are important in many applications of Wave Optics, such as holography, interferometry, and optical communications. In these applications, the fixed relationship between the waves from a coherent source is used to create interference patterns or to transmit information.

- Coherent sources can be classified as either temporal or spatial. Temporal coherence refers to the stability of the phase relationship between waves over time, while spatial coherence refers to the stability of the phase relationship between waves at different points in space.

- The coherence length is a measure of the temporal coherence of a source. It is defined as the distance over which the phase relationship between waves remains stable. A high coherence length indicates a high degree of temporal coherence.

- Spatial coherence is often described in terms of the degree of coherence. This is a measure of the similarity between waves at different points in space. A high degree of coherence indicates a high degree of spatial coherence.

By understanding the concept of coherent sources and their applications, you can gain a better understanding of the behavior of light waves in Wave Optics. Keep these points in mind as you study Unit 3 of Engineering Physics.
