### V-I characteristics of semiconductor diode

- The V-I characteristics of a semiconductor diode is a graph that shows the relationship between the voltage across the diode and the current through it.
- The V-I characteristics can be divided into two regions: forward bias and reverse bias.
- Forward bias is the condition when the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the p-type material and the negative terminal to the n-type material of the diode. In this case, the diode allows the current to flow through it and acts as a closed switch.
- Reverse bias is the condition when the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the n-type material and the negative terminal to the p-type material of the diode. In this case, the diode blocks the current and acts as an open switch.

- The curve has the following features:
  - In the forward bias region, the current increases exponentially with the voltage. The voltage required to initiate the current flow is called the cut-in voltage or the threshold voltage. It is typically 0.7 V for silicon diodes and 0.3 V for germanium diodes.
  - In the reverse bias region, the current is very small and almost constant. This current is called the reverse saturation current or the leakage current. It is due to the thermally generated minority carriers in the diode. It increases with the temperature and decreases with the doping level of the diode.
  - At a certain value of the reverse voltage, called the breakdown voltage, the current increases abruptly. This is because the electric field across the diode becomes so high that it causes the breakdown of the depletion region. The diode can be damaged if the current is not limited by an external resistor. There are two types of breakdown mechanisms: avalanche breakdown and Zener breakdown. Avalanche breakdown occurs when the electric field accelerates the minority carriers to such an extent that they collide with the atoms and create more carriers. Zener breakdown occurs when the electric field is strong enough to pull the electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, creating a large number of carriers.
