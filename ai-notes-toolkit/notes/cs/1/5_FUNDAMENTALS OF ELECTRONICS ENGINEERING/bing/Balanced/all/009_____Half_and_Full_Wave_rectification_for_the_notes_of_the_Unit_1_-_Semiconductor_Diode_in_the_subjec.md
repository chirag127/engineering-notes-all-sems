# Half and Full Wave Rectification

## Introduction

Rectification is the process of converting an alternating current (AC) into a direct current (DC) by using one or more diodes. Diodes are semiconductor devices that allow current to flow in one direction only. Rectification is an important application of diodes in electronics, as many devices require a DC supply to operate.

## Half Wave Rectification

- A half wave rectifier is a circuit that uses a single diode to convert only one half cycle of the AC input into a pulsating DC output.
- The diode is connected in series with the AC source and the load resistor. The diode conducts only when the input voltage is positive, and blocks the negative voltage. Thus, the output voltage across the load resistor is positive for half of the input cycle, and zero for the other half.
- The output frequency of a half wave rectifier is equal to the input frequency. The output voltage has a large ripple factor, which is the ratio of the AC component to the DC component. The ripple factor of a half wave rectifier is 1.21.
- The efficiency of a half wave rectifier is low, as it wastes half of the input power. The efficiency is the ratio of the output power to the input power. The efficiency of a half wave rectifier is 40.6%.
- The advantage of a half wave rectifier is its simplicity and low cost. The disadvantage is its poor performance and high power loss.

## Full Wave Rectification

- A full wave rectifier is a circuit that uses two or four diodes to convert both half cycles of the AC input into a pulsating DC output.
- There are two types of full wave rectifiers: center-tapped and bridge. A center-tapped rectifier uses a transformer with a center-tapped secondary winding and two diodes. A bridge rectifier uses four diodes in a bridge configuration.
- In a center-tapped rectifier, the diodes are connected to the opposite ends of the secondary winding, and the load resistor is connected between the center tap and the common point of the diodes. The diodes conduct alternately, depending on the polarity of the input voltage. Thus, the output voltage across the load resistor is positive for both halves of the input cycle.
- In a bridge rectifier, the diodes are arranged in a diamond shape, with the AC source connected to the opposite corners, and the load resistor connected to the other two corners. The diodes conduct in pairs, depending on the polarity of the input voltage. Thus, the output voltage across the load resistor is positive for both halves of the input cycle.
- The output frequency of a full wave rectifier is twice the input frequency. The output voltage has a smaller ripple factor than a half wave rectifier. The ripple factor of a full wave rectifier is 0.482 for the center-tapped type, and 0.406 for the bridge type.
- The efficiency of a full wave rectifier is higher than a half wave rectifier, as it utilizes both halves of the input power. The efficiency of a full wave rectifier is 81.2% for the center-tapped type, and 81.8% for the bridge type.
- The advantage of a full wave rectifier is its better performance and lower power loss. The disadvantage is its complexity and higher cost.

## Summary

- Rectification is the process of converting AC into DC by using diodes.
- A half wave rectifier uses a single diode to convert only one half cycle of the AC input into a pulsating DC output. It has a low efficiency, a high ripple factor, and a high power loss.
- A full wave rectifier uses two or four diodes to convert both half cycles of the AC input into a pulsating DC output. It has a high efficiency, a low ripple factor, and a low power loss.
- There are two types of full wave rectifiers: center-tapped and bridge. A center-tapped rectifier uses a transformer with a center-tapped secondary winding and two diodes. A bridge rectifier uses four diodes in a bridge configuration.
