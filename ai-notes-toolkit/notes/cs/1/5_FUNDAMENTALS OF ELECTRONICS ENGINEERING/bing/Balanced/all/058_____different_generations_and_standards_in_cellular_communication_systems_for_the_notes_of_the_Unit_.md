# Different Generations and Standards in Cellular Communication Systems

- Cellular communication systems are wireless networks that use radio waves to transmit voice and data signals over a large area.
- Cellular communication systems are divided into different generations based on their technology, features, and standards.
- Each generation has its own advantages and disadvantages, and is compatible with certain devices and applications.

## 1G (First Generation Technology)

- 1G refers to the first generation of wireless cellular technology, which was introduced in the late 1970s and early 1980s.
- 1G systems are voice-oriented analog cellular and cordless telephones, which use frequency division multiple access (FDMA) to allocate channels to users.
- 1G systems have low capacity, poor voice quality, high power consumption, and no security or encryption.
- 1G systems use different standards in different regions, such as AMPS (Advanced Mobile Phone System) in North America, TACS (Total Access Communication System) in Europe, and NMT (Nordic Mobile Telephone) in Scandinavia .

## 2G (Second Generation Cellular Network)

- 2G refers to the second generation of wireless cellular technology, which was commercially launched in 1991 by Radiolinja in Finland using the GSM (Global System for Mobile Communications) standard.
- 2G systems are voice-oriented digital cellular and PCS (Personal Communications Service) systems, which use time division multiple access (TDMA) or code division multiple access (CDMA) to allocate channels to users.
- 2G systems have higher capacity, better voice quality, lower power consumption, and more security and encryption than 1G systems.
- 2G systems also support data services, such as SMS (Short Message Service), MMS (Multimedia Messaging Service), and GPRS (General Packet Radio Service), but at low speeds (up to 384 kbps).
- 2G systems use different standards in different regions, such as GSM, D-AMPS (Digital AMPS), IS-95 (Interim Standard 95), and PDC (Personal Digital Cellular)   .

## 3G (Third Generation Technology)

- 3G refers to the third generation of wireless cellular technology, which was commercially launched in 2001 by NTT DoCoMo in Japan using the W-CDMA (Wideband CDMA) standard.
- 3G systems are data-oriented cellular and wireless broadband systems, which use CDMA or orthogonal frequency division multiple access (OFDMA) to allocate channels to users.
- 3G systems have higher capacity, better voice quality, lower power consumption, and more security and encryption than 2G systems.
- 3G systems also support data services, such as email, web browsing, video streaming, and video calling, at high speeds (up to 42 Mbps).
- 3G systems use a common standard, called IMT-2000 (International Mobile Telecommunications-2000), which has different variants, such as W-CDMA, CDMA2000, TD-SCDMA (Time Division Synchronous CDMA), and WiMAX (Worldwide Interoperability for Microwave Access)   .

## 4G (Fourth Generation Technology)

- 4G refers to the fourth generation of wireless cellular technology, which was commercially launched in 2009 by TeliaSonera in Norway and Sweden using the LTE (Long Term Evolution) standard.
- 4G systems are data-oriented cellular and wireless broadband systems, which use OFDMA or single carrier frequency division multiple access (SC-FDMA) to allocate channels to users.
- 4G systems have higher capacity, better voice quality, lower power consumption, and more security and encryption than 3G systems.
- 4G systems also support data services, such as email, web browsing, video streaming, and video calling, at very high speeds (up to 1 Gbps).
- 4G systems use a common standard, called IMT-Advanced (International Mobile Telecommunications-Advanced), which has two variants, namely LTE and LTE-Advanced   .
