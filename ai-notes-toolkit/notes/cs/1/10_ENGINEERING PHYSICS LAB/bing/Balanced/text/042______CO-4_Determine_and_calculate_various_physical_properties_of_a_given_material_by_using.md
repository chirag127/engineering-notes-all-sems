Physical properties are the traits a material has before it is used, such as colour, hardness, density, etc. They can be observed or measured without changing the composition of the material. Physical properties are important for selecting and designing materials for different applications. Some common physical properties of materials are:

- Density: The mass per unit volume of a material. It is a measure of how compact or heavy a material is. Density can be calculated by dividing the mass by the volume of a material. The unit of density is kg/m^3 or g/cm^3. Density can affect the strength, durability and weight of a material. For example, metals have high density and are strong and heavy, while plastics have low density and are light and flexible.
- Specific gravity: The ratio of the density of a material to the density of a reference substance, usually water. It is a dimensionless quantity that indicates how much heavier or lighter a material is than water. Specific gravity can be calculated by dividing the density of a material by the density of water (1 g/cm^3 or 1000 kg/m^3). Specific gravity can affect the buoyancy and flotation of a material. For example, materials with specific gravity less than 1 can float on water, while materials with specific gravity greater than 1 can sink in water.
- State of matter: The physical form of a material, such as solid, liquid or gas. It depends on the temperature and pressure of the material. State of matter can affect the shape, volume and compressibility of a material. For example, solids have fixed shape and volume and are difficult to compress, while liquids have variable shape and fixed volume and are slightly compressible, and gases have variable shape and volume and are highly compressible.
- Melting point: The temperature at which a solid material changes into a liquid state. It is a measure of how much heat energy is required to break the bonds between the atoms or molecules of a solid material. Melting point can be determined by heating a solid material until it melts and measuring the temperature. The unit of melting point is °C or K. Melting point can affect the stability and resistance of a material to heat. For example, metals have high melting points and can withstand high temperatures, while plastics have low melting points and can melt or deform easily under heat.
- Boiling point: The temperature at which a liquid material changes into a gas state. It is a measure of how much heat energy is required to overcome the intermolecular forces of attraction between the molecules of a liquid material. Boiling point can be determined by heating a liquid material until it boils and measuring the temperature. The unit of boiling point is °C or K. Boiling point can affect the volatility and evaporation of a material. For example, water has a moderate boiling point and can evaporate at normal atmospheric pressure, while mercury has a high boiling point and can remain liquid at high temperatures.
- Thermal conductivity: The ability of a material to transfer heat through itself. It is a measure of how fast heat can flow through a material. Thermal conductivity can be calculated by dividing the heat flux (the amount of heat transferred per unit area per unit time) by the temperature gradient (the change in temperature per unit length) of a material. The unit of thermal conductivity is W/mK or J/smK. Thermal conductivity can affect the heat dissipation and insulation of a material. For example, metals have high thermal conductivity and can conduct heat quickly and efficiently, while plastics have low thermal conductivity and can retain heat and prevent heat loss.
- Electrical conductivity: The ability of a material to allow electric current to pass through itself. It is a measure of how easily electrons can move through a material. Electrical conductivity can be calculated by dividing the electric current by the potential difference (the voltage) across a material. The unit of electrical conductivity is S/m or A/Vm. Electrical conductivity can affect the electrical performance and resistance of a material. For example, metals have high electrical conductivity and can carry electric current easily and with low resistance, while plastics have low electrical conductivity and can block electric current and have high resistance.
- Optical properties: The properties of a material that affect how it interacts with light, such as reflection, refraction, absorption, transmission, etc. They depend on the wavelength and frequency of light and the structure and composition of the material. Optical properties can be measured by using various instruments and methods, such as spectrophotometers, polarimeters, refractometers, etc. Optical properties can affect the appearance, colour, transparency and visibility of a material
