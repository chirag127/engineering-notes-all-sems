#### Experiments

- An experiment is a scientific method of testing a hypothesis or a research question by manipulating one or more variables and observing their effects on another variable.
- The purpose of an experiment is to establish a causal relationship between the independent variable (the factor that is changed by the experimenter) and the dependent variable (the factor that is measured or observed as the outcome of the experiment).
- Experiments can be classified into two types: controlled experiments and natural experiments.
  - A controlled experiment is one in which the experimenter has full control over all the variables and can randomly assign the participants or units to different groups or conditions. This ensures that the only difference between the groups is the independent variable, and any other factors are kept constant or balanced. A controlled experiment can be conducted in a laboratory or in the field, depending on the nature of the research question and the availability of resources.
  - A natural experiment is one in which the experimenter cannot manipulate the independent variable, but instead observes the effects of a naturally occurring event or situation on the dependent variable. A natural experiment can take advantage of a historical event, a policy change, a natural disaster, or a geographical variation that creates different groups or conditions. A natural experiment can provide evidence of causality, but it may also suffer from confounding factors or selection bias that affect the validity of the results.
- The design of an experiment depends on the research question, the hypothesis, the independent and dependent variables, and the possible confounding variables. Some common experimental designs are:
  - Randomized controlled trial (RCT): A type of controlled experiment in which the participants or units are randomly assigned to either a treatment group (that receives the intervention or manipulation of the independent variable) or a control group (that receives no intervention or a placebo). The RCT is considered the gold standard of experimental design, as it minimizes the risk of confounding factors and selection bias, and allows for the estimation of the causal effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable.
  - Pretest-posttest design: A type of controlled experiment in which the participants or units are measured on the dependent variable before (pretest) and after (posttest) the intervention or manipulation of the independent variable. The pretest-posttest design can be used to compare the changes in the dependent variable between the treatment and control groups, or within the same group over time. The pretest-posttest design can also be combined with randomization to form a randomized pretest-posttest design, which increases the internal validity of the experiment.
  - Factorial design: A type of controlled experiment in which the experimenter manipulates two or more independent variables simultaneously and observes their effects on the dependent variable. The factorial design can be used to test the main effects of each independent variable, as well as the interaction effects between them. The factorial design can also be combined with randomization to form a randomized factorial design, which reduces the confounding effects of other variables.
  - Quasi-experimental design: A type of natural experiment in which the experimenter does not have full control over the assignment of the participants or units to different groups or conditions, but instead uses some criteria or method to create the groups or conditions. The quasi-experimental design can be used to approximate the causal effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable, but it may also be affected by confounding factors or selection bias that limit the internal validity of the experiment. Some examples of quasi-experimental designs are:
    - Matching: A method of creating comparable groups or conditions by matching the participants or units on one or more variables that are related to the dependent variable or the independent variable. Matching can be used to reduce the confounding effects of these variables and increase the similarity between the groups or conditions. Matching can be done by using exact matching, propensity score matching, or other statistical techniques.
    - Regression discontinuity design (RDD): A method of creating comparable groups or conditions by using a cutoff point or a threshold on a continuous variable that determines the assignment of the participants or units to the treatment or control group. The RDD can be used to estimate the causal effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable by comparing the outcomes of the participants or units that are just above and just below the cutoff point. The RDD assumes that the only difference between the groups or conditions is the independent variable, and that there is no discontinuity in the dependent variable or other variables at the cutoff point.
    - Difference-in-differences (DID): A method of estimating the causal effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable by comparing the changes in the outcomes of the treatment and control groups or conditions before and after the intervention or manipulation of the independent variable.
