Physical properties are the traits a material has before it is used, such as colour, hardness, density, etc. They can be observed or measured without changing the composition of the material . Physical properties are important for selecting and designing materials for different applications.

Some of the common physical properties of materials are:

- Density: The mass per unit volume of a material. It is a measure of how compact or heavy a material is. Density can be calculated by dividing the mass by the volume.
- Specific gravity: The ratio of the density of a material to the density of a reference substance, usually water. It is a measure of how heavy a material is compared to water. Specific gravity can be calculated by dividing the density of the material by the density of water.
- State: The physical form of a material, such as solid, liquid or gas. The state of a material depends on the temperature and pressure. Some materials can change their state by heating or cooling, such as water, which can be solid (ice), liquid or gas (steam).
- Melting point: The temperature at which a solid material changes to a liquid state. It is a measure of how much heat energy is needed to break the bonds between the atoms or molecules of the material.
- Boiling point: The temperature at which a liquid material changes to a gas state. It is a measure of how much heat energy is needed to overcome the intermolecular forces of the material.
- Thermal conductivity: The ability of a material to transfer heat through itself. It is a measure of how fast heat can flow through a material. Thermal conductivity can be calculated by dividing the heat flux (the amount of heat transferred per unit area per unit time) by the temperature gradient (the change in temperature per unit length) across the material.
- Electrical conductivity: The ability of a material to allow electric current to flow through itself. It is a measure of how easily electrons can move through a material. Electrical conductivity can be calculated by dividing the electric current by the electric potential difference (the voltage) across the material.
- Magnetic properties: The ability of a material to be attracted or repelled by a magnet, or to become a magnet itself. There are three types of magnetic materials: ferromagnetic, paramagnetic and diamagnetic. Ferromagnetic materials are strongly attracted by a magnet and can retain their magnetism even when the external magnetic field is removed, such as iron, nickel and cobalt. Paramagnetic materials are weakly attracted by a magnet and lose their magnetism when the external magnetic field is removed, such as aluminium, platinum and oxygen. Diamagnetic materials are weakly repelled by a magnet and do not become magnetized, such as copper, silver and water.
- Optical properties: The ability of a material to interact with light, such as reflection, refraction, absorption, transmission, scattering, polarization, etc. Optical properties depend on the wavelength and frequency of the light, as well as the structure and composition of the material. Some examples of optical properties are: colour, transparency, opacity, shininess, etc.
- Mechanical properties: The ability of a material to resist or deform under applied forces, such as stress, strain, elasticity, plasticity, hardness, toughness, etc. Mechanical properties are important for determining the strength and durability of a material. Some examples of mechanical properties are: Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, yield strength, ultimate strength, fracture toughness, etc.

To determine and calculate the physical properties of a given material, various methods and instruments can be used, such as:

- Mass balance: A device that measures the mass of a material by comparing it with a known mass or a standard weight.
- Vernier caliper: A device that measures the length, width, height or diameter of a material by using a scale and a sliding jaw.
- Hydrometer: A device that measures the specific gravity of a liquid by using a graduated glass tube that floats in the liquid and indicates the density at the surface level.
- Thermometer: A device that measures the temperature of a material by using a liquid (such as mercury or alcohol) that expands or contracts with the change in temperature.
