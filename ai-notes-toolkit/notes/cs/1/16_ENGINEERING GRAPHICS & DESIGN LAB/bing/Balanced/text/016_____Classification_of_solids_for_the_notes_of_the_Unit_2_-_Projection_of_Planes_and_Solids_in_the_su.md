### Classification of Solids

- A solid is an object that has three dimensions, i.e., length, breadth and height.
- Solids can be classified into two types: **crystalline solids** and **amorphous solids**.
- Crystalline solids are solids that have a regular and orderly arrangement of atoms, ions or molecules in a three-dimensional lattice. Crystalline solids have sharp melting points, definite shapes and angles, and can be cleaved along specific planes.
- Amorphous solids are solids that have a random and irregular arrangement of atoms, ions or molecules in a three-dimensional structure. Amorphous solids have no sharp melting points, no definite shapes and angles, and break into irregular pieces when cut.
- Crystalline solids can be further classified into four types based on the nature of the bonding between the constituent particles. These are: **ionic solids**, **metallic solids**, **covalent solids**, and **molecular solids**.
- Ionic solids are solids that consist of cations and anions held together by strong electrostatic forces. Ionic solids have high melting points, low electrical conductivity in solid state, and are hard and brittle. Examples of ionic solids are sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, and calcium carbonate.
- Metallic solids are solids that consist of metal atoms held together by metallic bonds. Metallic solids have high melting points, high electrical and thermal conductivity, and are malleable and ductile. Examples of metallic solids are iron, copper, and gold.
- Covalent solids are solids that consist of atoms held together by covalent bonds. Covalent solids have very high melting points, low or no electrical conductivity, and are hard and rigid. Examples of covalent solids are diamond, graphite, and silicon.
- Molecular solids are solids that consist of molecules held together by intermolecular forces. Molecular solids have low melting points, low or no electrical conductivity, and are soft and flexible. Examples of molecular solids are ice, sugar, and wax.
- In engineering graphics, solids are represented by their projections on two perpendicular planes, called the horizontal plane (HP) and the vertical plane (VP) . The projections of solids are obtained by drawing the outlines of the faces of the solids on the planes .
- The projections of solids can be drawn by different methods, such as the first angle projection, the third angle projection, the change of position method, and the auxiliary plane method .
- The projections of solids can also be classified into three types based on the orientation of the axis of the solid with respect to the planes . These are: **simple position**, **axis inclined to one plane and parallel to the other**, and **axis inclined to both the planes** .
- The projections of solids can be used to study the shape, size, and position of the solids in different views . The projections of solids can also be used to draw the sections of the solids by cutting them with planes.
- The sections of solids can show the true shape and size of the cross-section of the solid, as well as the internal details of the solid. The sections of solids can be drawn by planes that are either inclined to one plane or perpendicular to both the planes.
- The projections and sections of solids can be modeled by using solid modeling techniques, such as boundary representation, constructive solid geometry, and implicit surfaces. Solid modeling is a set of principles for mathematical and computer modeling of three-dimensional shapes. Solid modeling can be used for various applications, such as computer-aided design, computer-aided manufacturing, computer-aided engineering, and computer graphics.
