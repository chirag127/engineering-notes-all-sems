# Dimensioning

- Dimensioning is the process of adding measurements and other information to a drawing to specify the size, shape, location, and features of an object.
- Dimensioning is essential for communicating the design intent and ensuring the accuracy and quality of the product.
- Dimensioning follows certain rules and conventions to avoid ambiguity and confusion.
- Some of the basic rules and conventions of dimensioning are:

  - Dimensions should be placed outside the view, unless there is not enough space or clarity.
  - Dimensions should be aligned with the dimension lines and placed above, below, or beside them.
  - Dimensions should be placed between extension lines, which are thin lines that extend from the object to the dimension line.
  - Dimensions should not cross each other or any other lines in the drawing.
  - Dimensions should be given in millimeters (mm) or meters (m) for metric units, and in inches (in) or feet (ft) for imperial units.
  - Dimensions should be given to the nearest 0.1 mm for metric units, and to the nearest 1/16 in for imperial units.
  - Dimensions should be given only once for each feature, and should not be duplicated or implied by other dimensions.
  - Dimensions should be placed in the most descriptive view, and should avoid foreshortened or hidden features.
  - Dimensions should be grouped and arranged in a logical order, and should follow the general-to-specific principle.
  - Dimensions should be placed parallel to the feature being dimensioned, and should follow the direction of projection.

- Some of the common types of dimensions are:

  - Linear dimensions: These are used to specify the length, width, height, or depth of an object or a feature. They are represented by a dimension line with arrowheads at both ends, and a numerical value in the middle.
  - Angular dimensions: These are used to specify the angle between two lines, planes, or surfaces. They are represented by an arc with arrowheads at both ends, and a numerical value in the center of the arc.
  - Radial dimensions: These are used to specify the radius of a circle or an arc. They are represented by a leader line with an arrowhead at one end, and a numerical value with the letter R at the other end.
  - Diameter dimensions: These are used to specify the diameter of a circle or an arc. They are represented by a leader line with an arrowhead at one end, and a numerical value with the symbol Ø at the other end.
  - Location dimensions: These are used to specify the distance or position of a feature from a reference point, line, or plane. They are represented by a dimension line with arrowheads at both ends, and a numerical value in the middle.
  - Chamfer dimensions: These are used to specify the size and angle of a chamfer, which is a beveled edge or corner. They are represented by a leader line with an arrowhead at one end, and a numerical value with the symbol X at the other end, followed by the chamfer angle in degrees.
  - Fillet dimensions: These are used to specify the radius of a fillet, which is a curved transition between two surfaces. They are represented by a leader line with an arrowhead at one end, and a numerical value with the letter R at the other end.
  - Tolerance dimensions: These are used to specify the allowable variation or deviation from the nominal or exact dimension. They are represented by a numerical value with a plus-minus symbol (+/-) or a limit dimension (upper and lower values separated by a dash).
