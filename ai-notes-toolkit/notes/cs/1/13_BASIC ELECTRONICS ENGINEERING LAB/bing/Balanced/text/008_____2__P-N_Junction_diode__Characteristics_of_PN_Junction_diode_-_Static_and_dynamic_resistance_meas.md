### 2. P-N Junction diode: Characteristics of PN Junction diode - Static and dynamic resistance measurement from graph.

- A P-N junction diode is a semiconductor device that consists of a P-type and an N-type region joined together by a junction.
- The P-type region has a high concentration of holes (positive charge carriers) and the N-type region has a high concentration of electrons (negative charge carriers).
- The junction forms a depletion region, where the charge carriers are depleted due to diffusion and recombination, creating an electric field that opposes further diffusion.
- The depletion region acts as a potential barrier that prevents the flow of current in the absence of an external voltage.
- When an external voltage is applied across the diode, the current flow depends on the polarity of the voltage.
- If the P-type region is connected to the positive terminal of the voltage source and the N-type region is connected to the negative terminal, the diode is said to be forward biased.
- In forward bias, the external voltage reduces the potential barrier and allows the charge carriers to cross the junction, creating a current flow.
- The current increases exponentially with the increase in voltage, as shown by the forward characteristic curve of the diode.
- The forward characteristic curve can be plotted by measuring the current and voltage across the diode using a circuit like this:

![Forward bias circuit](https://www.electronics-tutorials.ws/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/diode-dio2.gif)

- The static resistance of the diode in forward bias is defined as the ratio of the applied voltage to the current at a given point on the curve.
- The static resistance can be calculated by drawing a straight line from the origin to the point of interest on the curve and finding its slope.
- The static resistance decreases with the increase in voltage, as the curve becomes steeper.
- The dynamic resistance of the diode in forward bias is defined as the ratio of the small change in voltage to the corresponding small change in current at a given point on the curve.
- The dynamic resistance can be calculated by drawing a tangent to the curve at the point of interest and finding its slope.
- The dynamic resistance is smaller than the static resistance and also decreases with the increase in voltage, as the curve becomes more curved.

- If the P-type region is connected to the negative terminal of the voltage source and the N-type region is connected to the positive terminal, the diode is said to be reverse biased.
- In reverse bias, the external voltage increases the potential barrier and prevents the charge carriers from crossing the junction, creating a negligible current flow.
- The current remains almost constant with the increase in voltage, as shown by the reverse characteristic curve of the diode.
- The reverse characteristic curve can be plotted by measuring the current and voltage across the diode using a circuit like this:

![Reverse bias circuit](https://www.electronics-tutorials.ws/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/diode-dio3.gif)

- The static resistance of the diode in reverse bias is defined as the ratio of the applied voltage to the current at a given point on the curve.
- The static resistance can be calculated by drawing a straight line from the origin to the point of interest on the curve and finding its slope.
- The static resistance increases with the increase in voltage, as the curve becomes flatter.
- The dynamic resistance of the diode in reverse bias is defined as the ratio of the small change in voltage to the corresponding small change in current at a given point on the curve.
- The dynamic resistance can be calculated by drawing a tangent to the curve at the point of interest and finding its slope.
- The dynamic resistance is larger than the static resistance and also increases with the increase in voltage, as the curve becomes less curved.

- The graph also shows the cut-in voltage or the threshold voltage, which is the minimum voltage required to make the diode conduct in forward bias.
- The cut-in voltage depends on the type of the diode and the temperature, and is typically around 0.7 V for silicon diodes and 0.3 V for germanium diodes.
