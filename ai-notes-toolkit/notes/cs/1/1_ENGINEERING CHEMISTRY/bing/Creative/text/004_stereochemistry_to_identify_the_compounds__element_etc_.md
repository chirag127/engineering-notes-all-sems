# Stereochemistry

Stereochemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies the spatial arrangement of atoms and molecules in three-dimensional space. Stereochemistry is important for understanding the properties, reactions, and interactions of different chemical species.

## Stereoisomers

Stereoisomers are molecules that have the same molecular formula and the same connectivity of atoms, but differ in the orientation of their atoms in space. Stereoisomers can be classified into two main types: **configurational isomers** and **conformational isomers**.

### Configurational isomers

Configurational isomers are stereoisomers that cannot be interconverted by rotation around single bonds. They require breaking and reforming of bonds to change from one isomer to another. Configurational isomers can be further divided into two subtypes: **geometric isomers** and **optical isomers**.

#### Geometric isomers

Geometric isomers are configurational isomers that differ in the arrangement of atoms or groups around a double bond or a ring. Geometric isomers are also called **cis-trans isomers** or **E-Z isomers**. For example, 2-butene has two geometric isomers: cis-2-butene and trans-2-butene.

![2-butene](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Cis-2-butene-2D-skeletal.png/220px-Cis-2-butene-2D-skeletal.png) ![2-butene](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Trans-2-butene-2D-skeletal.png/220px-Trans-2-butene-2D-skeletal.png)

In cis-2-butene, the two methyl groups are on the same side of the double bond, while in trans-2-butene, they are on opposite sides. Geometric isomers have different physical and chemical properties, such as melting point, boiling point, polarity, and reactivity.

#### Optical isomers

Optical isomers are configurational isomers that differ in the arrangement of atoms or groups around a chiral center. A chiral center is an atom, usually carbon, that is bonded to four different atoms or groups. Optical isomers are also called **enantiomers** or **mirror-image isomers**. For example, 2-butanol has two optical isomers: (R)-2-butanol and (S)-2-butanol.

![2-butanol](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/R-2-butanol-2D-skeletal.png/220px-R-2-butanol-2D-skeletal.png) ![2-butanol](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/S-2-butanol-2D-skeletal.png/220px-S-2-butanol-2D-skeletal.png)

In (R)-2-butanol, the hydroxyl group is on the right of the chiral center, while in (S)-2-butanol, it is on the left. Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images of each other, meaning that they cannot be overlaid by rotating or flipping. Optical isomers have the same physical and chemical properties, except for their interaction with plane-polarized light. Optical isomers can rotate the plane of polarized light in opposite directions, either clockwise (dextrorotatory) or counterclockwise (levorotatory). Optical isomers are denoted by the prefixes (+) or (-), or by the symbols (R) or (S), depending on the convention used.

### Conformational isomers

Conformational isomers are stereoisomers that can be interconverted by rotation around single bonds. They do not require breaking and reforming of bonds to change from one isomer to another. Conformational isomers are also called **rotamers** or **conformers**. For example, ethane has two conformational isomers: staggered ethane and eclipsed ethane.
