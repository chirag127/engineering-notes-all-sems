# Process of Determination of Hardness and Alkalinity

## Alkalinity
- Alkalinity is the measure of the capacity of water to neutralize acids.
- Alkalinity is mainly due to the presence of bicarbonates, carbonates, and hydroxides of calcium, magnesium, and other metals in water.
- Alkalinity is expressed as milligrams per liter of equivalent calcium carbonate (CaCO3).

### Measurement of Alkalinity
- Alkalinity measurements are based on titration of a water sample to a designated pH using diluted sulfuric acid (H2SO4) as a titrant and a pH meter for measuring the pH.
- The titration curve shows the change in pH as the acid is added to the water sample.
- The inflection points of the curve indicate the equivalence points of the titration, where the amount of acid added is equal to the amount of alkalinity present in the water sample.
- The alkalinity can be calculated from the volume of acid used and its concentration.
- The alkalinity can be classified into different types depending on the pH at the equivalence point: phenolphthalein alkalinity (pH 8.3), total alkalinity (pH 4.5), and methyl orange alkalinity (pH 3.7).
- The alkalinity can also be calculated from the pH and the concentrations of bicarbonate, carbonate, and hydroxide ions in the water sample using the following equations:

  - pH = pKa1 + log([HCO3-]/[H2CO3])
  - pH = pKa2 + log([CO3-2]/[HCO3-])
  - pH = 14 + log([OH-]/[H+])

### Tips for Measurement of Alkalinity
- Use a clean and calibrated pH meter and a standard acid solution.
- Use a suitable indicator or a pH meter to detect the end point of the titration.
- Use a magnetic stirrer or a stirring rod to mix the water sample and the acid during the titration.
- Record the initial and final volumes of the acid solution and the initial and final pH values of the water sample.
- Repeat the titration until consistent results are obtained.

### Interpretation of Alkalinity
- Alkalinity is an important parameter for water quality and treatment, as it affects the pH, corrosion, and precipitation of metals in water.
- Alkalinity can indicate the presence of natural or anthropogenic sources of carbonates and bicarbonates in water, such as limestone, dolomite, or industrial effluents.
- Alkalinity can also indicate the buffering capacity of water, which is the ability to resist changes in pH when acids or bases are added.
- The recommended range of alkalinity for drinking water is 20 to 200 mg/L as CaCO3.

## Hardness
- Hardness is the measure of the concentration of calcium and magnesium ions in water.
- Hardness is mainly due to the dissolution of minerals such as limestone, dolomite, gypsum, and fluorite in water.
- Hardness is expressed as milligrams per liter of equivalent calcium carbonate (CaCO3).

### Measurement of Hardness
- Hardness measurements are based on titration of a water sample with a standard solution of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) in the presence of a metal indicator such as Eriochrome Black T or Calmagite.
- The titration curve shows the change in color of the indicator as the EDTA is added to the water sample.
- The end point of the titration is when the color of the indicator changes from red to blue, indicating that all the calcium and magnesium ions have been complexed by the EDTA.
- The hardness can be calculated from the volume of EDTA used and its concentration.
- The hardness can be classified into different types depending on the source of the calcium and magnesium ions: carbonate hardness (due to bicarbonates and carbonates) and noncarbonate hardness (due to sulfates, chlorides, nitrates, and other anions).
