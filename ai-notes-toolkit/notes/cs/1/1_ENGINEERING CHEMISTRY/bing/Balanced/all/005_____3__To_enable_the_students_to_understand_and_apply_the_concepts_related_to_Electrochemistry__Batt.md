# 3. To enable the students to understand and apply the concepts related to Electrochemistry, Batteries, Corrosion

- Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies the relationship between electricity and chemical reactions.
- Electrochemical reactions involve the transfer of electrons between substances, either through a direct contact or through an external circuit.
- Electrochemical cells are devices that convert chemical energy into electrical energy, or vice versa, by using electrochemical reactions.
- Electrochemical cells can be classified into two types: galvanic cells and electrolytic cells.
- Galvanic cells are electrochemical cells that produce electrical energy from spontaneous chemical reactions. They consist of two electrodes, called the anode and the cathode, immersed in an electrolyte solution, and connected by a wire or a salt bridge. The anode is the electrode where oxidation (loss of electrons) occurs, and the cathode is the electrode where reduction (gain of electrons) occurs. The flow of electrons from the anode to the cathode generates an electric current. The voltage or potential difference between the electrodes is called the cell potential or electromotive force (emf).
- Electrolytic cells are electrochemical cells that consume electrical energy to drive non-spontaneous chemical reactions. They also consist of two electrodes, called the anode and the cathode, immersed in an electrolyte solution, and connected to an external power source. The anode is the electrode where oxidation (loss of electrons) occurs, and the cathode is the electrode where reduction (gain of electrons) occurs. The external power source provides the electric current that forces the electrons to flow from the anode to the cathode. The voltage or potential difference between the electrodes is called the cell potential or electromotive force (emf).
- Batteries are devices that consist of one or more electrochemical cells connected in series or parallel to provide a source of electrical energy. Batteries can be classified into two types: primary batteries and secondary batteries.
- Primary batteries are batteries that cannot be recharged, and are discarded after their chemical energy is exhausted. They are also called disposable batteries. Examples of primary batteries are zinc-carbon batteries, alkaline batteries, lithium batteries, etc.
- Secondary batteries are batteries that can be recharged, and are reused after their chemical energy is restored. They are also called rechargeable batteries. Examples of secondary batteries are lead-acid batteries, nickel-cadmium batteries, lithium-ion batteries, etc.
- Corrosion is the process of deterioration of metals or alloys due to their reaction with the environment. Corrosion can be classified into two types: dry corrosion and wet corrosion.
- Dry corrosion is the corrosion that occurs when metals or alloys react with gases, such as oxygen, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, etc. Dry corrosion can result in the formation of oxides, carbonates, sulfides, etc. on the surface of the metal or alloy. Dry corrosion can be prevented or reduced by applying protective coatings, such as paints, varnishes, etc., or by alloying the metal with other elements, such as chromium, nickel, etc.
- Wet corrosion is the corrosion that occurs when metals or alloys react with liquids, such as water, acids, bases, salts, etc. Wet corrosion can result in the formation of hydroxides, sulfates, chlorides, etc. on the surface of the metal or alloy. Wet corrosion can be prevented or reduced by applying protective coatings, such as paints, varnishes, etc., or by using cathodic protection, which is the technique of making the metal or alloy the cathode of an electrochemical cell, and supplying electrons to it from an external source, such as a sacrificial anode or an impressed current.
