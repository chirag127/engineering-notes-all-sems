# Isomerism, Chiral Drugs

- Isomerism is the phenomenon of having two or more compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures or arrangements of atoms.
- Isomers can be classified into two main types: constitutional isomers and stereoisomers.
- Constitutional isomers have different connectivity of atoms, meaning they differ in the order or sequence of bonds. For example, butane and isobutane are constitutional isomers with the same molecular formula C4H10 but different structures.
- Stereoisomers have the same connectivity of atoms, meaning they have the same order or sequence of bonds, but differ in the spatial orientation of atoms or groups. For example, cis-2-butene and trans-2-butene are stereoisomers with the same molecular formula C4H8 and the same connectivity, but different geometries around the double bond.
- Stereoisomers can be further divided into two subtypes: enantiomers and diastereomers.
- Enantiomers are stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. They have the same physical and chemical properties, except for their interaction with plane-polarized light and other chiral molecules. For example, L-alanine and D-alanine are enantiomers with the same molecular formula C3H7NO2 and the same connectivity, but different configurations at the chiral carbon atom.
- Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other. They have different physical and chemical properties, and may have different biological activities. For example, thalidomide and its R-enantiomer are diastereomers with the same molecular formula C13H10N2O4 and the same connectivity, but different configurations at two chiral carbon atoms.
- Chiral drugs are drugs that contain one or more chiral centers, meaning they have at least one carbon atom that is bonded to four different atoms or groups. Chiral drugs can exist as enantiomers or diastereomers, and may have different pharmacological effects, metabolism, toxicity, and side effects depending on their stereochemistry.
- For example, ibuprofen is a chiral drug that exists as a racemic mixture of two enantiomers, S-ibuprofen and R-ibuprofen. S-ibuprofen is the active form that inhibits the enzyme cyclooxygenase and reduces inflammation and pain, while R-ibuprofen is inactive and is converted to S-ibuprofen in the body. Therefore, only the S-enantiomer is responsible for the therapeutic effect of ibuprofen, while the R-enantiomer may contribute to the adverse effects or drug interactions.
- Another example is warfarin, a chiral drug that exists as a pair of diastereomers, R-warfarin and S-warfarin. S-warfarin is more potent and more selective than R-warfarin in inhibiting the enzyme vitamin K epoxide reductase and preventing blood clotting. However, S-warfarin is also more susceptible to drug interactions and genetic variations that affect its metabolism and clearance. Therefore, the dose and response of warfarin may vary depending on the ratio and availability of the two diastereomers.
