# Prevention and Control of Corrosion

Corrosion is the deterioration of a metal or its properties due to a chemical or electrochemical reaction with its environment. Corrosion can cause loss of material, strength, functionality, and aesthetics of metal structures and components. Corrosion can also pose safety and environmental hazards, such as leaks, fires, explosions, and pollution.

There are various methods to prevent or control corrosion, depending on the type, mechanism, and extent of corrosion, as well as the cost, availability, and feasibility of the methods. Some of the common methods are:

- **Material selection**: Choosing a metal or alloy that is resistant to corrosion in a given environment, or using a non-metallic material instead. For example, stainless steel, titanium, and aluminum are more corrosion-resistant than carbon steel in many environments. Plastics, ceramics, and composites are non-metallic materials that can be used in corrosive environments.
- **Coating**: Applying a protective layer of a different material on the surface of the metal to isolate it from the corrosive environment. The coating can be metallic, such as zinc, nickel, or chromium, or non-metallic, such as paint, epoxy, or rubber. The coating should be uniform, adherent, and impermeable to the corrosive agents. The coating can also provide aesthetic or functional benefits, such as color, texture, or lubricity.
- **Cathodic protection**: Applying an external electric current or a sacrificial anode to the metal to make it the cathode of an electrochemical cell, and thus prevent it from corroding. The sacrificial anode is a metal that is more easily oxidized than the metal to be protected, and thus corrodes preferentially, sparing the metal. For example, zinc or magnesium anodes are used to protect steel structures in seawater or soil. The external electric current is supplied by a power source or a rectifier, and is adjusted to match the corrosion rate of the metal. For example, pipelines, storage tanks, and ship hulls are often cathodically protected by impressed current systems.
- **Anodic protection**: Applying an external electric current or a passive film to the metal to make it the anode of an electrochemical cell, and thus prevent it from corroding. The passive film is a thin layer of oxide or other compound that forms on the surface of the metal and reduces its reactivity. The passive film can be formed naturally or artificially, by chemical or electrochemical means. The external electric current is supplied by a power source or a rectifier, and is adjusted to maintain the passive film. For example, stainless steel and titanium are often anodically protected by passive films in acidic or oxidizing environments.
- **Corrosion inhibitors**: Adding chemical substances to the corrosive environment to reduce the corrosion rate of the metal. The corrosion inhibitors can act by adsorbing on the metal surface and forming a protective barrier, by scavenging the corrosive agents and reducing their concentration, by altering the pH or the redox potential of the environment, or by interfering with the corrosion mechanism. For example, chromates, phosphates, and nitrites are common corrosion inhibitors for steel in water or coolant systems.
- **Environmental modification**: Changing the physical or chemical conditions of the corrosive environment to reduce its aggressiveness. The environmental modification can involve controlling the temperature, pressure, humidity, oxygen, acidity, salinity, or other factors that affect the corrosion rate of the metal. For example, deaeration, desalination, and pH adjustment are common methods to modify the water quality and reduce its corrosivity.
