### Stereochemistry: Optical isomerism in compounds without chiral carbon, Geometrical

- Optical isomerism is a type of stereoisomerism that arises due to the presence of a chiral center in a compound. However, not all compounds with stereoisomers have a chiral center. Geometrical isomerism is another type of stereoisomerism that arises due to the restricted rotation around a double bond in a molecule. 

- Compounds that do not have a chiral center and have a double bond can exhibit geometrical isomerism. In such compounds, the two atoms or groups attached to each carbon of the double bond can either be on the same side of the bond (cis) or on opposite sides (trans).

- Geometrical isomers are not mirror images of each other, and they have different physical and chemical properties. For example, cis and trans isomers of 1,2-dichloroethene have different boiling points, densities, and solubilities.

- In compounds with more than one double bond, the number of possible geometrical isomers increases. For example, but-2-ene can exist as two cis isomers and two trans isomers, making a total of four possible isomers.

- Geometrical isomers can also arise in cyclic compounds, such as cycloalkenes and cyclic alkenyl compounds. In these compounds, the cis and trans isomers can have different ring conformations, leading to different physical and chemical properties.

- Geometrical isomerism is an important aspect of stereochemistry and has many applications in fields such as organic synthesis, drug design, and material science. Understanding the principles of geometrical isomerism is crucial for the design and synthesis of compounds with specific properties and functions.
