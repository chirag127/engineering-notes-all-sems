# Tools and Methods: Inductive Deductive; Exposition; Linear; Interrupted; Spatial

- Inductive and deductive are two methods of reasoning that are based on different premises and lead to different conclusions.
- Inductive reasoning moves from specific observations to broad generalizations. It is a bottom-up approach that aims at developing a theory or a hypothesis from the data. For example, if you observe that all the swans you have seen are white, you might infer that all swans are white. However, this conclusion is not certain, as there might be exceptions or counterexamples that you have not encountered yet. Inductive reasoning is often used in scientific research, especially in the exploratory phase, when the researcher is trying to identify patterns, trends, or causal relationships in the data .
- Deductive reasoning moves from general premises to specific conclusions. It is a top-down approach that aims at testing an existing theory or a hypothesis by deriving logical implications from it. For example, if you know that all mammals are warm-blooded, and that whales are mammals, you can deduce that whales are warm-blooded. This conclusion is certain, as long as the premises are true and valid. Deductive reasoning is often used in scientific research, especially in the confirmatory phase, when the researcher is trying to verify or falsify a hypothesis by conducting experiments or observations .
- Exposition is a method of writing that explains, informs, or describes something. It is often used in academic, technical, or journalistic writing, when the writer wants to convey factual information or arguments to the reader. Exposition can be organized in different ways, such as by definition, classification, comparison, contrast, cause and effect, problem and solution, etc. For example, an expository essay might explain the causes and effects of global warming, or the features and benefits of a new product.
- Linear is a method of writing that follows a logical and sequential order. It is often used in academic or scientific writing, when the writer wants to present a clear and coherent argument or analysis. Linear writing can be organized in different ways, such as by introduction, body, and conclusion, or by thesis, antithesis, and synthesis, etc. For example, a linear essay might start with a thesis statement, followed by supporting evidence, and end with a summary or a recommendation.
- Interrupted is a method of writing that breaks the linear or chronological order by inserting flashbacks, flash-forwards, digressions, or other elements that disrupt the flow of the narrative. It is often used in creative or literary writing, when the writer wants to create suspense, surprise, or complexity in the story. Interrupted writing can be organized in different ways, such as by alternating between different time periods, perspectives, or locations, or by using transitions, cues, or signals to indicate the shifts in the narrative. For example, an interrupted novel might start with the present, then jump to the past, then return to the present, then move to the future, etc.
- Spatial is a method of writing that describes something in terms of its physical arrangement or location. It is often used in descriptive or narrative writing, when the writer wants to create a vivid picture or a sense of place in the reader's mind. Spatial writing can be organized in different ways, such as by left to right, top to bottom, near to far, inside to outside, etc. For example, a spatial essay might describe a room, a landscape, or a city, by focusing on the details and features of each part.
