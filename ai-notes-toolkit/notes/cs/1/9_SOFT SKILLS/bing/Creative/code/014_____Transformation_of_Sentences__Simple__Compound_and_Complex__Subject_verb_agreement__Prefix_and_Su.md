### Transformation of Sentences: Simple, Compound and Complex

- A sentence is a group of words that expresses a complete thought. A sentence can be classified into different types based on its structure and meaning.
- A simple sentence is a sentence that has only one independent clause. An independent clause is a group of words that can stand alone as a complete sentence. For example, "She sings well."
- A compound sentence is a sentence that has two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon (;). For example, "She sings well and plays the guitar."
- A complex sentence is a sentence that has one independent clause and one or more dependent clauses. A dependent clause is a group of words that cannot stand alone as a complete sentence and depends on the independent clause for its meaning. A dependent clause is usually introduced by a subordinating conjunction (such as because, although, when, etc.) or a relative pronoun (such as who, which, that, etc.). For example, "She sings well because she practices every day."
- A sentence can be transformed from one type to another by adding or removing clauses, conjunctions, or punctuation marks. For example, the simple sentence "She sings well." can be transformed into a compound sentence by adding another independent clause and a coordinating conjunction: "She sings well and plays the guitar." It can also be transformed into a complex sentence by adding a dependent clause and a subordinating conjunction: "She sings well because she practices every day."

### Subject-verb agreement

- Subject-verb agreement is the rule that the verb in a sentence must agree with the subject in number (singular or plural) and person (first, second, or third). For example, "He sings well." (singular subject, singular verb, third person) "They sing well." (plural subject, plural verb, third person)
- Some common rules for subject-verb agreement are:
  - The verb agrees with the subject, not the complement. For example, "The singer is a woman." (singular subject, singular verb) "The singers are women." (plural subject, plural verb)
  - The verb agrees with the subject, not the modifier. For example, "The singer with the guitar sings well." (singular subject, singular verb) "The singers with the guitars sing well." (plural subject, plural verb)
  - The verb agrees with the subject closest to it when the subject is a compound noun joined by or or nor. For example, "The singer or the guitarist sings well." (singular subject, singular verb) "The singer or the guitarists sing well." (plural subject, plural verb)
  - The verb agrees with the subject in number when the subject is a collective noun that refers to a group as a unit. For example, "The team is ready." (singular subject, singular verb) However, the verb agrees with the subject in person when the subject is a collective noun that refers to the individual members of the group. For example, "The team are wearing their uniforms." (plural subject, plural verb)
  - The verb agrees with the subject in number when the subject is an indefinite pronoun that refers to a singular or plural entity. For example, "Someone is calling." (singular subject, singular verb) "Some are calling." (plural subject, plural verb)

### Prefix and Suffix

- A prefix is a word part that is added to the beginning of a word to change its meaning. For example, the prefix un- means not, so adding un- to the word happy makes unhappy, which means not happy.
- A suffix is a word part that is added to the end of a word to change its meaning. For example, the suffix -ful means full of, so adding -ful to the word joy makes joyful, which means full of joy.
