### Concept of animal and plant classification

- Animal and plant classification is the process of grouping living organisms into categories based on their similarities and differences.
- Classification helps to organize the diversity of life and to understand the evolutionary relationships among different groups of organisms.
- The most widely used system of classification is the Linnaean taxonomy, which was developed by Carl Linnaeus in the 18th century.
- Linnaean taxonomy assigns each organism a binomial name, consisting of a genus and a species, and places it in a hierarchy of ranks, such as kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species.
- The Linnaean taxonomy has been modified and expanded over time, and now includes additional ranks, such as domain, subkingdom, superphylum, subphylum, superclass, subclass, superorder, suborder, infraorder, superfamily, subfamily, tribe, and subtribe.
- The Linnaean taxonomy is based on morphological and anatomical features, such as body shape, size, structure, and function. However, these features may not always reflect the true evolutionary history of organisms, as some groups may have convergent or divergent evolution, or may have undergone hybridization or horizontal gene transfer.
- Therefore, modern classification also uses molecular and genetic data, such as DNA sequences, to infer the phylogeny, or the evolutionary history, of organisms. Phylogenetic methods, such as cladistics, use shared derived characters, or synapomorphies, to construct branching diagrams, or cladograms, that show the evolutionary relationships among different groups of organisms.
- One of the major outcomes of phylogenetic analysis is the recognition of three domains of life: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. These domains are based on the differences in the structure and function of the cellular components, such as the cell membrane, the cell wall, the ribosomes, and the genetic material.
- The domain Eukarya includes four kingdoms: Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia. These kingdoms are based on the differences in the mode of nutrition, the presence or absence of a cell wall, the type of cell division, and the degree of multicellularity and differentiation.
- The kingdom Protista is a diverse and polyphyletic group of mostly unicellular eukaryotes that do not fit into the other kingdoms. Protists can be autotrophic, heterotrophic, or mixotrophic, and can have various modes of locomotion, such as flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia. Protists include algae, protozoa, slime molds, and water molds.
- The kingdom Fungi is a monophyletic group of heterotrophic eukaryotes that obtain their nutrients by secreting digestive enzymes and absorbing the organic molecules. Fungi have cell walls made of chitin, and reproduce by spores. Fungi include mushrooms, yeasts, molds, and lichens.
- The kingdom Plantae is a monophyletic group of autotrophic eukaryotes that use photosynthesis to produce organic molecules. Plants have cell walls made of cellulose, and reproduce by alternation of generations, which involves a multicellular haploid gametophyte and a multicellular diploid sporophyte. Plants include bryophytes, ferns, gymnosperms, and angiosperms.
- The kingdom Animalia is a monophyletic group of heterotrophic eukaryotes that ingest their food and digest it internally. Animals lack cell walls, and reproduce by sexual reproduction, which involves a multicellular diploid zygote and a multicellular haploid gamete. Animals include sponges, cnidarians, flatworms, roundworms, annelids, mollusks, arthropods, echinoderms, and chordates.
