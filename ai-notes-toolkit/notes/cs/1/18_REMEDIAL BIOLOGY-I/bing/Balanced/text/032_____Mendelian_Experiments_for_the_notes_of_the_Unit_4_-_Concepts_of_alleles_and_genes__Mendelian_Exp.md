### Mendelian Experiments

- Mendelian experiments are the studies of inheritance patterns in pea plants conducted by Gregor Mendel, a monk and a scientist, in the 19th century  .
- Mendel chose pea plants because they have distinct and easily observable traits, such as flower color, seed shape, and plant height, and they can self-fertilize or cross-fertilize .
- Mendel selected seven pairs of contrasting traits and performed controlled crosses between pure-breeding plants with different traits, such as purple and white flowers  .
- Mendel observed the phenotypes (visible traits) of the offspring in each generation and counted the number of plants with each trait. He found that some traits disappeared in the first generation (F1) but reappeared in the second generation (F2) in a predictable ratio  .
- Mendel proposed that each trait is controlled by a pair of factors (now called genes) that are inherited from the parents, one from each parent. He also proposed that each factor can exist in two forms (now called alleles) that are either dominant or recessive  .
- Mendel's first law of segregation states that the two alleles of a gene separate during the formation of gametes (sperm and egg cells), so that each gamete carries only one allele of each gene .
- Mendel's second law of independent assortment states that the alleles of different genes are inherited independently of each other, as long as they are located on different chromosomes .
- Mendel's third law of dominance states that when two different alleles of a gene are present in an organism, only the dominant allele determines the phenotype, while the recessive allele has no visible effect.
- Mendel's experiments laid the foundation for modern genetics and revealed the basic principles of how traits are inherited and expressed in living organisms    .
