### Principles of classification in each kingdom

The five kingdom classification system was proposed by R.H. Whittaker in 1969 based on the following criteria   :

- Cellular structure: prokaryotic or eukaryotic
- Body organization: unicellular or multicellular
- Mode of nutrition: autotrophic or heterotrophic
- Phylogenetic relationships: evolutionary history and molecular evidence
- Reproduction: sexual or asexual

The five kingdoms are:

- Monera: prokaryotic, unicellular, autotrophic or heterotrophic, asexual reproduction, no membrane-bound organelles, examples are bacteria and cyanobacteria
- Protista: eukaryotic, mostly unicellular, autotrophic or heterotrophic, sexual or asexual reproduction, diverse group of organisms, examples are protozoa, algae, slime molds and water molds
- Fungi: eukaryotic, mostly multicellular, heterotrophic, saprophytic or parasitic, sexual or asexual reproduction, cell walls made of chitin, examples are mushrooms, yeasts, molds and lichens
- Plantae: eukaryotic, multicellular, autotrophic, photosynthetic, sexual or asexual reproduction, cell walls made of cellulose, examples are mosses, ferns, conifers and flowering plants
- Animalia: eukaryotic, multicellular, heterotrophic, ingestive, sexual reproduction, no cell walls, examples are sponges, worms, insects, fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals

Each kingdom is further divided into smaller groups based on more specific characteristics, such as body symmetry, tissue differentiation, segmentation, appendages, etc. The hierarchy of classification is:

- Kingdom
- Phylum (or Division for plants)
- Class
- Order
- Family
- Genus
- Species

The scientific name of an organism is composed of its genus and species, and is written in italics or underlined. This is called the binomial system of nomenclature, and was developed by Carolus Linnaeus in the 18th century. For example, the scientific name of human is Homo sapiens.
