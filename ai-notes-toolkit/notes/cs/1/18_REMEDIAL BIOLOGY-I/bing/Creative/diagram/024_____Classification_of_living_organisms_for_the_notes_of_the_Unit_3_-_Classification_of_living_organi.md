### Classification of living organisms

- Classification is the scientific process of arranging living organisms into different groups and subgroups based on their similarities and differences .
- Classification helps to study the diversity of life forms in an organized and systematic way.
- Classification also helps to understand the evolutionary relationships among different groups of organisms.

#### Five kingdom classification

- The five kingdom classification is a system of grouping living organisms into five major categories based on their characteristics.
- The five kingdoms are:

  - **Animalia**: All multicellular animals that are heterotrophic (obtain food from other organisms) and have a nervous system and sensory organs.
  - **Plantae**: All green plants that are autotrophic (make their own food by photosynthesis) and have a cell wall and chloroplasts.
  - **Fungi**: All organisms that are saprophytic (feed on dead or decaying organic matter) or parasitic (feed on living hosts) and have a cell wall and no chloroplasts.
  - **Protista**: All unicellular or simple multicellular organisms that are either autotrophic or heterotrophic and have a nucleus and other organelles.
  - **Monera**: All prokaryotic organisms (lack a nucleus and other organelles) that are either autotrophic or heterotrophic and have a cell wall and no chloroplasts.

#### Major groups and principles of classification in each kingdom

- The major groups and principles of classification in each kingdom are:

  - **Animalia**: Animals are classified into phyla based on their body symmetry, level of organization, presence or absence of coelom (body cavity), segmentation, and type of skeleton. Some examples of phyla are Porifera (sponges), Cnidaria (jellyfish), Platyhelminthes (flatworms), Nematoda (roundworms), Annelida (segmented worms), Mollusca (snails), Arthropoda (insects), Echinodermata (starfish), and Chordata (vertebrates).
  - **Plantae**: Plants are classified into divisions based on their vascular tissue (xylem and phloem), seed production, and presence or absence of flowers. Some examples of divisions are Bryophyta (mosses), Pteridophyta (ferns), Gymnospermae (conifers), and Angiospermae (flowering plants).
  - **Fungi**: Fungi are classified into phyla based on their mode of reproduction, type of spores, and structure of hyphae (filaments). Some examples of phyla are Zygomycota (bread molds), Ascomycota (yeasts), Basidiomycota (mushrooms), and Deuteromycota (imperfect fungi).
  - **Protista**: Protists are classified into phyla based on their mode of nutrition, locomotion, and life cycle. Some examples of phyla are Euglenophyta (euglenoids), Chrysophyta (diatoms), Pyrrophyta (dinoflagellates), Rhodophyta (red algae), Chlorophyta (green algae), Phaeophyta (brown algae), Myxomycota (slime molds), and Protozoa (amoebas, paramecia, etc.).
  - **Monera**: Monerans are classified into phyla based on their shape, cell wall structure, mode of nutrition, and metabolic diversity. Some examples of phyla are Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae), Archaebacteria (extremophiles), and Eubacteria (true bacteria).

#### Systematic and binomial system of nomenclature

- Systematic is the branch of biology that deals with the identification, naming, and classification of living organisms.
- Binomial system of nomenclature is a method of naming living organisms using two words: the genus name and the species name.
- The binomial system of nomenclature was developed by Carl Linnaeus, a Swedish botanist, in the 18th century.
- The rules of binomial nomenclature are:

  - The genus name is always capitalized and the species name is always lowercase.
  - The binomial name is always written in italics or underlined.
