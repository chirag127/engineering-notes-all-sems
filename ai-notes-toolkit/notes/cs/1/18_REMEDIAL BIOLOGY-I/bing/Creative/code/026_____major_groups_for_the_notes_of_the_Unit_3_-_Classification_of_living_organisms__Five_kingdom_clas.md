### Major Groups for the Notes of the Unit 3 - Classification of Living Organisms

- Classification of living organisms is the process of grouping them based on their similarities and differences in various aspects such as structure, function, evolution, etc.
- Classification helps to organize the diversity of life and to understand the evolutionary relationships among different groups of organisms.
- One of the most widely used systems of classification is the five-kingdom classification, proposed by Robert H. Whittaker in 1969  .
- The five-kingdom classification divides all living organisms into five major groups or kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia.
- The criteria for the five-kingdom classification are:
  - Cell structure: whether the cells are prokaryotic (lacking a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles) or eukaryotic (having a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles).
  - Mode of nutrition: whether the organisms are autotrophic (making their own food by photosynthesis or chemosynthesis) or heterotrophic (obtaining food from other sources by ingestion, absorption, or parasitism).
  - Thallus organization: whether the organisms are unicellular (consisting of a single cell) or multicellular (consisting of many cells), and whether the multicellular organisms have a simple or complex body structure.
  - Reproduction: whether the organisms reproduce asexually (by binary fission, budding, fragmentation, etc.) or sexually (by fusion of gametes), and whether the sexual reproduction involves alternation of generations (a cycle of haploid and diploid phases).
  - Phylogenetic relationships: the evolutionary history and ancestry of the organisms based on molecular and morphological evidence.

- The main characteristics and examples of each kingdom are:

  - Kingdom Monera: It includes all prokaryotic organisms, such as bacteria and cyanobacteria (blue-green algae). They are mostly unicellular, but some form colonies or filaments. They have a cell wall made of peptidoglycan or other substances. They can be autotrophic or heterotrophic, and some are capable of nitrogen fixation. They reproduce asexually by binary fission or other methods. They are the most abundant and diverse group of organisms on Earth. Examples are Escherichia coli, Streptococcus, and Anabaena.
  - Kingdom Protista: It includes all eukaryotic organisms that are not plants, animals, or fungi. They are mostly unicellular, but some are colonial or multicellular. They have a variety of cell structures and organelles, such as cilia, flagella, pseudopodia, chloroplasts, etc. They can be autotrophic or heterotrophic, and some are parasitic or symbiotic. They reproduce asexually or sexually, and some have alternation of generations. They are mostly aquatic or moist terrestrial habitats. Examples are Amoeba, Paramecium, Euglena, and Plasmodium.
  - Kingdom Fungi: It includes all eukaryotic organisms that are heterotrophic by absorption. They are mostly multicellular, but some are unicellular (yeasts). They have a cell wall made of chitin. They have a body structure called mycelium, which is composed of thread-like structures called hyphae. They reproduce asexually by spores or budding, or sexually by fusion of hyphae or gametes. They are mostly terrestrial and decompose organic matter or form associations with plants or animals. Examples are Mushrooms, Molds, Yeasts, and Lichens.
  - Kingdom Plantae: It includes all eukaryotic organisms that are autotrophic by photosynthesis. They are multicellular and have a cell wall made of cellulose. They have a body structure called thallus, which is composed of roots, stems, and leaves. They reproduce asexually by vegetative propagation or spores, or sexually by alternation of generations. They are mostly terrestrial and produce oxygen and organic matter for other organisms. Examples are Mosses, Ferns, Conifers, and Flowering plants.
  - Kingdom Animalia: It includes all eukaryotic organisms that are heterotrophic by ingestion. They are multicellular and have no cell wall. They have a body structure called tissue, which is composed of cells specialized for different functions. They reproduce asexually by regeneration or budding, or sexually by fusion of gametes.
