### Mendelian Experiments

- Mendelian experiments are the studies of inheritance patterns in pea plants conducted by Gregor Mendel, a monk and a scientist, in the 19th century  .
- Mendel chose pea plants because they had distinct and easily observable traits, such as flower color, seed shape, and plant height, and they could be easily cross-pollinated by hand .
- Mendel selected seven traits that had two contrasting forms, such as purple or white flowers, round or wrinkled seeds, and tall or dwarf plants .
- Mendel performed two types of crosses: monohybrid and dihybrid. A monohybrid cross involves one trait, such as flower color, while a dihybrid cross involves two traits, such as seed shape and color.
- Mendel's first experiment was a monohybrid cross between pure-breeding plants with different forms of the same trait, such as purple and white flowers. He called the parental generation P, and the offspring generation F1 .
- Mendel observed that all the F1 plants had the same form of the trait as one of the parents, such as purple flowers. He called this form dominant, and the other form recessive .
- Mendel then self-pollinated the F1 plants to produce the second offspring generation, F2. He observed that the recessive form of the trait reappeared in some of the F2 plants, in a ratio of 3:1 to the dominant form .
- Mendel repeated this experiment with the other six traits and obtained similar results. He concluded that each trait is controlled by a pair of factors (now called genes) that separate during gamete formation and recombine during fertilization .
- Mendel's second experiment was a dihybrid cross between pure-breeding plants with different forms of two traits, such as round yellow and wrinkled green seeds. He called the parental generation P, and the offspring generation F1.
- Mendel observed that all the F1 plants had the same form of both traits as one of the parents, such as round yellow seeds. He called this form dominant, and the other form recessive.
- Mendel then self-pollinated the F1 plants to produce the second offspring generation, F2. He observed that the F2 plants showed four different combinations of the two traits, in a ratio of 9:3:3:1.
- Mendel concluded that the two traits are inherited independently of each other, and that each trait is controlled by a pair of factors that separate and recombine during gamete formation and fertilization.
- Mendel's experiments led to the discovery of three fundamental laws of inheritance: the law of segregation, the law of independent assortment, and the law of dominance .
- The law of segregation states that each individual has two factors for each trait, and that these factors separate during gamete formation, so that each gamete receives one factor for each trait.
- The law of independent assortment states that factors for different traits are distributed to gametes independently of each other, so that each gamete has a random combination of factors for different traits.
- The law of dominance states that in a heterozygous individual, one factor (the dominant) masks the expression of the other factor (the recessive) for the same trait.
- Mendel's experiments are considered the foundation of modern genetics, and his factors are now known as alleles, which are alternative forms of a gene that code for different variants of a trait.
