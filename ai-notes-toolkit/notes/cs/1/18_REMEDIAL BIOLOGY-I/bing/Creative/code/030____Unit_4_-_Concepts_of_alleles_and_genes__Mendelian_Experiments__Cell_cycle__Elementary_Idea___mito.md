## Concepts of alleles and genes
- Genes are segments of DNA that code for a specific trait or function.
- Alleles are different versions of the same gene that may have different effects on the trait or function.
- For example, the gene for eye color has different alleles that determine whether the eyes are blue, brown, green, etc.
- Each individual inherits two alleles for each gene, one from each parent.
- The combination of alleles determines the phenotype (observable trait) and the genotype (genetic makeup) of the individual.
- Some alleles are dominant, meaning they mask the effect of the other allele, and some are recessive, meaning they are only expressed when both alleles are the same.
- For example, the allele for brown eyes (B) is dominant over the allele for blue eyes (b), so an individual with the genotype BB or Bb will have brown eyes, while only an individual with the genotype bb will have blue eyes.

## Mendelian Experiments
- Mendel was a monk who experimented with pea plants and discovered the basic principles of inheritance.
- He crossed plants with different traits, such as flower color, seed shape, pod color, etc., and observed the patterns of inheritance in the offspring.
- He formulated the following laws of inheritance:
  - Law of Segregation: Each individual has two alleles for each gene, and these alleles separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one allele for each gene.
  - Law of Independent Assortment: The alleles of different genes are inherited independently of each other, as long as they are on different chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome.
  - Law of Dominance: Some alleles are dominant over others and determine the phenotype of the individual.
- Mendel's laws can be used to predict the genotypic and phenotypic ratios of the offspring of a cross using Punnett squares.

## Cell cycle (Elementary Idea)
- The cell cycle is the series of events that a cell undergoes from one division to the next.
- The cell cycle consists of two main phases: interphase and mitosis.
- Interphase is the longest phase of the cell cycle, where the cell grows, replicates its DNA, and prepares for division.
- Interphase can be divided into three subphases: G1, S, and G2.
  - G1: The cell grows and performs its normal functions.
  - S: The cell synthesizes a copy of its DNA, resulting in two identical sister chromatids attached at the centromere.
  - G2: The cell continues to grow and prepares for mitosis by synthesizing proteins and organelles.
- Mitosis is the phase of the cell cycle where the cell divides its nucleus and cytoplasm into two daughter cells, each with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell.
- Mitosis can be divided into four stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase.
  - Prophase: The chromosomes condense and become visible, the nuclear envelope breaks down, and the spindle fibers form from the centrosomes and attach to the centromeres of the chromosomes.
  - Metaphase: The chromosomes align at the equator of the cell, forming the metaphase plate, and the spindle fibers exert tension on the chromosomes.
  - Anaphase: The sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles of the cell, pulled by the spindle fibers, and the cell elongates.
  - Telophase: The chromosomes reach the poles and decondense, the nuclear envelope reforms, and the spindle fibers disappear.
- Cytokinesis is the process of dividing the cytoplasm and the organelles into two daughter cells, usually occurring simultaneously with telophase.
- Cytokinesis differs in animal and plant cells:
  - In animal cells, a cleavage furrow forms at the equator of the cell and pinches the cell into two.
  - In plant cells, a cell plate forms at the equator of the cell and grows outward, forming a new cell wall between the two daughter cells.

- Mitosis and meiosis are two types of cell division that have different purposes and outcomes.
