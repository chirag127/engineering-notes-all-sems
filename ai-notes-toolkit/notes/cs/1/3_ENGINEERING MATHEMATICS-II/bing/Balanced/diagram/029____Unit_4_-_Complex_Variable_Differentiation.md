## Unit 4 - Complex Variable–Differentiation

- Complex variable differentiation is the extension of the concept of differentiation from real functions to complex functions.
- A complex function is a function that maps a complex variable to another complex variable, such as f(z) = z^2 + 2z + 1, where z is a complex variable.
- A complex variable is a variable that can take values in the complex plane, which is the set of all numbers of the form x + iy, where x and y are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit, such that i^2 = -1.
- The complex plane can be visualized as a two-dimensional coordinate system, where the horizontal axis is the real axis and the vertical axis is the imaginary axis.
- A complex function can be written in terms of its real and imaginary parts, such as f(z) = u(x,y) + iv(x,y), where u and v are real functions of x and y.
- The derivative of a complex function is defined as the limit of the ratio of the change in the function value to the change in the variable, as the change in the variable approaches zero, such as f'(z) = lim_(Δz->0) (f(z + Δz) - f(z))/Δz, where Δz is a complex number.
- The derivative of a complex function measures the rate of change of the function value with respect to the variable, and it is also a complex function.
- A complex function is said to be differentiable at a point z if the limit defining the derivative exists and is independent of the direction of approach of Δz to zero.
- A complex function is said to be analytic or holomorphic at a point z if it is differentiable at z and at every point in some neighborhood of z.
- A complex function is said to be entire if it is analytic at every point in the complex plane.
- The derivative of a complex function has some properties that are similar to those of the derivative of a real function, such as the sum, product, quotient, and chain rules.
- The derivative of a complex function also has some properties that are unique to complex analysis, such as the Cauchy-Riemann equations, which relate the partial derivatives of the real and imaginary parts of the function, and the conformality property, which states that an analytic function preserves the angles between curves at the points where it is differentiable.
