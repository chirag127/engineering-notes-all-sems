# Continuity and Differentiability of Complex Functions

- A complex function is a function that maps a complex variable to a complex value, i.e., f: C → C, where C is the set of complex numbers.
- A complex function can be written as f(z) = u(x, y) + iv(x, y), where z = x + iy, u and v are real functions of x and y, and i is the imaginary unit.
- A complex function f(z) is said to be **continuous** at a point z0 if for any given positive number ε, there exists a positive number δ such that |f(z) - f(z0)| < ε whenever |z - z0| < δ .
- Continuity of a complex function implies that the function does not have any breaks, jumps, or gaps in its graph.
- A complex function f(z) is said to be **differentiable** at a point z0 if the limit f ′ (z0) = lim z→z0 f(z) − f(z0) z − z0 exists.
- Differentiability of a complex function implies that the function has a well-defined slope or rate of change at every point.
- Differentiability of a complex function is a stronger condition than continuity, i.e., if f(z) is differentiable at z0, then f(z) is also continuous at z0, but the converse is not true.
- A complex function f(z) is said to be **analytic** at a point z0 if f(z) is differentiable at z0 and at every point in some neighborhood of z0.
- Analyticity of a complex function implies that the function satisfies the **Cauchy-Riemann equations**, i.e., ∂u ∂x = ∂v ∂y and ∂u ∂y = − ∂v ∂x, where u and v are the real and imaginary parts of f(z) .
- Analytic functions have many remarkable properties, such as the **Cauchy integral formula**, the **Taylor series expansion**, and the **residue theorem**.
