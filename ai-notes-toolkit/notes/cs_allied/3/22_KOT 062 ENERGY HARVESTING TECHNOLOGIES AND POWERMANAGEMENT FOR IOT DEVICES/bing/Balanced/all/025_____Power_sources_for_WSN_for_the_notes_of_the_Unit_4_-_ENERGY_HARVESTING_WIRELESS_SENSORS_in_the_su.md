# Power sources for WSN

- Wireless sensor networks (WSN) are composed of small, low-power devices that can sense, process, and communicate data wirelessly.
- WSN have many applications in environmental monitoring, industrial automation, health care, smart buildings, etc.
- One of the main challenges in WSN is to provide reliable and long-lasting power sources for the sensor nodes, which are often deployed in remote or inaccessible locations.
- Power sources for WSN can be classified into three categories :
  - Energy reservoirs: These are devices that store energy on the node, such as batteries, capacitors, or fuel cells. They have a finite amount of energy and need to be replaced or recharged periodically.
  - Power distribution: These are methods that deliver power to the node from an external source, such as wires, electromagnetic induction, or radio frequency. They require a fixed infrastructure and may not be feasible or cost-effective for large-scale or dynamic networks.
  - Power scavenging: These are techniques that harvest ambient energy from the environment, such as solar, thermal, vibration, or wind. They can provide continuous and self-sustaining power for the node, but they depend on the availability and intensity of the energy source and may have low power output.
- Some examples of power sources for WSN are   :
  - Batteries: They are the most common and widely used energy reservoirs for WSN. They have high energy density, low cost, and easy integration. However, they have limited lifetime, environmental impact, and self-discharge. They also need to be matched with the power consumption and voltage requirements of the node.
  - Solar cells: They are the most popular and promising power scavenging devices for WSN. They convert sunlight into electrical energy and can provide high power output in outdoor environments. However, they have low efficiency, high cost, and dependence on weather and diurnal cycles. They also need to be coupled with energy storage devices to provide power during the night or cloudy days.
  - Piezoelectric generators: They are devices that convert mechanical strain or vibration into electrical energy. They can scavenge energy from human motion, machinery, vehicles, or wind. They have high power density, low cost, and wide frequency range. However, they have low efficiency, nonlinear output, and dependence on the amplitude and frequency of the vibration source. They also need to be matched with the resonance frequency of the node and the vibration source.
  - Thermoelectric generators: They are devices that convert temperature gradients or differences into electrical energy. They can scavenge energy from body heat, industrial waste heat, or environmental temperature variations. They have high reliability, low maintenance, and no moving parts. However, they have low efficiency, high cost, and dependence on the temperature difference and the thermal resistance of the materials. They also need to be optimized for the operating temperature range and the heat transfer rate.
