# Powering Wireless SHM Sensor Nodes

- Wireless sensor nodes (WSNs) are devices that can sense, process, and communicate data wirelessly.
- Structural health monitoring (SHM) is an application of WSNs that aims to detect and assess the damage or degradation of structures such as bridges, buildings, pipelines, etc.
- Powering WSNs for SHM is a challenge because they need to operate for long periods of time, often in harsh or inaccessible environments, and with limited or no access to external power sources.
- Energy harvesting (EH) is a technique that can provide power to WSNs by converting ambient energy sources such as solar, thermal, vibration, wind, etc. into electrical energy.
- EH can extend the lifetime, reliability, and autonomy of WSNs for SHM, as well as reduce the maintenance and replacement costs of batteries.
- EH can also enable new applications of WSNs for SHM that require high power consumption, such as active sensing, data fusion, or actuation.
- EH for WSNs for SHM can be classified into two categories: local EH and remote EH.
- Local EH refers to the case where the energy harvester is attached to or integrated with the sensor node, and the energy source is in the vicinity of the node.
- Remote EH refers to the case where the energy harvester is separated from the sensor node, and the energy source is at a distance from the node.
- Remote EH can be achieved by wireless energy transmission (WET), which uses electromagnetic waves to transfer energy from a transmitter to a receiver.
- WET can overcome the limitations of local EH, such as the variability, intermittency, or scarcity of ambient energy sources, or the mismatch between the energy source and the sensor location.
- WET can also provide a controlled and constant power supply to the sensor node, which can improve the performance and functionality of the node.
- WET for WSNs for SHM can be implemented using different frequency bands, such as radio frequency (RF), microwave, or laser.
- RF WET uses low-frequency waves (below 6 GHz) to transmit energy, and has the advantages of low cost, easy implementation, and high safety. However, RF WET also has the disadvantages of low efficiency, low power density, and high interference.
- Microwave WET uses high-frequency waves (above 6 GHz) to transmit energy, and has the advantages of high efficiency, high power density, and low interference. However, microwave WET also has the disadvantages of high cost, complex implementation, and low safety.
- Laser WET uses optical waves (visible or infrared) to transmit energy, and has the advantages of high efficiency, high power density, and low interference. However, laser WET also has the disadvantages of high cost, complex implementation, and low safety.
- The choice of the frequency band for WET depends on the application requirements, such as the distance, power, and data rate of the sensor node, as well as the environmental conditions, such as the weather, obstacles, or regulations.
- Some examples of WET for WSNs for SHM are:

  - A prototype system for delivering energy to SHM sensor nodes by microwave WET in the 10 GHz X-band . The system achieved a power transfer efficiency of 10% and a power density of 0.1 W/cm2 at a distance of 10 m.
  - A feasibility study of using RF WET to provide power for SHM sensor nodes embedded in the host structure. The study showed that RF WET can deliver up to 1 mW of power to the sensor node at a distance of 10 cm.
  - A concept of using laser WET to power SHM sensor nodes on rotating structures, such as wind turbines or helicopter blades. The concept proposed a laser transmitter on the ground and a photovoltaic receiver on the structure, and estimated a power transfer efficiency of 20% and a power density of 1 W/cm2 at a distance of 100 m.
