# Unit 2 - Piezoelectric Energy Harvesting and Electromechanical Modeling

- Piezoelectric energy harvesting is the process of converting mechanical vibrations into electrical energy using piezoelectric materials, which exhibit a voltage response when subjected to mechanical stress or strain.
- Piezoelectric energy harvesters can be used to power low-power devices such as sensors, wireless transmitters, or microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) by scavenging energy from ambient sources such as wind, human motion, or machinery.
- Electromechanical modeling of piezoelectric energy harvesters is the mathematical description of the coupled dynamics of the mechanical structure and the electrical circuit, which can be used to predict the performance, optimize the design, and analyze the stability of the system.
- Electromechanical modeling can be done at different levels of complexity and accuracy, depending on the assumptions and simplifications made about the geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, and excitation of the system.
- Some common electromechanical modeling approaches for piezoelectric energy harvesters are:
  - Lumped-parameter models, which treat the system as a single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) or a multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) oscillator with equivalent mass, stiffness, damping, and piezoelectric coefficients. These models are simple and easy to implement, but they may not capture the distributed effects and higher modes of the system.
  - Distributed-parameter models, which account for the spatial variation of the mechanical and electrical variables along the system using partial differential equations (PDEs). These models are more accurate and general, but they may require numerical methods or analytical techniques such as the Rayleigh-Ritz method or the Galerkin method to obtain the solutions.
  - Finite element models, which discretize the system into a finite number of elements with nodal degrees of freedom and apply the principle of virtual work or the principle of minimum potential energy to obtain the equations of motion and the output voltage. These models can handle complex geometries and nonlinearities, but they may be computationally expensive and require specialized software tools.
