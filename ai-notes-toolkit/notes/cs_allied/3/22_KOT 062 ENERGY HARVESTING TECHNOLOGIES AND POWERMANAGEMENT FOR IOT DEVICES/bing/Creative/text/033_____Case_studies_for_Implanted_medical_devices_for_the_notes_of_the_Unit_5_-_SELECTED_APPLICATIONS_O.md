### Case studies for Implanted medical devices

- Implantable medical devices (IMDs) are electronic devices that are inserted into the human body to monitor, diagnose, or treat various medical conditions. Examples of IMDs include pacemakers, cochlear implants, neurostimulators, drug delivery systems, and biosensors.
- IMDs require a reliable and long-lasting power source to function properly. However, conventional batteries have several limitations, such as limited lifespan, toxicity, infection risk, and the need for surgical replacement. Therefore, energy harvesting techniques have been proposed to provide sustainable and wireless power for IMDs.
- Energy harvesting is the process of converting ambient energy sources, such as mechanical, thermal, or chemical energy, into electrical energy. Energy harvesting devices can be classified into two types: external and implantable. External energy harvesters are located outside the body and transmit power to the IMDs wirelessly, while implantable energy harvesters are located inside the body and harvest energy from the biological environment.
- Some examples of implantable energy harvesters (IEHs) are:

  - Piezoelectric energy harvesters: These devices convert mechanical vibrations or deformations into electrical voltage using piezoelectric materials. The mechanical energy can be derived from sources such as heartbeat, blood flow, respiration, or body movement. For instance, a piezoelectric energy harvester was developed to power a pacemaker by harvesting energy from the heart motion .
  - Thermoelectric energy harvesters: These devices convert temperature gradients into electrical voltage using thermoelectric materials. The temperature gradients can be generated by the difference between the body core temperature and the skin or ambient temperature, or by the metabolic heat of the tissues. For example, a thermoelectric energy harvester was designed to power a wireless temperature sensor by harvesting energy from the temperature difference between the esophagus and the stomach.
  - Biofuel cells: These devices convert chemical energy into electrical energy by using enzymes or microorganisms as catalysts. The chemical energy can be obtained from the oxidation of biomolecules, such as glucose or oxygen, that are present in the body fluids. For instance, a biofuel cell was fabricated to power a glucose sensor by harvesting energy from the glucose and oxygen in the interstitial fluid .

- IEHs have several advantages over batteries, such as longer lifespan, biocompatibility, miniaturization, and reduced infection risk. However, IEHs also face some challenges, such as low power output, variable energy availability, biodegradation, and biocompatibility. Therefore, further research and development are needed to improve the performance and reliability of IEHs and to integrate them with IMDs.
