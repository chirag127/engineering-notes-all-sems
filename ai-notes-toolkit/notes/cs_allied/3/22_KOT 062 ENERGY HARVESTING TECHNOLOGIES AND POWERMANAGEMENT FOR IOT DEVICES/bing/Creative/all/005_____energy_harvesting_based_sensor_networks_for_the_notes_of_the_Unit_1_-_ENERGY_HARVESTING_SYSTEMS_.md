# Energy Harvesting Based Sensor Networks

- Energy harvesting based sensor networks are wireless sensor networks that use ambient energy sources such as solar, wind, thermal, vibration, etc. to power the sensor nodes and extend their lifetime .
- Energy harvesting based sensor networks have several advantages over battery-powered sensor networks, such as:
  - They can reduce the maintenance cost and environmental impact of replacing batteries.
  - They can enable the deployment of sensor networks in remote or inaccessible areas where battery replacement is difficult or impossible.
  - They can improve the reliability and availability of sensor networks by avoiding battery failures or depletion.
  - They can support higher data rates and more complex functionalities by providing more energy to the sensor nodes.
- Energy harvesting based sensor networks also have some challenges, such as:
  - The energy sources are often intermittent, unpredictable, and variable, which requires efficient energy management and storage techniques to ensure the quality of service of the sensor network.
  - The energy harvesting devices may introduce additional size, weight, and cost to the sensor nodes, which may limit their scalability and flexibility.
  - The energy harvesting devices may interfere with the sensing or communication functions of the sensor nodes, which requires careful design and optimization of the sensor network architecture and protocols.
- Energy harvesting based sensor networks can be classified into two types based on the energy harvesting mode:
  - Energy-neutral sensor networks, where the sensor nodes harvest and consume energy at the same rate, and maintain a constant energy level.
  - Energy-surplus sensor networks, where the sensor nodes harvest more energy than they consume, and store the excess energy for future use or share it with other nodes.
- Energy harvesting based sensor networks can be applied to various domains and applications, such as:
  - Environmental monitoring, where the sensor nodes can use solar, wind, or water energy to measure and report the temperature, humidity, pressure, air quality, etc. of the surroundings .
  - Structural health monitoring, where the sensor nodes can use vibration or acoustic energy to detect and locate cracks, faults, or damages in buildings, bridges, pipelines, etc. .
  - Biomedical and wearable sensing, where the sensor nodes can use thermal, kinetic, or biochemical energy to monitor the vital signs, activity, or health status of humans or animals .
