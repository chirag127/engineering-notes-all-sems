### Stratified Sampling

Stratified sampling is a sampling technique wherein the population is divided into homogeneous subgroups called strata, and samples are selected from each stratum.

-   The strata should be mutually exclusive and collective exhaustive. This means the entire population should be divided into strata, and an element should belong to only one stratum.
-   Samples are selected from each stratum in a proportional allocation. This means that the ratio of sample size to population size in each stratum should be the same.
-   Stratified sampling reduces sampling error as it results in a sample that is more representative of the population.
-   It is useful when the population contains distinct subgroups and we want to ensure that all subgroups are adequately represented in the sample.
-   Examples: Students can be stratified based on gender and then samples selected. Voters can be stratified based on age, income, geography, etc. and then samples selected from each stratum for a survey.

The key benefits of stratified sampling are:

-   It results in increased precision as compared to simple random sampling.
-   It ensures that the sample represents all subgroups of the population.
-   It can lead to cost reduction if the cost of sampling each stratum is different. We can allocate a greater portion of the sample to strata that are less expensive to sample.

The drawbacks of stratified sampling are:

-   It requires identification of strata and homogeneous subgroups in the population, which may not always be straightforward.
-   It can be more complex to implement as compared to simple random sampling.
