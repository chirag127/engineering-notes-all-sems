# Conditional Probability

- Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring given that another event has already occurred.
- Conditional probability is denoted by P(A|B), which means the probability of event A happening given that event B has already happened.
- Conditional probability can be calculated using the formula: P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B), where P(A and B) is the joint probability of both events happening, and P(B) is the marginal probability of event B happening.
- Conditional probability can be used to evaluate how prior information affects probabilities. For example, what is the probability of a person having a fever given that they have a sore throat?
- Conditional probability can also be used to test the independence of two events. Two events are independent if the occurrence of one event does not affect the probability of the other event. In other words, P(A|B) = P(A) and P(B|A) = P(B) for independent events A and B.
- Conditional probability can be illustrated using a contingency table, a Venn diagram, or a tree diagram. These are graphical tools that show the frequencies or probabilities of different events and their relationships.

## Examples of Conditional Probability

- Example 1: Suppose a fair coin is tossed twice. What is the probability of getting two heads, given that the first toss is a head?
  - Solution: Let A be the event of getting two heads, and B be the event of getting a head on the first toss. We want to find P(A|B).
  - Using the formula, P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B). P(A and B) is the probability of getting two heads in two tosses, which is 1/4. P(B) is the probability of getting a head on the first toss, which is 1/2. Therefore, P(A|B) = (1/4) / (1/2) = 1/2.
  - This means that the probability of getting two heads, given that the first toss is a head, is 1/2.
