# Markov Inequality

- Markov inequality is a result in probability theory that gives an upper bound for the probability that a non-negative function of a random variable is greater than or equal to some positive constant.
- Markov inequality holds for any distribution with positive values, no matter what other features that it has.
- Markov inequality can be stated as follows: if X is a non-negative random variable and a > 0, then P(X >= a) <= E[X] / a, where E[X] is the expected value of X .
- Markov inequality can be used to derive other inequalities, such as Chebyshev's inequality and Chernoff's bound .
- Markov inequality can also be applied to non-decreasing functions of random variables, such as the absolute value or the square.
- Markov inequality can be illustrated by an example: if the average income of a population is $20,000, then no more than 20% of the population can have an income of $100,000 or more, assuming no income is negative.
