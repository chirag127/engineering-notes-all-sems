# Stratified Sampling

- Stratified sampling is a method of sampling from a population that can be divided into subgroups or strata that share some common characteristics or attributes.
- The purpose of stratified sampling is to ensure that each subgroup is adequately represented in the sample, and to reduce the sampling error and increase the precision of the estimates.
- Stratified sampling can be used for both probability and non-probability sampling methods, depending on how the subgroups are selected and how the sample size is determined.
- Stratified sampling can be classified into two types: proportional and disproportionate.
  - Proportional stratified sampling means that the sample size of each subgroup is proportional to its size in the population. For example, if the population consists of 40% males and 60% females, and the sample size is 100, then the sample should have 40 males and 60 females.
  - Disproportionate stratified sampling means that the sample size of each subgroup is not proportional to its size in the population, but is based on some other criteria, such as the variability of the subgroup, the importance of the subgroup, or the cost of sampling the subgroup. For example, if the population consists of 40% males and 60% females, but the researcher is more interested in the males, then the sample may have more males than females, such as 60 males and 40 females.
- Stratified sampling has some advantages and disadvantages compared to other sampling methods.
  - Advantages:
    - It can improve the representativeness and accuracy of the sample by reducing the sampling bias and the sampling error.
    - It can allow for more efficient and detailed analysis of the subgroups and their differences.
    - It can reduce the cost and time of data collection and processing by focusing on the relevant subgroups.
  - Disadvantages:
    - It requires prior knowledge of the population and its subgroups, which may not be available or accurate.
    - It may increase the complexity and difficulty of the sampling design and the data analysis, especially if there are many subgroups or if the subgroups are not mutually exclusive or exhaustive.
    - It may introduce some errors or biases if the subgroups are not properly defined or selected, or if the sample size is not adequate for each subgroup.
