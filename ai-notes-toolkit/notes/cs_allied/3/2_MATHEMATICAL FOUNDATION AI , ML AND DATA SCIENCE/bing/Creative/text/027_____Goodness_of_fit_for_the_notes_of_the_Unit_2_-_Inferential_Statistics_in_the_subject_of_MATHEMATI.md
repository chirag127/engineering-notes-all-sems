### Goodness of fit

- Goodness of fit is a measure of how well a statistical model or distribution fits a given set of observed data.
- Goodness of fit tests are used to compare the observed frequencies or proportions of a categorical variable with the expected or theoretical frequencies or proportions under a null hypothesis.
- Goodness of fit tests can be classified into two types: parametric and non-parametric.
- Parametric tests assume that the data follow a certain distribution, such as normal, binomial, Poisson, etc., and compare the observed data with the expected values based on the parameters of the distribution.
- Non-parametric tests do not assume any specific distribution for the data, and compare the observed data with the expected values based on the relative frequencies or proportions of the categories.
- Some examples of parametric goodness of fit tests are: chi-square test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, Anderson-Darling test, etc.
- Some examples of non-parametric goodness of fit tests are: chi-square test, G-test, Fisher's exact test, etc.
- The choice of the appropriate goodness of fit test depends on the type and size of the data, the distributional assumptions, and the level of significance.
- The null hypothesis for a goodness of fit test is usually that the data follow a certain distribution or that the observed frequencies or proportions are equal to the expected or theoretical frequencies or proportions.
- The alternative hypothesis is usually that the data do not follow a certain distribution or that the observed frequencies or proportions are not equal to the expected or theoretical frequencies or proportions.
- The test statistic for a goodness of fit test is usually a measure of the discrepancy or deviation between the observed and expected values, such as the chi-square statistic, the G-statistic, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic, etc.
- The p-value for a goodness of fit test is the probability of obtaining a test statistic as extreme or more extreme than the observed one, under the null hypothesis.
- The decision rule for a goodness of fit test is to reject the null hypothesis if the p-value is less than or equal to the level of significance, and to fail to reject the null hypothesis otherwise.
- The interpretation of a goodness of fit test is to conclude that there is a significant difference between the observed and expected values if the null hypothesis is rejected, and to conclude that there is no significant difference between the observed and expected values if the null hypothesis is not rejected.
