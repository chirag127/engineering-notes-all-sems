### Stratified Sampling
- Stratified sampling is a method of sampling from a population that is divided into subgroups or strata that are homogeneous in some characteristic.
- The purpose of stratified sampling is to ensure that each stratum is adequately represented in the sample and to improve the precision of the estimates.
- Stratified sampling can be done in two ways: proportional and disproportional.
- Proportional stratified sampling means that the sample size for each stratum is proportional to the population size of that stratum. For example, if the population consists of 40% males and 60% females, and the sample size is 100, then the sample should have 40 males and 60 females.
- Disproportional stratified sampling means that the sample size for each stratum is not proportional to the population size of that stratum, but is determined by some other criterion, such as the variability of the stratum or the importance of the stratum for the research question. For example, if the population consists of 40% males and 60% females, and the sample size is 100, then the sample could have 50 males and 50 females, if the researcher wants to compare the two groups equally.
- Stratified sampling can be done with or without replacement. With replacement means that a unit can be selected more than once from the same stratum. Without replacement means that a unit can be selected only once from the same stratum.
- Stratified sampling can be done with simple random sampling or systematic sampling within each stratum. Simple random sampling means that each unit in the stratum has an equal chance of being selected. Systematic sampling means that the units in the stratum are ordered in some way and every kth unit is selected, where k is a constant. For example, if the stratum has 100 units and the sample size is 10, then k = 100/10 = 10, and the units with numbers 10, 20, 30, ..., 100 are selected.
- Stratified sampling has some advantages and disadvantages over simple random sampling. Some advantages are:
  - It can reduce the sampling error by increasing the homogeneity within each stratum and reducing the variability between strata.
  - It can ensure that all subgroups of interest are represented in the sample and avoid the problem of undercoverage or overcoverage.
  - It can allow for different sampling methods or designs for different strata, depending on the research objectives or constraints.
- Some disadvantages are:
  - It can be more complex and costly to implement than simple random sampling, especially if the strata are not clearly defined or easily identifiable.
  - It can introduce bias if the strata are not mutually exclusive or collectively exhaustive, meaning that some units belong to more than one stratum or some units do not belong to any stratum.
  - It can increase the sampling error if the strata are not homogeneous or the sample sizes are not optimal for each stratum.
