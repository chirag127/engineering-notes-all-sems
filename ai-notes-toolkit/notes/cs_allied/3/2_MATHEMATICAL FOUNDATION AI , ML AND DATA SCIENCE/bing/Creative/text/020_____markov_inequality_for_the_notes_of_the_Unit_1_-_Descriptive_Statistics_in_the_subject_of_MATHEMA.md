### Markov Inequality

- Markov inequality is a result in probability theory that gives an upper bound for the probability that a non-negative function of a random variable is greater than or equal to some positive constant.
- The inequality can be stated as follows: Let X be a non-negative random variable and a > 0 be a positive constant. Then, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a, where E[X] is the expected value of X .
- The inequality is useful because it holds for any distribution with positive values, regardless of its shape or other features.
- The inequality can be generalized to non-negative functions of random variables. Let X be a random variable and g be a non-negative function. Then, P(g(X) ≥ a) ≤ E[g(X)]/a, where E[g(X)] is the expected value of g(X).
- The inequality can also be extended to non-decreasing functions of random variables. Let X be a random variable and f be a non-decreasing function. Then, P(f(X) ≥ a) ≤ E[f(X)]/a, where E[f(X)] is the expected value of f(X).
- Markov inequality can be used to derive other inequalities, such as Chebyshev's inequality and Chernoff's bound.
- Markov inequality can also be used to illustrate some properties of probability distributions. For example, assuming no income is negative, Markov's inequality shows that no more than 1/5 of the population can have more than 5 times the average income.
