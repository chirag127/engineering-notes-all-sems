## Unit 2 - Inferential Statistics

Inferential statistics is the branch of statistics that uses sample data to draw conclusions about a population or a process. Inferential statistics can help us to:

- Test hypotheses and make decisions based on data
- Estimate unknown parameters or quantities of interest
- Compare different groups or treatments
- Assess the relationship between variables
- Predict future outcomes or behaviors

Some of the common methods and techniques of inferential statistics are:

- Confidence intervals: A range of values that is likely to contain the true population parameter with a certain level of confidence
- Hypothesis testing: A procedure to test whether a claim or assumption about a population is supported by the data
- t-tests: A type of hypothesis test that compares the means of two groups or the mean of a group to a hypothesized value
- ANOVA: A type of hypothesis test that compares the means of more than two groups
- Chi-square tests: A type of hypothesis test that compares the observed frequencies of categorical data to the expected frequencies
- Correlation: A measure of the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables
- Regression: A method of modeling the relationship between one or more explanatory variables and a response variable
- Prediction: A method of using a regression model to estimate the value of the response variable for a given set of explanatory variables
- Classification: A method of using a regression model to assign a category or label to a given set of explanatory variables

Inferential statistics relies on some assumptions and conditions that must be checked before applying the methods and techniques. Some of the common assumptions and conditions are:

- Randomness: The sample data must be obtained by a random process or a random sampling method
- Independence: The observations in the sample data must be independent of each other
- Normality: The distribution of the sample data or the sampling distribution must be approximately normal or follow a known distribution
- Equal variance: The variability of the sample data or the populations must be equal or similar
- Linearity: The relationship between the explanatory and response variables must be linear or approximately linear
- Homoscedasticity: The variability of the response variable must be constant or similar for different values of the explanatory variables

Inferential statistics also involves some sources of uncertainty and error that must be considered and minimized. Some of the common sources of uncertainty and error are:

- Sampling error: The difference between the sample statistic and the population parameter due to random variation
- Sampling bias: The tendency of the sample data to be unrepresentative of the population due to a non-random or flawed sampling method
- Measurement error: The difference between the observed value and the true value of a variable due to inaccurate or imprecise measurement
- Confounding: The effect of a third variable that influences both the explanatory and response variables and obscures the true relationship between them
- Extrapolation: The prediction of the response variable for values of the explanatory variables that are outside the range of the sample data
- Overfitting: The tendency of a regression model to fit the sample data too closely and capture the noise or random variation rather than the underlying pattern or trend
