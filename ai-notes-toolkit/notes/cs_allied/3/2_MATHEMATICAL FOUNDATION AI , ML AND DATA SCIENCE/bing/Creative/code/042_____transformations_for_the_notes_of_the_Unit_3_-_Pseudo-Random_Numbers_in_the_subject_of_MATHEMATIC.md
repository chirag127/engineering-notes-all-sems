### Transformations for the notes of the Unit 3 - Pseudo-Random Numbers

- Pseudo-random numbers are numbers that are generated by a deterministic algorithm that mimics the properties of random numbers.
- Pseudo-random numbers are useful for applications such as simulation, cryptography, gaming, and statistical analysis.
- Pseudo-random numbers can be generated from different probability distributions, such as uniform, normal, exponential, etc.
- One of the most common and oldest algorithms for generating pseudo-random numbers from a uniform distribution is the **linear congruential generator (LCG)**. The LCG is defined by the recurrence relation:

```math
X_{n+1} = (aX_n + c) \mod m
```

where $X_n$ is the sequence of pseudo-random values, $m$ is the modulus, $a$ is the multiplier, $c$ is the increment, and $X_0$ is the seed or start value.

- To generate pseudo-random numbers from other distributions, there are several methods that can be used, such as:

  - The **probability integral transform**, which uses the inverse of the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the target distribution to map uniform pseudo-random numbers to the desired distribution. For example, if $F$ is the CDF of the target distribution and $U$ is a uniform pseudo-random number, then $F^{-1}(U)$ is a pseudo-random number from the target distribution.
  - The **convolution method**, which uses the sum of several generated pseudo-random numbers with the appropriate distribution to approximate the target distribution. For example, if $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) pseudo-random numbers from an exponential distribution with mean $\lambda$, then $\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ is a pseudo-random number from a gamma distribution with shape parameter $n$ and scale parameter $\lambda$.
  - The **transformation method**, which uses the relationship between different probability distributions to generate pseudo-random numbers from one distribution using another. For example, if $X$ is a pseudo-random number from a standard normal distribution, then $e^X$ is a pseudo-random number from a log-normal distribution.

- Some examples of R functions that can generate pseudo-random numbers from different distributions are:

  - `runif(n, a, b)` generates `n` pseudo-random numbers from a uniform distribution on the interval `[a, b]`.
  - `rnorm(n, mean, sd)` generates `n` pseudo-random numbers from a normal distribution with mean `mean` and standard deviation `sd`.
  - `rexp(n, rate)` generates `n` pseudo-random numbers from an exponential distribution with rate parameter `rate`.
  - `rgamma(n, shape, scale)` generates `n` pseudo-random numbers from a gamma distribution with shape parameter `shape` and scale parameter `scale`.
  - `rlnorm(n, meanlog, sdlog)` generates `n` pseudo-random numbers from a log-normal distribution with mean of the logarithm `meanlog` and standard deviation of the logarithm `sdlog`.

- To ensure the quality and reliability of pseudo-random numbers, some criteria and tests can be applied, such as:

  - The **period** of a pseudo-random number generator, which is the length of the sequence before it repeats itself. A good generator should have a long period, preferably larger than the number of values that can be generated.
  - The **uniformity** of a pseudo-random number generator, which is the degree to which the generated values are evenly distributed over the range of possible values. A good generator should produce values that are close to the expected frequency of the target distribution.
  - The **independence** of a pseudo-random number generator, which is the degree to which the generated values are not correlated or predictable from each other. A good generator should produce values that are not affected by the previous or future values in the sequence.
  - The **randomness** of a pseudo-random number generator, which is the degree to which the generated values are indistinguishable from a truly random sequence. A good generator should pass various statistical tests that check for patterns, anomalies, or deviations from the target distribution.
