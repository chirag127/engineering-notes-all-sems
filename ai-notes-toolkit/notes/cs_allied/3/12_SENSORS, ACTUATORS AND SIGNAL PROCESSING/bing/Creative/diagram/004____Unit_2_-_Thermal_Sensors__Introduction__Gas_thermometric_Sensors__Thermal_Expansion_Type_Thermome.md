## Unit 2 - Thermal Sensors

Thermal sensors are devices that measure temperature or heat flux by converting thermal signals into electrical signals. Thermal sensors have various types, principles, and applications depending on the physical phenomenon they exploit and the environment they operate in. Some of the common types of thermal sensors are:

- **Gas thermometric sensors**: These sensors use the change in pressure, volume, or density of a gas due to temperature change. For example, a gas thermometer consists of a sealed bulb filled with a gas (such as helium or nitrogen) and connected to a manometer. The gas expands or contracts as the temperature changes, causing the pressure and the height of the liquid column in the manometer to vary. Gas thermometric sensors are suitable for measuring very low or very high temperatures, but they are slow, bulky, and sensitive to external pressure changes.

- **Thermal expansion type thermometric sensors**: These sensors use the change in length, area, or volume of a solid or liquid due to temperature change. For example, a bimetallic strip consists of two thin strips of different metals (such as brass and steel) bonded together. The metals have different coefficients of thermal expansion, so they bend or curl as the temperature changes. Bimetallic strips are used in thermostats, thermometers, and fire alarms. Thermal expansion type thermometric sensors are simple, cheap, and reliable, but they have low accuracy, resolution, and sensitivity.

- **Acoustic temperature sensor**: This sensor uses the change in speed of sound in a medium due to temperature change. For example, a quartz crystal resonator consists of a thin slice of quartz crystal sandwiched between two electrodes. The crystal vibrates at a natural frequency when an alternating voltage is applied. The frequency depends on the temperature, as the speed of sound in the crystal changes with temperature. Acoustic temperature sensors are fast, stable, and accurate, but they require complex electronics and calibration.

- **Dielectric constant and refractive index thermo-sensors**: These sensors use the change in dielectric constant or refractive index of a material due to temperature change. For example, a capacitance thermometer consists of a capacitor with a dielectric material (such as air, oil, or plastic) between the plates. The capacitance depends on the temperature, as the dielectric constant of the material changes with temperature. Dielectric constant and refractive index thermo-sensors are sensitive, linear, and easy to integrate, but they are affected by humidity, pressure, and electromagnetic interference.

- **Helium low temperature thermometer**: This sensor uses the change in specific heat of helium gas due to temperature change. For example, a helium gas thermometer consists of a sealed bulb filled with helium gas and connected to a heater and a thermometer. The heater supplies a constant amount of heat to the gas, and the thermometer measures the temperature of the gas. The specific heat of helium gas depends on the temperature, and it has a sharp transition at 2.17 K, the lambda point. Helium low temperature thermometers are used to measure temperatures below 4 K, but they are expensive, complex, and require liquid helium.

- **Nuclear thermometer**: This sensor uses the change in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) or nuclear quadrupole resonance (NQR) of a material due to temperature change. For example, a NMR thermometer consists of a sample of a material (such as copper or silver) placed in a magnetic field and exposed to a radio frequency pulse. The NMR signal depends on the temperature, as the spin-lattice relaxation time of the material changes with temperature. Nuclear thermometers are very accurate, precise, and stable, but they require high magnetic fields, sophisticated electronics, and calibration.

- **Magnetic thermometer**: This sensor uses the change in magnetic properties of a material due to temperature change. For example, a Curie point thermometer consists of a sample of a ferromagnetic material (such as iron or nickel) placed in a magnetic field and heated or cooled. The magnetic susceptibility of the material depends on the temperature, and it drops to zero at the Curie point, the temperature at which the material loses its magnetization. Magnetic thermometers are used to measure temperatures above 1000 K, but they are nonlinear, hysteresis, and sensitive to external magnetic fields.
