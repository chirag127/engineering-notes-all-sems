## Unit 5 - Introduction of Signal Processing

Signal processing is the analysis, interpretation, and manipulation of signals. Signals can be classified in several ways, including:

1. **Continuous vs. Discrete**: Continuous signals are defined for all values of time, while discrete signals are defined only at specific times.
2. **Energy vs. Power**: Energy signals have finite energy, while power signals have finite power.
3. **Mathematical Representation**: Signals can be represented mathematically using functions, equations, or series.

Systems can also be classified in several ways, including:

1. **Continuous vs. Discrete**: Continuous systems operate on continuous signals, while discrete systems operate on discrete signals.
2. **Linear vs. Nonlinear**: Linear systems obey the principle of superposition, while nonlinear systems do not.
3. **Causal vs. Noncausal**: Causal systems depend only on past and present inputs, while noncausal systems can also depend on future inputs.
4. **Stable vs. Unstable**: Stable systems produce bounded outputs for bounded inputs, while unstable systems do not.
5. **Dynamic vs. Static**: Dynamic systems have memory, while static systems do not.
6. **Recursive vs. Nonrecursive**: Recursive systems use feedback, while nonrecursive systems do not.
7. **Time-Invariant vs. Time-Variant**: Time-invariant systems do not change over time, while time-variant systems do.

Spectral density is a measure of the distribution of power in a signal over frequency. Sampling is the process of converting a continuous signal into a discrete signal by taking measurements at regular intervals. Quantization is the process of approximating a continuous signal by a discrete signal. Quantization error is the difference between the original signal and the quantized signal. The Nyquist rate is the minimum sampling rate required to avoid aliasing. Aliasing is the distortion that occurs when a signal is sampled at a rate lower than the Nyquist rate.
