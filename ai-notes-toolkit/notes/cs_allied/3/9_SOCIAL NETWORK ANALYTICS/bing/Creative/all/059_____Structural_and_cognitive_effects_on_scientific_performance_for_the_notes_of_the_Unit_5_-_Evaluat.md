# Structural and cognitive effects on scientific performance

- Scientific performance is the outcome of scientific activities, such as publishing papers, obtaining grants, or receiving citations.
- Scientific performance can be influenced by various factors, such as individual characteristics, environmental conditions, and social networks.
- Social networks are the patterns of relationships among actors (e.g., scientists, institutions, journals) in a scientific field or domain.
- Social network analysis (SNA) is a methodological approach that examines the structure and properties of social networks, such as the size, density, centrality, and clustering of the network.
- SNA can be used to evaluate the impact of social networks on scientific performance, by measuring how the position and role of an actor in the network affects their productivity, quality, or impact of their scientific work.
- SNA can also be used to identify the optimal network structure and configuration that can enhance scientific performance, by comparing different network models and scenarios.
- Structural effects refer to the effects of the network topology, or the shape and arrangement of the network, on scientific performance.
- Cognitive effects refer to the effects of the network content, or the information and knowledge that flow through the network, on scientific performance.
- Structural and cognitive effects can be interrelated and mutually reinforcing, as the network structure can influence the network content, and vice versa.
- Some examples of structural effects on scientific performance are:

  - Centrality: The degree to which an actor is connected to other actors in the network. Higher centrality can indicate higher visibility, prestige, and influence in the network, which can lead to higher scientific performance .
  - Density: The proportion of existing connections among actors in the network. Higher density can indicate higher cohesion, collaboration, and trust in the network, which can lead to higher scientific performance.
  - Clustering: The extent to which actors in the network form subgroups or communities. Higher clustering can indicate higher specialization, diversity, and innovation in the network, which can lead to higher scientific performance.

- Some examples of cognitive effects on scientific performance are:

  - Homophily: The tendency of actors in the network to associate with similar others. Higher homophily can indicate higher similarity, compatibility, and agreement in the network, which can lead to higher scientific performance.
  - Heterophily: The tendency of actors in the network to associate with different others. Higher heterophily can indicate higher complementarity, variety, and creativity in the network, which can lead to higher scientific performance.
  - Transitivity: The extent to which actors in the network share common connections. Higher transitivity can indicate higher diffusion, learning, and feedback in the network, which can lead to higher scientific performance.

- Structural and cognitive effects on scientific performance can vary depending on the context, the level of analysis, and the type of performance indicator.
- For example, centrality can have positive effects on productivity, but negative effects on quality, as highly central actors may have more pressure and competition, and less time and resources, to produce high-quality work .
- Similarly, homophily can have positive effects on cohesion, but negative effects on innovation, as highly homophilous actors may have more consensus and harmony, but less diversity and novelty, to generate innovative work.
- Therefore, SNA can help to identify the trade-offs and synergies among different structural and cognitive effects, and to design and implement strategies that can optimize the network structure and content for improving scientific performance.
