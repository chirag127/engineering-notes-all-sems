### Digital Evidence

- Digital evidence is any information stored or transmitted in binary form that may be relied on in court .
- Digital evidence can be found on various devices, such as computers, hard drives, mobile phones, cameras, etc .
- Digital evidence is often associated with electronic crimes, such as child pornography, credit card fraud, cyberstalking, hacking, etc .
- Digital evidence can be classified into four categories: stored data, transient data, network data and metadata.
  - Stored data is the data that is permanently saved on a device, such as files, folders, emails, etc.
  - Transient data is the data that is temporarily stored in the memory of a device, such as cache, RAM, clipboard, etc.
  - Network data is the data that is transmitted or received over a network, such as packets, logs, protocols, etc.
  - Metadata is the data that describes the characteristics or context of other data, such as timestamps, file names, geolocation, etc.
- Digital evidence can be volatile or non-volatile, depending on whether it can be easily altered or deleted.
  - Volatile evidence is the evidence that can be lost or changed if the device is powered off, disconnected, rebooted, etc. Examples of volatile evidence are transient data and network data.
  - Non-volatile evidence is the evidence that can be preserved even if the device is powered off, disconnected, rebooted, etc. Examples of non-volatile evidence are stored data and metadata.
- Digital evidence can be direct or circumstantial, depending on whether it can prove or imply a fact or event.
  - Direct evidence is the evidence that directly proves a fact or event, such as a video recording of a crime, a confession email, etc.
  - Circumstantial evidence is the evidence that indirectly implies a fact or event, such as a log file of a suspicious activity, a deleted file, etc.
