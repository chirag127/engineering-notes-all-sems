### Circumstantial evidence

Circumstantial evidence is evidence that is not directly related to the fact in question, but rather infers or suggests it based on the surrounding circumstances. Circumstantial evidence can be used to support or refute a hypothesis, but it cannot prove it conclusively. Circumstantial evidence is often used in cyber forensics, as direct evidence may be difficult to obtain or preserve.

Some examples of circumstantial evidence in cyber forensics are:

- The IP address of a device that accessed a website or sent an email. This can indicate the location and identity of the user, but it can also be spoofed or changed by using proxies or VPNs.
- The metadata of a digital file, such as the creation date, modification date, author name, or file format. This can provide information about the origin and history of the file, but it can also be altered or deleted by using tools or techniques.
- The logs of a system or network, such as the firewall, router, server, or application. This can show the activity and events that occurred on the system or network, but it can also be incomplete, inaccurate, or tampered with by hackers or insiders.
- The digital traces of a user, such as the browser history, cookies, cache, bookmarks, or passwords. This can reveal the interests, preferences, habits, and actions of the user, but it can also be cleared, encrypted, or hidden by using software or settings.

Circumstantial evidence can be useful in cyber forensics, but it also has some limitations and challenges, such as:

- It may not be reliable or valid, as it can be manipulated, fabricated, or contaminated by human or technical errors.
- It may not be relevant or sufficient, as it may not directly address the issue or provide enough information to support a conclusion.
- It may not be admissible or acceptable, as it may not meet the legal standards or criteria for evidence in a court of law.

Therefore, circumstantial evidence should be collected, analyzed, and presented with caution and care, and should be corroborated by other sources of evidence, such as direct evidence, testimonial evidence, or physical evidence. Circumstantial evidence can help to establish the context and the probability of a cybercrime, but it cannot prove it beyond a reasonable doubt.
