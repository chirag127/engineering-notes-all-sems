### Reasoning Systems for Categories

- Reasoning is the process of drawing logical conclusions from a set of facts, rules, and beliefs.
- In artificial intelligence, reasoning systems are used to represent and manipulate knowledge, and to infer new information from existing knowledge.
- Reasoning systems can be classified into different categories based on the type of logic, the type of knowledge, and the type of inference they use.
- Some of the common categories of reasoning systems are:

  - **Deductive reasoning systems**: These systems use rules of logic to derive conclusions from a set of premises. For example, if A implies B, and A is true, then B is true. Deductive reasoning systems are sound, meaning that they never produce false conclusions from true premises, but they may be incomplete, meaning that they may not produce all possible conclusions from the given premises. Deductive reasoning systems are often used for formal verification, theorem proving, and logic programming .
  - **Inductive reasoning systems**: These systems use examples or observations to infer general rules or patterns. For example, if all the birds observed so far can fly, then one may induce that all birds can fly. Inductive reasoning systems are not sound, meaning that they may produce false conclusions from true premises, but they are often complete, meaning that they can produce all possible conclusions from the given premises. Inductive reasoning systems are often used for machine learning, data mining, and natural language processing .
  - **Abductive reasoning systems**: These systems use a set of facts and a goal to infer the most likely explanation or hypothesis. For example, if one observes that the grass is wet, and the goal is to explain why, then one may abduct that it rained. Abductive reasoning systems are not sound, meaning that they may produce false explanations from true facts, and they are not complete, meaning that they may not produce all possible explanations from the given facts. Abductive reasoning systems are often used for diagnosis, planning, and creativity .
  - **Common sense reasoning systems**: These systems use general knowledge about the world and human behavior to infer new information or to resolve ambiguities. For example, if one hears that John went to the bank, then one may use common sense to infer that he went to the financial institution, not the river bank. Common sense reasoning systems are not sound, meaning that they may produce false information from true knowledge, and they are not complete, meaning that they may not produce all possible information from the given knowledge. Common sense reasoning systems are often used for natural language understanding, computer vision, and human-computer interaction.
  - **Monotonic reasoning systems**: These systems use knowledge that is consistent and does not change over time. For example, if one knows that A implies B, and A is true, then B is true, and this will remain true even if new knowledge is added. Monotonic reasoning systems are sound, meaning that they never produce false conclusions from true premises, but they may be incomplete, meaning that they may not produce all possible conclusions from the given premises. Monotonic reasoning systems are often used for mathematical logic, formal methods, and deductive databases .
  - **Non-monotonic reasoning systems**: These systems use knowledge that is uncertain or may change over time. For example, if one knows that A implies B, and A is true, then B is true, but this may not remain true if new knowledge is added that contradicts or revises the previous knowledge. Non-monotonic reasoning systems are not sound, meaning that they may produce false conclusions from true premises, and they are not complete, meaning that they may not produce all possible conclusions from the given premises. Non-monotonic reasoning systems are often used for commonsense reasoning, default reasoning, and belief revision .
  - **Procedural reasoning systems**: These systems use a set of actions or procedures to achieve a goal or to solve a problem. For example, if one wants to bake a cake, then one may use a procedural reasoning system to follow a recipe or a plan. Procedural reasoning systems are not sound, meaning that they may produce incorrect actions from true goals, and they are not complete, meaning that they may not produce all possible actions from the given goals. Procedural reasoning systems are often used for planning, control, and robotics.
