## Unit 3 - KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATION

- Knowledge representation is the process of encoding information about a domain in a form that can be used by a computer system for reasoning and problem-solving.
- Knowledge representation involves choosing a suitable representation language, defining the syntax and semantics of the language, and constructing a knowledge base that contains facts and rules about the domain.
- Some of the goals of knowledge representation are:
  - Expressiveness: the ability to represent complex and diverse knowledge in a natural and concise way.
  - Efficiency: the ability to perform inference and manipulation of knowledge in a fast and scalable way.
  - Adequacy: the ability to capture the relevant and essential aspects of the domain and avoid unnecessary or misleading details.
  - Learnability: the ability to acquire and update knowledge from various sources, such as observation, communication, or feedback.
- Some of the challenges of knowledge representation are:
  - Uncertainty: the difficulty of dealing with incomplete, inconsistent, or noisy information.
  - Common sense: the difficulty of encoding general and implicit knowledge that humans use in everyday reasoning.
  - Context: the difficulty of taking into account the situational and temporal aspects of knowledge and reasoning.
  - Evolution: the difficulty of adapting to changes in the domain or the user's needs and preferences.
- Some of the common types of knowledge representation languages are:
  - Logic: a formal language that uses symbols and rules to represent and manipulate propositions and arguments. Examples are propositional logic, first-order logic, modal logic, and description logic.
  - Semantic networks: a graphical language that uses nodes and links to represent concepts and relations. Examples are conceptual graphs, frames, and ontologies.
  - Production rules: a declarative language that uses conditional statements to represent and apply knowledge. Examples are if-then rules, decision trees, and expert systems.
  - Probabilistic models: a mathematical language that uses variables and functions to represent and quantify uncertainty and dependence. Examples are Bayesian networks, Markov models, and hidden Markov models.
