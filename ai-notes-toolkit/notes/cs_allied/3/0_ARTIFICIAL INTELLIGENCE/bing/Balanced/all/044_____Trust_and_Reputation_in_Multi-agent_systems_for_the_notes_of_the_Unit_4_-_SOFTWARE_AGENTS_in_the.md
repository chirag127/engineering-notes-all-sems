# Trust and Reputation in Multi-Agent Systems

- Trust and reputation are important concepts for effective interactions in open multi-agent systems (MAS), where agents may belong to different owners and have different goals and behaviors.
- Trust is the degree of belief that an agent has about the reliability, honesty, or competence of another agent, based on its own direct experiences or observations.
- Reputation is the collective opinion or evaluation of an agent by a group of other agents, based on their indirect experiences or information from other sources.
- Trust and reputation can help agents to select suitable partners, avoid malicious or incompetent agents, and cope with uncertainty and risk in dynamic and complex environments.
- Several models and mechanisms have been proposed in the literature to represent, compute, and update trust and reputation in MAS, based on different assumptions, criteria, and methods.
- Some of the well-known models are:
  - Marsh's model: a cognitive model that defines trust as a subjective and context-dependent concept, based on utility, importance, and belief.
  - Sen et al.'s model: a game-theoretic model that defines trust as the probability of cooperation, based on the history of interactions and the expected payoffs.
  - SPORAS: a statistical model that defines reputation as a numerical score, based on the frequency and recency of ratings from other agents.
  - eBay's model: a simple model that defines reputation as the difference between positive and negative feedbacks from other agents, based on a binary rating system.
  - Certified reputation: a cryptographic model that defines reputation as a verifiable certificate, based on a trusted third party or a distributed ledger.
- Trust and reputation models can be evaluated and compared based on various criteria, such as:
  - Accuracy: how well the model reflects the true trustworthiness or reputation of an agent, based on the available evidence.
  - Robustness: how well the model resists to attacks or manipulations by malicious or dishonest agents, such as lying, collusion, or sybil attacks.
  - Scalability: how well the model handles the growth of the number and diversity of agents and interactions in the system, without compromising the performance or quality of the model.
  - Adaptability: how well the model adapts to the changes in the environment or the behavior of the agents, such as learning, forgetting, or updating.
  - Generality: how well the model applies to different domains, scenarios, and types of agents and interactions, without requiring too much domain-specific knowledge or customization.
