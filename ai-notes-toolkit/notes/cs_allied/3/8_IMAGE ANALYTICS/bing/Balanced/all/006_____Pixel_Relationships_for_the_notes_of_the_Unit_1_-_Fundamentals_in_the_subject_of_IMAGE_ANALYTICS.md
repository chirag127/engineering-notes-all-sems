# Pixel Relationships

- A pixel is a discrete unit of information that represents the intensity or color of an image at a specific location.
- Pixels are arranged in a two-dimensional grid, forming an image matrix or raster.
- The size of the image matrix is determined by the number of rows and columns of pixels, also known as the image resolution.
- The value of each pixel is determined by the number of bits used to store it, also known as the image depth or bit depth.
- The value of each pixel can range from 0 to 2^n - 1, where n is the number of bits per pixel.
- The value of each pixel can be interpreted as a grayscale level, a color component, or an index to a color palette, depending on the image format and color model.
- Pixels are related to each other by their spatial position and neighborhood.
- The spatial position of a pixel is defined by its row and column coordinates in the image matrix, also known as the pixel address or location.
- The neighborhood of a pixel is defined by a set of pixels that are adjacent or close to it in the image matrix, also known as the pixel window or region of interest.
- The neighborhood of a pixel can be specified by its shape, size, and orientation, such as a square, a circle, or a cross.
- The neighborhood of a pixel can be used to perform local operations on the image, such as filtering, enhancement, segmentation, or feature extraction.
- Pixels are also related to each other by their intensity or color similarity or dissimilarity.
- The intensity or color similarity or dissimilarity of two pixels can be measured by a distance function, such as the Euclidean distance, the Manhattan distance, or the Mahalanobis distance.
- The distance function can be used to perform global operations on the image, such as clustering, classification, or compression.
