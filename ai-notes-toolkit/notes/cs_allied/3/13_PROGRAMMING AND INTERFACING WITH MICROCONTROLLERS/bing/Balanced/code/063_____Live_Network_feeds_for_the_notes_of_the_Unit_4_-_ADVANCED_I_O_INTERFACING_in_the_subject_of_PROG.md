- I/O interfacing is the process of connecting input and output devices to a microprocessor or a microcontroller.
- I/O interfacing involves some key factors to match with the device requirements and microprocessor signals, such as address decoding, data transfer, control signals, timing, and interrupt handling.
- Address decoding is the technique of generating chip select signals for each device based on the address lines of the microprocessor. It can be done using logic gates, decoders, or programmable logic devices (PLDs).
- Data transfer is the movement of data between the microprocessor and the I/O device. It can be done in three modes: parallel, serial, or wireless.
- Parallel data transfer involves sending or receiving multiple bits of data at the same time using multiple data lines. It is faster but requires more hardware and wiring.
- Serial data transfer involves sending or receiving one bit of data at a time using a single data line. It is slower but requires less hardware and wiring.
- Wireless data transfer involves sending or receiving data using electromagnetic waves, such as radio, infrared, or optical signals. It eliminates the need for physical connections but introduces issues such as interference, security, and power consumption.
- Control signals are the signals that control the operation of the I/O device, such as read, write, enable, or disable. They can be generated by the microprocessor or by external circuits, such as buffers, latches, or transceivers.
- Timing is the synchronization of the microprocessor and the I/O device. It can be done in two ways: synchronous or asynchronous.
- Synchronous timing means that the microprocessor and the I/O device share a common clock signal and operate at the same speed. It simplifies the design but limits the performance and flexibility.
- Asynchronous timing means that the microprocessor and the I/O device have independent clock signals and operate at different speeds. It increases the performance and flexibility but complicates the design and requires additional circuits, such as handshaking or polling.
- Interrupt handling is the mechanism of allowing the microprocessor to temporarily suspend its normal operation and service an external event, such as an I/O request. It can be done in two ways: vectored or non-vectored.
- Vectored interrupt means that the microprocessor receives a unique code or vector from the I/O device that indicates the source and type of the interrupt. It reduces the overhead and latency of the interrupt service routine (ISR).
- Non-vectored interrupt means that the microprocessor does not receive any code or vector from the I/O device and has to determine the source and type of the interrupt by polling or testing. It increases the overhead and latency of the ISR.
