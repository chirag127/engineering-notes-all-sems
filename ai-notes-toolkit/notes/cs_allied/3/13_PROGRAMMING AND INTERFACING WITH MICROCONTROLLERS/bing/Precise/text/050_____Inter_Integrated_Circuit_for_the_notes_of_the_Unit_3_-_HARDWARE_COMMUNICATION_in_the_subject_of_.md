### Inter-Integrated Circuit (I2C)

- Inter-Integrated Circuit (I2C) is a multi-master, multi-slave, packet-switched, single-ended, serial computer bus.
- It was invented by Philips Semiconductor (now NXP Semiconductors) in 1982.
- I2C is used for attaching lower-speed peripheral ICs to processors and microcontrollers in short-distance, intra-board communication.
- I2C uses only two bidirectional open-drain lines, Serial Data Line (SDA) and Serial Clock Line (SCL), pulled up with resistors.
- Typical data rates for I2C are 100 kbit/s (standard mode) and 400 kbit/s (fast mode).
- High-speed mode (3.4 Mbit/s) and ultra-fast mode (5 Mbit/s) are also available.
- I2C is widely used in various applications such as EEPROMs, thermal sensors, and real-time clocks.
- I2C is a synchronous protocol, meaning that the clock signal is generated by the master device and is used to synchronize the communication with the slave devices.
- The I2C protocol defines a set of rules for transmitting and receiving data, including addressing, arbitration, and acknowledgment.
- I2C is a simple and efficient protocol that can be used to communicate with a wide range of devices.
