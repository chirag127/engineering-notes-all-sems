# ADC for the notes of the Unit 2 - SOFTWARE FRAMEWORKS in the subject of PROGRAMMING AND INTERFACING WITH MICROCONTROLLERS

- ADC stands for Analog to Digital Converter, which is a device that converts an analog voltage to a digital value that can be used by a microcontroller.
- Many microcontrollers have built-in ADCs, which means you can use a single device to both digitize analog signals and manipulate the results.
- Analog signals are continuous and vary in amplitude and frequency, while digital signals are discrete and have only two states: high or low.
- Analog signals can come from various sources, such as sensors, audio, video, or physical phenomena, and they need to be converted to digital signals before they can be processed by a microcontroller.
- The ADC has several parameters that determine its performance and suitability for different applications, such as resolution, sampling rate, accuracy, linearity, and noise.
- Resolution is the number of bits that the ADC can output for each input voltage. It determines the number of discrete levels that the ADC can distinguish. For example, a 10-bit ADC can output 1024 levels, while a 16-bit ADC can output 65536 levels.
- Sampling rate is the frequency at which the ADC takes samples of the input voltage and converts them to digital values. It determines how fast the ADC can capture the changes in the input signal. For example, a 1 Msps ADC can take one million samples per second, while a 1 Gsps ADC can take one billion samples per second.
- Accuracy is the degree of closeness between the ADC output and the true value of the input voltage. It depends on various factors, such as offset, gain, and nonlinearity errors, as well as external noise and interference. Accuracy can be expressed as an absolute error (in volts) or a relative error (in percentage or parts per million).
- Linearity is the measure of how well the ADC output follows a straight line as the input voltage changes. It indicates how consistent the ADC is in converting different input voltages. Linearity can be expressed as a deviation from the ideal straight line (in volts or percentage).
- Noise is the unwanted variation in the ADC output that is not related to the input voltage. It can be caused by internal or external sources, such as thermal noise, quantization noise, clock jitter, or power supply noise. Noise can affect the accuracy and resolution of the ADC output.
- There are different types of ADCs, such as successive approximation register (SAR) ADCs, sigma-delta ADCs, and pipeline ADCs, each with its own advantages and disadvantages in terms of speed, resolution, power consumption, and complexity.
- SAR ADCs use a binary search algorithm to compare the input voltage with a series of reference voltages and output the corresponding digital value. They are fast, simple, and low power, but they have limited resolution and accuracy.
- Sigma-delta ADCs use an oversampling technique to filter and integrate the input voltage and output a stream of 1-bit values that represent the average of the input voltage. They are high resolution, high accuracy, and low noise, but they are slow, complex, and high power.
- Pipeline ADCs use a cascade of stages, each consisting of a sample-and-hold circuit, a sub-ADC, and a sub-DAC, to convert the input voltage in parallel and output a digital value. They are high speed, high resolution, and moderate power, but they are complex and have latency.
- ADCs are integrated into microcontrollers, FPGAs, processors, or systems on chips (SoCs) to provide analog and digital sensing and processing capabilities for various applications, such as automatic control, measurements, data acquisition, and signal processing.
