### Issues for the notes of the Unit 6 - Introduction to Visualization in the subject of INTRODUCTION TO DATA ANALYTICS AND VISUALIZATION

- Data visualization is the process of transforming data into graphical or visual representations that can communicate insights, patterns, trends, and relationships more effectively than numbers or text alone.
- Data visualization can also pose some challenges or issues that need to be addressed carefully to avoid misleading, confusing, or distorting the intended message or information. Some of these issues are    :
  - **Correlation is not causation**: Correlations make us see false relationships or imply causality where there is none. For example, a graph showing a positive correlation between ice cream sales and shark attacks does not mean that ice cream causes shark attacks or vice versa. Correlations should be interpreted with caution and supported by other evidence or logic.
  - **Similarities now do not mean similarities forever**: On top of false correlations, a relationship happening within our observational set does not imply a relationship always. For example, a graph showing a similarity between two variables at a certain point in time does not mean that they will remain similar in the future or in the past. Data visualization should account for temporal changes and variability in the data.
  - **Axes make all the difference**: The size and number of axes can change the way we interpret data. For example, a graph with a truncated or distorted y-axis can exaggerate or minimize the differences between data points or groups. A graph with too many axes can confuse or overwhelm the viewers. Data visualization should use appropriate and consistent scales and labels for the axes and avoid unnecessary or misleading manipulations.
  - **Abusing the Law of Large Numbers (LLN)**: The LLN states that as the sample size increases, the sample mean approaches the population mean. However, this does not mean that larger samples are always better or more representative than smaller samples. For example, a large sample that is biased or skewed can produce misleading results or conclusions. Data visualization should consider the quality and distribution of the data, not just the quantity.
  - **Lack of better data visualization literacy**: A lack of proper knowledge of data visualization can do more harm than good. First, it confuses the audience, or worse, it misleads them. Additionally, a poorly structured data visualization denotes a loss of time and effort, which delays the decision-making process in deadline-driven projects. Data visualization should follow best practices and principles of design, clarity, accuracy, and ethics.
  - **Data quality and accessibility**: Data visualization is often framed as a solution to the data-access problem, but it also depends on the availability and reliability of the data. Data quality and accessibility issues can include missing, incomplete, inaccurate, outdated, inconsistent, or incompatible data, as well as data privacy, security, and ownership concerns. Data visualization should ensure that the data is valid, relevant, and trustworthy, and that the data sources are transparent and credible.
  - **Choosing the right format and tool**: Data visualization can be done in various formats and tools, such as charts, graphs, maps, dashboards, infographics, animations, etc. However, not all formats and tools are suitable for all types of data or purposes. Choosing the wrong format or tool can result in ineffective, inefficient, or inappropriate data visualization. Data visualization should match the format and tool to the nature and goal of the data, as well as the needs and preferences of the audience.
