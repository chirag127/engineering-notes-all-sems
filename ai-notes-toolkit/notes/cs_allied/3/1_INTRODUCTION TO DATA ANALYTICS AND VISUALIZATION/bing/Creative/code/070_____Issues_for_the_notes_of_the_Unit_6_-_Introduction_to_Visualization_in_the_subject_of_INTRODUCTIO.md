### Issues for the notes of the Unit 6 - Introduction to Visualization in the subject of INTRODUCTION TO DATA ANALYTICS AND VISUALIZATION

- Data visualization is the process of transforming data into graphical or pictorial representations that can communicate information effectively and efficiently to the audience.
- Data visualization can have many benefits, such as revealing patterns, trends, outliers, correlations, and causalities in the data, as well as enhancing the aesthetic appeal and engagement of the presentation.
- However, data visualization can also have many issues, such as misleading, confusing, or deceiving the audience, violating ethical principles, or failing to meet the objectives of the analysis   .
- Some of the common issues for data visualization are:

  - **Correlation is not causation**: Correlations make us see false relationships between variables that may not have any causal link. For example, a graph showing a positive correlation between ice cream sales and shark attacks does not imply that eating ice cream causes shark attacks, or vice versa.
  - **Similarities now do not mean similarities forever**: On top of false correlations, a relationship happening within our observational set does not imply a relationship always. For example, a graph showing a positive correlation between GDP and life expectancy in the current year does not mean that this relationship will hold in the future, or that it was true in the past.
  - **Axes make all the difference**: The size and number of axes can change the way we interpret data. For example, a graph showing a linear trend can appear to be exponential or logarithmic if the axes are not scaled properly, or a graph showing a small difference can appear to be dramatic if the axes are truncated or manipulated  .
  - **Abusing the Law of Large Numbers (LLN)**: The LLN states that as the sample size increases, the sample mean approaches the population mean. However, this does not mean that any large sample is representative of the population, or that any small sample is unreliable. For example, a graph showing the average height of 1000 people from a certain country may not reflect the true height distribution of the entire population, or a graph showing the average income of 10 people from a certain city may not be inaccurate or irrelevant.
  - **Misleading color contrast**: Color is among the most persuasive design elements. Even subtle shade variations elicit strong emotional responses. In data visualization, high degrees of color contrast may cause viewers to believe that value disparities are greater than they really are. For example, a map showing the political preferences of different regions using red and blue colors may exaggerate the differences or hide the nuances in the data.
  - **Using inappropriate formats**: The nature of the data usually dictates the format of the visualization. The most important characteristic is whether the data is qualitative (it describes or categorizes) or quantitative (meaning, it’s measurable). For example, a pie chart is suitable for showing the proportions of different categories, but not for showing the trends over time or the comparisons between groups. Similarly, a line chart is suitable for showing the changes over time or the relationships between variables, but not for showing the distributions or the frequencies.
  - **Lack of better data visualization literacy**: A lack of proper knowledge of data visualization can do more harm than good. First, it confuses the audience, or worse, it misleads them. Additionally, a poorly structured data visualization denotes a loss of time and effort, which delays the decision-making process in deadline-driven projects. For example, a graph that uses unclear labels, inconsistent units, ambiguous symbols, or unnecessary elements can make the data hard to understand or interpret.
  - **Violating ethical principles**: Data visualization can have ethical implications, such as respecting the privacy and confidentiality of the data sources, avoiding bias and discrimination, acknowledging the limitations and uncertainties of the data, and being transparent and accountable for the data analysis and presentation. For example, a graph that reveals the personal information of the data subjects, or that manipulates the data to support a certain agenda, or that hides the errors or gaps in the data, or that does not cite the data sources, can violate the ethical principles of data visualization.
