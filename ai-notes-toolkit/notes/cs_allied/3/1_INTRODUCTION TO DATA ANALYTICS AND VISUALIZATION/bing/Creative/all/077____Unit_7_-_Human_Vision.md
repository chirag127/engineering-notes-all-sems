# Unit 7 - Human Vision

Human vision is the ability to perceive and interpret visual information from the environment. It involves the interaction of the eyes, the brain, and the nervous system. Human vision has the following features and limits:

- **Binocular vision**: Humans have two eyes that are located on the left and right sides of the face. Each eye forms a different image on the retina, which is the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. The brain combines these two images to create a single perception of vision that allows humans to see objects in three dimensions and estimate their distance and depth .
- **Eye structure**: The human eye is a complex organ that consists of several parts, such as the cornea, the pupil, the iris, the lens, the vitreous, the retina, the optic nerve, and the extraocular muscles. Each part has a specific function in the process of vision . For example, the cornea is the clear front layer of the eye that bends light to help the eye focus. The pupil is the opening in the center of the iris that controls the amount of light that enters the eye. The lens is the transparent structure behind the pupil that changes shape to focus light onto the retina. The retina is the layer of nerve cells that converts light into electrical signals that are sent to the brain via the optic nerve. The extraocular muscles are the six muscles that control the movements of the eye .
- **Color perception**: Humans can perceive a range of colors that are derived from the combination of three primary colors: red, green, and blue. The retina contains two types of photoreceptor cells that are sensitive to different wavelengths of light: rods and cones. Rods are more abundant and work better in dim light, but they cannot distinguish colors. Cones are less numerous and work better in bright light, and they are responsible for color vision. There are three types of cones that respond to different ranges of wavelengths: S-cones (short-wavelength, blue), M-cones (medium-wavelength, green), and L-cones (long-wavelength, red). The brain interprets the signals from the cones to create the perception of color .
- **Visual acuity**: Visual acuity is the measure of how well a person can see the details of an object at a certain distance. It depends on several factors, such as the size and contrast of the object, the lighting conditions, the refractive errors of the eye, and the health of the eye and the brain. Visual acuity is usually expressed as a fraction, such as 20/20, which means that a person can see an object at 20 feet that a normal person can see at 20 feet. A lower fraction, such as 20/40, means that a person can see an object at 20 feet that a normal person can see at 40 feet, indicating a poorer vision.
- **Visual field**: Visual field is the area of the environment that a person can see at any given moment without moving the eyes or the head. It is determined by the position and angle of the eyes, the size and shape of the eyelids, and the presence of any obstructions or defects in the eye or the brain. The normal visual field for each eye is about 160 degrees horizontally and 135 degrees vertically, but the two eyes overlap to create a binocular visual field of about 200 degrees horizontally and 135 degrees vertically. The visual field can be affected by various conditions, such as glaucoma, stroke, brain tumor, or eye injury, that can cause blind spots or reduced peripheral vision.
