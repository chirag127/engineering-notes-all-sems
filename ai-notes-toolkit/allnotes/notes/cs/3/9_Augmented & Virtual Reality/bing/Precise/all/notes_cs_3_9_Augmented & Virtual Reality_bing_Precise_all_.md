- The development of virtual reality (VR) began in the 1950s .
- The first VR head-mounted display (HMD) system, The Sword of Damocles, was invented in 1968 by computer scientist Ivan Sutherland and his student Bob Sproull .
- The term “virtual reality” was popularized by Jaron Lanier in the 1980s .
- Morton Heilig is considered a pioneer in the field of VR, having created the first immersive cinema in 1962 .
- Morton Heilig also invented the Sensorama in 1957, which is considered one of the earliest VR systems .
- The role of the federal government, particularly the Department of Defense and the National Science Foundation, was significant in the early research and development of VR technology in the United States .
- VPL Research developed a range of VR equipment .
- Between 1986-1989, Furness developed a flight simulator known as the Super Cockpit, which featured computer-generated 3D maps, advanced infrared and radar imagery, and real-time audio and visual feedback for the pilot .
- The history of VR can be traced back to the 1800s, with the invention of the first stereoscope in 1838, which used twin mirrors to project a single image .

# Haptic Displays

Haptic displays are interfaces that deliver haptic feedback, typically by stimulating somatic receptors to generate a sensation of touch. The rendered feedback is generally categorized into cutaneous as well as kinesthetic or proprioceptive feedback, respectively .

Haptic display represents a promising interface for human–computer interaction tools that provides users with nuanced information in a virtual environment. Humans utilize tactile and haptic information to determine object properties using thousands of mechanoreceptors located in the hands .

Haptic displays can be categorized by the type of stimuli/output they generate, and correspondingly by the type of sensory receptors that are stimulated. Kinesthetic display is concerned with the rendering of forces. The latter are generated based on computational models, remote interaction, recordings or data-driven approaches .

Due to the amount of information required to adequately represent surface and object characteristics, haptic displays (particularly force displays) require a more complete model of the world than worlds displayed only visually and/or aurally .

Haptic display devices range from small units that sit on the desktop or are worn on the hand to large robotic devices capable of lifting a person off the ground. Because there are so many different aspects to haptic feedback (taction, proprioception, thermo-reception, electroreception, etc.) and so many different body parts that the display can be applied to, there is a wide range of haptic display devices .

Haptic technology (also kinaesthetic communication or 3D touch) is technology that can create an experience of touch by applying forces, vibrations, or motions to the user. These technologies can be used to create virtual objects in a computer simulation, to control virtual objects, and to enhance remote control of machines and devices .

# Input Device Characteristics

Input devices are hardware components that allow users to interact with a computer or other digital device by providing input or control signals. In the context of 3D user interfaces and virtual and augmented reality, input devices play a crucial role in enabling users to interact with and manipulate virtual objects and environments.

Some important characteristics of input devices for 3D user interfaces and virtual and augmented reality include:

1. **Degrees of Freedom (DOF):** The number of independent movements or actions that an input device can detect and transmit. For example, a 6DOF device can detect and transmit 3 translational movements (x, y, z) and 3 rotational movements (pitch, yaw, roll).

2. **Tracking:** The ability of an input device to track the position and orientation of the user or the device itself in 3D space. Tracking can be achieved through various technologies, such as optical, magnetic, or inertial tracking.

3. **Resolution:** The smallest detectable change in position or orientation that an input device can detect and transmit. Higher resolution input devices provide more precise and accurate control.

4. **Latency:** The time delay between a user's action and the corresponding response from the input device or the computer system. Low latency is important for providing a responsive and immersive user experience.

5. **Ergonomics:** The design of an input device with respect to the comfort, safety, and ease of use for the user. Ergonomically designed input devices can reduce fatigue and strain and improve the user experience.

6. **Compatibility:** The ability of an input device to work with different computer systems, software, and virtual and augmented reality platforms. Compatibility can be an important consideration when selecting input devices for use in a particular application or environment.

These are some of the key characteristics to consider when selecting and using input devices for 3D user interfaces and virtual and augmented reality applications. Understanding these characteristics can help to ensure that the chosen input devices provide the desired level of control, precision, and user experience.

### World Coordinate

World Coordinate is a term used in the field of Augmented and Virtual Reality. It refers to the coordinate system that is used to represent the position and orientation of virtual objects in a 3D space.

1. In a virtual environment, the world coordinate system is used to define the position and orientation of virtual objects in relation to the virtual world.
2. The world coordinate system is typically a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system, with the X, Y, and Z axes representing the width, height, and depth of the virtual world, respectively.
3. The origin of the world coordinate system is usually located at the center of the virtual world, with the positive X-axis pointing to the right, the positive Y-axis pointing up, and the positive Z-axis pointing towards the viewer.

# Cullers and Occluders

Cullers and occluders are techniques used in computer graphics to improve the performance of rendering 3D scenes. These techniques are used to reduce the number of objects that need to be rendered, thus reducing the computational load on the graphics hardware.

## Cullers

Culling is the process of removing objects from the rendering pipeline that are not visible to the camera. This can be done in several ways, including:

- **View frustum culling:** Objects outside the camera's view frustum (the volume of space visible to the camera) are not rendered.

- **Backface culling:** The backfaces of objects (the sides facing away from the camera) are not rendered.

- **Occlusion culling:** Objects that are occluded (hidden) by other objects in the scene are not rendered.

## Occluders

Occlusion culling is the process of determining which objects in a 3D scene are hidden from view by other objects. This is done by using occluders, which are objects that can potentially block the view of other objects. Occluders can be static (such as walls or terrain) or dynamic (such as moving objects).

Occlusion culling can be performed using several techniques, including:

- **Hardware occlusion queries:** The graphics hardware can be used to determine if an object is occluded by other objects in the scene.

- **Software occlusion culling:** Occlusion culling can be performed using software algorithms, such as the Hierarchical Z-Buffer or the Hierarchical Occlusion Map.

Cullers and occluders are important techniques for improving the performance of 3D rendering, especially in complex scenes with many objects. By reducing the number of objects that need to be rendered, these techniques can help to maintain high frame rates and smooth, responsive interaction in virtual and augmented reality applications.

# Augmented Reality Methods

Augmented reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the user's perception of the real world by overlaying digital information onto it. There are several methods used to achieve this, including:

1. **Marker-based AR:** This method uses a physical marker, such as a QR code or image, to trigger the display of digital information. The device's camera is used to recognize the marker, and the digital content is then overlaid onto the real world in the correct position relative to the marker.

2. **Markerless AR:** This method does not rely on a physical marker, but instead uses the device's sensors, such as the accelerometer and gyroscope, to determine the position and orientation of the device. The digital content is then overlaid onto the real world based on this information.

3. **Projection-based AR:** This method involves projecting digital content onto a physical surface, such as a wall or table. The digital content is then visible to the user without the need for a device.

4. **Superimposition-based AR:** This method replaces the view of the real world with a digitally augmented view. The device's camera is used to capture the real world, and the digital content is then overlaid onto the image, replacing the original view.

These are some of the most common methods used in augmented reality, each with its own advantages and limitations. It is important to choose the right method for the specific application to achieve the best results.

# Evaluating AR Systems

2. **Latency**: Latency refers to the delay between the user's actions and the corresponding response from the AR system. Low latency is important for creating a responsive and interactive experience.
