

## Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS

- Virtual reality (VR) is a computer-generated simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with in a seemingly real or physical way by a person using special equipment, such as a headset, gloves, or controllers.
- Virtual environments (VEs) are specific types of VR that aim to create a realistic and immersive representation of a real or imagined world, where the user can navigate, explore, and manipulate objects.
- VR and VEs have many applications in various domains, such as entertainment, education, training, health care, engineering, and art.
- VR and VEs are based on several technologies and concepts, such as computer graphics, human-computer interaction, perception, presence, and immersion.
- Computer graphics are the methods and techniques for creating and displaying images, animations, and effects on a screen, using mathematical models and algorithms.
- Human-computer interaction (HCI) is the study and design of how humans and computers communicate and interact with each other, using various input and output devices, such as keyboards, mice, touchscreens, voice, gestures, etc.
- Perception is the process of acquiring, interpreting, and organizing sensory information from the external world, such as vision, hearing, touch, etc.
- Presence is the subjective feeling of being in a VE, as if it were the actual physical environment, and not mediated by a computer.
- Immersion is the degree to which a VE can provide a realistic and engaging sensory experience, and block out the stimuli from the real world. Immersion depends on factors such as the quality of the graphics, the field of view, the sound, the haptics, etc.



### The historical development of VR

- Virtual reality (VR) is the simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with in a seemingly real way by a person using special equipment, such as a headset and gloves.
- The history of VR can be traced back to the 1800s, when the first stereoscope was invented by Charles Wheatstone in 1838, using twin mirrors to project a single image that appeared to have depth.
- In the 1950s, Morton Heilig, a filmmaker and inventor, created the Sensorama, a multimedia device that stimulated multiple senses, such as sight, sound, smell, and touch, to create an immersive experience. He also patented the first head-mounted display (HMD) in 1960, called the Telesphere Mask, which showed stereoscopic images and sound.
- In 1968, Ivan Sutherland and Bob Sproull developed the first VR HMD system, called the Sword of Damocles, which was connected to a computer and displayed simple wireframe graphics that changed according to the user's head movements.
- In the 1970s and 1980s, VR research and development was mainly driven by the military and academic institutions, such as the US Department of Defense, NASA, and MIT, which created various VR systems for training, simulation, and visualization purposes. Some examples are the Aspen Movie Map, the VIEW system, and the Super Cockpit.
- In the late 1980s and early 1990s, VR became more popular and accessible to the public, thanks to the efforts of entrepreneurs and companies, such as Jaron Lanier, who coined the term "virtual reality" and founded VPL Research, which produced VR products, such as the DataGlove and the EyePhone . Other notable VR devices from this era include the Virtuality arcade machines, the Sega VR headset, and the Nintendo Virtual Boy.
- In the 2000s and 2010s, VR technology improved significantly, with advances in graphics, processing power, display resolution, motion tracking, and haptic feedback. Some of the most influential VR products from this period are the Google Cardboard, the Oculus Rift, the HTC Vive, the PlayStation VR, and the Samsung Gear VR, which made VR more affordable and immersive for consumers and developers.
- Today, VR is used for various applications, such as entertainment, education, health care, tourism, social interaction, and art. VR is also evolving to incorporate more realistic and interactive elements, such as eye tracking, facial expression recognition, and brain-computer interfaces. VR is expected to grow and expand in the future, as new technologies and innovations emerge.

## Unit 2 - Hardware Technologies for 3D User Interfaces

  - Aesthetics: the appearance, style, and attractiveness of the device.



### Visual Displays

Visual displays are output devices that present graphical information to the user in 3D user interfaces. They can vary in terms of size, shape, resolution, field of view, field of regard, stereoscopy, and immersion. Some of the main types of visual displays used in 3D user interfaces are :

- **Monitors**: These are flat-screen displays that show 2D or 3D images on a rectangular surface. They are usually placed on a desk or a wall and have a limited field of view and field of regard. They can use different technologies such as LCD, LED, OLED, or plasma to produce the images. Monitors can be used for desktop or windowed 3D user interfaces, where the user interacts with a 3D scene through a 2D window on the screen.
- **Surround-screen displays**: These are large-scale displays that consist of multiple monitors or projectors arranged in a circular or cylindrical configuration around the user. They provide a wider field of view and field of regard than monitors, and can create a more immersive experience for the user. They can also support stereoscopic vision by using polarized glasses or shutter glasses. Surround-screen displays can be used for immersive or semi-immersive 3D user interfaces, where the user is surrounded by a 3D scene and can interact with it using various input devices.
- **Workbenches**: These are table-like displays that show 3D images on a horizontal or tilted surface. They are usually viewed from above or from an oblique angle, and can support stereoscopic vision by using polarized glasses or shutter glasses. They can also support head tracking and gesture recognition to provide a more natural and intuitive interaction. Workbenches can be used for collaborative or co-located 3D user interfaces, where multiple users can share and manipulate a 3D scene on the same display.
- **Hemispherical displays**: These are dome-like displays that show 3D images on a curved surface that covers a large portion of the user's field of view and field of regard. They can be either projected or backlit, and can support stereoscopic vision by using polarized glasses or shutter glasses. They can also support head tracking and spatial sound to enhance the immersion and realism. Hemispherical displays can be used for immersive or semi-immersive 3D user interfaces, where the user is inside or under a 3D scene and can interact with it using various input devices.
- **Head-mounted displays**: These are wearable displays that show 3D images on a small screen in front of each eye. They provide a high degree of immersion and realism, as they occlude the real world and move with the user's head. They can also support head tracking and spatial sound to create a more convincing 3D scene. Head-mounted displays can be used for fully-immersive or semi-immersive 3D user interfaces, where the user is completely or partially immersed in a 3D scene and can interact with it using various input devices.
- **Arm-mounted displays**: These are wearable displays that show 3D images on a small screen attached to the user's arm. They provide a low degree of immersion and realism, as they do not occlude the real world and have a limited field of view and field of regard. They can also support gesture recognition and haptic feedback to provide a more natural and intuitive interaction. Arm-mounted displays can be used for augmented reality or mixed reality 3D user interfaces, where the user can see and interact with virtual objects overlaid on the real world.
- **Autostereoscopic displays**: These are displays that show 3D images without requiring the user to wear any special glasses or devices. They use different techniques such as parallax barriers, lenticular lenses, or holograms to create the illusion of depth and perspective. They can also support head tracking and eye tracking to provide a more realistic and comfortable viewing experience. Autostereoscopic displays can be used for desktop or windowed 3D user interfaces, where the user can see and interact with a 3D scene on a 2D screen.

### Haptic Displays

Haptic displays are devices that provide users with a sense of touch in a virtual or augmented environment. They can enhance the realism and immersion of 3D user interfaces by delivering tactile and kinesthetic feedback.

Some of the main points to know about haptic displays are:

- Haptic feedback is the stimulation of somatic receptors, such as mechanoreceptors, thermoreceptors, or electroreceptors, to create a sensation of touch. Haptic feedback can be categorized into cutaneous (skin) and kinesthetic (muscle and joint) feedback .
- Cutaneous feedback is the rendering of surface properties, such as texture, shape, friction, or temperature, by applying forces or vibrations to the skin. Cutaneous feedback can be delivered by devices such as gloves, thimbles, or tactile arrays .
- Kinesthetic feedback is the rendering of object properties, such as weight, stiffness, or inertia, by applying forces or torques to the limbs or body. Kinesthetic feedback can be delivered by devices such as exoskeletons, haptic arms, or motion platforms .
- Haptic displays can be classified by the type of output they generate, such as force feedback, tactile feedback, thermal feedback, or electrical feedback. They can also be classified by the body part they target, such as the hand, the arm, the foot, or the whole body .
- Haptic displays can be used for various applications, such as virtual reality, teleoperation, gaming, education, or rehabilitation. They can enhance the user's perception, interaction, and performance in 3D user interfaces .



### Choosing Output Devices for 3D User Interfaces

Output devices, also called display devices, allow the machine to provide information or feedback to one or more users through the human perceptual system. Most of them are focused on stimulating the visual, auditory, or haptic senses.

Some of the factors to consider when choosing output devices for 3D user interfaces are:

- The type and amount of information to be displayed: Different output devices have different capabilities and limitations in terms of spatial resolution, field of view, color depth, refresh rate, etc. For example, head-mounted displays (HMDs) can provide immersive and stereoscopic views of 3D scenes, but they may have low resolution, narrow field of view, and high latency. On the other hand, projection displays can offer high resolution, wide field of view, and low latency, but they may not support stereoscopy or occlusion .
- The intended user experience and interaction style: Different output devices can create different levels of immersion, presence, and realism for the users. For example, HMDs can create a sense of being inside the virtual environment, but they may also cause isolation, discomfort, or motion sickness. Projection displays can create a sense of being in front of the virtual environment, but they may also allow social interaction, collaboration, or multitasking .
- The physical and environmental constraints: Different output devices have different requirements and trade-offs in terms of size, weight, power, cost, portability, setup, calibration, etc. For example, HMDs are relatively small, light, and portable, but they may also be expensive, fragile, and require batteries. Projection displays are relatively large, heavy, and fixed, but they may also be cheap, robust, and require power outlets .
- The user preferences and expectations: Different output devices may appeal to different users depending on their personal tastes, needs, goals, and backgrounds. For example, some users may prefer HMDs for their immersive and private experience, while others may prefer projection displays for their social and public experience. Some users may also have prior experience or familiarity with certain output devices, which may influence their perception and performance .

Therefore, choosing output devices for 3D user interfaces is a complex and context-dependent task that requires careful analysis and evaluation of the design goals, the application domain, and the user characteristics. There is no single best output device for all 3D user interfaces, but rather a range of possible options that have different advantages and disadvantages .



## Unit 3 - 3D User Interface Input Hardware

- The main characteristics of 3D user interface input hardware are:

- Some examples of 3D user interface input hardware are:

  - 2D mice and trackballs: devices that can sense the movement of a user's hand on a flat surface or a ball. They are typically used in desktop 3D UIs and have two or three DOF, continuous input, and passive interaction.

  - Joysticks: devices that can sense the direction and magnitude of a user's hand movement on a stick. They are typically used in immersive 3D UIs and have two or three DOF, continuous input, and active interaction.
  - Gloves: devices that can sense the position and orientation of a user's hand and fingers. They are typically used in immersive 3D UIs and have six or more DOF, continuous input, and passive interaction.
  - Trackers: devices that can sense the position and orientation of a user's head, body, or limbs. They are typically used in immersive 3D UIs and have six or more DOF, continuous input, and passive interaction.

### Input device characteristics

- Input devices are hardware that allow the user to communicate with the system by providing input in 3D space .
- Input devices can be classified by several characteristics, such as:
  - Degrees of freedom (DOFs): the number and type of independent parameters that define the position and orientation of an object in 3D space . For example, a 6-DOF device can control the x, y, z coordinates and the pitch, yaw, roll angles of an object.
  - DOF composition: the way the DOFs are grouped and controlled by the device. DOFs can be integral, meaning they are controlled simultaneously by a single input, or separable, meaning they are controlled independently by different inputs . For example, a mouse is a 2-DOF device with separable DOFs, while a joystick is a 2-DOF device with integral DOFs.
  - Type of electronics: the way the device converts physical input into digital signals. Devices can be digital, meaning they have discrete states or values, or analog, meaning they have continuous states or values . For example, a keyboard is a digital device, while a potentiometer is an analog device.
  - Range of reported values: the set of possible values that the device can produce. Devices can be discrete, meaning they have a finite number of values, continuous, meaning they have an infinite number of values, or hybrid, meaning they have a combination of discrete and continuous values . For example, a button is a discrete device, while a tracker is a continuous device.
  - Input modality: the type of input that the device uses. Devices can use different modalities, such as position, orientation, motion, gesture, voice, etc . For example, a mouse uses position input, while a microphone uses voice input.
  - Sensing technology: the way the device measures or detects the input. Devices can use different technologies, such as mechanical, optical, magnetic, acoustic, inertial, etc . For example, a mouse uses mechanical sensing, while a camera uses optical sensing.
  - Sensing mode: the way the device reports the input. Devices can be active, meaning they generate signals or emit energy, or passive, meaning they receive signals or detect energy . For example, a laser pointer is an active device, while a photodiode is a passive device.
  - Sensing range: the distance or area that the device can cover. Devices can have different ranges, such as near-field, far-field, local, global, etc . For example, a touch screen has a near-field range, while a GPS has a global range.
  - Sensing accuracy: the degree of error or deviation that the device has. Devices can have different levels of accuracy, depending on the sensing technology, the environmental conditions, the calibration, etc . For example, a tracker can have high accuracy in a controlled environment, but low accuracy in a noisy environment.

### Cullers and Occluders

- Cullers and occluders are techniques for improving the performance and realism of augmented and virtual reality applications by eliminating unnecessary rendering of objects or parts of objects that are not visible to the user.
- Culling is the process of discarding objects or polygons that are outside the view frustum, which is the region of space that is projected onto the screen by the camera. Culling can reduce the number of draw calls and the amount of geometry that needs to be processed by the graphics pipeline.
- Occlusion is the phenomenon of objects being hidden or partially hidden by other objects in the scene. Occlusion culling is the process of discarding objects or polygons that are occluded by other objects, such as walls, furniture, or the user's own body. Occlusion culling can further reduce the number of draw calls and the amount of geometry that needs to be processed by the graphics pipeline.
- Cullers and occluders can be implemented in different ways, depending on the type and complexity of the scene, the available hardware and software resources, and the desired level of accuracy and realism. Some common methods are:

  - **Bounding volume culling**: This method uses simple geometric shapes, such as spheres, boxes, or cylinders, to enclose objects or groups of objects in the scene. These shapes are called bounding volumes. The culling algorithm tests whether the bounding volumes intersect with the view frustum or are occluded by other bounding volumes. If not, the objects inside the bounding volumes are culled. This method is fast and easy to implement, but it can be inaccurate and conservative, as some objects may be culled even if they are partially visible.
  - **Portal culling**: This method divides the scene into connected regions, such as rooms, corridors, or outdoor areas, and identifies the openings or transitions between them, such as doors, windows, or arches. These openings are called portals. The culling algorithm traverses the scene from the region where the camera is located, and tests whether the portals intersect with the view frustum. If not, the regions behind the portals are culled. This method is effective for indoor scenes or scenes with a lot of occlusion, but it requires manual or automatic preprocessing of the scene to create the regions and portals.
  - **Occlusion query**: This method uses the graphics hardware to perform occlusion tests on the fly. The culling algorithm renders a simplified version of the scene, using low-resolution or low-polygon models, and assigns a unique identifier to each object or group of objects. Then, it queries the graphics hardware to determine how many pixels of each identifier are visible on the screen. If the number is zero or below a certain threshold, the object or group of objects is culled. This method is accurate and adaptive, but it can introduce latency and overhead, as the culling algorithm has to wait for the results of the queries from the graphics hardware.
  - **Depth buffer culling**: This method uses the depth buffer, which is a data structure that stores the distance of each pixel on the screen from the camera, to perform occlusion tests. The culling algorithm renders the scene from the camera's point of view, and updates the depth buffer with the depth values of the pixels. Then, it tests whether the depth values of the objects or polygons are greater than the depth values stored in the depth buffer. If so, the objects or polygons are occluded and culled. This method is accurate and fast, but it can be wasteful, as it requires rendering the scene twice, once for the depth buffer and once for the final image.

### Design Guidelines - Theoretical Foundations of Wayfinding

Wayfinding is the process of finding one's way in an unfamiliar environment, such as a building, a campus, a city, or a virtual space. Wayfinding involves cognitive, perceptual, and behavioral aspects, such as forming a mental map, recognizing landmarks, choosing a route, and following directions.

Designing effective wayfinding systems is a challenge for architects, designers, and developers, especially in complex and dynamic spaces, such as airports, hospitals, museums, or virtual reality environments. Wayfinding design aims to provide clear and consistent information and cues to help users navigate the space, reduce stress and confusion, and enhance the user experience.

Some of the theoretical foundations of wayfinding design are:

- **Cognitive mapping**: This is the process of creating and using a mental representation of the spatial layout and structure of the environment. Cognitive maps help users to orient themselves, plan their routes, and remember their locations. Cognitive maps are influenced by the user's prior knowledge, experience, and preferences, as well as the salience, complexity, and organization of the environmental features.
- **Landmarks**: These are distinctive and recognizable features of the environment that serve as reference points for orientation and navigation. Landmarks can be natural or artificial, such as buildings, monuments, sculptures, signs, or colors. Landmarks should be visible, memorable, and meaningful, and should contrast with the surrounding context .
- **Routes**: These are sequences of actions or directions that lead from one location to another. Routes can be based on landmarks, paths, turns, distances, or time. Routes should be simple, logical, and consistent, and should avoid unnecessary detours, loops, or dead ends. Routes should also provide feedback and confirmation to the user, such as signs, maps, or sounds .
- **Regions**: These are areas or zones of the environment that have a common identity, function, or theme. Regions help users to organize and categorize the space, and to associate locations with activities or purposes. Regions should be clearly defined, labeled, and differentiated from each other, and should reflect the user's expectations and needs .

Some of the design principles for wayfinding are   :

- **Create an identity at each location, different from all others**: This helps users to distinguish and remember different places, and to associate them with their functions or meanings.
- **Use landmarks to provide orientation cues and memorable locations**: This helps users to locate themselves, to recognize their destinations, and to recall their routes.
- **Create well-structured paths**: This helps users to follow a clear and logical sequence of steps, and to avoid getting lost or confused.
- **Provide signs at decision points to help wayfinding decisions**: This helps users to choose the right direction, to confirm their progress, and to correct their errors.
- **Use maps to show the overall layout and relative locations**: This helps users to form a cognitive map, to plan their routes, and to update their positions.
- **Use consistent and simple graphics and language**: This helps users to understand and interpret the information and cues, and to avoid ambiguity or confusion.
- **Use appropriate lighting, color, texture, and sound**: This helps users to perceive and identify the environmental features, and to enhance the aesthetic and emotional aspects of the space.

### Design Guidelines - System Control

System control is the task of performing actions that affect the state or mode of the system, such as opening menus, selecting commands, changing settings, or quitting the application. System control is often integrated with another 3D interaction task, such as navigation or manipulation. Therefore, system control techniques should be designed to avoid disturbing the flow of action of an interaction task.

Some design guidelines for system control are:

- Use consistent and intuitive mappings between input devices and system actions. For example, use a button or gesture that is easy to remember and perform for a frequently used command, or use a spatial metaphor that matches the function of the command, such as moving up to increase the volume.
- Provide feedback for system actions, such as visual, auditory, or haptic cues that indicate the state or mode of the system, the availability of commands, or the result of an action. For example, use a highlight or a sound to show that a menu item is selected, or use a color change or a vibration to show that a setting has been changed.
- Support undo and redo operations for system actions that have irreversible or undesirable consequences. For example, allow the user to revert to a previous state or mode of the system, or to cancel an ongoing action, by using a button or gesture that is easy to access and perform.
- Minimize the number of steps or interactions required to perform a system action. For example, use direct manipulation or voice commands instead of nested menus or dialogs, or use context-sensitive or adaptive menus that show only the relevant or frequently used commands.
- Avoid occlusion or interference between system control elements and the 3D scene or objects. For example, use transparent or semi-transparent menus or dialogs, or use spatial or temporal separation between system control elements and the 3D scene or objects.
- Consider the trade-offs between immersion and usability when designing system control techniques. For example, use natural or realistic input devices or gestures that enhance the sense of presence and engagement in the 3D environment, but also ensure that they are easy to use and reliable. Alternatively, use abstract or symbolic input devices or gestures that are more efficient and accurate, but also provide feedback or cues that maintain the consistency and coherence of the 3D environment.

### AR systems and functionality

- AR systems are systems that combine real and virtual worlds, real-time interaction, and accurate 3D registration of virtual and real objects.
- AR systems can be classified into three types based on the display technology: head-mounted displays (HMDs), handheld displays, and spatial displays.
- HMDs are devices that are worn on the head and project virtual images onto the user's field of view. HMDs can be optical see-through, video see-through, or optical video see-through.
- Handheld displays are devices that are held in the hand and use a camera or a sensor to capture the real environment and overlay virtual images on a screen. Handheld displays can be smartphones, tablets, or dedicated devices.
- Spatial displays are devices that project virtual images onto physical surfaces or objects in the real environment. Spatial displays can be projectors, holograms, or augmented reality mirrors.
- AR systems can also be classified into three types based on the tracking technology: marker-based, markerless, and hybrid.
- Marker-based AR systems use predefined visual markers or fiducials to identify the position and orientation of the virtual objects in the real environment. Markers can be 2D or 3D, black and white or colored, and have different shapes and patterns.
- Markerless AR systems use natural features or sensors to track the position and orientation of the virtual objects in the real environment. Natural features can be edges, corners, textures, or colors, and sensors can be GPS, accelerometer, gyroscope, or magnetometer.
- Hybrid AR systems use a combination of markers and natural features or sensors to track the position and orientation of the virtual objects in the real environment. Hybrid AR systems can improve the accuracy and robustness of the tracking.

### Evaluating AR Systems

- Evaluating AR systems is the process of assessing the quality, usability, effectiveness, and user satisfaction of AR applications and technologies.
- Evaluating AR systems is important for identifying the strengths and weaknesses of AR systems, improving the design and development of AR systems, and comparing different AR systems or approaches.
- Evaluating AR systems is challenging because of the complexity and diversity of AR systems, the lack of established standards and guidelines for AR evaluation, the difficulty of measuring the impact of AR on the real world, and the ethical and social issues involved in AR evaluation.
- Evaluating AR systems can be done using different methods and techniques, depending on the goals, context, and resources of the evaluation.
- Evaluating AR systems can be classified into two main categories: user-based evaluation and non-user-based evaluation.

#### User-based evaluation

- User-based evaluation involves testing the AR system with real or potential users, either in a controlled laboratory setting or in a naturalistic field setting.
- User-based evaluation can measure various aspects of the AR system, such as usability, user experience, user performance, user behavior, user preferences, user acceptance, user learning, and user emotions.
- User-based evaluation can use various methods and techniques, such as questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observations, video recordings, eye tracking, physiological measures, task analysis, cognitive walkthroughs, heuristic evaluation, and usability testing.
- User-based evaluation can provide valuable feedback and insights from the users' perspective, but it can also be costly, time-consuming, and influenced by various factors, such as user characteristics, task complexity, environmental conditions, and evaluator bias.

#### Non-user-based evaluation

- Non-user-based evaluation involves testing the AR system without involving real or potential users, either by using automated tools or by using expert evaluators.
- Non-user-based evaluation can measure various aspects of the AR system, such as functionality, reliability, performance, efficiency, compatibility, security, and scalability.
- Non-user-based evaluation can use various methods and techniques, such as software testing, debugging, benchmarking, simulation, modeling, analysis, and inspection.
- Non-user-based evaluation can provide objective and quantitative data about the AR system, but it can also be limited, incomplete, and detached from the users' needs and expectations.
