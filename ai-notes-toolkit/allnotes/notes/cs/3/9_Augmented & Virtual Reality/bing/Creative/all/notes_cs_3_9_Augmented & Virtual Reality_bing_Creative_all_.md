

# Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS

- Virtual reality (VR) is a technology that creates a simulated environment that is different from the real world. VR users can interact with the virtual environment using various devices, such as headsets, controllers, gloves, etc. VR can create a sense of presence, immersion and realism for the users.  
- Virtual environments (VEs) are the computer-generated scenes or worlds that VR users can perceive and explore. VEs can be realistic or fantastical, depending on the purpose and design of the VR application. VEs can also be interactive, allowing users to manipulate objects, navigate spaces, communicate with others, etc.  
- There are different types of VR, depending on the degree of immersion, interaction and realism. Some of the common types are:
  - Fully immersive VR: This type of VR provides the highest level of immersion, where the user is completely isolated from the real world and experiences a high-fidelity virtual environment. This type of VR requires a head-mounted display (HMD), headphones, motion tracking sensors and controllers. Examples of fully immersive VR are Oculus Rift, HTC Vive, PlayStation VR, etc. 
  - Non-immersive VR: This type of VR provides the lowest level of immersion, where the user is still aware of the real world and experiences a low-fidelity virtual environment. This type of VR does not require a HMD, but uses a desktop computer, a monitor, a keyboard and a mouse. Examples of non-immersive VR are Google Earth, flight simulators, video games, etc. 
  - Semi-immersive VR: This type of VR provides a medium level of immersion, where the user is partially isolated from the real world and experiences a medium-fidelity virtual environment. This type of VR requires a large screen, a projector, speakers and sometimes a HMD. Examples of semi-immersive VR are CAVE systems, IMAX theaters, virtual reality arcades, etc. 
  - Augmented reality (AR): This type of VR provides a different kind of immersion, where the user is not isolated from the real world, but experiences a computer-generated overlay on top of the real environment. This type of VR requires a smartphone, a tablet, a smart glass or a HMD. Examples of AR are Pokemon Go, Snapchat filters, Google Glass, etc.  
  - Mixed reality (MR): This type of VR provides a combination of VR and AR, where the user can interact with both the real and the virtual environments. This type of VR requires a HMD, cameras, sensors and controllers. Examples of MR are Microsoft HoloLens, Magic Leap, etc. 
- VR and VEs have many applications and benefits in various domains, such as education, entertainment, health care, tourism, military, etc. VR and VEs can provide immersive learning experiences, engaging gaming experiences, therapeutic interventions, virtual tours, realistic simulations, etc.  
- VR and VEs also have some challenges and limitations, such as technical issues, ethical issues, social issues, psychological issues, etc. VR and VEs can cause motion sickness, cyber sickness, privacy concerns, addiction, isolation, etc.  
- VR and VEs are constantly evolving and improving, thanks to the advances in artificial intelligence (AI), computer graphics, human-computer interaction, etc. AI can help create better VR experiences by generating realistic and dynamic VEs, enhancing user interaction and feedback, adapting to user preferences and behaviors, etc.



# The historical development of VR

- Virtual reality (VR) is the simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with in a seemingly real way by a person using special equipment, such as a headset and gloves.
- The concept of VR can be traced back to the 1800s, when stereoscopic photography was invented, creating the illusion of depth by using two slightly different images.
- In the 1950s, Morton Heilig, a filmmaker and inventor, developed the Sensorama, a device that stimulated multiple senses, such as sight, sound, smell, and touch, by showing 3D movies with stereo sound, wind, vibration, and aromas .
- In the 1960s, Ivan Sutherland and Bob Sproull created the first head-mounted display (HMD) system, called the Sword of Damocles, which was connected to a computer and displayed simple wireframe graphics.
- In the 1970s, Myron Krueger developed the concept of artificial reality, which used projectors, video cameras, and on-screen silhouettes to create interactive environments, such as Videoplace.
- In the 1980s, Jaron Lanier coined the term "virtual reality" and founded VPL Research, a company that developed various VR products, such as the DataGlove, the EyePhone, and the AudioSphere .
- In the 1990s, VR became more popular and accessible, with the release of consumer products, such as the Sega VR, the Nintendo Virtual Boy, and the Sony Glasstron. VR also found applications in various fields, such as entertainment, education, medicine, and military.
- In the 2000s, VR technology improved with the advancement of computer graphics, hardware, and software. VR devices, such as the Oculus Rift, the HTC Vive, and the PlayStation VR, offered more immersive and realistic experiences. VR also became more social and collaborative, with platforms, such as Second Life, Minecraft, and Facebook Spaces.
- In the 2010s, VR continued to evolve and innovate, with the emergence of new trends, such as augmented reality (AR), mixed reality (MR), and extended reality (XR), which combined virtual and real elements. VR also became more accessible and affordable, with the use of smartphones, web browsers, and standalone headsets, such as the Oculus Quest and the Google Cardboard.



# Scientific landmarks for the notes of the Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS in the subject of Augmented & Virtual Reality

- VR has been used for various purposes in science, such as:
  - Scientific visualization: VR can help scientists express complex ideas and concepts, such as molecular models, statistical results, or astronomical phenomena, in a more intuitive and engaging way  .
  - Neuroscience research and therapy: VR can enable researchers to study the brain and behavior in controlled and realistic scenarios, such as navigation, social interaction, or phobias.   - Discovery and exploration: VR can allow scientists to access and manipulate data that are otherwise difficult or impossible to observe, such as the structure and dynamics of the human genome, the formation and evolution of stars and galaxies, or the origin and fate of the universe .
- Some of the scientific landmarks of VR are:
  - The first VR system: The Sensorama, invented by Morton Heilig in 1957, was a mechanical device that simulated a motorcycle ride through New York City, using a stereoscopic display, stereo sound, vibration, wind, and smell.
  - The first head-mounted display (HMD): The Sword of Damocles, developed by Ivan Sutherland in 1968, was a computer-generated graphics system that projected simple wireframe images onto a user's eyes, creating a primitive form of augmented reality.
  - The first immersive VR environment: The Aspen Movie Map, created by MIT in 1978, was a video-based simulation of the city of Aspen, Colorado, that allowed users to navigate through the streets using a joystick and a large projection screen.
  - The first VR glove: The DataGlove, invented by Thomas Zimmerman and Jaron Lanier in 1985, was a wearable device that tracked the movements and gestures of the user's hand and fingers, enabling natural and intuitive interaction with virtual objects.
  - The first VR CAVE: The Cave Automatic Virtual Environment, developed by the University of Illinois at Chicago in 1992, was a room-sized VR system that surrounded the user with stereoscopic images projected on the walls, floor, and ceiling, creating a high-fidelity and immersive experience.



# Computer Graphics for the notes of the Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS in the subject of Augmented & Virtual Reality

- Virtual reality (VR) is the use of computer graphics systems in combination with various display and interface devices to provide the effect of immersion in the interactive 3D computer-generated environment .
- We call such an environment a virtual environment (VE).
- VR applications immerse the user in a computer-generated environment that simulates reality through the use of interactive devices, which send and receive information from the user and the system.
- The imagery of VR may be constructed from photographs or film of real places, or it can be entirely computer generated — CGI VR.
- VR allows people to explore just about any world that can be imagined, from the streets of a foreign city to the surface of a distant, fictional planet.
- VR can be used for various purposes, such as entertainment, education, training, simulation, therapy, art, and research.
- VR can create different types of experiences, such as immersive, non-immersive, collaborative, and mixed reality.
- Immersive VR is the most common type of VR, where the user wears a head-mounted display (HMD) or a head-up display (HUD) that covers the user's field of view and blocks out the real world.
- Non-immersive VR is where the user views the virtual environment on a screen or a projector, without wearing any special devices.
- Collaborative VR is where multiple users can interact with each other and the virtual environment, either in the same physical location or remotely.
- Mixed reality (MR) is where the virtual environment is blended with the real world, creating a hybrid experience that can be either augmented reality (AR) or augmented virtuality (AV).
- AR is where the virtual elements are overlaid on the real world, enhancing the user's perception of reality.
- AV is where the real elements are integrated into the virtual world, creating a more realistic simulation.
- VR requires various components to create a convincing and interactive experience, such as computer graphics, sound, haptics, tracking, and rendering.
- Computer graphics are the visual representation of the virtual environment, which can be either 2D or 3D, realistic or stylized, static or dynamic.
- Sound is the auditory component of the VR experience, which can be either spatial or non-spatial, natural or synthetic, ambient or directional.
- Haptics are the tactile and force feedback of the VR experience, which can be either passive or active, vibrotactile or kinesthetic, contact or non-contact.
- Tracking is the process of measuring the position and orientation of the user and the devices in the VR system, which can be either optical or non-optical, inside-out or outside-in, absolute or relative.
- Rendering is the process of generating the images and sounds of the VR experience, which can be either rasterization or ray tracing, real-time or offline, stereoscopic or monoscopic.
- VR faces various challenges and limitations, such as technical, ethical, social, and psychological issues.
- Technical issues include the high cost, complexity, and maintenance of VR systems, the low resolution, latency, and bandwidth of VR devices, and the lack of standards, interoperability, and compatibility of VR software and hardware.
- Ethical issues include the potential misuse, abuse, and addiction of VR, the privacy and security risks of VR data, and the moral and legal implications of VR actions and consequences.
- Social issues include the isolation, alienation, and disconnection of VR users from the real world, the lack of social norms, etiquette, and trust in VR interactions, and the impact of VR on the culture, identity, and diversity of VR communities.
- Psychological issues include the motion sickness, cybersickness, and simulator sickness of VR users, the presence, immersion, and engagement of VR users in the virtual environment, and the transfer, learning, and memory of VR users from the virtual to the real world.



# Real-time computer graphics

- Real-time computer graphics is the sub-field of computer graphics focused on producing and analyzing images in real time .
- Real-time rendering is the process of generating images from 3D data and displaying them on a screen at a high frame rate.
- Real-time rendering is used for interactive applications such as video games, simulations, virtual reality, augmented reality, and mixed reality .
- Real-time rendering requires a trade-off between quality and performance, as the images must be generated within a limited time budget.
- Real-time rendering engines are software systems that handle the various tasks involved in real-time rendering, such as scene management, geometry processing, shading, lighting, texturing, post-processing, and output.
- Real-time rendering engines can be classified into different types based on their architecture, such as forward rendering, deferred rendering, tiled rendering, and ray tracing.
- Real-time rendering engines can also be adapted to support autostereoscopy, which is a technique for creating 3D images without the need for special glasses or headsets.

# Visual Displays for 3D User Interfaces

- Visual displays are the output devices that present the 3D graphics and virtual environments to the user's eyes.
- Visual displays can be classified into two categories: fully-immersive and semi-immersive.
  - Fully-immersive displays occlude the real world and provide a high degree of presence and immersion to the user. Examples are head-mounted displays, arm-mounted displays, and virtual retinal displays.
  - Semi-immersive displays allow the user to see the real world and the virtual environment simultaneously or alternately. Examples are monitors, surround-screen displays, workbenches, hemispherical displays, and autostereoscopic displays.
- Visual displays can be characterized by several parameters, such as:
  - Field of regard (FOR): the total angular extent of the display system that can be seen by the user.
  - Field of view (FOV): the angular extent of the display that is currently visible to the user.
  - Spatial resolution: the number of pixels per unit area or angle that determine the visual quality and detail of the display.
  - Refresh rate: the frequency at which the display updates the image, measured in hertz (Hz).
  - Color depth: the number of bits used to represent the color of each pixel, which affects the range and accuracy of colors that can be displayed.
  - Stereoscopy: the technique of creating the illusion of depth by presenting slightly different images to each eye, which requires special hardware and software.
  - Latency: the delay between the user's input and the corresponding change in the display, which affects the responsiveness and realism of the interaction.
- Visual displays have different advantages and disadvantages depending on the application and the user's preferences. Some factors to consider are:
  - Cost: the price of the display hardware and software, as well as the maintenance and operation costs.
  - Portability: the size, weight, and power requirements of the display, as well as the ease of setup and transportation.
  - Comfort: the physical and psychological effects of the display on the user, such as eye strain, fatigue, nausea, and cybersickness.
  - Compatibility: the ability of the display to work with other devices and systems, such as input devices, tracking systems, and network protocols.
  - Usability: the ease of use and learnability of the display, as well as the user satisfaction and performance.

# Haptic Displays

Haptic displays are devices that provide users with a sense of touch in a virtual or augmented environment. They can simulate various types of tactile and kinesthetic sensations, such as texture, shape, force, vibration, temperature, and pain. Haptic displays can enhance the realism and immersion of 3D user interfaces, as well as provide feedback and guidance for interaction and manipulation tasks.

Some of the main topics related to haptic displays are:

- **Types of haptic feedback**: Haptic feedback can be classified into cutaneous, kinesthetic, and proprioceptive feedback, depending on the type of sensory receptors that are stimulated and the type of information that is conveyed. Cutaneous feedback refers to the stimulation of the skin surface, which can produce sensations of pressure, texture, vibration, temperature, and pain. Kinesthetic feedback refers to the stimulation of the muscles and joints, which can produce sensations of force, torque, weight, and stiffness. Proprioceptive feedback refers to the stimulation of the inner ear and the vestibular system, which can produce sensations of orientation, balance, and motion.  

- **Types of haptic devices**: Haptic devices can be categorized by the type of stimuli they generate, the number of degrees of freedom they support, the body parts they target, and the form factor they have. Some common types of haptic devices are:

  - **Force feedback devices**: These devices apply forces or torques to the user's hand or arm, usually through a mechanical linkage or a robotic arm. They can simulate the interaction with rigid or deformable objects, as well as the effects of gravity, friction, and inertia. Examples of force feedback devices are the PHANToM, the SPIDAR, and the HapticMaster.  

  - **Tactile feedback devices**: These devices stimulate the skin surface, usually through an array of pins, electrodes, or actuators. They can simulate the texture, shape, or temperature of objects, as well as the contact or collision events. Examples of tactile feedback devices are the TactaPad, the Tactile Brush, and the Thermal Ring.  

  - **Vibrotactile feedback devices**: These devices generate vibrations, usually through a motor, a piezoelectric element, or a voice coil. They can simulate the roughness, friction, or impact of objects, as well as the auditory cues or alerts. Examples of vibrotactile feedback devices are the Rumble Pack, the C2 Tactor, and the Woojer.  

  - **Wearable haptic devices**: These devices are attached or worn on the user's body, such as the hand, the arm, the wrist, the chest, or the head. They can provide haptic feedback in a more portable and unobtrusive way, as well as target different body parts or modalities. Examples of wearable haptic devices are the HaptiGo, the Haptic Vest, and the Haptic Helmet.  

- **Haptic rendering**: Haptic rendering is the process of computing and generating haptic feedback based on the interaction between the user and the virtual environment. It involves two main steps: collision detection and force computation. Collision detection determines if and where the user's haptic device intersects with the virtual objects, and force computation determines the magnitude and direction of the force to be applied to the user's haptic device. Haptic rendering can be challenging due to the high update rates, the stability issues, and the perceptual factors that affect the quality and realism of the haptic feedback.  

- **Haptic perception**: Haptic perception is the process of sensing and interpreting the haptic feedback by the user. It involves the integration of different sensory modalities, such as touch, vision, and audition, as well as the cognitive and affective aspects of the user's experience. Haptic perception can be influenced by various factors, such as the user's expectations, preferences, emotions, and attention. Haptic perception can also be measured and evaluated using different methods, such as psychophysical experiments, subjective ratings, and physiological responses.

# Unit 3 - 3D User Interface Input Hardware

- 3D user interface input hardware are devices that capture and interpret the actions performed by the user in a 3D environment .
- The degrees of freedom (DOF) are one of the main features of these devices. DOF refers to the number of independent ways that a body can move in space. For example, a 3D mouse can move in three translational DOF (x, y, z) and three rotational DOF (pitch, yaw, roll).
- Input devices can be classified by the type and frequency of data they generate. Discrete devices produce single data values, such as Boolean values or keystrokes. Continuous devices generate multiple data values, such as pixel coordinates or orientation angles.
- Input devices can also be classified by the amount of physical interaction they require. Isomorphic devices have a one-to-one correspondence between the physical and virtual actions, such as a steering wheel or a joystick. Non-isomorphic devices have a different mapping between the physical and virtual actions, such as a keyboard or a mouse.
- Some examples of 3D user interface input hardware are:

  - Keyboards: They are discrete devices that provide alphanumeric and symbolic input. They are commonly used in desktop 3D UIs for commands, text entry, and shortcuts .
  - 2D mice and trackballs: They are continuous devices that provide two translational DOF. They are commonly used in desktop 3D UIs for cursor control, selection, and manipulation .
  - Pen- and touch-based tablets: They are continuous devices that provide two or three translational DOF and pressure sensitivity. They are commonly used in desktop 3D UIs for sketching, drawing, and gesturing .
  - Joysticks: They are continuous devices that provide two or three translational DOF and one or two rotational DOF. They are commonly used in desktop and immersive 3D UIs for navigation, steering, and flying .

# Tracking Devices for 3D User Interface Input Hardware

## Technology
- Different technologies are used to track the 3D position and/or orientation of a sensor or a user, such as magnetic, mechanical, acoustic, inertial, optical, and hybrid.
- Magnetic tracking devices use magnetic fields to measure the position and orientation of a sensor. They are robust to occlusion, but sensitive to interference from metallic objects or other magnetic fields.
- Mechanical tracking devices use rigid or flexible links to measure the position and orientation of a sensor. They are accurate and fast, but limited by the range and mobility of the links.
- Acoustic tracking devices use sound waves to measure the position and/or orientation of a sensor. They are inexpensive and scalable, but affected by noise and environmental factors.
- Inertial tracking devices use accelerometers and gyroscopes to measure the acceleration and rotation of a sensor. They are compact and wireless, but prone to drift and error accumulation.
- Optical tracking devices use cameras and markers to measure the position and/or orientation of a sensor. They are precise and reliable, but require line of sight and calibration.
- Hybrid tracking devices use a combination of two or more technologies to overcome the limitations of each technology and improve the performance and accuracy of the tracking.

- Cullers are algorithms or techniques that reduce the number of objects or polygons that need to be rendered in a virtual environment, by discarding those that are not visible to the viewer or are too far away to be noticeable.

  - View frustum culling: a technique that discards objects or polygons that are outside the viewing volume of the camera or the user's eye.

  - Cullers and occluders may introduce visual artifacts or errors, such as popping, flickering, or aliasing, if they are not implemented carefully or accurately.
  - Cullers and occluders may not be compatible with some rendering techniques or hardware, such as ray tracing, stereoscopic rendering, or head-mounted displays.

## Unit 5 - 3D INTERACTION TECHNIQUES

- 3D interaction techniques are methods for manipulating, navigating, and selecting objects in 3D virtual environments.
- 3D interaction techniques can be classified into three categories: direct manipulation, indirect manipulation, and abstract manipulation.
- Direct manipulation techniques allow the user to directly manipulate objects in 3D space using natural gestures, such as grabbing, rotating, scaling, and throwing. Examples of direct manipulation techniques are ray-casting, virtual hand, and go-go arm.
- Indirect manipulation techniques use intermediate devices or representations to manipulate objects in 3D space, such as mice, keyboards, joysticks, menus, and widgets. Examples of indirect manipulation techniques are point-and-click, drag-and-drop, and 3D widgets.
- Abstract manipulation techniques use high-level commands or gestures to manipulate objects in 3D space, such as voice, gaze, or body movements. Examples of abstract manipulation techniques are speech commands, eye tracking, and gesture recognition.
- 3D interaction techniques can also be classified into two types: discrete and continuous. Discrete techniques perform a single action or operation, such as selecting, activating, or deleting an object. Continuous techniques perform a series of actions or operations, such as moving, rotating, or scaling an object.
- 3D interaction techniques can be evaluated based on several criteria, such as usability, efficiency, effectiveness, learnability, satisfaction, and presence. Usability refers to how easy and intuitive the technique is to use. Efficiency refers to how fast and accurate the technique is to perform a task. Effectiveness refers to how well the technique achieves the desired goal or outcome. Learnability refers to how easy and quick the technique is to learn and remember. Satisfaction refers to how enjoyable and satisfying the technique is to use. Presence refers to how immersive and realistic the technique is to create a sense of being in the virtual environment.



# 3D Manipulation Tasks

3D manipulation tasks are the tasks of physically handling objects in a 3D environment. They are essential for creating and modifying 3D scenes, as well as interacting with 3D applications. According to some categorizations, there are three basic 3D manipulation tasks: selection, positioning, and rotation . Some sources also include scaling as a basic manipulation task.

- Selection is the task of acquiring or identifying a particular object or subset of objects from the entire set of objects available. Selection can be done by pointing, clicking, touching, or using voice commands. Selection can also be based on attributes, such as color, shape, or name. Selection techniques can be classified into direct and indirect techniques. Direct techniques involve direct contact or proximity between the user and the object, such as ray-casting or virtual hand. Indirect techniques involve using an intermediate device or representation, such as a cursor or a menu  .

- Positioning is the task of changing the 3D position of an object. Positioning can be done by dragging, throwing, snapping, or using constraints. Positioning techniques can be classified into absolute and relative techniques. Absolute techniques involve specifying the exact coordinates or location of the object, such as using a 3D widget or a map. Relative techniques involve specifying the displacement or direction of the object, such as using a 3D mouse or a gesture  .

- Rotation is the task of changing the orientation of an object. Rotation can be done by twisting, turning, aligning, or using constraints. Rotation techniques can be classified into absolute and relative techniques. Absolute techniques involve specifying the exact angles or axes of the object, such as using a 3D widget or a dial. Relative techniques involve specifying the rotation amount or direction of the object, such as using a 3D mouse or a gesture  .

- Scaling is the task of changing the size of an object. Scaling can be done by stretching, shrinking, or using constraints. Scaling techniques can be classified into absolute and relative techniques. Absolute techniques involve specifying the exact dimensions or scale factor of the object, such as using a 3D widget or a slider. Relative techniques involve specifying the scaling amount or direction of the object, such as using a 3D mouse or a gesture.

3D manipulation tasks can be performed using various input devices, such as 3D mice, gloves, trackers, or controllers. The number and integration of control dimensions can affect the performance and usability of the manipulation techniques. Generally, multiple integrated degrees of freedom (DOFs) are preferred for 3D manipulation, as they allow more natural and intuitive interaction. However, some trade-offs may exist between accuracy, speed, and ease of use. Position control is usually preferred for manipulation, as it provides direct feedback and control. Force control can be more suitable for controlling rates, such as velocity or acceleration  .

# Evaluating Wayfinding Aids for 3D Interaction Techniques

- Wayfinding is the process of finding one's way in an unfamiliar environment, such as a virtual reality (VR) or a 3D user interface (3D UI).

- Some of the evaluation methods and metrics for WAs are:

  - **Effectiveness**: the degree to which users can successfully reach their destinations or complete their tasks using WAs. It can be measured by the number of errors, the number of times users get lost, the accuracy of spatial knowledge, etc.
  - **Efficiency**: the amount of time, effort, and resources required to use WAs. It can be measured by the task completion time, the number of interactions, the cognitive load, the mental workload, etc.

# Design Guidelines - System Control

System control is the task of issuing commands to the system or changing system parameters, such as switching modes, changing views, or adjusting settings. System control is often integrated with another 3D interaction task, such as navigation, selection, or manipulation. Therefore, system control techniques should be designed to avoid disturbing the flow of action of an interaction task.

Some design guidelines for system control are:

- Use graphical menus for 3D UIs that are easy to access, read, and select from. Graphical menus can be adapted from 2D menus, such as pop-up menus, pie menus, or toolbars, or designed specifically for 3D UIs, such as 1-DOF menus, 3D widgets, or 3D icons.
- Use natural mappings and affordances for system control techniques that are based on physical objects or gestures. For example, using a virtual camera to change the view, or using a hand gesture to switch modes.
- Use multimodal feedback for system control techniques that involve speech, gesture, or haptic input. For example, using speech recognition to confirm commands, using visual cues to indicate gesture recognition, or using haptic feedback to indicate mode changes.
- Use two-handed interaction for system control techniques that require simultaneous or sequential input from both hands. For example, using one hand to select an object and the other hand to manipulate it, or using one hand to hold a tool and the other hand to activate it.
- Use consistent and predictable system control techniques across different tasks and contexts. For example, using the same gesture or command to switch modes, or using the same menu layout or widget style for different parameters.



# Classification of 3D Interaction Techniques

3D interaction techniques are methods that allow users to perform tasks in virtual environments using 3D input devices and feedback. They can be classified according to the type of task they support, such as:

- **Selection and manipulation**: These techniques enable users to select, move, rotate, scale, or deform virtual objects in 3D space. Examples of these techniques are ray-casting, virtual hand, HOMER, Go-Go, and 3D widgets .
- **Navigation**: These techniques enable users to change their viewpoint or position in the virtual environment. Examples of these techniques are flying, walking, steering, teleporting, and world-in-miniature .
- **System control**: These techniques enable users to access menus, dialogs, or commands that control the system or the application. Examples of these techniques are 3D buttons, voice commands, gestures, and gaze-based selection .

3D interaction techniques can also be classified according to other criteria, such as:

- **Input dimensionality**: This refers to the number of degrees of freedom (DOF) of the input device or the technique. For example, a mouse has 2 DOF (x and y), a joystick has 3 DOF (x, y, and rotation), and a 6 DOF tracker has 6 DOF (x, y, z, pitch, yaw, and roll).
- **Mapping**: This refers to the relationship between the input device movement and the resulting action in the virtual environment. # Technology and Features of Augmented Reality

- Augmented reality (AR) is the technology that expands our physical world, adding layers of digital information onto it.
- Unlike virtual reality (VR), AR does not create the whole artificial environments to replace real with a virtual one. AR appears in direct view of an existing environment and adds sounds, videos, graphics to it.
- AR incorporates three features: a combination of digital and physical worlds, interactions made in real time, and accurate 3D identification of virtual and real objects.
- AR can be achieved through different devices, such as smartphones, tablets, head-mounted displays (HMDs), smart glasses, and projectors.
- AR can be classified into three types based on the level of immersion and interaction: marker-based AR, markerless AR, and projection-based AR.
- Marker-based AR uses a camera and a visual marker, such as a QR code or a barcode, to detect the position and orientation of the marker and overlay a digital image or a 3D model on it.
- Markerless AR uses sensors, such as GPS, accelerometer, gyroscope, and compass, to track the location and orientation of the device and overlay digital information based on the user's context, such as location, time, or activity.
- Projection-based AR uses a projector to cast digital images or videos onto a physical surface, such as a wall or a table, and allows the user to interact with them through touch or gesture.
- AR can be used for various applications, such as education, entertainment, gaming, tourism, retail, manufacturing, healthcare, and military   .
- AR can offer benefits, such as enhancing user experience, improving learning outcomes, increasing productivity, reducing errors, and saving costs  .
- AR can also pose challenges, such as technical limitations, privacy and security issues, ethical and social implications, and user acceptance and adoption .



# Difference between AR and VR

- AR stands for **augmented reality**, while VR stands for **virtual reality**.
- AR **enhances** the real-world environment by adding virtual elements to it, while VR **entirely replaces** the current setting with a digital simulation .
- AR is **only 25 percent virtual**, while VR is **75 percent virtual**.
- AR **does not require** a headset device, but VR **requires** a headset device .
- AR users can **control** their presence in the real world, while VR users are **controlled** by the system.
- AR users are in **contact** with the real world, while VR users move in a **completely fictional** world.

# Augmented Reality Methods

Augmented reality (AR) is a method by which we can alter our real-world environment by adding some digital elements to it. This is done by superimposing a digital image on the person’s current view, thus enhancing the experience of reality.

There are different types of AR technology that are suitable for different types of tasks. The two main types of AR are:

- **Marker-based AR**: This type of AR uses a marker, which is a special visual object like a sign or a symbol, and a camera to initiate the 3D digital animations. The system will calculate the orientation and position of the marker to position the content effectively. Marker-based AR example: A marker-based mobile-based AR furnishing app.
- **Marker-less AR**: This type of AR does not require any marker, but instead uses the location and orientation data from the device's sensors, such as GPS, accelerometer, and compass, to position the digital content in the real world. Marker-less AR example: A marker-less AR navigation app.

There are also other types of AR technology that are less common, such as:

- **Projection-based AR**: This type of AR projects a digital image onto a physical surface, such as a wall or a table, and allows the user to interact with it by touching or gesturing. Projection-based AR example: A projection-based AR keyboard.
- **Superimposition-based AR**: This type of AR replaces a part or the whole of the real-world view with a digital image, such as an object or a scene. The system needs to recognize the object or the scene to be replaced and to align the digital image with it. Superimposition-based AR example: A superimposition-based AR app that shows how a furniture item would look in a room.

There are various technologies that are used in AR rendering, such as:

- **Optical projection systems**: These are devices that project a digital image onto a transparent medium, such as a glass or a film, that can be seen by the user. Optical projection systems example: A head-up display (HUD) in a car.
- **Monitors**: These are devices that display a digital image on a screen, such as a computer monitor or a TV, that can be seen by the user. Monitors example: A monitor-based AR game.
- **Handheld devices**: These are devices that have a camera and a screen, such as a smartphone or a tablet, that can capture the real-world view and overlay the digital content on it. Handheld devices example: A handheld device-based AR app that shows information about a product.
- **Display systems worn on the human body**: These are devices that are worn on the head or the body, such as a helmet or a vest, that have a camera and a display, such as a head-mounted display (HMD) or a smart glasses, that can capture the real-world view and overlay the digital content on it. Display systems worn on the human body example: A HMD-based AR app that shows instructions for a task.

These are some of the methods of augmented reality that are used for various purposes, such as entertainment, education, navigation, marketing, and training. AR is a rapidly evolving technology that has many potential applications and benefits for the users.

# Mobile Projection Interfaces for Augmented Reality

- Mobile projection interfaces are devices that combine a camera and a projector to create interactive and immersive augmented reality (AR) applications.
- Mobile projection interfaces can be classified into three types :
  - Object-adaptive applications: These applications use the camera to detect and track objects in the environment and project relevant information or graphics onto them. For example, a projector phone can project a virtual keyboard onto a flat surface or a product information onto a product package.
  - Context-adaptive applications: These applications use the camera to sense the context of the environment and project appropriate content or interfaces. For example, a projector phone can project a map or a navigation system onto a wall or a floor.
  - Camera-controlled applications: These applications use the camera as an input device to control the projected content or interface. For example, a projector phone can project a game or a menu onto a table and use the camera to detect the user's gestures or touches.
- Mobile projection interfaces have several advantages over other types of AR displays  :
  - They can create large-scale and high-resolution AR experiences without the need for head-mounted displays or special glasses.
  - They can adapt to different surfaces and objects and create seamless transitions between them.
  - They can support collaborative and social AR interactions by allowing multiple users to share the same projected content or interface.
  - They can enhance the user's sense of presence and engagement by creating a direct connection between the physical and the virtual world.
- Mobile projection interfaces also face some challenges and limitations :
  - They require sufficient ambient light and contrast to project clear and visible images.
  - They depend on the availability and suitability of projection surfaces and objects in the environment.
  - They may cause occlusion or distortion of the projected content or interface by the user's hands or body.
  - They may consume more power and resources than other AR devices.



# Marker-less Tracking for Augmented Reality

- Marker-less tracking is a technique that allows augmented reality (AR) applications to display virtual content on top of real-world scenes without the need for predefined markers, such as images or objects.
- Marker-less tracking relies on detecting and tracking natural features in the environment, such as edges, corners, textures, or colors, and using them as reference points for estimating the camera pose and orientation.
- Marker-less tracking can be classified into two main categories: model-based and appearance-based.
  - Model-based tracking uses a 3D model of the scene or the object of interest to match the features extracted from the camera image and compute the camera pose. This method requires prior knowledge of the scene geometry and appearance, and can handle occlusions and changes in illumination. However, it also requires more computational resources and storage space.
  - Appearance-based tracking uses a set of 2D images of the scene or the object of interest to match the features extracted from the camera image and compute the camera pose. This method does not require prior knowledge of the scene geometry and appearance, and can handle dynamic and complex scenes. However, it also requires more images to cover different viewpoints and lighting conditions, and can be affected by occlusions and changes in illumination.
- Marker-less tracking can also use different algorithms for feature detection, description, and matching, such as ORB, FREAK, SIFT, SURF, etc. These algorithms vary in their speed, accuracy, robustness, and scalability.
- Marker-less tracking can also use different classifiers for the tracking task, such as KNN, Random Forest, Extremely Randomized Trees, SVM, and Bayes classifier. These classifiers vary in their performance, complexity, and generalization ability.
- Marker-less tracking has many advantages over marker-based tracking, such as:
  - It can work with any scene or object, without the need for artificial markers or triggers.
  - It can provide a more immersive and realistic AR experience, as the virtual content can blend seamlessly with the natural environment.
  - It can handle larger and more diverse scenes, as it does not depend on the visibility and orientation of the markers.
  - It can enable more interactive and dynamic AR applications, as the virtual content can respond to the changes in the environment or the user's actions.
- Marker-less tracking also has some challenges and limitations, such as:
  - It requires more computational power and memory, as it has to process more features and images, and store more data.
  - It requires more calibration and initialization, as it has to estimate the camera parameters and the initial pose.
  - It can be affected by noise, blur, motion, occlusions, and changes in illumination, as it relies on the quality and consistency of the features and images.
  - It can be less accurate and stable, as it can suffer from drift and jitter, especially in long-term tracking scenarios.

# Enhancing Interactivity in AR Environments

- Interactivity is the degree to which a user can manipulate and influence the virtual content in an augmented reality (AR) environment.
- Interactivity is essential for creating engaging and immersive AR experiences that can enhance the user's perception, cognition, and emotion.
- Interactivity in AR can be enhanced by using various techniques, such as:
  - Handheld AR: using mobile devices as windows to the virtual world, allowing the user to interact with the content through touch, gestures, and motion.
  - Spatial AR: using projectors or displays to augment the physical environment with virtual content, allowing the user to interact with the content through natural actions and physical objects.
  - Wearable AR: using head-mounted displays or smart glasses to overlay virtual content on the user's field of view, allowing the user to interact with the content through voice, gaze, and hand tracking.
- Interactivity in AR can also be enhanced by using artificial intelligence (AI) to improve the quality, personalization, and adaptation of the virtual content, as well as the interaction between the user and the technology.
- Interactivity in AR can face various challenges, such as:
  - Technical limitations: such as latency, accuracy, and robustness of the AR system, which can affect the user's sense of presence and realism.
  - User factors: such as individual preferences, expectations, and abilities, which can affect the user's satisfaction and acceptance of the AR system.
  - Context factors: such as environmental conditions, social norms, and ethical issues, which can affect the user's safety and privacy in the AR system.
- Interactivity in AR can be evaluated by using various methods, such as:
  - User feedback: such as questionnaires, interviews, and focus groups, which can measure the user's subjective opinions and experiences of the AR system.
  - Behavioral measures: such as task performance, error rates, and eye movements, which can measure the user's objective actions and outcomes in the AR system.
  - Physiological measures: such as heart rate, skin conductance, and brain activity, which can measure the user's emotional and cognitive responses to the AR system.



# Evaluating AR Systems

Augmented reality (AR) systems are systems that combine real and virtual elements in a user's environment, enhancing the user's perception and interaction with the real world. AR systems can be used for various purposes, such as education, entertainment, navigation, maintenance, and medical applications. However, designing and developing effective AR systems is not a trivial task, as it involves many challenges and trade-offs, such as:

- Choosing the appropriate hardware and software components for the AR system, such as display devices, tracking devices, sensors, and rendering engines.
- Designing the AR interface and interaction techniques that suit the user's needs, preferences, and context, such as the type of information, the level of realism, the degree of immersion, and the mode of input and output.
- Evaluating the AR system's usability, functionality, performance, and user experience, such as the ease of use, the usefulness, the efficiency, the accuracy, the reliability, and the satisfaction.

Therefore, evaluating AR systems is an essential step in the AR development process, as it can provide valuable feedback and insights for improving the AR system's design and implementation. Evaluating AR systems can also help to compare different AR systems or techniques, to identify the strengths and weaknesses of each, and to determine the best practices and guidelines for AR development.

However, evaluating AR systems is also a challenging task, as it involves many issues and difficulties, such as:

- Choosing the appropriate evaluation methods and metrics for the AR system, such as user-based or non-user-based methods, qualitative or quantitative metrics, subjective or objective measures, and task-based or experience-based metrics.
- Choosing the appropriate evaluation environment and scenario for the AR system, such as laboratory or field settings, controlled or natural conditions, and realistic or artificial tasks and situations.
- Choosing the appropriate evaluation participants and procedures for the AR system, such as the number and characteristics of the participants, the recruitment and selection methods, the training and briefing sessions, the data collection and analysis methods, and the ethical and legal considerations.

Therefore, evaluating AR systems requires a careful planning and execution, as well as a thorough understanding of the AR system's goals, features, and context. Evaluating AR systems also requires a critical and creative thinking, as well as a flexible and adaptive approach, as there is no one-size-fits-all solution for evaluating AR systems.
