

## Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS

- Virtual reality (VR) is a technology that creates an immersive and interactive simulation of a three-dimensional environment, using computer-generated graphics, sounds, and other sensory inputs.
- Virtual environments (VEs) are the specific settings or scenarios that are simulated in VR, such as a virtual classroom, a virtual museum, or a virtual battlefield.
- VR and VEs can be used for various purposes, such as education, entertainment, training, therapy, and research.
- VR and VEs can be classified into different types, based on the degree of immersion, realism, and interactivity they provide.
- Immersion is the extent to which the user feels present and engaged in the VE, and loses awareness of the real world.
- Realism is the degree to which the VE resembles the real world, in terms of visual, auditory, haptic, and other cues.
- Interactivity is the level of control and feedback the user has over the VE, and the responsiveness of the VE to the user's actions and inputs.
- Some examples of VR and VE types are:

  - Non-immersive VR: The user views the VE on a desktop monitor or a large screen, and interacts with it using a mouse, keyboard, or other devices. The user is aware of the real world and can easily switch attention between the two. An example of non-immersive VR is Google Earth.
  - Semi-immersive VR: The user views the VE on a large screen or a head-mounted display (HMD), and interacts with it using a joystick, a glove, or other devices. The user is partially immersed in the VE, but still has some awareness of the real world. An example of semi-immersive VR is a flight simulator.
  - Fully immersive VR: The user views the VE on a HMD, and interacts with it using a motion tracking system, a haptic suit, or other devices. The user is fully immersed in the VE, and has little or no awareness of the real world. An example of fully immersive VR is a virtual reality game.
  - Augmented reality (AR): The user views the real world through a device, such as a smartphone, a tablet, or a HMD, that overlays virtual objects or information on the real world. The user can interact with the virtual objects or information using touch, gesture, or voice commands. An example of AR is Pokemon Go.
  - Mixed reality (MR): The user views and interacts with both the real world and the VE, which are seamlessly integrated and coexist in the same space. The user can manipulate the virtual objects or information as if they were real, and the virtual objects or information can react to the real world. An example of MR is Microsoft HoloLens.



# The historical development of VR

- Virtual reality (VR) is the simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with in a seemingly real way by a person using special equipment, such as a headset and gloves.
- The concept of VR dates back to the 1800s, when stereoscopes were invented to create the illusion of depth in photographs.
- The first VR device that allowed users to experience immersive multimedia was the Sensorama, invented by Morton Heilig in 1957. It was a cabinet that played 3D films with stereo sound, aromas, wind, and vibrations.
- The first VR head-mounted display (HMD) was the Sword of Damocles, created by Ivan Sutherland and Bob Sproull in 1968. It was a large and heavy device that was suspended from the ceiling and tracked the user's head movements.
- The term "virtual reality" was popularized by Jaron Lanier in the 1980s, who founded VPL Research, a company that developed various VR products, such as the EyePhone, the DataGlove, and the DataSuit .
- In the late 1980s and early 1990s, VR became more accessible to the public, with the release of consumer-oriented HMDs, such as the Sega VR, the Virtual Boy, and the iGlasses. However, these devices suffered from technical limitations, such as low resolution, high latency, and poor ergonomics.
- In the 1990s and 2000s, VR was mainly used for military, medical, and educational purposes, such as flight simulators, surgical training, and virtual classrooms . Some notable examples of VR applications in this period are the Super Cockpit, the CAVE, and the Second Life.
- In the 2010s, VR experienced a resurgence, thanks to the advancement of technology, such as high-definition displays, motion sensors, and graphics processors. The Oculus Rift, launched in 2012, was the first modern VR HMD that offered a high-quality and affordable VR experience. Since then, many other VR devices have been released, such as the HTC Vive, the PlayStation VR, and the Samsung Gear VR.
- Today, VR is used for various purposes, such as entertainment, gaming, social media, art, education, tourism, and therapy. VR is also integrated with other technologies, such as augmented reality, haptic feedback, and artificial intelligence, to create more immersive and realistic experiences. VR is expected to grow and evolve in the future, as new innovations and applications emerge.

  - The first VR glove was invented by Thomas Zimmerman in 1982, which could sense the position and orientation of the hand and fingers, and provide tactile feedback.
  - The first VR cave was built by Carolina Cruz-Neira and colleagues in 1992, called the CAVE Automatic Virtual Environment. It was a room-sized VR system that projected images on the walls, floor, and ceiling, creating a 360-degree immersive experience.

  - The first VR simulation of the Milky Way was created by Marc Kuchner and Susan Higashio in 2020, using data from the Gaia mission. It was a VR app that animated the speed and direction of 4 million stars in the local galaxy, revealing new patterns and structures.

# Real-time computer graphics

- Real-time computer graphics is the sub-field of computer graphics focused on producing and analyzing images in real time .
- The term can refer to anything from rendering an application's graphical user interface (GUI) to real-time image analysis, but is most often used in reference to interactive 3D computer graphics, typically using a graphics processing unit (GPU), with video games the most noticeable users .
- Real-time rendering is often contrasted with offline rendering, which is the process of generating images from 3D models by using complex algorithms and techniques that are not feasible in real time.
- Real-time rendering requires a high level of performance and optimization, as well as trade-offs between quality and speed, to achieve interactive frame rates (typically 30 or 60 frames per second).
- Some of the techniques used in real-time rendering include rasterization, ray tracing, ray casting, ray marching, deferred shading, forward shading, shadow mapping, ambient occlusion, global illumination, anti-aliasing, texture mapping, bump mapping, normal mapping, displacement mapping, tessellation, animation, skeletal animation, inverse kinematics, physics simulation, collision detection, particle systems, and post-processing effects.
- Real-time computer graphics can be used for various applications, such as video games, virtual reality, augmented reality, simulations, training, education, entertainment, art, and visualization  .
- Real-time computer graphics can also adapt to autostereoscopy, which is a technique that allows the viewer to perceive 3D images without the need for special glasses or headsets.

## Unit 2 - Hardware Technologies for 3D User Interfaces

- Hardware technologies for 3D user interfaces are the devices that enable the communication between a user (or users) and a system in a 3D environment.
- Hardware technologies for 3D user interfaces can be classified into two main categories: input devices and output devices.
- Input devices allow the user to provide information, commands, or actions to the system, such as keyboards, mice, touchscreens, joysticks, trackers, gloves, cameras, microphones, etc.
- Output devices allow the system to provide information, feedback, or responses to the user, such as monitors, projectors, speakers, headphones, haptic devices, etc.
- Hardware technologies for 3D user interfaces should be designed to match the characteristics and requirements of the 3D environment, the tasks, and the users.
- Hardware technologies for 3D user interfaces should also consider the trade-offs between performance, cost, usability, comfort, safety, and aesthetics.
- Hardware technologies for 3D user interfaces can be evaluated based on various criteria, such as accuracy, resolution, latency, bandwidth, reliability, compatibility, portability, scalability, etc.
- Hardware technologies for 3D user interfaces are constantly evolving and improving, as new technologies emerge and existing ones are refined.



# Visual Displays for 3D User Interfaces

- Visual displays are output devices that present 3D information to the user's eyes.
- Visual displays can be classified into two categories: fully-immersive and semi-immersive.
- Fully-immersive displays occlude the real world and provide a high degree of presence and immersion in the virtual environment. Examples are head-mounted displays, arm-mounted displays and virtual retinal displays.
- Semi-immersive displays allow the user to see both the real and the virtual world, but with different levels of immersion and realism. Examples are monitors, surround-screen displays, workbenches, hemispherical displays and autostereoscopic displays .
- Visual display characteristics that affect the quality and performance of 3D user interfaces are:
  - Field of regard (FOR): the total angular extent of the display that can be seen by the user.
  - Field of view (FOV): the angular extent of the display that can be seen by the user at any given moment.
  - Spatial resolution: the number and size of pixels that make up the display image.
  - Temporal resolution: the refresh rate or frame rate of the display image.
  - Brightness: the luminance or intensity of the display image.
  - Color contrast: the difference in brightness between the brightest and the darkest colors in the display image.
  - Gamma correction: the adjustment of the display image to match the human perception of brightness .



# Auditory Displays for 3D User Interfaces

- Auditory displays are devices that present information through the human auditory system, which is especially useful when supplying location and spatial information to the users.
- Auditory displays can complement or substitute visual displays, depending on the task and the environment. For example, auditory displays can provide feedback, warnings, cues, or guidance in situations where visual attention is limited or overloaded.
- Auditory displays can also enhance the sense of immersion and presence in virtual environments, by creating realistic and dynamic soundscapes that match the visual scene.
- Auditory displays can be classified into two main types: 3D sound and sonification.
  - 3D sound is the generation of sound that simulates the natural characteristics of the human auditory system, such as interaural time and level differences, head-related transfer functions, and reverberation. 3D sound can create the illusion of sound sources located in any direction and distance from the listener.
  - Sonification is the transformation of certain types of information into sound, such as data, events, or states. Sonification can convey information that is difficult to perceive visually, such as trends, patterns, or anomalies. Sonification can also provide continuous and non-intrusive feedback, such as the sound of a heartbeat or a Geiger counter.
- Auditory displays can be implemented using various hardware technologies, such as headphones, speakers, microphones, or bone conduction devices. The choice of hardware depends on the application requirements, such as portability, comfort, fidelity, and spatial accuracy .
- Auditory displays can be designed using various methods and tools, such as sound synthesis, sound recording, sound editing, sound spatialization, and sound mapping. The design of auditory displays should consider the auditory perception and cognition of the users, as well as the auditory aesthetics and semantics of the sounds .



# Haptic Displays

Haptic displays are interfaces that provide users with haptic feedback, which is the sensation of touch, in a virtual or augmented reality environment. Haptic feedback can enhance the realism and interactivity of the virtual experience, as well as provide information about the properties and actions of the virtual objects. Haptic displays can be classified into two main types: cutaneous and kinesthetic.

- Cutaneous haptic displays stimulate the skin receptors, such as mechanoreceptors, thermoreceptors, and nociceptors, to create sensations of pressure, texture, temperature, and pain. Cutaneous haptic displays can be implemented using devices such as gloves, rings, patches, or belts that apply tactile stimuli to the user's fingers, palms, arms, or torso. Cutaneous haptic displays can also use electrostatic, ultrasonic, or magnetic forces to create tactile sensations without direct contact with the skin .

- Kinesthetic haptic displays stimulate the muscle and joint receptors, such as proprioceptors and vestibular receptors, to create sensations of force, position, movement, and balance. Kinesthetic haptic displays can be implemented using devices such as exoskeletons, joysticks, or haptic arms that apply force feedback to the user's limbs, head, or body. Kinesthetic haptic displays can also use inertial, pneumatic, or hydraulic actuators to create force feedback without direct contact with the user's body .

Haptic displays can be used for various applications, such as education, training, entertainment, gaming, art, medicine, and rehabilitation. Haptic displays can also be combined with other modalities, such as visual, auditory, or olfactory, to create multimodal interfaces that provide a richer and more immersive experience for the user. Haptic displays can also be integrated with other technologies, such as wireless communication, artificial intelligence, or brain-computer interfaces, to enable novel and advanced forms of interaction and feedback .

## Unit 3 - 3D User Interface Input Hardware

- 3D user interface input hardware are devices that capture and interpret the actions performed by the user in a 3D environment.
- The main characteristics of 3D user interface input hardware are:
  - Degrees of freedom (DOF): the number of independent ways that a body can move in space. For example, a 3D mouse can have six DOF: three for translation and three for rotation.
  - Input type/frequency of data: the kind and amount of data that the device generates. For example, discrete input devices produce single data values, such as a button press, while continuous input devices produce multiple data values, such as a joystick position.
  - Physical interaction: the amount and type of physical effort and feedback that the device requires from the user. For example, a glove can provide tactile feedback, while a tracker can be passive or active.
- Some examples of 3D user interface input hardware are:
  - Keyboards: a common input device for text entry and command selection. They can be used in desktop or immersive 3D UIs, but they have limited DOF and physical interaction.
  - 2D mice and trackballs: a common input device for pointing and selection. They can be used in desktop 3D UIs, but they have low DOF and physical interaction.
  - Pen- and touch-based tablets: a common input device for drawing and gesture recognition. They can be used in desktop or immersive 3D UIs, but they have low DOF and physical interaction.
  - Joysticks: a common input device for navigation and manipulation. They can be used in desktop or immersive 3D UIs, and they have high DOF and physical interaction.
  - Gloves: a common input device for hand and finger tracking and gesture recognition. They can be used in immersive 3D UIs, and they have high DOF and physical interaction.
  - Trackers: a common input device for head and body tracking and position sensing. They can be used in immersive 3D UIs, and they have high DOF and physical interaction. They can be passive or active, depending on the sensing technology.



# Input device characteristics

- Input devices are hardware that allow the user to communicate with the system by providing input in 3D space  .
- Input devices can be classified according to the following characteristics :
  - Degrees of freedom (DOFs): the number and type of independent parameters that define the position and orientation of an object in 3D space. For example, a 6-DOF device can report the x, y, z coordinates and the pitch, yaw, roll angles of an object. DOFs can be integral (combined in a single device) or separable (divided among multiple devices).
  - Type of electronics: digital (binary) or analog (continuous) signals that are sent from the device to the system. For example, a button is a digital device that can be either on or off, while a joystick is an analog device that can report a range of values along two axes.
  - Range of reported values: discrete (finite) or continuous (infinite) or hybrid (combination) values that the device can produce. For example, a keyboard is a discrete device that can only produce a limited number of characters, while a mouse is a continuous device that can produce an unlimited number of positions on a plane.
  - Sensing technology: active (emitting) or passive (receiving) sensors that are used to track the position and orientation of the device or the user. For example, a magnetic tracker is an active device that emits a magnetic field and measures its distortion, while a camera is a passive device that captures images of the environment and analyzes them.
  - Input modality: the type of human sensory channel that is used to provide input to the system. For example, a glove is a haptic device that uses touch and force feedback, while a microphone is an auditory device that uses speech and sound recognition.
  - Special purpose: the degree of generality or specificity of the device for a particular task or application. For example, a mouse is a general-purpose device that can be used for many tasks, while a steering wheel is a special-purpose device that is designed for driving simulations.



# Desktop input devices for 3D user interface

- Desktop input devices are devices that allow the user to interact with a 3D user interface on a desktop system, such as a computer monitor or a projection screen.
- Desktop input devices can be classified into two categories: 2D input devices and 3D input devices.

- 3D input devices are devices that capture the user's input in three dimensions, such as the position and orientation of a hand or a head. 3D input devices can be used to control a 3D user interface by directly mapping the 3D input to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a hand gesture to manipulate a 3D object or to interact with a 3D menu.
- Some examples of desktop input devices for 3D user interface are:

  - Keyboards: Keyboards are 2D input devices that allow the user to enter text or commands by pressing keys. Keyboards can be used to control a 3D user interface by assigning keys to specific functions or actions, such as using the arrow keys to move a 3D camera or to select a 3D option.
  - Mice and trackballs: Mice and trackballs are 2D input devices that allow the user to move a cursor on a 2D screen by moving the device on a surface or by rotating a ball. Mice and trackballs can be used to control a 3D user interface by mapping the cursor movement to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a mouse to point and click on a 3D object or to drag and drop a 3D object.
  - Pen- and touch-based tablets: Pen- and touch-based tablets are 2D input devices that allow the user to draw or write on a surface using a stylus or a finger. Pen- and touch-based tablets can be used to control a 3D user interface by mapping the pen or finger movement to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a pen to sketch a 3D shape or to trace a 3D path.
  - Joysticks: Joysticks are 2D input devices that allow the user to control a 2D or 3D direction by tilting a stick. Joysticks can be used to control a 3D user interface by mapping the stick tilt to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a joystick to fly a 3D airplane or to steer a 3D car.
  - Desktop 6-DOF input devices: Desktop 6-DOF input devices are 3D input devices that allow the user to control a 3D position and orientation by moving or rotating the device in 3D space. Desktop 6-DOF input devices can be used to control a 3D user interface by directly mapping the device movement or rotation to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a 6-DOF mouse to grab and move a 3D object or to look around a 3D scene.
  - Hand or body gesture sensors: Hand or body gesture sensors are 3D input devices that allow the user to control a 3D user interface using hand or body gestures, such as waving, pointing, or grabbing. Hand or body gesture sensors can be used to control a 3D user interface by directly mapping the gesture to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a hand gesture to select or manipulate a 3D object or to interact with a 3D menu.



# Tracking Devices for 3D User Interface Input Hardware

- Tracking devices are input hardware that measure the position, orientation, and/or motion of a sensor or a user in 3D space .
- Tracking devices enable 3D interaction by allowing the user to manipulate virtual objects, navigate in virtual environments, or control the viewpoint of the display.
- Tracking devices can be classified into different types based on the technology they use, such as magnetic, mechanical, acoustic, inertial, optical, or hybrid .
- Tracking devices can also be categorized based on the form factor, such as handheld, user-worn, or direct human input .
- Some examples of tracking devices are:
  - Motion trackers: measure the 3D position and orientation of a sensor attached to a user's body part, such as the head, hand, or foot .
  - Eye trackers: measure the direction of the user's gaze by detecting the corneal reflections or the electric potential differences of the eye .
  - Data gloves: measure the hand posture and gestures of the user by using bend sensors or pinch sensors .
  - 3D mice: measure the 3D position and orientation of a device that the user physically moves in 3D space .
  - GestIC® controllers: measure the 3D gestures and motions of the user's hand or finger by using electrical near-field sensing.

# Bounding Volume

- A bounding volume is a closed volume that completely contains a set of objects in computer graphics and computational geometry .
- Bounding volumes are used to improve the efficiency of geometrical operations by using simple volumes to contain more complex objects .
- Bounding volumes can be used for visibility culling, collision detection, ray tracing, occlusion queries, and other applications  .
- Bounding volumes can be classified into two types: discrete and continuous .
  - Discrete bounding volumes are composed of a finite number of primitives, such as points, lines, triangles, or polygons .
  - Continuous bounding volumes are defined by mathematical equations, such as spheres, cylinders, cones, or ellipsoids .
- Bounding volumes can also be classified into two categories: exact and approximate .
  - Exact bounding volumes are the smallest possible volumes that contain the objects .
  - Approximate bounding volumes are larger than the exact ones, but easier to compute and manipulate .
- Bounding volumes can be nested into hierarchies to speed up the queries and reduce the number of tests  .
  - Bounding volume hierarchies (BVHs) are trees of bounding volumes, where each node encloses its children nodes  .
  - BVHs can be constructed top-down or bottom-up, depending on the criteria and the data structure used  .
- Bounding volumes can be chosen based on several factors, such as the shape, size, and orientation of the objects, the type and frequency of the queries, and the trade-off between accuracy and performance  .
  - Bounding spheres are the simplest and most symmetric bounding volumes, but they may overestimate the volume of the objects  .
  - Bounding boxes are the most common and versatile bounding volumes, but they may be sensitive to the orientation of the objects .
  - Bounding cylinders, cones, and ellipsoids are more complex and less common bounding volumes, but they may fit better to some objects than spheres or boxes .

3D interaction techniques are methods for interacting with 3D environments and objects using 3D input devices and modalities. They enable users to perform various tasks such as navigation, selection, manipulation, and system control in 3D user interfaces (3D UIs). 3D interaction techniques can be classified into four categories:

- **Navigation techniques** allow users to move around and explore 3D environments. They can be based on physical metaphors (e.g., walking, flying, steering), virtual metaphors (e.g., teleportation, portals, maps), or hybrid metaphors (e.g., redirected walking, scaled world grab).
- **Selection techniques** allow users to specify one or more 3D objects as the target of an interaction. They can be based on pointing (e.g., ray-casting, cone-casting, image plane selection), reaching (e.g., virtual hand, go-go technique, HOMER technique), or indirect methods (e.g., voice, gaze, menus).
- **Manipulation techniques** allow users to change the position, orientation, size, shape, or appearance of 3D objects. They can be based on direct manipulation (e.g., virtual hand, two-handed manipulation, tangible interaction), indirect manipulation (e.g., widgets, handles, constraints), or deformation-based manipulation (e.g., free-form deformation, sculpting, sketching).
- **System control techniques** allow users to access and modify system parameters, modes, and functions. They can be based on menus (e.g., pie menus, 3D menus, marking menus), gestures (e.g., hand gestures, body gestures, speech gestures), or commands (e.g., voice, keyboard, buttons).

3D interaction techniques can be designed and developed using various strategies, such as:

- Adapting 2D interaction techniques to 3D environments, by adding a third dimension, using depth cues, or using 3D input devices.
- Using "magic" interaction techniques that exploit the capabilities of 3D UIs, such as non-physical behaviors, non-Euclidean geometries, or non-linear mappings.
- Using "natural" interaction techniques that mimic the real world, such as physical simulations, natural metaphors, or natural modalities.
- Using multimodal interaction techniques that combine different input and output modalities, such as vision, sound, touch, or gesture.
- Using two-handed interaction techniques that leverage the bimanual coordination and parallelism of human hands, such as symmetric, asymmetric, or complementary actions.

3D interaction techniques can be evaluated using various methods and criteria, such as:

- Usability testing, which measures the effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction of users performing tasks with 3D UIs.
- User studies, which compare different 3D interaction techniques or 3D UIs using quantitative or qualitative data.
- Heuristic evaluation, which assesses the compliance of 3D UIs with a set of design principles or guidelines.
- Predictive models, which estimate the performance or workload of users using 3D UIs based on mathematical or psychological theories.

3D interaction techniques can be applied to various domains and applications, such as :

- Virtual reality (VR), which immerses users in synthetic 3D environments that simulate real or imaginary scenarios.
- Augmented reality (AR), which overlays virtual 3D objects or information on top of the real world.

# Manipulation Techniques and Input Devices for 3D Interaction

- Manipulation techniques are methods for controlling the position, orientation, size, shape, or other attributes of objects in 3D virtual environments.
- Input devices are hardware or software components that capture user input and translate it into commands or data for the system.
- Manipulation techniques and input devices are closely related, as the choice of input device may affect the design and performance of manipulation techniques, and vice versa.
- Some of the factors that influence the selection and design of manipulation techniques and input devices are:
  - The number and type of degrees of freedom (DOFs) involved in the manipulation task. DOFs are independent parameters that define the state of an object, such as its x, y, and z coordinates, or its pitch, yaw, and roll angles.
  - The integration of control dimensions, which refers to how the input device maps its DOFs to the object's DOFs. For example, a mouse can control two DOFs (x and y) with one device, while a 3D tracker can control six DOFs (x, y, z, pitch, yaw, roll) with one device.
  - The mode of control, which refers to whether the input device controls the position or the force of the object. Position control means that the input device directly determines the position of the object, while force control means that the input device determines the rate of change of the position of the object.
- Some of the common categories of manipulation techniques are:
  - Direct manipulation, which involves using the input device to directly grab and move the object, as if it were a physical object. This technique is intuitive and natural, but may require high accuracy and precision from the input device and the user.
  - Indirect manipulation, which involves using the input device to control a cursor or a handle that is attached to the object, and then moving the cursor or the handle to manipulate the object. This technique can reduce the need for accuracy and precision, but may introduce a level of indirection and abstraction that reduces the sense of presence and immersion.
  - Gesture-based manipulation, which involves using the input device to perform gestures that are recognized by the system and translated into manipulation commands. This technique can enable expressive and efficient manipulation, but may require the user to learn and remember the gestures and their meanings.
  - Constraint-based manipulation, which involves using the input device to specify constraints that limit the possible movements of the object, such as snapping, aligning, or scaling. This technique can simplify and facilitate manipulation, but may reduce the flexibility and control of the user.
- Some of the common categories of input devices are:
  - Traditional input devices, such as mouse, keyboard, joystick, or trackball, that are widely used for 2D interaction, but can also be adapted for 3D interaction by using buttons, keys, or modes to switch between DOFs or control dimensions.
  - Special purpose input devices, such as 3D trackers, gloves, wands, or 3D mice, that are designed specifically for 3D interaction, and can provide more DOFs, integration, and naturalness than traditional input devices, but may also be more expensive, complex, or cumbersome to use.
  - Direct human input devices, such as biosensors, eye trackers, or brain-computer interfaces, that capture input directly from the user's body or brain signals, and can provide novel and intuitive ways of interaction, but may also be more invasive, unreliable, or difficult to calibrate than other input devices.

  - Flashlight: The user can select or manipulate 3D objects by pointing a virtual flashlight at them.

# Design Guidelines - 3D Travel Tasks

- 3D travel tasks are the tasks that involve moving the user's viewpoint or virtual body in a 3D environment.
- 3D travel tasks can be classified into different types based on the following characteristics:
  - The goal of the travel: whether it is to reach a specific destination, to explore or search the environment, or to follow a predefined path.
  - The distance to be traveled: whether it is short, medium, or long.
  - The amount of curvature or number of turns in the path: whether it is straight, curved, or complex.
  - The visibility of the target from the starting location: whether it is visible, partially visible, or invisible.
  - The number of degrees of freedom (DOF) required for the movement: whether it is 1D, 2D, or 3D.
  - The required accuracy of the movement: whether it is high, medium, or low.
  - The other primary tasks that take place during travel: whether they are navigation, manipulation, or communication tasks.
- 3D travel techniques are the methods that enable the user to perform 3D travel tasks. They can be categorized into three main types:
  - Target-based techniques: the user specifies a target location or object and the system moves the user there automatically or semi-automatically. Examples are point-and-fly, world-in-miniature, and portals.
  - Steering techniques: the user controls the direction and speed of the movement continuously using an input device or a body gesture. Examples are walking, flying, and driving.
  - Physical locomotion techniques: the user moves in the real world and the system maps the movement to the virtual world. Examples are treadmill, omni-directional surface, and redirected walking.
- 3D travel techniques should be designed according to the following guidelines :
  - The most common travel tasks should require a minimum amount of effort from the user.
  - Use high-speed transitional motions, not instant teleportation, if overall environment context is important.
  - Provide multiple travel techniques to support different travel tasks in the same application.
  - Make simple travel tasks easier by using target-based techniques for goal-oriented travel and steering techniques for exploration and search.
  - Use a physical locomotion technique if user exertion or naturalism is required.



# Travel Techniques

Travel techniques are 3D interaction techniques that allow the user to move between two locations in a virtual environment, setting the position and orientation of the user's viewpoint. Travel is the motor component of navigation, which also involves wayfinding (the cognitive process of determining and following a route) and cognitive mapping (the mental representation of the environment).

Travel techniques can be classified by the metaphor they use, such as:

- Walking: the user moves in the virtual environment by mimicking the natural walking motion, either with physical steps or with a controller that simulates the walking speed and direction. Walking techniques can provide a high sense of presence and spatial awareness, but they may also cause fatigue, motion sickness, or collisions with physical obstacles. Examples of walking techniques are treadmill-based interfaces, redirected walking, and gesture-based walking.

- Steering: the user moves in the virtual environment by controlling a direction and a speed, either with a controller (such as a joystick, a mouse, or a hand-held device) or with a head-tracking device. Steering techniques can provide a fast and flexible way of traveling, but they may also cause disorientation, loss of spatial awareness, or simulator sickness. Examples of steering techniques are flying, driving, and orbiting.

- Selection-based travel: the user moves in the virtual environment by selecting a target location or direction, either with a pointing device (such as a ray-casting or a cone-casting technique) or with a gaze-based device (such as a head-mounted display or an eye-tracker). Selection-based travel techniques can provide a precise and easy way of traveling, but they may also cause teleportation effects, such as spatial discontinuity, loss of context, or cognitive overload. Examples of selection-based travel techniques are world-in-miniature, image plane, and gaze-directed steering.

- Manipulation-based travel: the user moves in the virtual environment by manipulating the virtual world or the user's viewpoint, either with a direct manipulation technique (such as grabbing, scaling, or rotating the virtual world) or with an indirect manipulation technique (such as using a map or a menu to control the travel parameters). Manipulation-based travel techniques can provide a natural and intuitive way of traveling, but they may also cause spatial distortion, loss of realism, or user fatigue. Examples of manipulation-based travel techniques are hand-held worlds, magic carpet, and bird's eye view.



# Design Guidelines - Theoretical Foundations of Wayfinding

Wayfinding is the process of finding one's way in an unfamiliar environment, such as a virtual or augmented reality space. Wayfinding involves cognitive, perceptual, and behavioral aspects, such as orientation, route planning, decision making, and navigation. Wayfinding design is the application of principles and techniques to facilitate wayfinding in a given space. Wayfinding design can enhance the usability, accessibility, and enjoyment of a space for its users.

Some of the design guidelines for wayfinding are:

- **Create an identity at each location, different from all others.** This helps users to recognize where they are and distinguish different places from each other. Identity can be created by using distinctive features, such as landmarks, colors, shapes, textures, sounds, or symbols.
- **Use landmarks to provide orientation cues and memorable locations.** Landmarks are salient and stable features that can be seen from a distance and used as reference points. Landmarks can help users to orient themselves, estimate distances, and remember routes .
- **Organize the space into meaningful and coherent regions.** Regions are areas that share some common characteristics or functions, such as rooms, floors, zones, or themes. Regions can help users to structure the space and reduce cognitive load by grouping information and locations.
- **Provide consistent and clear signage and graphic communication.** Signage and graphic communication are visual elements that convey information, such as directions, names, categories, or instructions. Signage and graphic communication should be consistent in style, format, and placement, and clear in content, legibility, and visibility  .
- **Provide multiple cues and redundant information.** Cues are hints or clues that help users to infer or confirm information, such as arrows, icons, sounds, or feedback. Redundant information is information that is repeated or available in more than one form, such as text, symbols, or colors. Providing multiple cues and redundant information can help users to avoid confusion, uncertainty, and errors.
- **Design for different levels of user knowledge and experience.** Users may have different levels of familiarity with the space, the task, or the technology. Designers should consider the needs and preferences of different user groups, such as novices, experts, children, elderly, or people with disabilities. Designers should also provide different modes of interaction, such as exploration, guidance, or personalization, to suit different user goals and situations .

# Evaluating Wayfinding Aids for 3D Interaction Techniques

- Wayfinding aids (WATs) are tools or methods that help users navigate in 3D virtual environments (VEs) by providing spatial information or guidance.
- WATs can be classified into four categories: maps, compasses, landmarks, and signs .
- Maps show the layout of the VE from a top-down or bird's-eye perspective, and can be either static or dynamic, embedded or overlaid, or global or local.
- Compasses indicate the direction of the user's orientation or the destination, and can be either 2D or 3D, embedded or overlaid, or absolute or relative.
- Landmarks are distinctive objects or features in the VE that serve as reference points or cues for navigation, and can be either natural or artificial, static or dynamic, or global or local.
- Signs are textual or graphical symbols that provide directions or instructions for navigation, and can be either static or dynamic, embedded or overlaid, or global or local.
- Evaluating WATs is important to understand their impact on user performance, experience, and preference in 3D VEs.
- Evaluation methods can be either quantitative or qualitative, objective or subjective, or experimental or observational.
- Quantitative methods measure numerical data such as task completion time, error rate, distance traveled, or number of turns.
- Qualitative methods collect descriptive data such as user feedback, comments, ratings, or preferences.
- Objective methods rely on observable and measurable data such as system logs, sensors, or eye tracking.
- Subjective methods depend on user-reported data such as questionnaires, interviews, or think-aloud protocols.
- Experimental methods involve controlled settings, predefined tasks, and predefined WATs, and compare different WATs or conditions.
- Observational methods involve natural settings, open-ended tasks, and user-selected WATs, and observe user behavior and interaction with WATs.
- Evaluation criteria can be based on different aspects of user performance, experience, and preference, such as effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction, usability, learnability, memorability, or presence.
- Effectiveness measures how well the user can complete the navigation task or reach the destination with the help of WATs.
- Efficiency measures how fast or how much effort the user can complete the navigation task or reach the destination with the help of WATs.
- Satisfaction measures how pleased or comfortable the user is with the WATs or the navigation task.
- Usability measures how easy or intuitive the WATs are to use or understand.
- Learnability measures how quickly or easily the user can learn to use or understand the WATs.
- Memorability measures how well the user can remember the spatial information or the WATs after the navigation task.
- Presence measures how immersed or engaged the user feels in the VE or the navigation task.



# Design Guidelines - System Control

System control is the task of issuing commands or changing settings in a 3D user interface (3D UI). It is often integrated with other 3D interaction tasks, such as navigation, selection, or manipulation. System control techniques should be designed to avoid disturbing the flow of action of an interaction task.

Some design guidelines for system control are:

- Use graphical menus for 3D UIs that are consistent, efficient, and easy to use. Graphical menus can be adapted from 2D menus, or designed as 1-DOF menus or 3D widgets.
- Use voice commands for 3D UIs that are natural, intuitive, and hands-free. Voice commands can be used to complement or replace graphical menus, especially for complex or infrequent commands.
- Use gestures for 3D UIs that are expressive, flexible, and ergonomic. Gestures can be used to perform direct or indirect commands, such as pointing, grabbing, or swiping.
- Use multimodal interaction for 3D UIs that are robust, redundant, and adaptable. Multimodal interaction combines different input modalities, such as speech, gesture, or gaze, to enhance the user experience and performance.
- Use feedback for 3D UIs that are informative, responsive, and appropriate. Feedback can be visual, auditory, or haptic, and should provide the user with clear and timely information about the system state and the effects of their actions.



# Classification of 3D Interaction Techniques

3D interaction techniques are methods that allow users to perform tasks in virtual environments using 3D input devices and feedback. 3D interaction techniques can be classified according to the type of task they support, the type of input they use, and the type of feedback they provide.

## Task-based Classification

According to this classification, 3D interaction techniques can be grouped into four categories based on the task they support :

- **Navigation techniques** allow users to move and orient themselves in the virtual environment. Examples of navigation techniques are flying, walking, steering, and teleporting.
- **Selection techniques** allow users to specify one or more objects of interest in the virtual environment. Examples of selection techniques are ray-casting, cone-casting, and spotlight selection.
- **Manipulation techniques** allow users to change the position, orientation, size, shape, or appearance of selected objects in the virtual environment. Examples of manipulation techniques are direct manipulation, handle-based manipulation, and gesture-based manipulation.
- **System control techniques** allow users to access menus, dialogs, or other system functions in the virtual environment. Examples of system control techniques are pie menus, voice commands, and gaze-based menus.

## Input-based Classification

According to this classification, 3D interaction techniques can be grouped into three categories based on the type of input they use:

- **Position-based techniques** use the absolute or relative position of the input device to control the interaction. Examples of position-based techniques are direct manipulation, ray-casting, and flying.
- **Rate-based techniques** use the rate of change of the input device to control the interaction. Examples of rate-based techniques are steering, handle-based manipulation, and voice commands.
- **Gesture-based techniques** use the recognition of predefined gestures or patterns of the input device to control the interaction. Examples of gesture-based techniques are gesture-based manipulation, cone-casting, and spotlight selection.

## Feedback-based Classification

According to this classification, 3D interaction techniques can be grouped into two categories based on the type of feedback they provide:

- **Visual feedback** uses graphical elements to convey information to the user. Examples of visual feedback are cursors, highlights, and shadows.
- **Non-visual feedback** uses auditory, haptic, or olfactory elements to convey information to the user. Examples of non-visual feedback are sounds, vibrations, and smells.



# Graphical Menus

- Graphical menus are an integral and essential component of user interfaces and have been widely used for 2D desktop applications .
- Graphical menus for 3D UIs are the 3D equivalent of the 2D menus that have proven to be a successful system control technique in desktop UIs .
- Graphical menus in 3D UIs can be subdivided into three categories: adapted 2D menus, 1-DOF menus, and 3D widgets .
- Adapted 2D menus are the simplest form of graphical menus for 3D UIs. They are basically 2D menus that are embedded in a 3D environment, either as a hand-held flat panel display or as a fixed screen in the virtual world  .
- 1-DOF menus are graphical menus that use one degree of freedom (DOF) for navigation and selection. They can be either linear or circular, and can be either attached to the user's hand or body, or placed in the virtual world .
- 3D widgets are graphical menus that take advantage of the extra DOF available in a 3D environment to enable more complex menu structures or better visual affordances for menu entries. They can be either 3D objects that represent menu items, or 3D structures that organize menu items .
- Some examples of 3D widgets are pie menus, marking menus, flower menus, and 3D radial menus .
- Graphical menus for 3D UIs have some advantages and disadvantages compared to other system control techniques, such as voice commands, gestures, or physical buttons .
- Some advantages are: familiarity to users, ease of use, high expressiveness, low error rate, and high feedback .
- Some disadvantages are: occlusion, clutter, visual fatigue, limited scalability, and low immersion .
- Some design issues for graphical menus for 3D UIs are: positioning of graphical user interface elements in space, choosing an appropriate selection technique, representation of the menu and its overall structure, and adaptation to different devices and contexts  .

- 3D interaction techniques are methods for manipulating and interacting with 3D objects and environments in virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) applications.
- 3D interaction techniques can be classified into four categories: selection, manipulation, navigation, and system control.
- Selection techniques are used to select one or more 3D objects from the virtual or augmented scene, such as ray-casting, cone-casting, or image-based selection .
- Manipulation techniques are used to change the position, orientation, size, or shape of 3D objects, such as direct manipulation, virtual hand, or gesture-based manipulation .
- Navigation techniques are used to move the user's viewpoint or body in the 3D space, such as walking, flying, or teleporting.
- System control techniques are used to access menus, dialogs, or commands in the 3D interface, such as voice, gaze, or hand gestures.
- Some design guidelines for 3D interaction techniques are :
  - Match the interaction technique to the task and the user's goals, such as using direct manipulation for precise positioning or ray-casting for distant selection.
  - Provide feedback to the user on the state and outcome of the interaction, such as highlighting the selected object, showing the manipulation handles, or displaying the navigation path.
  - Avoid occlusion and clutter in the 3D scene, such as using transparent or wireframe objects, or using depth cues to indicate the relative distance of objects.
  - Support multiple modalities and input devices, such as using voice, gesture, or touch to complement the visual and spatial interaction.
  - Consider the physical and cognitive limitations of the user, such as avoiding fatigue, motion sickness, or confusion caused by the 3D interaction.

- 3D user interfaces (3D UIs) are interfaces that allow users to interact with 3D environments and objects using various input and output devices, such as head-mounted displays, controllers, trackers, haptic devices, etc.

- Virtual reality (VR) is a technology that creates immersive and interactive simulations of real or imagined environments.

- Non-immersive VR uses a desktop computer and a monitor to display a 3D virtual environment that the user can navigate and manipulate with a mouse, keyboard, or joystick.
- Semi-immersive VR uses a large screen or a projection system to display a 3D virtual environment that the user can interact with using a tracking device, such as a wand, a glove, or a headset.
- Fully immersive VR uses a head-mounted display (HMD) or a head-up display (HUD) to provide a 360-degree view of a 3D virtual environment that the user can explore and manipulate with a tracking device and a motion controller, such as a hand-held device, a glove, or a body suit.

- Computer-generated VR uses computer graphics and software to create and render a 3D virtual environment that can be dynamic and interactive.
- 360-degree video VR uses a camera or a set of cameras to capture and stitch a 360-degree video of a real environment that can be static or dynamic, but not interactive.
- VR requires four main components to create an immersive and interactive experience: a virtual environment, a VR device, a tracking system, and a user interface.
- A virtual environment is a 3D representation of a real or imagined space that can be populated with objects, characters, sounds, and effects.
- A VR device is a hardware device that displays the virtual environment to the user and provides sensory feedback, such as visual, auditory, haptic, and olfactory stimuli.
- A tracking system is a hardware and software system that tracks the user's position, orientation, and movement in the real world and maps them to the virtual world.

- A taxonomy of AR and MR applications can help to understand the different types, purposes, features, and challenges of these technologies, as well as to guide their design, development, and evaluation.

  - **Educational purpose**: the intended learning outcome or objective of the application, such as knowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, or evaluation .
  - **Application domain**: the field or area of knowledge or practice that the application supports, such as science, engineering, arts, humanities, health, or entertainment .
  - **Output technology**: the device or system that displays the virtual information or objects, such as head-mounted displays, handheld devices, projection-based systems, or spatial displays  .
  - **Visual guidance**: the technique or method that helps the user to perceive, understand, and interact with the virtual information or objects, such as annotations, labels, icons, arrows, highlights, or cues  .
  - **Interaction technique**: the technique or method that allows the user to manipulate, control, or communicate with the virtual information or objects, such as gestures, voice, touch, buttons, or menus  .
  - **Reality-virtuality continuum**: the degree of integration or separation between the real and virtual environments, ranging from fully real to fully virtual, with AR and MR in between  .

# Technology and Features of Augmented Reality

- Augmented reality (AR) is the technology that expands our physical world, adding layers of digital information onto it.
- Unlike virtual reality (VR), AR does not create the whole artificial environments to replace real with a virtual one. AR appears in direct view of an existing environment and adds sounds, videos, graphics to it.
- AR incorporates three features: a combination of digital and physical worlds, interactions made in real time, and accurate 3D identification of virtual and real objects.
- AR can be experienced through various devices, such as smartphones, tablets, head-mounted displays (HMDs), smart glasses, and projectors.
- AR can be classified into three types based on the degree of immersion and interaction: marker-based AR, markerless AR, and projection-based AR.
- Marker-based AR uses a camera and a visual marker, such as a QR code or a logo, to trigger the display of digital content on the screen. The marker acts as a reference point for the position and orientation of the virtual object.
- Markerless AR uses sensors, such as GPS, accelerometer, gyroscope, and compass, to detect the location and orientation of the device and overlay digital content on the screen. This type of AR does not require any predefined markers and can work in any environment.
- Projection-based AR uses a projector to cast digital images onto physical surfaces, such as walls, floors, or objects. The projected images can be interactive and respond to touch or gesture inputs from the user.
- AR can be used for various applications, such as education, entertainment, gaming, tourism, navigation, marketing, retail, manufacturing, healthcare, and military  .
- AR can offer benefits such as enhancing user experience, increasing engagement, improving learning outcomes, providing real-time information, facilitating collaboration, and reducing costs and errors .



# Difference between AR and VR

- AR stands for **augmented reality**, while VR stands for **virtual reality**.
- AR **enhances** the real-world environment by adding virtual elements to it, while VR **entirely replaces** the current setting with a digital simulation .
- AR uses a real-world setting, while VR is completely virtual .
- AR is only 25 percent virtual, while VR is 75 percent virtual.
- AR does not require a headset device, but VR does .
- AR users can control their presence in the real world, while VR users are controlled by the system.
- AR users are in contact with the real world, while VR users move in a completely fictional world.
- Examples of AR experiences include Snapchat lenses and the game Pokemon Go, while examples of VR experiences include Oculus Rift and HTC Vive.



# Challenges with AR

Augmented reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real world with digital information and media, such as 3D models, videos, sounds, and texts. AR has many potential applications in various domains, such as education, entertainment, health, tourism, and industry. However, AR also faces some challenges that limit its adoption and development. Some of the main challenges are:

- **Hardware quality**: AR requires high-performance devices that can support complex graphics, sensors, cameras, and displays. However, most of the current AR devices are either too expensive, too bulky, or too low-quality to provide a satisfying user experience. For example, some AR headsets have limited field of view, low resolution, or poor battery life. Some AR glasses have privacy and safety issues, such as recording without consent or obstructing the user's vision. Some AR smartphones have compatibility and connectivity problems, such as requiring specific models or operating systems, or relying on unstable internet access. Therefore, AR hardware needs to be improved in terms of affordability, usability, and reliability .

- **Content availability**: AR depends on the quality and quantity of the digital content that can be overlaid on the real world. However, creating and delivering AR content is not easy, as it requires specialized skills, tools, and platforms. Moreover, AR content needs to be context-aware, meaning that it should adapt to the user's location, orientation, preferences, and interactions. However, there is a lack of standardized and widely adopted formats, protocols, and frameworks for AR content creation and distribution. Therefore, AR content needs to be more accessible, diverse, and dynamic .

- **Awareness and education**: AR is still a relatively new and unfamiliar technology for many people, especially for the general public. Many people do not know what AR is, how it works, or what benefits it can offer. Moreover, many people have misconceptions or fears about AR, such as thinking that it is too complex, too intrusive, or too dangerous. Therefore, AR needs to be more widely promoted, demonstrated, and explained to raise awareness and educate potential users and stakeholders .

- **Technical issues**: AR involves many technical challenges that affect the functionality and quality of the AR experience. Some of these challenges are:

  - **Tracking and registration**: AR requires accurate and robust tracking and registration of the user's position, orientation, and movement, as well as the alignment and synchronization of the digital content with the real world. However, tracking and registration can be affected by various factors, such as lighting conditions, occlusions, dynamic environments, and sensor errors. Therefore, AR needs to employ more advanced and reliable tracking and registration methods and algorithms.

  - **Interaction and feedback**: AR requires natural and intuitive interaction and feedback mechanisms that can enable the user to control and manipulate the AR content, as well as to receive information and guidance from the AR system. However, interaction and feedback can be challenging in AR, as they depend on the availability and suitability of the input and output devices, such as touchscreens, gestures, voice, buttons, haptics, sounds, and visuals. Therefore, AR needs to design and implement more effective and user-friendly interaction and feedback techniques and modalities.

  - **Performance and scalability**: AR requires high performance and scalability to support large-scale and complex AR scenarios and applications, such as multi-user, multi-device, and multi-modal AR. However, performance and scalability can be limited by the computational and storage capabilities of the AR devices, as well as by the bandwidth and latency of the network connections. Therefore, AR needs to optimize and enhance the performance and scalability of the AR systems and architectures, such as by using cloud computing, edge computing, or distributed computing.



# AR systems and functionality

- AR stands for augmented reality, which is a technology that enhances the perception of the real world with digital information, such as images, sounds, or text.
- AR systems are composed of three main components: a display device, a tracking device, and a computing device.
- The display device can be a head-mounted display (HMD), a handheld device (such as a smartphone or a tablet), or a projection-based device (such as a smart glass or a hologram).
- The tracking device can be a camera, a sensor, a GPS, or a combination of them, that captures the position and orientation of the user and the environment.
- The computing device can be a laptop, a desktop, a mobile device, or a cloud server, that processes the input data and generates the output content.
- AR systems can be classified into two types: marker-based and markerless.
- Marker-based AR systems use predefined images or patterns (such as QR codes or fiducial markers) to anchor the virtual content to the real world.
- Markerless AR systems use natural features (such as edges, corners, or textures) or location data (such as GPS or Wi-Fi) to align the virtual content to the real world.
- AR systems can be used for various purposes, such as education, entertainment, gaming, tourism, navigation, health care, industrial design, and military training   .
- AR systems can provide several benefits, such as enhancing the user's perception, interaction, and collaboration, improving the user's learning and performance, and creating immersive and engaging experiences   .
- AR systems can also pose some challenges, such as technical limitations, ethical issues, privacy concerns, and user acceptance   .

Augmented reality (AR) is a method by which we can alter our real world by adding some digital elements to it. This is done by superimposing a digital image on the person’s current view thus it enhances the experience of reality.

There are also other types of AR that are less common but still useful for some applications. These are:

- **Projection-based AR**: This type of AR projects a digital image onto a physical surface, such as a wall or a table. The projected image can be interactive and respond to touch or gesture. Projection-based AR example: A projection-based AR keyboard that allows typing on any surface.
- **Superimposition-based AR**: This type of AR replaces a part or the whole of the original view with a new image. The system needs to recognize the object and its shape to overlay the new image accurately. Superimposition-based AR example: A superimposition-based AR app that shows how a furniture item would look in a room.

# Visualization Techniques for Augmented Reality

- Augmented Reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real world environment with synthetic information, such as images, text, sound, or video.
- Visualization techniques for AR are methods and patterns that help to present the synthetic information in a way that is coherent, meaningful, and effective for the user's task and context.
- Visualization techniques for AR can be classified into four categories: data integration, scene management, interaction, and evaluation.

## Data Integration

- Data integration refers to the process of aligning, registering, and blending the synthetic information with the real world environment.
- Data integration techniques can be further divided into three subcategories: spatial, temporal, and semantic.
- Spatial integration techniques deal with the geometric alignment of the synthetic information with the real world coordinates, such as using markers, sensors, or computer vision methods.
- Temporal integration techniques deal with the synchronization of the synthetic information with the real world events, such as using timestamps, triggers, or event-based methods.
- Semantic integration techniques deal with the logical and contextual alignment of the synthetic information with the real world meaning, such as using ontologies, rules, or context-aware methods.

## Scene Management

- Scene management refers to the process of organizing, arranging, and rendering the synthetic information in the AR scene.
- Scene management techniques can be further divided into three subcategories: layout, occlusion, and visualization.
- Layout techniques deal with the spatial distribution and arrangement of the synthetic information in the AR scene, such as using grids, clusters, or graphs.
- Occlusion techniques deal with the visibility and transparency of the synthetic information in the AR scene, such as using depth cues, shadows, or cutaways.
- Visualization techniques deal with the graphical representation and appearance of the synthetic information in the AR scene, such as using icons, labels, or colors.

## Interaction

- Interaction refers to the process of enabling the user to manipulate, explore, and control the synthetic information in the AR scene.
- Interaction techniques can be further divided into three subcategories: input, output, and feedback.
- Input techniques deal with the methods and devices that allow the user to provide commands and data to the AR system, such as using gestures, voice, or touch.
- Output techniques deal with the methods and devices that allow the AR system to present information and responses to the user, such as using audio, haptics, or projection.
- Feedback techniques deal with the methods and devices that allow the AR system to indicate the state and status of the interaction to the user, such as using sounds, vibrations, or highlights.

## Evaluation

- Evaluation refers to the process of assessing the effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction of the visualization techniques for AR.
- Evaluation techniques can be further divided into three subcategories: metrics, methods, and criteria.
- Metrics deal with the quantitative and qualitative measures that can be used to evaluate the visualization techniques for AR, such as using accuracy, speed, or preference.
- Methods deal with the experimental and analytical procedures that can be used to collect and analyze the evaluation data, such as using surveys, interviews, or simulations.
- Criteria deal with the standards and guidelines that can be used to compare and judge the visualization techniques for AR, such as using usability, aesthetics, or functionality.

# Mobile Projection Interfaces for Augmented Reality

- Mobile projection interfaces are a type of augmented reality (AR) interfaces that use a mobile device with a built-in or attached projector to display digital content on physical surfaces.
- Mobile projection interfaces can overcome some of the limitations of handheld display devices, such as small screen size, limited field of view, and occlusion issues.
- Mobile projection interfaces can also enable new forms of interaction and collaboration, such as projecting on objects, walls, floors, or other people, and using gestures, touch, or voice to control the projection.
- Mobile projection interfaces can be classified into different application classes, depending on how they adapt to the object, context, or user input. Some examples are:

  - Object-adaptive applications: These applications use the shape, color, texture, or features of the object as a basis for the projection. For example, projecting a virtual keyboard on a flat surface, or projecting a game on a toy car.
  - Context-adaptive applications: These applications use the location, orientation, or movement of the device or the user as a basis for the projection. For example, projecting a map on the ground, or projecting a compass on the wall.
  - Camera-controlled applications: These applications use the camera of the device to capture the projection and provide feedback or interaction. For example, projecting a barcode scanner on a product, or projecting a drawing tool on a paper.

- Mobile projection interfaces face some challenges and limitations, such as:

  - Projector brightness and resolution: The projector should be bright enough to be visible in different lighting conditions, and have a high enough resolution to display clear and detailed content.
  - Projector alignment and calibration: The projector should be aligned and calibrated with the object or the surface to avoid distortion or misalignment of the content.
  - Projector focus and depth: The projector should be able to adjust the focus and depth of the projection to match the distance and shape of the object or the surface.
  - Projector occlusion and interference: The projector should avoid occlusion and interference from other objects or people that may block or reflect the projection.
  - Projector battery and heat: The projector should have a long battery life and low heat generation to enable prolonged and comfortable use of the device.

- Mobile projection interfaces are a promising and emerging technology for augmented reality applications, that can offer new possibilities for interaction and collaboration, as well as enhance the user experience and engagement.



# Marker-less Tracking for Augmented Reality

- Marker-less tracking is a technique that allows augmented reality (AR) applications to display virtual content on top of real-world scenes without the need for predefined markers or targets.
- Marker-less tracking can use different methods to estimate the pose (position and orientation) of the camera relative to the scene, such as feature-based, model-based, or learning-based approaches.
- Feature-based methods use algorithms to detect and describe distinctive points or regions in the images, such as corners, edges, or blobs, and match them across frames or with a reference image or map. Examples of feature-based algorithms are ORB, FREAK, SIFT, SURF, etc .
- Model-based methods use a 3D model of the scene or object to estimate the pose of the camera by aligning the model with the image features or contours. Examples of model-based algorithms are edge-based, region-based, or point-based methods.
- Learning-based methods use machine learning techniques, such as classifiers, regressors, or neural networks, to learn the pose of the camera from the image data, either directly or indirectly. Examples of learning-based algorithms are KNN, Random Forest, Extremely Randomized Trees, SVM, Bayes classifier, etc .
- Marker-less tracking has several advantages over marker-based tracking, such as:
  - It can work with any scene or object, without requiring the user to print or place markers .
  - It can provide more realistic and immersive AR experiences, as the virtual content can blend with the natural environment .
  - It can enable more complex and interactive AR applications, such as games, navigation, education, etc .
- Marker-less tracking also has some challenges and limitations, such as:
  - It requires more computational resources and processing power than marker-based tracking, as it involves more complex algorithms and data .
  - It may suffer from occlusion, illumination, or motion issues, as the scene or object may change or move in unpredictable ways .
  - It may not work well in featureless or cluttered environments, as it may not find enough or reliable features to track .

# Evaluating AR Systems

Augmented Reality (AR) systems are systems that combine real and virtual elements in a user's perception, enhancing the user's interaction with the real world. AR systems can be used for various purposes, such as education, entertainment, navigation, maintenance, and medical applications. However, designing and developing effective AR systems is not a trivial task, as it involves many technical and human factors that need to be considered and evaluated.

Evaluating AR systems is the process of assessing the quality, usability, and effectiveness of AR systems, using various methods and metrics. Evaluation can help identify the strengths and weaknesses of AR systems, compare different design alternatives, and provide feedback for improvement. Evaluation can also help validate the hypotheses and claims of AR research, and demonstrate the impact and value of AR systems to the users and stakeholders.

There are different types of evaluation methods that can be used for AR systems, depending on the goals, context, and resources of the evaluation. Some of the common types of evaluation methods are:

- User-based evaluation: This type of evaluation involves testing the AR system with real or potential users, and collecting data on their behavior, performance, preferences, satisfaction, and feedback. User-based evaluation can be conducted in controlled laboratory settings or in naturalistic field settings, depending on the level of realism and ecological validity required. User-based evaluation can provide insights into the usability, user experience, and effectiveness of the AR system, as well as the user's needs, expectations, and preferences. User-based evaluation can use various data collection methods, such as questionnaires, interviews, observations, video recordings, eye tracking, physiological measurements, and logging.
- Non-user-based evaluation: This type of evaluation does not involve testing the AR system with users, but rather relies on other sources of data, such as expert reviews, heuristic evaluations, cognitive walkthroughs, simulations, and benchmarks. Non-user-based evaluation can be useful for evaluating the technical aspects of the AR system, such as the accuracy, stability, and performance of the tracking, registration, and rendering components. Non-user-based evaluation can also be useful for identifying potential usability problems and design flaws, before conducting user-based evaluation. Non-user-based evaluation can be faster, cheaper, and easier to conduct than user-based evaluation, but it may not capture the user's perspective and context.
- Mixed evaluation: This type of evaluation combines user-based and non-user-based methods, to obtain a more comprehensive and balanced assessment of the AR system. Mixed evaluation can help triangulate the data from different sources, and provide a richer and deeper understanding of the AR system and its impact. Mixed evaluation can also help address the limitations and challenges of each evaluation method, such as the trade-off between realism and control, the validity and reliability of the data, and the ethical and practical issues of involving users.

Evaluating AR systems is a complex and challenging task, as it involves many factors and dimensions that need to be considered and measured. Some of the common factors and dimensions that can be used for evaluating AR systems are:

- Usability: This factor refers to the ease of use, learnability, efficiency, and satisfaction of the AR system, from the user's perspective. Usability can be measured by various metrics, such as task completion time, error rate, user ratings, and user comments. Usability can be influenced by many aspects of the AR system, such as the interface design, the interaction techniques, the feedback mechanisms, and the user's cognitive load.
- User experience: This factor refers to the subjective and emotional aspects of the user's interaction with the AR system, such as the enjoyment, engagement, immersion, presence, and flow. User experience can be measured by various metrics, such as user ratings, user comments, facial expressions, and physiological responses. User experience can be influenced by many aspects of the AR system, such as the content, the aesthetics, the narrative, and the social presence.
- Effectiveness: This factor refers to the extent to which the AR system achieves its intended goals and outcomes, such as learning, entertainment, navigation, or maintenance. Effectiveness can be measured by various metrics, such as test scores, performance indicators, behavioral changes, and user ratings. Effectiveness can be influenced by many aspects of the AR system, such as the functionality, the fidelity, the relevance, and the feedback.

Evaluating AR systems is an essential and integral part of the AR design and development process, as it can help ensure the quality, usability, and effectiveness of the AR system, and provide valuable insights and feedback for improvement.
