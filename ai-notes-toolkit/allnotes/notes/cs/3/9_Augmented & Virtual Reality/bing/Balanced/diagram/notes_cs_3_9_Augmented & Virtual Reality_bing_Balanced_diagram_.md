

## Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS

- Virtual reality (VR) is a technology that creates an immersive and interactive simulation of a three-dimensional environment, using computer-generated graphics, sounds, and other sensory inputs.
- Virtual environments (VEs) are the specific settings or scenarios that are simulated in VR, such as a virtual classroom, a virtual museum, or a virtual battlefield.
- VR and VEs can be used for various purposes, such as education, entertainment, training, therapy, and research.
- VR and VEs can be classified into different types, based on the degree of immersion, realism, and interactivity they provide.
- Immersion is the extent to which the user feels present and engaged in the VE, and loses awareness of the real world.
- Realism is the degree to which the VE resembles the real world, in terms of visual, auditory, haptic, and other cues.
- Interactivity is the level of control and feedback the user has over the VE, and the responsiveness of the VE to the user's actions and inputs.
- Some examples of VR and VE types are:

  - Non-immersive VR: The user views the VE on a desktop monitor or a large screen, and interacts with it using a mouse, keyboard, or joystick. The user is aware of the real world and has limited immersion and realism. An example is a flight simulator game.
  - Semi-immersive VR: The user views the VE on a head-mounted display (HMD) or a projection screen, and interacts with it using a handheld controller, a glove, or a body suit. The user is partially immersed and has moderate realism. An example is a virtual roller coaster ride.
  - Fully immersive VR: The user views the VE on a HMD or a projection screen, and interacts with it using a full-body motion capture system, a haptic device, or a brain-computer interface. The user is fully immersed and has high realism. An example is a virtual surgery training system.



### The historical development of VR

- Virtual reality (VR) is the simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with in a seemingly real way by a person using special equipment, such as a headset and gloves.
- The concept of VR dates back to the 1800s, when stereoscopes were invented to create the illusion of depth in photographs by using twin mirrors to project a single image.
- In the 1950s, Morton Heilig, a filmmaker and inventor, developed the Sensorama, a device that could display 3D images, sounds, smells, and vibrations to create an immersive cinematic experience.
- In 1968, Ivan Sutherland and Bob Sproull created the first VR head-mounted display (HMD) system, called the Sword of Damocles, which could track the user's head movements and display simple wireframe graphics.
- In the 1970s and 1980s, VR research and development was mainly driven by the military and academic institutions, such as the US Department of Defense, the National Science Foundation, and NASA, who used VR for training, simulation, and visualization purposes.
- In 1987, Jaron Lanier, a computer scientist and entrepreneur, coined the term "virtual reality" and founded VPL Research, a company that produced VR products, such as the EyePhone, the DataGlove, and the DataSuit .
- In the 1990s, VR became more popular and accessible to the public, with the emergence of arcade games, home consoles, and personal computers that could support VR applications, such as Sega VR, Nintendo Virtual Boy, and VRML.
- In the 2000s and 2010s, VR technology advanced rapidly, with the introduction of more sophisticated and affordable HMDs, such as the Oculus Rift, the HTC Vive, the PlayStation VR, and the Samsung Gear VR, as well as the development of VR content for entertainment, education, health, and social media.
- In the 2020s, VR is expected to continue to evolve and expand, with the integration of artificial intelligence, haptics, eye tracking, and 5G, as well as the emergence of new applications, such as VR tourism, VR therapy, and VR art.

Visual displays are output devices that present visual information to the user in 3D user interfaces. - **Workbenches**: These are table-top displays that show 3D images on a horizontal surface. ### Haptic Displays

- Haptic displays are interfaces that provide haptic feedback, which is the sensation of touch, to the user .
- Haptic feedback can be divided into two types: cutaneous feedback and kinesthetic feedback.
  - Cutaneous feedback refers to the stimulation of the skin receptors, which can convey information about the shape, texture, and temperature of objects .
  - Kinesthetic feedback refers to the stimulation of the muscle and joint receptors, which can convey information about the position, orientation, and force of objects .
- Haptic displays can be categorized by the type of stimuli they generate, and correspondingly by the type of sensory receptors they stimulate.
  - Kinesthetic displays are devices that render forces to the user, such as force-feedback joysticks, haptic gloves, and exoskeletons .
  - Cutaneous displays are devices that render tactile sensations to the user, such as vibrotactile actuators, thermal displays, and electrocutaneous stimulators .
- Haptic displays can be used for various applications, such as virtual reality, teleoperation, gaming, education, and rehabilitation .
- Haptic displays can enhance the realism, immersion, and interaction of 3D user interfaces by providing multisensory feedback and natural manipulation .



### Choosing Output Devices for 3D User Interfaces

Output devices, also called display devices, allow the machine to provide information or feedback to one or more users through the human perceptual system. Most of them are focused on stimulating the visual, auditory, or haptic senses.

Some of the factors to consider when choosing output devices for 3D user interfaces are:

- The type and amount of information to be displayed. For example, if the information is complex, dynamic, or spatial, then a 3D visual display may be more suitable than a 2D one. If the information is multimodal, then a combination of output devices may be needed to convey it effectively.
- The level of immersion and presence desired. Immersion refers to the degree to which the user feels surrounded by and involved in the virtual environment, while presence refers to the degree to which the user feels that the virtual environment is real and that he or she is actually there. Different output devices can create different levels of immersion and presence, depending on their characteristics such as field of view, resolution, stereoscopy, latency, etc. For example, a head-mounted display (HMD) can provide a high level of immersion and presence, but it may also cause discomfort, fatigue, or motion sickness.
- The number and location of users. Some output devices are designed for single-user or multi-user scenarios, while others can support both. Some output devices are fixed in a certain location, while others are portable or wearable. The choice of output devices may depend on how many users need to access the information, where they are located, and how they interact with each other.
- The cost and availability of the output devices. The output devices vary in their cost, complexity, and availability. Some output devices are more expensive, require more maintenance, or are less accessible than others. The choice of output devices may depend on the budget, resources, and constraints of the application.

Some of the common output devices for 3D user interfaces are:

- Visual displays. These output devices provide visual information to the user, such as images, text, graphics, or animations. They can be classified into two categories: immersive and non-immersive. Examples of immersive visual displays are HMDs, cave automatic virtual environments (CAVEs), and dome projection systems. Examples of non-immersive visual displays are monitors, projectors, and tablets  .
- Audio output. These output devices provide auditory information to the user, such as sounds, speech, or music. They can be classified into two categories: spatial and non-spatial. Spatial audio output creates a sense of direction and distance of the sound sources, while non-spatial audio output does not. Examples of spatial audio output are headphones, speakers, and sound domes. Examples of non-spatial audio output are earphones, telephones, and radios .
- Tactile and haptic output. These output devices provide tactile or haptic information to the user, such as force, vibration, or texture. They can be classified into two categories: kinesthetic and cutaneous. Kinesthetic haptic output creates a sense of movement and position of the user's body or limbs, while cutaneous haptic output creates a sense of contact and pressure on the user's skin. Examples of kinesthetic haptic output are force feedback devices, exoskeletons, and motion platforms. Examples of cutaneous haptic output are gloves, vests, and belts .



## Unit 3 - 3D User Interface Input Hardware

- 3D user interface input hardware are devices that capture and interpret the actions performed by the user in a 3D environment.
- The main characteristics of these devices are:
  - Degrees of freedom (DOF): the number of independent ways that a body can move in space. For example, a 3D mouse can have six DOF: three for translation and three for rotation.
  - Input type/frequency of data: the kind and amount of data that the device generates. For example, discrete input devices produce single data values, such as a button press, while continuous input devices produce multiple data values, such as a position or orientation.
  - Physical interaction: the amount and type of physical effort required by the user to operate the device. For example, some devices are passive, meaning they do not require any external power or force, while others are active, meaning they provide some form of feedback or resistance to the user.
- Some examples of 3D user interface input hardware are:
  - Keyboards: a set of keys that can be used to enter text, commands, or shortcuts. They are typically used in desktop 3D UIs, where they can complement other input devices such as mice or trackballs. They have low DOF, discrete input, and passive interaction.
  - 2D mice and trackballs: devices that can sense the movement of a user's hand on a flat surface or a ball. They are also typically used in desktop 3D UIs, where they can provide 2D cursor control or 3D navigation. They have two or three DOF, continuous input, and passive interaction.
  - Pen- and touch-based tablets: devices that can sense the position and pressure of a user's finger or stylus on a flat surface. They can be used in desktop or immersive 3D UIs, where they can provide direct manipulation, sketching, or gesture input. They have two or three DOF, continuous input, and passive or active interaction.
  - Joysticks: devices that can sense the direction and magnitude of a user's hand movement on a stick. They can be used in desktop or immersive 3D UIs, where they can provide 3D navigation, selection, or manipulation. They have two to six DOF, continuous input, and passive or active interaction.



### Input device characteristics

- Input devices are hardware that allow the user to communicate with the system by providing input in 3D space  .

### Cullers and Occluders

- Cullers and occluders are techniques for improving the performance and realism of virtual and augmented reality applications by reducing the number of objects that need to be rendered and by hiding virtual objects behind real ones, respectively.
- Culling is the process of eliminating objects or parts of objects that are not visible to the user, either because they are outside the field of view, too far away, or occluded by other objects. Culling can save computational resources and improve the frame rate of the application.
- Occlusion is the phenomenon of an object blocking the view of another object, either partially or completely. In virtual reality, occlusion can be achieved by using depth information to determine which objects are closer to the user and which are farther away. In augmented reality, occlusion is more challenging, because it requires the ability to detect and segment real objects in the scene and to overlay virtual objects behind them in a realistic way.
- Cullers and occluders can be classified into different types based on the criteria they use to determine the visibility of objects. Some common types are:

  - Frustum culling: This technique eliminates objects that are outside the viewing frustum, which is the pyramid-shaped volume defined by the user's position, orientation, and field of view. Frustum culling can be done by testing the bounding boxes or spheres of the objects against the six planes of the frustum.
  - Level of detail (LOD) culling: This technique reduces the complexity of objects that are far away from the user by using simplified models or textures. LOD culling can be done by using a distance threshold or a screen-space error metric to decide which level of detail to use for each object.
  - Occlusion culling: This technique eliminates objects that are hidden by other objects in the scene. Occlusion culling can be done by using occlusion queries, which test the visibility of an object by rendering a small portion of it and checking the number of pixels that pass the depth test, or by using occlusion maps, which store the depth information of the scene in a texture and use it to compare the depth of the objects.
  - Image-based occlusion: This technique is specific to augmented reality and uses the camera image to detect and segment real objects in the scene and to overlay virtual objects behind them. Image-based occlusion can be done by using computer vision algorithms, such as edge detection, contour extraction, or semantic segmentation, or by using deep learning models, such as convolutional neural networks or generative adversarial networks.

### Design Guidelines - Theoretical Foundations of Wayfinding

Wayfinding is the process of orienting oneself in a physical or virtual environment and navigating from one place to another. Wayfinding is essential for 3D interaction techniques, as it helps users to explore, understand, and use complex information spaces. Wayfinding design is the application of principles and methods to create effective and intuitive wayfinding systems for users.

Some of the theoretical foundations of wayfinding design are:

- **Cognitive mapping**: This is the mental representation of spatial information that users construct and use to navigate. Cognitive maps are influenced by the structure, layout, landmarks, and cues of the environment, as well as the user's goals, preferences, and prior knowledge. Cognitive maps are not accurate or complete, but rather subjective and dynamic. Wayfinding design should support the formation and use of cognitive maps by providing clear and consistent information, feedback, and orientation.
- **Environmental legibility**: This is the degree to which the environment can be perceived, understood, and remembered by users. Environmental legibility depends on the organization, differentiation, and identity of the elements of the environment, such as paths, nodes, edges, districts, and landmarks. Wayfinding design should enhance the environmental legibility by creating a coherent and meaningful structure, using distinctive and memorable features, and highlighting the relationships and hierarchies among the elements.
- **User-centered design**: This is the approach of designing for the needs, preferences, and characteristics of the users, rather than the system or the content. User-centered design involves understanding the user's context, goals, tasks, and expectations, as well as testing and evaluating the design with the users. Wayfinding design should adopt a user-centered approach by involving the users in the design process, tailoring the information and interaction to the user's level of expertise and interest, and providing multiple modes and options for wayfinding.
- **Universal design**: This is the principle of designing for the widest possible range of users, regardless of their abilities, disabilities, age, culture, or background. Universal design aims to create inclusive and accessible environments that can accommodate the diversity and variability of the users. Wayfinding design should follow the universal design principle by ensuring that the information and interaction are perceivable, understandable, operable, and adaptable for all users.

- Wayfinding is the process of navigating and orienting oneself in an unfamiliar environment.
- User centered wayfinding support is the design of wayfinding systems that consider the needs, preferences, and abilities of different users.
- User centered wayfinding support can improve the usability, accessibility, and satisfaction of users in complex environments such as hospitals, campuses, or virtual reality .
- User centered wayfinding support can involve various techniques, such as:
  - Providing clear and consistent signage and landmarks .

  - Providing maps and overview displays .

  - Observing and interviewing users in the environment .
  - Conducting usability tests and surveys .
  - Measuring objective and subjective indicators of wayfinding performance, such as time, errors, distance, confidence, and satisfaction .
  - Using eye tracking, physiological sensors, or virtual reality to measure attention, cognitive load, and emotional state .

### Evaluating Wayfinding Aids for 3D Interaction Techniques

- Wayfinding aids (WATs) are tools or techniques that help users navigate in 3D virtual environments (VEs) by providing spatial information or guidance.
- WATs can be classified into four categories: maps, compasses, landmarks, and signs .
- Maps show the layout of the VE from a top-down or bird's eye perspective, and can be either static or dynamic, embedded or overlaid, or global or local   .
- Compasses indicate the direction of the user's orientation or the destination, and can be either 2D or 3D, embedded or overlaid, or absolute or relative .
- Landmarks are distinctive objects or features in the VE that help users recognize their location or direction, and can be either natural or artificial, static or dynamic, or global or local .
- Signs are textual or graphical symbols that provide instructions or information for navigation, and can be either static or dynamic, embedded or overlaid, or global or local .
- Evaluating WATs can be done by measuring their effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction  .
- Effectiveness refers to the accuracy and completeness of the navigation task, such as the number of errors, the number of visits to the destination, or the spatial knowledge acquired  .
- Efficiency refers to the time and effort required to complete the navigation task, such as the task completion time, the distance traveled, or the mental workload  .
- User satisfaction refers to the subjective preference and comfort of the user, such as the ease of use, the enjoyment, or the presence  .
- Evaluating WATs can be done by conducting empirical studies with human participants, using either controlled experiments or user studies.
- Controlled experiments compare different WATs or conditions in a rigorous and systematic way, using predefined tasks, measures, and hypotheses.
- User studies collect qualitative and quantitative feedback from users in a more natural and flexible way, using open-ended tasks, interviews, and questionnaires.

### Classification of 3D Interaction Techniques

3D interaction techniques are methods that allow users to perform tasks in virtual environments using 3D input devices and feedback. They can be classified according to the type of task they support, such as:

- **Mapping**: This refers to the relationship between the input device or technique and the virtual object or action. For example, a direct mapping means that the input device or technique mimics the virtual object or action, such as a virtual hand. An indirect mapping means that the input device or technique uses a different representation or metaphor, such as a ray-casting technique.
- **Feedback**: This refers to the type and modality of the feedback that the user receives from the system or application. For example, visual feedback is the most common form of feedback, but other modalities such as auditory, haptic, or olfactory can also be used. Feedback can also be classified as continuous or discrete, depending on whether it changes smoothly or abruptly.



### Graphical Menus

- 3D widgets are graphical menus that take advantage of the extra DOF available in a 3D environment to enable more complex menu structures or better visual affordances for menu entries. They can be either 3D objects, 3D icons, or 3D text .
- Some of the challenges and design issues for graphical menus in 3D UIs are: positioning of graphical user interface elements in space, choosing an appropriate selection technique, representation of the menu and its overall structure, and providing feedback and feedforward  .
- Some of the advantages and disadvantages of graphical menus in 3D UIs are: familiarity and ease of use, flexibility and scalability, occlusion and clutter, and performance and accuracy .

### Augmented reality methods

Augmented reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real-world environment by adding digital elements to it. AR can be used for various purposes, such as entertainment, education, marketing, gaming, and navigation. AR can be delivered through different devices, such as smartphones, tablets, smart glasses, and head-mounted displays (HMDs).

There are different types of AR methods that are based on how the digital content is triggered and positioned in the real-world environment. Some of the common AR methods are:

- **Marker-based AR**: This method uses a special visual object, such as a QR code, a logo, or a symbol, as a marker to initiate the digital content. The AR device scans the marker and calculates its orientation and position to overlay the digital content on top of it. For example, a marker-based mobile app can show how a furniture item would look in the user's room by scanning a marker on a catalog.

- **Marker-less AR**: This method does not require any marker to trigger the digital content. Instead, it uses the location and orientation data from the device's sensors, such as GPS, accelerometer, and gyroscope, to position the digital content in the real-world environment. For example, a marker-less mobile app can show the directions and landmarks on the user's screen by using the device's location and orientation data.

- **Projection-based AR**: This method uses a projector to project the digital content onto a physical surface, such as a wall, a floor, or a table. The projector can also sense the user's interaction with the projected content and respond accordingly. For example, a projection-based AR system can project a virtual keyboard on a table and detect the user's keystrokes.

- **Superimposition-based AR**: This method replaces or enhances a part or the whole of the real-world environment with the digital content. The AR device recognizes the object in the real-world environment and replaces it with the digital content. For example, a superimposition-based AR app can show how a user would look with different hairstyles or makeup by replacing the user's face with the digital content.

These are some of the common AR methods that are used to create immersive and interactive experiences for the users. AR methods can also be combined or customized to suit different applications and scenarios. AR is a rapidly evolving technology that has many potential benefits and challenges for the future.

### Marker-less tracking for augmented reality

- Marker-less tracking is a technique that allows developers to display augmented reality content without the need for predefined target images or objects (markers) in the real environment.
- Marker-less tracking relies on scanning the surrounding environment and detecting natural features such as edges, corners, textures, or planes that can be used as reference points for the virtual content.
- Marker-less tracking can be classified into two types: image-based and model-based.
  - Image-based tracking uses a database of images or video frames that are matched with the current camera view using feature detection and description algorithms such as ORB, FREAK, SIFT, SURF, etc. The matching features are then used to estimate the pose of the camera relative to the scene .
  - Model-based tracking uses a 3D model of the scene or object that is registered with the camera view using methods such as edge detection, contour matching, or point cloud alignment. The model can be either pre-built or reconstructed online using structure from motion or simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) techniques.
- Marker-less tracking has several advantages over marker-based tracking, such as:
  - It can work with any environment or object, without the need for artificial markers that may be occluded, damaged, or unavailable.
  - It can provide more realistic and immersive experiences, as the virtual content can be aligned with the natural features of the scene.
  - It can enable more interactive and dynamic applications, as the virtual content can respond to changes in the environment or user actions.
- Marker-less tracking also has some challenges and limitations, such as:
  - It requires more computational resources and processing power, as it involves complex algorithms and large amounts of data.
  - It may suffer from drift, noise, or inaccuracies, as it depends on the quality and stability of the features detected in the environment.
  - It may not work well in low-light, featureless, or cluttered environments, as it may fail to find enough or reliable features to track.

### Evaluating AR Systems

Augmented Reality (AR) systems are systems that combine real and virtual elements in a user's perception. AR systems can be used for various purposes, such as education, entertainment, navigation, and industrial applications. Evaluating AR systems is important to assess their usability, effectiveness, and user satisfaction.

There are different types of evaluation methods for AR systems, depending on the research questions, the system characteristics, and the available resources. Some of the common evaluation methods are:

- **User-based evaluation**: This method involves testing the system with real users in a controlled or natural setting. User-based evaluation can measure various aspects of the user experience, such as performance, preference, workload, presence, and enjoyment. User-based evaluation can be done using different techniques, such as questionnaires, interviews, observations, eye-tracking, physiological measures, and video analysis. User-based evaluation can provide valuable feedback and insights for improving the system design and functionality. However, user-based evaluation can also be costly, time-consuming, and challenging to conduct, especially for complex or novel AR systems.
- **Non-user-based evaluation**: This method involves testing the system without involving real users, but using other means, such as simulations, models, or expert reviews. Non-user-based evaluation can measure various aspects of the system quality, such as accuracy, robustness, efficiency, and compatibility. Non-user-based evaluation can be done using different techniques, such as benchmarks, metrics, algorithms, and heuristics. Non-user-based evaluation can provide objective and quantitative data for comparing and optimizing the system performance and reliability. However, non-user-based evaluation can also be limited, as it may not capture the subjective and contextual aspects of the user experience.
- **Mixed evaluation**: This method involves combining user-based and non-user-based evaluation techniques to obtain a comprehensive and balanced assessment of the system. Mixed evaluation can leverage the strengths and overcome the weaknesses of each evaluation method. Mixed evaluation can be done using different approaches, such as triangulation, complementarity, or integration. Mixed evaluation can provide a holistic and nuanced understanding of the system and its impact on the user and the environment.

Evaluating AR systems is a complex and multidisciplinary task that requires careful planning, execution, and analysis. Evaluating AR systems can help researchers and developers to identify the strengths and weaknesses of their systems, to validate their hypotheses and assumptions, and to generate new knowledge and insights for advancing the field of AR.
