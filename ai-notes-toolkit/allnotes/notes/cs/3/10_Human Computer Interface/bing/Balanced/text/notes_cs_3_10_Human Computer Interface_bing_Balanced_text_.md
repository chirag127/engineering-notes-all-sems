### Importance of 8 good design principles for human computer interface

Human computer interface (HCI) is the field of study that focuses on the interaction between humans and computers. HCI aims to design interfaces that are easy to use, efficient, and satisfying for the users. A good interface can enhance the user experience, productivity, and satisfaction, while a bad interface can cause frustration, errors, and dissatisfaction.

One of the most influential and widely cited sources of HCI design principles is Shneiderman's eight golden rules of interface design  . These rules provide a convenient and succinct summary of the key principles of interface design. The eight rules are:

- **Strive for consistency** by utilizing familiar icons, colors, menu hierarchy, call-to-actions, and user flows when designing similar situations and sequence of actions. Consistency reduces the cognitive load and learning curve for the users, and increases their confidence and efficiency.
- **Enable frequent users to use shortcuts** such as keyboard commands, abbreviations, function keys, hidden commands, etc. Shortcuts allow expert users to perform tasks faster and more conveniently, and enhance their sense of control and satisfaction.
- **Offer informative feedback** for every user action, such as visual, auditory, or tactile cues, progress indicators, confirmation messages, error messages, etc. Feedback informs the users about the system status, the outcome of their actions, and the possible next steps. Feedback enhances the user's understanding, engagement, and trust in the system.
- **Design dialogs to yield closure** by organizing the tasks into meaningful groups or sequences, and providing clear indications of the beginning, middle, and end of each group or sequence. Closure helps the users to keep track of their progress, goals, and expectations, and gives them a sense of accomplishment and satisfaction.
- **Prevent errors** by designing the interface to avoid or minimize the possibility of user mistakes, such as incorrect inputs, invalid commands, unintended actions, etc. Error prevention reduces the user's frustration, confusion, and dissatisfaction, and improves the system's reliability and usability.
- **Permit easy reversal of actions** by providing undo, redo, cancel, back, or other similar functions that allow the users to correct their mistakes or change their minds. Reversibility increases the user's confidence and willingness to explore the system, and reduces the negative consequences of errors.
- **Support internal locus of control** by designing the interface to give the users the initiative, flexibility, and freedom to perform their tasks, rather than forcing them to follow the system's pace, sequence, or logic. Locus of control enhances the user's sense of autonomy, empowerment, and satisfaction.
- **Reduce short-term memory load** by designing the interface to minimize the amount of information, options, or actions that the users have to remember or process at any given time, such as by using visual cues, chunking, recognition rather than recall, etc. Memory load reduction improves the user's efficiency, accuracy, and satisfaction.

These eight rules are not exhaustive or absolute, but they provide a useful framework and guidelines for designing effective and user-friendly interfaces. By following these rules, HCI designers can create interfaces that meet the user's needs, preferences, and expectations, and improve the overall quality of human-computer interaction.

### A brief history of screen design

- Screen design is the process of creating visual interfaces for digital devices, such as computers, smartphones, and tablets.
- Screen design has evolved over time, influenced by technological developments, user needs, and aesthetic trends.
- Some of the major milestones in the history of screen design are:

  - The first screen displays were attached to computers in the 1950s, using cathode ray tubes (CRTs) that emitted beams of electrons onto phosphor-coated screens. These screens could only display monochrome text and simple graphics, and had low resolution and refresh rates.
  - In the 1970s, screen design was mainly driven by developers, who created text-based interfaces with many fields, commands, and menus. These screens were often cluttered, confusing, and inconsistent, and required users to memorize complex syntax and commands.
  - In the 1980s, graphical user interfaces (GUIs) emerged, inspired by the work of Xerox PARC and popularized by Apple and Microsoft. GUIs used icons, windows, menus, and pointers to represent objects and actions, and allowed users to interact with the screen using a mouse. GUIs made screen design more user-friendly, intuitive, and attractive, and opened up new possibilities for graphics, animation, and multimedia.
  - In the 1990s, the advent of the World Wide Web and the Internet revolutionized screen design, as web pages became the dominant form of digital content. Web design required new skills and tools, such as HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, and introduced new challenges, such as cross-browser compatibility, accessibility, and usability. Web design also incorporated elements from print design, such as typography, layout, and color.
  - In the 2000s, screen design became more diverse and complex, as new devices, platforms, and applications emerged. Mobile devices, such as smartphones and tablets, introduced new constraints and opportunities for screen design, such as smaller screens, touch input, and location-awareness. Screen design also became more interactive, dynamic, and personalized, as web applications, social media, and user-generated content became more prevalent.
  - In the 2010s, screen design continued to evolve and innovate, as new technologies, such as artificial intelligence, augmented reality, and virtual reality, created new possibilities and challenges for screen design. Screen design also became more responsive, adaptive, and inclusive, as designers aimed to create screen experiences that could accommodate different devices, contexts, and users. Screen design also became more collaborative, participatory, and ethical, as designers engaged with users and stakeholders, and considered the social and environmental impacts of their work.



### The graphical user interface for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction in the subject of Human Computer Interface

- A graphical user interface (GUI) is a type of user interface that allows users to interact with a computer system through graphical elements such as icons, menus, windows, buttons, etc.
- A GUI is designed to make the user experience more intuitive, efficient, and enjoyable by reducing the need for memorizing commands or typing text.
- A GUI is composed of two main components: the input devices and the output devices.
  - The input devices are the hardware or software components that allow the user to provide information or commands to the system, such as a mouse, a keyboard, a touchscreen, a microphone, etc.
  - The output devices are the hardware or software components that display the information or feedback from the system to the user, such as a monitor, a speaker, a printer, etc.
- A GUI is based on the following principles:
  - Direct manipulation: the user can directly manipulate the graphical objects on the screen, such as dragging, resizing, selecting, etc., and see the immediate effects of their actions.
  - Visibility: the user can easily see the available options and functions of the system, such as menus, toolbars, icons, etc., and access them with minimal effort.
  - Consistency: the user can expect the same behavior and appearance of the graphical elements across different applications and contexts, such as color, shape, size, etc., and avoid confusion and errors.
  - Feedback: the user can receive clear and timely information or confirmation from the system about the status and outcome of their actions, such as sounds, messages, animations, etc., and correct or adjust their actions accordingly.
  - User control: the user can have the freedom and flexibility to choose their preferred way of interacting with the system, such as customizing the layout, the settings, the shortcuts, etc., and undo or cancel their actions if needed.

### The Concept of Direct Manipulation

- Direct manipulation is an approach to human-computer interaction (HCI) that involves continuous representation of objects of interest and rapid, reversible, and incremental actions and feedback.
- Direct manipulation aims to make the user feel like they are performing a task themselves, not through an intermediary like a computer.
- Direct manipulation is often associated with graphical user interfaces (GUIs) and "what you see is what you get" (WYSIWYG) interfaces, where the user can manipulate objects on the screen using physical actions such as dragging and dropping.
- Some examples of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - File managers, where the user can move, copy, delete, or rename files and folders by dragging and dropping them on the screen.
  - Drawing programs, where the user can create, modify, or erase shapes and colors by using a mouse or a stylus.
  - Video games, where the user can control the movements and actions of a character or an object by using a joystick, a keyboard, or a touch screen.
- Some advantages of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - They are easy to learn and use, as they rely on natural and intuitive actions and feedback.
  - They are engaging and satisfying, as they give the user a sense of control and accomplishment.
  - They are efficient and flexible, as they allow the user to perform multiple tasks and explore different options without switching modes or commands.
- Some disadvantages of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - They may require more screen space and graphical resources, as they need to display the objects and actions in a realistic and detailed way.
  - They may not support complex or abstract tasks, as they may lack the expressive power and precision of textual or symbolic commands.
  - They may not be accessible or adaptable, as they may depend on the user's physical abilities and preferences.

### Principles of user interface for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction in the subject of Human Computer Interface

User interface (UI) is the part of a software system that allows users to interact with it and perform tasks. UI design is the process of creating user interfaces that are effective, efficient, and satisfying for the users. UI design is based on some principles that guide the design decisions and help achieve the desired goals. Some of the common principles of UI design are:

- **Clarity**: The UI should communicate clearly and simply in the user's own language, using familiar concepts and terms. The UI should avoid ambiguity, confusion, and unnecessary complexity. The UI should provide clear feedback and guidance to the user, and explain the consequences of the user's actions.
- **Consistency**: The UI should follow consistent rules and patterns throughout the system, as well as adhere to the established conventions and standards of the platform and the domain. The UI should use consistent terminology, layout, color, typography, icons, and controls. The UI should behave consistently and predictably, and match the user's expectations.
- **Accessibility**: The UI should be accessible and usable by a wide range of users, regardless of their abilities, preferences, or contexts. The UI should support different input and output modes, such as keyboard, mouse, touch, voice, and screen readers. The UI should provide alternative ways of accessing and presenting information, such as text, images, audio, and video. The UI should also adapt to different screen sizes, resolutions, orientations, and devices.
- **Feedback**: The UI should provide timely, relevant, and appropriate feedback to the user, to inform them of the system's status, progress, and results. The UI should use visual, auditory, and tactile cues to indicate the state of the system, the user's actions, and the outcomes. The UI should also provide error messages and warnings that are clear, concise, and constructive, and help the user recover from mistakes.
- **Familiarity**: The UI should leverage the user's existing knowledge and experience, and use common metaphors, analogies, and symbols that the user can easily understand and relate to. The UI should also follow the norms and expectations of the user's culture, language, and environment. The UI should make the user feel comfortable and confident, and avoid surprises and frustrations.
- **Design Standards**: The UI should follow the established design guidelines and best practices of the platform and the domain, to ensure compatibility, usability, and quality. The UI should also comply with the relevant laws, regulations, and ethical principles, to ensure safety, security, and privacy. The UI should also respect the user's rights, preferences, and choices, and allow the user to control the system and the data.
- **Structure & Hierarchy**: The UI should organize the information and functionality in a logical and meaningful way, based on the user's goals and tasks. The UI should use clear, consistent, and recognizable models, categories, and labels, to help the user navigate and understand the system. The UI should also use visual elements, such as size, color, contrast, alignment, and spacing, to create a clear visual hierarchy, and emphasize the most important and relevant information and actions.
- **Simplicity**: The UI should make simple, common, and frequent tasks easy, and provide shortcuts and automation for them. The UI should also minimize the cognitive load and the memory demand on the user, and avoid unnecessary steps, options, and information. The UI should also reduce the visual clutter and the noise, and focus on the essential and the relevant.
- **Flexibility**: The UI should accommodate different user needs, preferences, and situations, and provide customization and personalization options for them. The UI should also support different levels of user expertise and experience, and provide learning and help resources for them. The UI should also allow the user to perform different types of tasks and interactions, such as exploration, discovery, creation, and collaboration.
- **Aesthetics**: The UI should be visually appealing and attractive, and create a positive and engaging user experience. The UI should use aesthetic elements, such as color, shape, texture, and animation, to create a mood, a tone, and a personality for the system. The UI should also balance the form and the function, and ensure that the aesthetics do not compromise the usability and the performance.



## Unit 2 - Design process

- The design process is a systematic and creative approach to solving problems or meeting needs by developing new or improved products, services, systems or environments.
- The design process typically involves the following stages:
  - Identify the problem or need: Define the scope, context and criteria of the design challenge.
  - Research and analyse: Gather and evaluate relevant information and data about the problem, the users, the existing solutions and the design constraints.
  - Generate ideas: Explore and generate possible design solutions using various methods such as brainstorming, sketching, modelling, prototyping, etc.
  - Select and refine: Compare and evaluate the design solutions against the criteria and select the most promising one. Refine and improve the selected solution by testing, feedback and iteration.
  - Communicate and present: Communicate and present the final design solution using appropriate media and formats such as reports, drawings, diagrams, posters, etc.
- The design process is not linear or fixed, but rather iterative and flexible. It can be adapted to different situations and contexts, and it can involve feedback loops, revisions and modifications at any stage.
- The design process requires various skills and knowledge, such as creativity, critical thinking, collaboration, communication, research, analysis, evaluation, etc. It also involves ethical, social, environmental and economic considerations.

### Importance of 8 human characteristics for design process

Designers need to consider how users remember their products, and how to reduce the cognitive load and enhance the recall. For example, designers can use mnemonics, chunking, repetition, and feedback to help users remember important information and actions.
- **Learning**: Learning is the process of acquiring new knowledge and skills through experience, observation, or instruction. Learning can be intentional or incidental, and can involve different types of cognitive processes, such as reasoning, problem-solving, and creativity. Designers need to consider how users learn their products, and how to support their learning goals and needs. For example, designers can use tutorials, guides, examples, and challenges to help users learn how to use their products effectively and efficiently.
- **Motivation**: Motivation is the drive that influences human behavior and actions. Motivation can be intrinsic or extrinsic, and can be affected by factors such as goals, values, interests, and rewards. Designers need to consider what motivates users to use their products, and how to enhance their motivation and engagement. For example, designers can use gamification, personalization, feedback, and social features to make their products more fun, relevant, rewarding, and social.
- **Emotion**: Emotion is the affective state that accompanies human experiences and reactions. Emotion can be positive or negative, and can influence human cognition, behavior, and well-being. Designers need to consider how users feel about their products, and how to elicit positive emotions and avoid negative ones. For example, designers can use aesthetics, humor, stories, and empathy to make their products more attractive, enjoyable, meaningful, and human-centered .
- **Communication**: Communication is the exchange of information and ideas between humans through verbal and non-verbal means. Communication can involve different modes, such as speech, writing, gestures, and images. Designers need to consider how users communicate with their products, and how to facilitate effective and efficient communication. - **Sociality**: Sociality is the tendency and ability of humans to form social relationships and groups. Sociality can involve different aspects, such as cooperation, competition, trust, and norms. ### Human interaction speeds

Human interaction speeds are the rates at which people can perform various tasks using different communication methods. They are important to consider in the design process of human-computer interfaces, as they affect the usability, efficiency and satisfaction of the users. Some of the common human interaction speeds are:

- Reading: The average adult reading speed for English prose text in the United States is around 250 to 300 words per minute. This reading speed can be substantially increased when using rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP), which displays words one at a time in the same location on the screen.
- Listening: People can comfortably hear words that are spoken at from 150 to 160 words per minute. However, the comprehension and retention of the information may vary depending on the content, context and speaker's accent.
- Speaking: People can speak at an average rate of 125 to 150 words per minute. However, this rate may vary depending on the language, topic, emotion and audience.
- Keying: People can type on a keyboard at an average speed of 40 to 60 words per minute. However, this speed may vary depending on the skill, experience, layout and feedback of the keyboard.
- Handwriting: People can write by hand at an average speed of 31 words per minute. However, this speed may vary depending on the legibility, style and medium of the handwriting.
- Reacting: People can react to a stimulus at an average time of 200 to 250 milliseconds. However, this time may vary depending on the type, intensity and location of the stimulus, as well as the attention, expectation and motivation of the person.

These human interaction speeds can be used as guidelines or benchmarks for designing human-computer interfaces that match the capabilities and limitations of the users. For example, the interface should provide feedback within 100 milliseconds to be perceived as instantaneous, and within 1 second to maintain the user's attention. The interface should also avoid presenting too much or too little information at a time, and use appropriate modalities and formats to facilitate the user's comprehension and retention.

### Organizing Screen Elements

- Organizing screen elements is the process of arranging the components of a user interface in a logical, consistent, and aesthetic way.
- Organizing screen elements helps to create a clear and intuitive layout that facilitates user interaction and navigation.
- Organizing screen elements involves applying the principles of alignment, proximity, contrast, repetition, and white space.

#### Alignment
- Alignment is the placement of screen elements along a common edge, axis, or center.
- Alignment creates a sense of order, harmony, and connection among screen elements.
- Alignment can be horizontal, vertical, or diagonal, depending on the orientation of the screen and the type of content.
- Alignment can be achieved by using grids, guides, rulers, or snap-to features in screen design tools.

#### Proximity
- Proximity is the grouping of related screen elements close together and separating unrelated elements far apart.
- Proximity creates a sense of association, hierarchy, and organization among screen elements.
- Proximity can be achieved by using margins, padding, borders, or containers to define the boundaries of screen elements.

#### Contrast
- Contrast is the difference between screen elements in terms of size, shape, color, texture, or value.
- Contrast creates a sense of emphasis, distinction, and interest among screen elements.
- Contrast can be achieved by using fonts, colors, images, icons, or backgrounds to create visual variety and hierarchy.

#### Repetition
- Repetition is the use of consistent screen elements across multiple screens or sections of a user interface.
- Repetition creates a sense of unity, coherence, and familiarity among screen elements.
- Repetition can be achieved by using logos, icons, colors, fonts, or styles to create a consistent look and feel.

#### White Space
- White space is the empty space between screen elements or around the edges of a screen.
- White space creates a sense of balance, clarity, and elegance among screen elements.
- White space can be achieved by using appropriate margins, padding, line spacing, or paragraph spacing to create breathing room and avoid clutter.

### Screen navigation and flow for the notes of the Unit 3 - Screen Designing in the subject of Human Computer Interface

- Screen navigation and flow refer to the way users move through the screens of an application or website, and how the screens are connected and organized.
- Screen navigation and flow are important aspects of screen design, as they affect the usability, efficiency, and user satisfaction of the application or website.
- Screen navigation and flow can be designed using various methods and techniques, such as:
  - Hierarchical navigation: The screens are arranged in a tree-like structure, where each screen has a parent screen and may have one or more child screens. The user can move up and down the hierarchy by following the links or buttons on each screen. This method is suitable for applications or websites that have a clear and logical structure of information or tasks, such as e-commerce, educational, or news websites.
  - Sequential navigation: The screens are arranged in a linear sequence, where the user can move forward and backward by following the steps or stages of a process or task. The user can also jump to a specific screen by using a progress indicator or a menu. This method is suitable for applications or websites that have a predefined and fixed order of actions or information, such as online forms, surveys, or tutorials.
  - Parallel navigation: The screens are arranged in a network-like structure, where the user can move freely among the screens without following a specific path or order. The user can access any screen from any other screen by using a global navigation system, such as a menu bar, a tab bar, or a sidebar. This method is suitable for applications or websites that have a diverse and flexible content or functionality, such as social media, entertainment, or gaming websites.
  - Hybrid navigation: The screens are arranged in a combination of the above methods, where the user can use different types of navigation depending on the context or the goal of the user. For example, an application or website may use hierarchical navigation for the main sections, sequential navigation for the sub-sections, and parallel navigation for the features or options within each sub-section. This method is suitable for applications or websites that have a complex and dynamic content or functionality, such as productivity, creativity, or collaboration tools.
- Screen navigation and flow can be evaluated and improved by using various methods and techniques, such as:
  - User testing: The user testing involves observing and interviewing real or potential users of the application or website, and collecting feedback and data on their behavior, preferences, and problems while using the screen navigation and flow. This method can help identify the strengths and weaknesses of the screen navigation and flow, and suggest possible improvements or changes.
  - Heuristic evaluation: The heuristic evaluation involves applying a set of general principles or guidelines for good screen navigation and flow, and checking whether the application or website conforms to them or not. This method can help identify the common errors or violations of the screen navigation and flow, and suggest possible corrections or enhancements.
  - Wireframing and prototyping: The wireframing and prototyping involve creating low-fidelity or high-fidelity representations of the screen navigation and flow, and testing them with users or experts. This method can help visualize and communicate the screen navigation and flow, and iterate and refine them based on the feedback or data.

### New and Navigation schemes selection of window for the notes of the Unit 4 - Windows in the subject of Human Computer Interface

- Windows can have different types, sizes, shapes, styles, and behaviors depending on the purpose and context of use.
- Windows can be classified into primary windows, secondary windows, modal windows, modeless windows, and pop-up windows.
- Primary windows are the main windows that display the core content and functionality of an application or a document. They usually have a title bar, a menu bar, a toolbar, a status bar, and a scroll bar.

- Pop-up windows are the windows that appear temporarily when the user performs a certain action, such as clicking a button, selecting a menu item, or hovering over an element. - Hierarchical navigation schemes are the navigation schemes that follow a tree-like structure or a nested arrangement of categories and subcategories. They can be used for tasks that involve browsing, exploring, or searching a large amount of information, such as navigating a file system, a website, or a database.
- Network navigation schemes are the navigation schemes that allow the user to move freely between any windows or elements that are related or linked to each other. They can be used for tasks that involve finding, comparing, or analyzing information from multiple sources, such as browsing the web, using a map, or reading a hypertext.

- Selection of window and navigation schemes depends on several factors, such as the user's goals, tasks, preferences, skills, and expectations, the application's purpose, functionality, and content, the device's capabilities, limitations, and constraints, and the environment's conditions, context, and culture.

  - Use modal windows for requiring the user's attention and action before they can return to the primary window.
  - Use modeless windows for allowing the user to interact with the primary window while accessing other information or functionality.
  - Use pop-up windows for showing temporary or contextual information or functionality that are triggered by the user's action.
  - Use sequential navigation schemes for tasks that have a clear start and end point and a fixed number of steps.
  - Use hierarchical navigation schemes for tasks that involve browsing, exploring, or searching a large amount of information that are organized into categories and subcategories.

### Interaction Devices

Interaction devices are the hardware and software components that enable the communication and exchange of information between humans and computers. Interaction devices can be classified into three main categories:

- Input devices: These are the devices that allow users to enter data, commands, or instructions into a computer system. Examples of input devices are keyboards, mice, touchscreens, microphones, scanners, cameras, etc.
- Output devices: These are the devices that allow users to receive feedback, results, or information from a computer system. Examples of output devices are monitors, speakers, printers, projectors, headphones, etc.

- Graphical user interface (GUI): This is the most common type of interaction device, where users interact with graphical elements on a screen, such as icons, menus, buttons, windows, etc. GUIs are usually controlled by input devices such as keyboards, mice, touchscreens, etc.
- Voice user interface (VUI): This is a type of interaction device where users interact with a computer system using speech or voice commands. VUIs are usually controlled by input devices such as microphones, and provide feedback through output devices such as speakers or headphones.
- Gesture user interface (GUI): This is a type of interaction device where users interact with a computer system using body movements, such as hand gestures, facial expressions, eye movements, etc. GUIs are usually controlled by input devices such as cameras, sensors, or motion trackers, and provide feedback through output devices such as monitors, projectors, or haptic devices.
- Brain-computer interface (BCI): This is a type of interaction device where users interact with a computer system using brain signals, such as electroencephalography (EEG) or functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). BCI are usually controlled by input devices such as electrodes, and provide feedback through output devices such as monitors, speakers, or haptic devices.

- Affordance: The interaction device should indicate its functionality and how it can be used by the user.
- Feedback: The interaction device should provide clear and timely feedback to the user about the status and outcome of the interaction.
- Consistency: The interaction device should follow the same rules and conventions across different contexts and applications.
- Learnability: The interaction device should be easy to learn and remember how to use by the user.
- Efficiency: The interaction device should allow the user to perform the desired tasks quickly and accurately.
- Satisfaction: The interaction device should provide a pleasant and enjoyable experience for the user.

### Drivers for Software Tools in Human Computer Interface

- A driver is a software that provides a **software interface** to a hardware device, enabling operating systems and other computer programs to access hardware functions without needing to know precise details about the hardware being used .
- A driver communicates with the device through the **computer bus or communications subsystem** to which the hardware connects .
- Drivers are essential for software tools in human computer interface because they allow the software to interact with the input and output devices that are used by humans to communicate with the computer, such as keyboards, mice, game controllers, touch screens, etc.
- There are different types of drivers for different types of devices, such as:
  - **Human Interface Devices (HID)**: A device class definition to replace PS/2-style connectors with a generic USB driver to support HID devices such as keyboards, mice, game controllers, and so on.   - **Audio Drivers**: A driver that controls the audio output and input of the computer, such as the speakers, headphones, microphone, or sound card. Audio drivers are responsible for processing sound signals, encoding and decoding audio formats, and supporting different sampling rates, bit depths, and channels.
  - **Printer Drivers**: A driver that controls the printing output of the computer, such as the printer, scanner, or fax machine. Printer drivers are responsible for converting the data sent by the software into a format that the printer can understand, such as PostScript, PCL, or GDI.
  - **Network Drivers**: A driver that controls the network communication of the computer, such as the Ethernet, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or modem. Network drivers are responsible for sending and receiving data packets, encoding and decoding network protocols, and supporting different network standards, such as TCP/IP, UDP, or HTTP.
