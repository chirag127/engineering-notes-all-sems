### State in Ancient India

- The evolution of the state in ancient India went through several phases:
- In the Indus Valley civilization, there was a complex system of town planning and administration but the nature of the state structure is unclear.
- In the Vedic period, tribal chiefdoms and small kingdoms emerged. The tribal chieftain was called rajan. The allegiance was personal and the state structure loose.
- The Gana-sanghas or republics (e.g. Shakyas, Mallas, Vajjis) had oligarchical and democratic elements with decision making by village assemblies or guilds.
- The Mahajanapadas that emerged in 6th-5th century BCE led to the growth of large kingdoms (e.g. Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa). The king derived his authority from the possession of sovereignty or supreme authority. Taxes and forced labour were collected.
- The Mauryan empire established by Chandragupta Maurya in 4th century BCE was the first large imperial state in India. It had a bureaucracy and administrative structure to collect taxes, maintain army, etc. The emperor wielded paramount sovereignty and authority.
- The Gupta empire from 4th to 6th century CE witnessed a powerful imperial state with a complex administrative structure. The emperor was considered divine and ultimate authority. There was decentralization of administration at the local level.
- In general, the evolution of the state mirrored the evolution from tribes and chiefdoms to large kingdoms and empires. The authority of the state over subjects increased but was not unlimited. There were no clear rules of royal succession. There was also no rigid separation of executive, legislative and judicial powers. The administrative structure was rudimentary.

### Contract Theory

* According to contract theory, the state is a result of an implicit or explicit agreement or contract between individuals in a society.
* The individuals in the 'state of nature' decide to form a civil society through a social contract by sacrificing some of their natural rights in exchange for state-provided security and stability.
* The most well-known proponents of the contract theory are Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau.
* For Hobbes, the state of nature was 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short' and individuals agreed to establish a powerful sovereign to escape this state.
* For Locke, individuals in the state of nature had natural rights to life, liberty, and property. They agreed to form a government to protect these rights, and if the government failed to do so, it could be overthrown.
* For Rousseau, the general will of the people as a whole should be the sole guiding principle of the state and that the people can change the social contract when necessary.
* The contract theory provides a useful framework to understand the origin and basis of the state and the rights and responsibilities of citizens and governments. However, it is difficult to empirically prove the existence of such a social contract or consensus in the distant past.

### Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

* The state formation in Ancient India went through several stages:
* Tribal stage: In the tribal stage, there were small kin-based tribes engaged in subsistence farming, cattle rearing, and hunting-gathering. They were egalitarian with tribal chieftains having limited powers.
* Chiefdom stage: Powerful tribal chieftains started emerging who exerted greater control and authority over larger territories and multiple tribes. They collected taxes and maintained armies.
* Early states: The early states were formed around the 6th century BCE, originating from powerful chiefdoms. The states collected regular taxes, had bureaucratic administrations, and maintained standing armies. The early states include the Mahajanapadas like Magadha, Anga, etc.
* Empires: The empires were larger politico-military entities that emerged starting the 4th century BCE. They possessed greater resources and more complex administrations. The Mauryan empire was the first large empire that covered most of the Indian subcontinent. The Gupta empire later dominated northern India.
* These stages were not necessarily sequential or uniform across India. However, this provides a useful framework to understand the increasing complexity of political organization in Ancient India. Environmental conditions, surplus production, and military advantage were some of the key factors enabling the progression through these stages.

### Purusārtha

- The literal meaning of Purusārtha is 'purpose of human existence'. According to Indian philosophy, the four main Purusārthas or goals of human life are:

1. Dharma: Righteousness, moral duty and obligation. Following the path of righteousness.
2. Artha: Material prosperity and wealth. Economic development and financial security.
3. Kāma: Pleasure, desires and passions. Aesthetic and sensual enjoyment.
4. Moksha: Liberation or salvation. Achieving union with the divine and freedom from the cycle of birth and death.

- All four Purusārthas are considered important and interdependent. An excessive focus on one may lead to ignoring the others. A balanced approach towards all four is ideal for holistic development and fulfillment.
- The hierarchy of Purusārthas has been interpreted differently by various Indian schools of thought. For example, some consider Moksha as the ultimate goal and the rest subordinate to it. Others consider Dharma as the foremost priority which regulates the pursuit of the other three. There is no single agreed upon order of precedence. The appropriate emphasis depends on one's stage of life and individual circumstances.

### Āshrama or the Stages of Life

The concept of Āshrama or stages of life was an important feature of the ancient Indian social system. According to this concept, a person's life was divided into four stages:

1. Brahmacharya Āshrama or the Student Stage: In this stage, a person leads a life of celibacy and acquires education. This stage lasts till the age of 25.

2. Grihastha Āshrama or the Householder Stage: In this stage, a person gets married, establishes a family and earns a livelihood. This stage lasts from 25 to 50 years of age.

3. Vānaprastha Āshrama or the Hermit Stage: In this stage, a person hands over family responsibilities to the next generation and retires to the forest to lead an ascetic life. This stage begins at 50 years of age.

4. Sannyāsa Āshrama or the Wandering Ascetic Stage: In this final stage, a person renounces all worldly possessions and spends his time in meditation and pursuit of Moksha or liberation. This stage begins after the age of 75.

The Āshrama system emphasized on the performance of duties prescribed for each stage of life. It ensured that individuals passed through different life stages and acquired diverse life experiences before attaining liberation. The system declined with the breakdown of the joint family system and the concept of renunciation losing prominence.

- Gender is a social construct and is different from biological sex. Biological sex refers to physical and physiological characteristics, while gender refers to attitudes, behaviors, roles, activities, and attributes that a given society considers appropriate for boys and girls.
- Gender roles are learned from family, friends, school, religious practices, and other agents of socialization. They shape our expectations of what is appropriate masculine and feminine behavior.

- There are strong stereotypes associated with gender roles. For example, women are expected to be emotional, submissive, and primarily caretakers while men are expected to be dominant, rational, and breadwinners. These stereotypes disadvantage both men and women and limit their choices.
- Gender is intersectional in nature. The experiences of gender are shaped by other identities like class, caste, religion, and sexual orientation. For example, upper-class women may experience gender differently than lower-class Dalit women. Intersectional analysis is important to understand the complex ways in which gender operates.
- The concept of gender as a social construct and hierarchy is being increasingly challenged by feminist and gender theories. They argue for dismantling rigid gender roles and recognizing gender as fluid and non-binary. However, traditional gender attitudes still persist in most societies.

- The Upanishads are a collection of ancient Indian texts which contain some of the central philosophical concepts and ideas of Hinduism.
- They are primarily concerned with the nature of the self and the soul's relationship with brahman, the ultimate reality.

-- Atman - the eternal, omnipresent self or soul which is the true essence of an individual.
-- Brahman - the eternal, infinite cosmic spirit which is the foundation of all reality.
-- The unity of atman and brahman - the central philosophy of Upanishads is that atman and brahman are identical. Realizing this identity through knowledge is the path to liberation.
-- Karma and rebirth - the Upanishads acknowledge the concepts of karma and rebirth. Liberation is attainable by transcending this cycle.
-- Asceticism and renunciation - the Upanishads encourage ascetic practices and renunciation of worldly attachments and desires as a path to self-knowledge and unity with brahman.
