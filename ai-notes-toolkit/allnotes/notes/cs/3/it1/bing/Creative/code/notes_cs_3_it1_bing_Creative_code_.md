### Evolutionary Theory

- Evolutionary theory is a perspective that explains the origin and development of human societies and cultures based on the principles of biological evolution.
- Evolutionary theory assumes that human societies and cultures are the products of natural selection, adaptation, and inheritance, and that they change over time in response to environmental and historical factors.
- Evolutionary theory also suggests that human societies and cultures can be classified into different stages or types according to their level of complexity, organization, and technology.
- Evolutionary theory was influenced by the works of Charles Darwin, Herbert Spencer, Lewis Henry Morgan, Edward Burnett Tylor, and others in the 19th and early 20th centuries.
- Evolutionary theory was criticized by later anthropologists and sociologists for being ethnocentric, deterministic, unilinear, and teleological, and for ignoring the diversity, agency, and contingency of human societies and cultures.
- Evolutionary theory was revived and modified by neo-evolutionists such as Leslie White, Julian Steward, Marshall Sahlins, and Elman Service in the mid-20th century, who introduced concepts such as cultural evolution, multilinear evolution, cultural ecology, and cultural materialism.
- Evolutionary theory is still relevant and influential in some fields of anthropology and sociology, such as evolutionary psychology, evolutionary sociology, cultural evolution, and sociobiology.

### Kingship in Ancient India

- Kingship in ancient India was a **sovereignty** over a territory by a **king** who functioned as its **protector**, a role which involved both **secular and religious power**.
- Ancient Indians believed that the kingship arose out of **military requirement**. A king must be a capable **general** whose leadership is recognized by all.
- The meaning and significance of kingship changed dramatically between the **Vedic and Later Vedic period**, and underwent further development under the times of the **Jain and Buddhist rulers**.
- Some of the famous kings and emperors of ancient India were **Ajatasatru**, **Ashoka**, **Chandragupta Maurya**, **Samudragupta**, **Harshavardhana**, **Vikramaditya**, **Rajaraja Chola**, **Krishnadevaraya**, **Akbar**, **Shivaji** and many more.
- There were different **theories of kingship** in ancient India, such as the **Brahmanic theory**, the **Kshatriya theory**, the **Shramanic theory**, the **Dharmashastra theory**, the **Arthashastra theory** and the **Tantric theory** .
- The Brahmanic theory held that the king was a **divine** or **semi-divine** being, who derived his authority from the **gods** and the **sacrifices** he performed.
- The Kshatriya theory asserted that the king was a **heroic** or **noble** warrior, who earned his right to rule by his **valor** and **conquest**.
- The Shramanic theory challenged the Brahmanic and Kshatriya theories, and advocated for a **republican** or **democratic** form of government, where the people had a say in the selection and removal of the ruler.
- The Dharmashastra theory emphasized that the king was a **guardian** or **upholder** of the **dharma** or the **moral law**, and that he had to follow the **scriptures** and the **traditions** of his ancestors.
- The Arthashastra theory proposed that the king was a **realist** or **pragmatist**, who had to use his **intelligence** and **strategy** to achieve his **political** and **economic** goals, and that he had to be **ruthless** and **cunning** when necessary.
- The Tantric theory suggested that the king was a **mystic** or **magician**, who had to harness the **cosmic** and **occult** forces to gain **power** and **domination**, and that he had to be **secretive** and **initiated** into the **esoteric** teachings.

### Purusārtha

## Samkhya
- Samkhya is the oldest of the orthodox philosophical systems, and it postulates that everything in reality stems from purusha (self or soul or mind) and prakriti (matter, creative agency, energy).

- Prakriti consists of three qualities or gunas: sattva (purity, harmony, intelligence), rajas (activity, passion, desire), and tamas (inertia, dullness, ignorance).
- Prakriti evolves into various forms of matter and mind, such as the five elements (earth, water, fire, air, and ether), the five senses (sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch), the five organs of action (speech, grasp, movement, excretion, and reproduction), the mind (manas), the intellect (buddhi), and the ego (ahamkara).

- The goal of Samkhya is to discriminate between purusha and prakriti, and to attain liberation (kaivalya) from the cycle of birth and death (samsara) caused by prakriti.

### Harappan Technologies

- The Harappan civilization, also known as the Indus Valley civilization, was a Bronze Age civilization that flourished in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent from about 2500 BCE to 1900 BCE.
- The Harappans developed various technologies and crafts, such as metallurgy, seal carving, pottery, jewelry, weights and measures, urban planning, drainage systems, and writing.
- Some of the notable features of Harappan technologies are:

  - **Metallurgy**: The Harappans used copper, bronze, lead, and tin to make tools, weapons, vessels, and ornaments. They knew how to alloy copper with tin to make bronze, which is harder and more durable than copper. They also knew how to cast metal objects using the lost-wax process, which involves making a wax model, covering it with clay, heating it to melt the wax, and then pouring molten metal into the hollow mold.
  - **Seal carving**: The Harappans carved distinctive seals, usually made of steatite, a soft stone, with various motifs and inscriptions. The seals were used to stamp clay on trade goods, to identify property, and possibly to serve as amulets or tokens of authority. The motifs on the seals include animals, such as bulls, elephants, rhinoceroses, tigers, and unicorns, as well as human figures, trees, and geometric patterns. The inscriptions are in a script that has not been deciphered yet.
  - **Pottery**: The Harappans produced fine pottery, mostly wheel-made, with various shapes, sizes, and decorations. The pottery was usually red or buff in color, with black or red painted designs. The designs include geometric patterns, floral motifs, animals, birds, and fish. Some of the pottery types include storage jars, cooking pots, dishes, bowls, cups, spouted vessels, and perforated jars.
  - **Jewelry**: The Harappans made jewelry from materials such as gold, silver, copper, bronze, ivory, shell, bone, terracotta, and stone. They used techniques such as hammering, soldering, wire-drawing, bead-making, and inlaying to create various ornaments, such as necklaces, bracelets, bangles, earrings, rings, pendants, and headbands. The jewelry was often adorned with semi-precious stones, such as carnelian, lapis lazuli, turquoise, and agate.
  - **Weights and measures**: The Harappans developed a system of standardized weights and measures, based on a decimal and binary system. The weights were made of chert, a type of stone, and were shaped like cubes, spheres, or cones. The smallest weight was about 0.8 grams, and the largest was about 12.5 kilograms. The weights were used for trade and taxation purposes. The measures were based on a unit of length called the Indus inch, which was about 1.6 centimeters. The measures were used for surveying, architecture, and engineering.
  - **Urban planning**: The Harappans built well-planned cities, with a grid layout, fortified walls, and gateways. The cities had public buildings, such as granaries, baths, citadels, and temples, as well as private houses, workshops, and markets. The houses were usually two or three stories high, made of baked bricks, and had flat roofs. The houses had rooms, courtyards, wells, bathrooms, and drains. The drains were connected to a sophisticated sewage system, which carried the waste water to the main drains and then to the rivers or the sea.
  - **Writing**: The Harappans developed a system of writing, which is one of the oldest in the world. The writing consisted of about 400 signs, which were mostly pictographic, representing objects, animals, plants, and abstract concepts. The writing was written from right to left, and sometimes boustrophedon, which means alternating the direction of writing in each line. The writing was used for various purposes, such as recording transactions, labeling goods, expressing religious beliefs, and communicating messages. The writing was inscribed on seals, tablets, pottery, copper plates, and other materials.



### Water Management in India

Water management is the planning, development, allocation, utilization, and conservation of water resources for various purposes such as drinking, irrigation, industry, energy, and ecology. Water management is essential for ensuring water security, health, and economic growth in India, which faces multiple challenges such as water scarcity, pollution, overexploitation, floods, and droughts. Some of the key aspects of water management in India are:

- Providing drinking water to rural areas: The government has been responsible for providing drinking water to 833 million rural people in some 640,000 villages across the country. However, many rural areas still lack access to safe and adequate water supply due to poor infrastructure, quality, and sustainability. The World Bank has supported several projects to improve rural water supply and sanitation, such as the National Rural Drinking Water Program, the Swachh Bharat Mission, and the Jal Jeevan Mission.
- Managing groundwater: Groundwater is the main source of water for domestic, agricultural, and industrial use in India. However, over the past few decades, groundwater extraction has risen exponentially, leading to depletion, salinization, and contamination of aquifers. India is now the world's largest user of groundwater, accounting for about 25% of the global total. The World Bank has supported initiatives to promote sustainable groundwater management, such as the Atal Bhujal Yojana, which is India's largest community-led groundwater management program, covering seven states with the highest rates of groundwater depletion.
- Improving access to water on dry rainfed lands: About 60% of India's net sown area is rainfed, which means it depends on rainfall for irrigation. Rainfed agriculture contributes to 44% of the country's food production and supports the livelihoods of millions of small and marginal farmers. However, rainfed areas face low and erratic rainfall, soil erosion, and low productivity. The World Bank has supported projects to enhance water availability and efficiency on rainfed lands, such as the Watershed Development Project, the National Hydrology Project, and the Climate Smart Agriculture Project.
- Cleaning the Ganga: The Ganga is India's most sacred and longest river, which supports the lives and livelihoods of about 500 million people. However, the river is severely polluted by domestic, industrial, and agricultural waste, affecting its water quality, biodiversity, and cultural significance. The World Bank has supported the National Ganga River Basin Project, which aims to improve the management and restoration of the Ganga basin through institutional strengthening, wastewater treatment, solid waste management, and riverfront development.
- Managing floods and droughts: India is prone to frequent and severe floods and droughts, which cause loss of lives, livelihoods, and assets. Climate change is expected to exacerbate the frequency and intensity of these extreme events. The World Bank has supported projects to improve the resilience and preparedness of communities and institutions to cope with floods and droughts, such as the National Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project, the Dam Rehabilitation and Improvement Project, and the National Disaster Risk Management Project.
- Enhancing dam safety in India: India has about 5,200 large dams, which provide irrigation, water supply, hydropower, and flood control benefits. However, many of these dams are aging and require urgent repair and maintenance to ensure their safety and functionality. The World Bank has supported the Dam Rehabilitation and Improvement Project, which aims to improve the safety and operational performance of selected dams across India.

### Indian Architecture

Indian architecture is the expression of the history, culture, and religion of India. It reflects the diversity and richness of the Indian civilization, as well as the influences of various invaders, rulers, and traders who shaped the Indian subcontinent. Indian architecture can be classified into several styles and traditions, each with its own characteristics and features. Some of the most prominent ones are:

- **Hindu temple architecture**: This is the oldest and most widespread form of Indian architecture, dating back to the ancient Vedic period. Hindu temples are sacred structures that house the images of gods and goddesses, and are designed to facilitate worship and rituals. Hindu temples typically have a square or rectangular plan, with one or more towers (shikhara) rising above the sanctum (garbhagriha). The temple walls and ceilings are often decorated with sculptures, paintings, and carvings depicting various aspects of Hindu mythology and cosmology. Some of the most famous examples of Hindu temple architecture are the Kailasa temple at Ellora, the Brihadeshwara temple at Thanjavur, the Khajuraho temples, and the Meenakshi temple at Madurai.

- **Indo-Islamic architecture**: This is the style of architecture that emerged from the interaction of Islamic and Hindu cultures, especially during the medieval period. Indo-Islamic architecture combines elements of Islamic art and architecture, such as domes, arches, minarets, and calligraphy, with indigenous forms and motifs, such as lotus, elephants, and peacocks. Indo-Islamic architecture can be further divided into sub-styles, such as Rajput architecture, Mughal architecture, Deccani architecture, and Indo-Saracenic architecture. Some of the most renowned examples of Indo-Islamic architecture are the Qutub Minar, the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort, and the Charminar.

- **South Indian architecture**: This is the style of architecture that developed in the southern part of India, especially in the states of Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, and Andhra Pradesh. South Indian architecture is influenced by the Dravidian culture and language, as well as by the Pallava, Chola, Chalukya, Hoysala, and Vijayanagara dynasties. South Indian architecture is characterized by the use of stone, brick, and wood, and by the elaborate ornamentation of pillars, ceilings, and doorways. South Indian architecture also includes distinctive features, such as gopurams (gateway towers), mandapas (pillared halls), and vimanas (pyramidal roofs). Some of the most notable examples of South Indian architecture are the Rameshwaram temple, the Hampi ruins, the Mahabalipuram monuments, and the Padmanabhaswamy temple.

- **Modern Indian architecture**: This is the style of architecture that emerged in the 20th and 21st centuries, in response to the challenges and opportunities of the contemporary world. Modern Indian architecture is influenced by various factors, such as colonialism, nationalism, globalization, urbanization, and environmentalism. ### Painting

Painting is one of the oldest and most diverse forms of artistic expression in India. It reflects the rich cultural heritage and diversity of the country, as well as the influences of various historical periods, regions, religions, and dynasties.

Some of the main features of Indian painting are:

- It can be broadly classified as murals, miniatures, and paintings on cloth .
- Murals are large works executed on the walls of solid structures, such as temples, caves, palaces, and forts. They depict religious, mythological, historical, or secular themes. Some of the famous examples of murals are the Ajanta Caves, the Kailashnath Temple, the Brihadeshwara Temple, and the Hampi monuments.
- Miniatures are small-scale paintings done on perishable materials such as paper, palm leaf, wood, ivory, or cloth. They are usually made for books, albums, or court records. They illustrate literary, religious, or historical texts, or portray scenes from court life, nature, or daily life. Some of the prominent styles of miniatures are the Pala, Mughal, Rajput, Deccan, and Pahari schools.
- Paintings on cloth are also known as patachitra, which means picture on cloth. They are usually made of cotton or silk, and painted with natural or synthetic colors. They depict religious stories, folk tales, or local customs. They are often used as scrolls for storytelling, or as banners for festivals or processions. Some of the well-known forms of paintings on cloth are the Madhubani, Warli, Kalamkari, Pichhwai, and Thangka paintings.
- Indian painting has a very long tradition and history, dating back to the prehistoric times. The earliest examples of Indian painting are the rock paintings of places like Bhimbetka, which show scenes of hunting, dancing, and animal life. Over the centuries, Indian painting has evolved and diversified, influenced by various factors such as religion, politics, trade, and foreign contact .
- Indian painting is characterized by its use of vibrant colors, intricate patterns, elaborate symbolism, and stylized representation of figures and landscapes. It also shows a remarkable diversity of styles, techniques, and themes, reflecting the regional and cultural variations of the country. Indian painting is not only a form of aesthetic expression, but also a medium of communication, education, and devotion .

### Martial Arts Traditions

Martial arts are systems of codified practices and traditions of training for combat. They are used for self-defense, physical fitness, mental and spiritual development, entertainment, and cultural preservation.

India has a rich and diverse heritage of martial arts that dates back to ancient times. Some of the martial arts traditions in India are:

- **Kushti**: A form of wrestling that originated in the Mughal era, when the local sport of Malla Yuddha was combined with the Persian sport of Varzesh-e-Bastani. It involves grappling, striking, and throwing techniques, as well as the use of a loincloth called langot. Kushti is popular in northern and western India, especially in Maharashtra, Punjab, Haryana, and Uttar Pradesh.
- **Silambam**: A form of stick fighting that originated in Tamil Nadu and has been mentioned in the Sangam literature. It involves the use of a bamboo staff called silambam, as well as other weapons such as swords, daggers, spears, and shields. Silambam is known for its fluid and circular movements, as well as its use of footwork and acrobatics. Silambam is practiced in southern India, especially in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Karnataka .
- **Kuttu Varisai**: A form of unarmed combat that originated in Tamil Nadu and is closely related to Silambam. It involves the use of punches, kicks, elbows, knees, locks, and throws, as well as the use of animal-inspired postures and movements. Kuttu Varisai is also known for its use of breathing techniques and meditation. Kuttu Varisai is practiced in southern India, especially in Tamil Nadu and Kerala.
- **Thoda**: A form of archery that originated in Himachal Pradesh and is influenced by the local culture and mythology. It involves the use of wooden bows and arrows, as well as the use of traditional costumes and music. Thoda is a team sport, where two teams of archers shoot at each other's targets, which are attached to the ends of long poles. Thoda is practiced in northern India, especially in Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand .
- **Gatka**: A form of sword fighting that originated in Punjab and is associated with the Sikh religion and history. It involves the use of a curved sword called kirpan, as well as other weapons such as daggers, axes, and chakrams. Gatka is known for its fast and agile movements, as well as its use of rhythmic patterns and chants. Gatka is practiced in northern India, especially in Punjab, Haryana, and Delhi .
- **Thang Ta**: A form of sword and spear fighting that originated in Manipur and is influenced by the local culture and history. It involves the use of a single-edged sword called thang, and a spear called ta, as well as other weapons such as shields, daggers, and axes. Thang Ta is known for its precise and powerful strikes, as well as its use of acrobatics and dance. Thang Ta is practiced in northeastern India, especially in Manipur, Assam, and Tripura .
- **Kalaripayattu**: A form of martial art that originated in Kerala and is considered to be one of the oldest and most comprehensive martial arts in the world. It involves the use of various weapons such as swords, daggers, spears, shields, and sticks, as well as unarmed techniques such as kicks, punches, locks, and throws. Kalaripayattu is also known for its use of body conditioning, healing, and spiritual practices. Kalaripayattu is practiced in southern India, especially in Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Karnataka .
- **Mardani Khel**: A form of martial art that originated in Maharashtra and is influenced by the local culture and history. It involves the use of 14 styles of wielding weapons such as swords, daggers, spears, shields, and whips, as well as unarmed techniques such as kicks, punches, and throws. Mardani Khel is known for its fierce and aggressive style, as well as its use of folk music and dance. Mardani Khel is practiced in western India, especially in Maharashtra, Gujarat, and Goa.

### Indian Cinema

Indian cinema is one of the most diverse and vibrant forms of artistic expression in the world. It encompasses a variety of genres, languages, styles, and themes, reflecting the rich and complex cultural heritage of India. Here are some points to note about the history and features of Indian cinema:

- Indian cinema began in the late 19th century, when the first public screening of a movie by the Lumiere brothers took place in Bombay in 1896. The first Indian filmmaker was Dadasaheb Phalke, who is considered the father of Indian cinema. He made the first full-length feature film, Raja Harishchandra, in 1913. It was a silent film based on a Hindu mythological story.
- The advent of sound in cinema changed the landscape of Indian cinema in the 1930s. The first talkie, Alam Ara, was released in 1931 by Ardeshir Irani. It was a musical drama that introduced songs and dialogues in Hindi. The sound era also saw the emergence of regional cinema, such as Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, and Malayalam, which developed their own distinctive styles and themes.
- The 1950s and 1960s are regarded as the golden age of Indian cinema, when some of the most acclaimed and influential filmmakers, such as Satyajit Ray, Guru Dutt, Raj Kapoor, Bimal Roy, and Mehboob Khan, made their mark. They explored social realism, humanism, romance, comedy, and musical genres, and created some of the most iconic characters and stars of Indian cinema, such as Dilip Kumar, Dev Anand, Nargis, Madhubala, and Rajesh Khanna .
- The 1970s and 1980s witnessed the rise of the masala film, a genre that blended action, drama, romance, comedy, and music, and catered to the mass audience. - The 1990s and 2000s saw the emergence of new trends and genres in Indian cinema, such as parallel cinema, art cinema, new wave cinema, and indie cinema, which challenged the mainstream conventions and explored new themes, techniques, and aesthetics. The period also saw the globalization of Indian cinema, as it reached wider audiences and markets in South Asia and abroad, especially among the Indian diaspora. Some of the films that achieved international recognition and acclaim are Salaam Bombay, Lagaan, Monsoon Wedding, Slumdog Millionaire, and Dangal .
- The present-day Indian cinema is characterized by its diversity and dynamism, as it continues to experiment with new genres, formats, platforms, and technologies. It also reflects the changing social, political, and cultural realities of India, and engages with contemporary issues and challenges. Some of the current trends and genres in Indian cinema are biopics, historical dramas, sports dramas, social dramas, thrillers, horror, comedy, and animation.
