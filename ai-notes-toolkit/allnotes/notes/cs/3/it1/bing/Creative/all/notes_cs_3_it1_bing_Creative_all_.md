

# Indian Traditions, Culture and Society

India is a diverse and ancient country with a rich and varied heritage. India has been influenced by many different civilizations, religions, languages, and cultures over the centuries, resulting in a unique and complex mosaic of traditions and customs. Some of the main aspects of Indian culture and society are:

- Arts and Language: India has a long and distinguished history of art and literature, dating back to the Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1500 BCE). India is home to many classical languages, such as Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, and Odia, as well as modern languages, such as Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Gujarati, Marathi, Punjabi, and English. India has produced many renowned poets, writers, philosophers, musicians, painters, sculptors, and architects, such as Kalidasa, Valmiki, Vyasa, Shankara, Ramanuja, Kabir, Mirabai, Tulsidas, Tagore, Gandhi, Ravi Shankar, M.F. Husain, and R.K. Narayan .

- Philosophy and Religion: India is the birthplace of four major world religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. Hinduism is the oldest and most dominant religion in India, with a diverse range of beliefs, practices, sects, and schools of thought. Hinduism is based on the concepts of karma, dharma, reincarnation, and moksha. Buddhism and Jainism emerged as reform movements from Hinduism in the 6th century BCE, and emphasized non-violence, compassion, and detachment. Sikhism was founded by Guru Nanak in the 15th century CE, and blended elements of Hinduism and Islam. India is also home to many other religions, such as Islam, Christianity, Zoroastrianism, Judaism, and Baha'i, which have contributed to the religious diversity and tolerance of the country .

- Food and Drink: The cuisine of India varies considerably across its various regions and ethnic communities. Indian food is known for its use of spices, herbs, grains, pulses, vegetables, fruits, dairy products, and meat. Some of the common dishes in Indian cuisine are dal (lentil soup), roti (flatbread), rice, curry, biryani (rice with meat or vegetables), dosa (rice and lentil crepe), idli (steamed rice cake), samosa (fried pastry with savory filling), chaat (snack food), lassi (yogurt drink), chai (tea with milk and spices), and sweets, such as jalebi (deep-fried flour batter in sugar syrup), ladoo (ball-shaped sweet), and halwa (pudding). Indian food is influenced by the religious and cultural preferences of the people, such as vegetarianism, halal, and fasting .

There are also many sub-castes (jatis) and outcastes (Dalits) within the caste system. The caste system is based on the concept of purity and pollution, and determines the social status, occupation, and marriage prospects of the people. India also has a class system, where people are divided into upper, middle, and lower classes based on their income, education, and lifestyle. The class system is more flexible and dynamic than the caste system, and allows for social mobility and change. Women in India face many challenges and inequalities, such as discrimination, violence, dowry, child marriage, female infanticide, and lack of education and employment opportunities. However, women in India also have many rights and achievements, such as suffrage, education, political representation, and social activism. # State in Ancient India

- The concept of state in ancient India was not a fixed or uniform one. Different regions and periods had different forms and functions of state.
- The earliest evidence of state formation in ancient India comes from the Harappan civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which had a centralized urban administration, trade networks, standardized weights and measures, and a distinctive script and culture.
- The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) saw the emergence of tribal polities called janapadas, which were ruled by chiefs or kings (rajas) who performed sacrifices and rituals to maintain their authority and legitimacy. The Vedic texts also mention larger territorial units called mahajanapadas, which were formed by the alliance or conquest of smaller janapadas.
- The later Vedic period (c. 1000-600 BCE) witnessed the rise of monarchical states, which were more hierarchical and complex than the earlier tribal polities. The kings (rajas) claimed divine or semi-divine status and were supported by priests, warriors, and officials. The kings also collected taxes and tributes from their subjects and maintained armies and forts for their protection and expansion.
- The sixth century BCE saw the emergence of two new forms of state in ancient India: the republics (ganas or sanghas) and the empires (samrajas). The republics were democratic or oligarchic associations of clans or tribes, which had a council of elders (sabha) and an assembly of people (samiti) as their governing bodies. The republics were mostly found in the north-western and northern regions of India, such as the Kuru-Panchala, the Vajji, and the Malla. The empires were large and centralized states, which were ruled by powerful kings (samratas) who claimed universal sovereignty and were supported by a large bureaucracy, army, and spy network. The empires were mostly found in the eastern and central regions of India, such as the Magadha, the Nanda, and the Maurya.
- The Mauryan empire (c. 321-185 BCE) was the first and largest empire in ancient India, which was founded by Chandragupta Maurya with the help of his mentor and minister Kautilya (also known as Chanakya), who wrote the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft and polity. The Mauryan empire reached its zenith under Ashoka (c. 268-232 BCE), who expanded the empire to cover most of South Asia and parts of Central Asia and Southeast Asia. Ashoka also embraced Buddhism and propagated it through his edicts and missions. The Mauryan empire collapsed after the death of Ashoka and was succeeded by various regional kingdoms and dynasties.
- The post-Mauryan period (c. 200 BCE-300 CE) saw the emergence of several regional states and empires in different parts of India, such as the Sunga, the Satavahana, the Kushana, the Shaka, the Indo-Greek, the Indo-Parthian, the Indo-Scythian, and the Indo-Sassanian. These states and empires had diverse political, economic, social, and cultural features, and interacted with each other through trade, war, diplomacy, and cultural exchange. They also contributed to the development of art, architecture, literature, religion, and science in ancient India.
- The Gupta empire (c. 320-550 CE) was the second and last major empire in ancient India, which was founded by Chandragupta I and reached its peak under Samudragupta and Chandragupta II. The Gupta empire is considered as the golden age of ancient India, as it witnessed the flourishing of art, literature, science, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy. # Evolutionary Theory

- Evolutionary theory is a sociological perspective that explains how societies change from simple to complex forms over time.
- Evolutionary theory is based on the assumption that societies gradually change from simple beginnings into even more complex forms.
- Evolutionary theory was influenced by Charles Darwin's theory of organic evolution, which proposed that living organisms adapt to their environment through natural selection.
- Evolutionary theory was also influenced by Herbert Spencer, who coined the term "survival of the fittest" and applied it to human societies, arguing that social change was a result of competition and adaptation.
- Evolutionary theory was popular in the 19th century, as it justified the differences in wealth and success among societies and people as natural and inevitable.
- Evolutionary theory was criticized for being unilinear, ethnocentric, and deterministic, as it assumed that all societies followed the same path of development and that Western society was the most advanced and superior.
- Evolutionary theory was challenged by other sociological perspectives, such as functionalism, conflict theory, and symbolic interactionism, which offered different explanations for social change and diversity.
- Evolutionary theory is still relevant today, as it provides insights into the origins and dynamics of human culture, behavior, and institutions.
- Evolutionary theory can also help us understand some of the pressing social, cultural, and political issues of our time, such as globalization, environmentalism, human rights, and social justice.



# Force Theory

- Force theory is a view on the origin of the state that argues that nations were created through violence and domination.
- According to this theory, the state originated due to force exerted by the strong over the weak, who then became their subjects and obeyed their commands.
- The theory is based on the old saying that "war begets the king", implying that the state is a product of war and conquest .
- The theory is supported by some historical examples of states that emerged from military conquests, such as the Roman Empire, the Mongol Empire, and the British Empire.
- The theory is criticized for being too simplistic and ignoring other factors that contribute to the formation of the state, such as social contract, divine right, natural law, and consent of the governed.
- The theory also fails to explain the existence of states that are not based on force, such as Switzerland, Iceland, and Costa Rica.
- The theory is mainly of historical interest and does not reflect the modern understanding of the state as a complex and dynamic institution that requires legitimacy, sovereignty, and public welfare.

# Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

- State formation is the process of emergence and development of a centralized political organization that exercises authority and control over a territory and its people.
- State formation in ancient India was influenced by various factors, such as geography, ecology, economy, society, culture, religion, and ideology.
- The stages of state formation in ancient India can be broadly divided into four phases:

## Phase 1: Pre-state Societies (Before 6th century BCE)

- In this phase, there were no states or kingdoms, but only tribal communities or clans (jana or vish) that lived in villages or pastoral settlements.
- These communities were governed by their elders, chiefs, or councils, who maintained law and order, settled disputes, and performed rituals.
- The main sources of livelihood were agriculture, animal husbandry, hunting, and gathering. Trade and commerce were limited and mainly barter-based.
- The main social divisions were based on kinship, occupation, and varna (the four-fold division of society into Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras).
- The main religious beliefs and practices were based on the worship of nature, ancestors, and various gods and goddesses. The Vedas, the oldest scriptures of Hinduism, were composed and transmitted orally in this phase.

## Phase 2: Early State Societies (6th century BCE to 4th century CE)

- In this phase, the first states or kingdoms (janapadas or mahajanapadas) emerged and expanded in the Indo-Gangetic plains, especially along the river valleys of the Ganges and the Yamuna.
- These states were ruled by kings (raja or maharaja) who claimed divine or heroic origin and exercised absolute power over their subjects. They were assisted by ministers, officials, and priests, who administered the state, collected taxes, and performed rituals.
- The main sources of livelihood were agriculture, animal husbandry, trade, and commerce. Urbanization, coinage, and writing developed in this phase. Trade and commerce flourished within and outside India, especially with the Persian, Greek, and Roman empires.
- The main social divisions were based on varna, jati (sub-castes), and ashrama (stages of life). The caste system became more rigid and hierarchical, with Brahmins and Kshatriyas dominating the social order. The status of women declined and they were subjected to various restrictions and inequalities.
- The main religious beliefs and practices were based on the Vedas, the Upanishads, and the Dharmashastras. The Vedic religion was challenged by new religious movements, such as Buddhism, Jainism, and Ajivikism, which preached non-violence, renunciation, and salvation. The Mauryan Empire (4th-2nd century BCE) was the first pan-Indian state that patronized Buddhism and promoted the ideals of dhamma (righteousness) and ahimsa (non-violence).

- In this phase, the second wave of state formation and expansion occurred in different regions of India, such as the Deccan, the South, and the East.

# Political Ideals in Ancient India

- Political ideals in ancient India were based on the concepts of **dharma**, **raja**, **samaj** and **artha**. Dharma was the moral and religious duty of the king and the people, raja was the sovereign authority of the king, samaj was the social order and artha was the material and economic well-being of the state.
- The ancient Indian political thought was influenced by the **Vedic** literature, which reflected the democratic principles and ideals of the early Indo-Aryan society. The Rig Veda, for example, described democracy as a deity called **Samjnana**, which meant the collective consciousness of the people. The Vedic society was divided into four **varnas** or classes, each with its own duties and rights. The Vedic assemblies, such as the **sabha**, **samiti**, **vidatha** and **gana**, were the institutions of participatory governance, where the people could voice their opinions and elect their leaders.
- The ancient Indian political thought also developed under the influence of the **monarchical** states of northern India, which emerged after the sixth century BCE. The monarchical states were based on the concept of **dandaniti** or the science of politics, which was derived from the **Itihasa** or the fifth Veda. The Itihasa consisted of the epics, such as the **Ramayana** and the **Mahabharata**, which narrated the stories of ideal kings and heroes, such as **Rama** and **Krishna**, who upheld the principles of dharma and justice. The monarchical states also adopted the concept of **chakravartin** or the universal ruler, who was supposed to conquer the four quarters of the world and establish a righteous and prosperous empire.
- The ancient Indian political thought reached its peak with the works of **Kautilya**, who wrote the **Arthashastra**, a treatise on statecraft and administration, around the fourth century BCE. Kautilya was the prime minister of **Chandragupta Maurya**, the founder of the Mauryan Empire, which was the first pan-Indian empire in history. Kautilya's Arthashastra was based on the concept of **artha**, which meant the material and economic well-being of the state. Kautilya advocated a pragmatic and realistic approach to politics, where the king had to use various means, such as **sama**, **dana**, **bheda** and **danda**, or conciliation, gift, division and punishment, to achieve his goals. Kautilya also laid down the principles of **foreign policy**, **warfare**, **espionage**, **taxation**, **law and order**, **welfare** and **trade** in his Arthashastra.
- The ancient Indian political thought was not static or conservative, but rather dynamic and adaptable to the changing times and circumstances. The ancient Indian political thinkers also recognized the diversity and plurality of the Indian society and culture, and tried to accommodate different views and interests. The ancient Indian political thought was also influenced by various **philosophical** and **religious** schools, such as **Buddhism**, **Jainism**, **Samkhya**, **Yoga**, **Nyaya**, **Vaisheshika**, **Mimamsa** and **Vedanta**, which offered different perspectives on the nature of reality, the role of human beings and the purpose of life.

# The Seven Limbs of the State

- The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) is a theory of statecraft given by Kautilya in his treatise Arthashastra .
- According to this theory, a state consists of seven essential elements that support its existence and functioning  .
- The seven limbs of the state are:
  - King: The king is the supreme authority and the head of the state. He is responsible for the peace, justice and stability of the state. He is independent and has the power to appoint and dismiss his ministers and officials .
  - Amatya: The amatya are the bureaucrats or the ministers who assist the king in administration and policy-making. They are selected on the basis of merit and loyalty. They are accountable to the king and the people for their actions .
  - Janapada: The janapada is the territory or the land of the state. It includes the natural resources, the population, the agriculture and the trade of the state. It is the source of revenue and security for the state .
  - Durga: The durga is the fort or the capital of the state. It is the center of political, economic and cultural activities of the state. It is the place where the king and his court reside. It is also the place where the state stores its treasure and weapons. It is the symbol of the state's strength and prestige .
  - Kosa: The kosa is the treasure or the wealth of the state. It includes the taxes, the fines, the gifts and the spoils of war collected by the state. It is used to finance the state's expenditure on administration, defense, welfare and development .
  - Danda: The danda is the coercive authority or the military force of the state. It is used to maintain law and order, to protect the state from external and internal enemies, and to enforce the king's commands. It is also used to expand the state's territory and influence  .
  - Mitra: The mitra is the ally or the friend of the state. It is another state that shares common interests and goals with the state. It is used to support the state in times of war and peace, to balance the power of the enemies, and to promote the state's welfare and prosperity .
- Kautilya argued that a state should have all the seven limbs in a balanced and harmonious manner. He also suggested various ways to strengthen and protect each limb from internal and external threats .

# Purusārtha

- Purusārtha is a Sanskrit word that means "object (ive) of men" or "human endeavour".

- Moksha means "liberation", "freedom", or "salvation". # Varnāshrama System

The Varnāshrama system is a concept of social and spiritual organization in Hinduism that divides the society into four classes (varnas) and four stages of life (ashramas). The system is based on the idea of dharma, or duty, that each individual has to perform according to their natural qualities and abilities.

## Varnas

The four varnas are:

- Brahmanas: The priests, teachers, and intellectuals who perform religious rituals and study the Vedas. They are considered the highest varna and have the duty of preserving and transmitting the sacred knowledge.
- Kshatriyas: The warriors, rulers, and administrators who protect the society and uphold justice. They are considered the second highest varna and have the duty of maintaining order and defending the country.
- Vaishyas: The merchants, farmers, and artisans who produce and trade goods and services. They are considered the third highest varna and have the duty of creating wealth and supporting the society.
- Shudras: The workers, servants, and laborers who perform various tasks and serve the other varnas. They are considered the lowest varna and have the duty of obeying and assisting the higher varnas.

The varna system is not rigid or hereditary, but flexible and based on one's qualities and actions. A person can change their varna by developing different qualities and performing different duties. The system is meant to promote harmony and cooperation among the different sections of the society.

## Ashramas

The four ashramas are:

- Brahmacharya: The student stage of life, in which one learns the scriptures and the skills required for their varna. This stage lasts until one is ready to enter the next stage, usually around the age of 25.
- Grihastha: The householder stage of life, in which one marries, raises a family, and fulfills their social and economic obligations. This stage lasts until one is ready to retire, usually around the age of 50.
- Vanaprastha: The forest dweller stage of life, in which one renounces worldly attachments and responsibilities, and retires to a secluded place to pursue spiritual goals. This stage lasts until one is ready to renounce everything, usually around the age of 75.
- Sannyasa: The renunciant stage of life, in which one gives up all possessions, relations, and identity, and dedicates oneself to the supreme reality. This stage lasts until one attains liberation, or moksha.

The ashrama system is not compulsory or sequential, but optional and flexible. A person can choose to enter or skip any stage, depending on their inclination and circumstances. The system is meant to provide a framework for spiritual growth and fulfillment at different stages of life.

Some of these scripts have also been adapted to write other languages, such as Hindi, Urdu, Marathi, Nepali, and Sinhala  .
- The evolution of script and languages in India reflects the historical, social, and cultural changes that have shaped the subcontinent. The scripts also show the interaction and exchange of ideas between different regions and peoples of India and beyond  .



# Harappan Script

- Harappan script, also known as Indus script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilisation.
- Harappan script was discovered during the excavation of Harappan city and other sites in the Indus Valley.
- Harappan script consists of very few signs and symbols, ranging from 400 to 600, which lack bilingual inscriptions and underlying language.
- Harappan script is mostly found on seals, pottery, copper tablets, and other objects, and most inscriptions are extremely short, making it difficult to judge whether or not they constituted a writing system .
- Harappan script has long defied attempts to read it, and therefore the language remains unknown. Several scholars have proposed different theories about the origin, nature, and meaning of the script, but none have been widely accepted.
- Harappan script is believed to be a logo-syllabic system, where some symbols express ideas or words while others represent sounds. This view is based on the fact that some signs are repeated or modified in patterns that suggest phonetic value.
- Harappan script is one of the oldest known scripts of India and a major source of information about the Indus Valley Civilisation. It reflects the culture, trade, religion, and administration of the Harappan people .



# Brahmi Script

- Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script.
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi.
- It appeared as a fully developed script in the third century BCE.
- It is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations.
- It is a syllabic script, meaning that each symbol represents a syllable, usually consisting of a consonant and a vowel.
- It is written from left to right, with spaces between words.
- It has 33 basic consonants and 9 basic vowels, as well as various modifiers and diacritics.
- It has two main variants: northern Brahmi and southern Brahmi, which differ in the shapes and forms of some letters.
- It was used for writing various languages, such as Sanskrit, Prakrit, Pali, Tamil, and others.
- It was gradually replaced by its descendants, the Brahmic scripts, from around the 5th century CE onwards.
- Some of the Brahmic scripts that evolved from Brahmi are Devanagari, Bengali, Gujarati, Gurmukhi, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Sinhala, Thai, Lao, Tibetan, and Khmer.
- Brahmi script is considered a cultural and linguistic heritage of India and Asia.



# The Vedas

- The Vedas are the oldest and most authoritative scriptures of Hinduism, the religion of India. They are composed in Vedic Sanskrit, a language that is no longer spoken but is the ancestor of many modern Indian languages. The word Veda means "knowledge" or "vision" and refers to the divine wisdom revealed to the ancient sages or rishis .
- The Vedas are divided into four collections: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda. Each collection consists of four parts: the Samhitas, the Brahmanas, the Aranyakas and the Upanishads. The Samhitas are the hymns and prayers to various gods and goddesses. The Brahmanas are the ritual manuals and explanations of the sacrifices. The Aranyakas are the forest texts that deal with the mystical and philosophical aspects of the rituals. The Upanishads are the philosophical texts that explore the nature of the self, the supreme reality and the relationship between them .
- The Vedas are considered to be shruti, or "that which is heard", meaning that they are not human compositions but divine revelations. They are also considered to be apaurusheya, or "not of human origin", meaning that they are eternal and uncreated. The Vedas are the source of dharma, or "duty", "law" or "righteousness", which is the moral and ethical code of conduct for Hindus. The Vedas also contain various branches of knowledge, such as astronomy, mathematics, medicine, grammar, music and poetry  .
- The Vedas are the foundation of the Vedic culture, which flourished in ancient India from around 1500 BCE to 500 BCE. The Vedic culture was characterized by the performance of elaborate fire sacrifices, the worship of a pantheon of gods and goddesses, the development of a complex social hierarchy based on the varna or caste system, and the emergence of various schools of thought and literature. The Vedic culture influenced the later developments of Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism, as well as the Indian civilization as a whole .



# The Upanishads

- The Upanishads are late Vedic Sanskrit texts that supplied the basis of later Hindu philosophy.
- The Upanishads are commonly referred to as Vedānta, which means "the end of the Veda" or "the highest purpose of the Veda".
- The aim of all Upanishads is to investigate the nature of Ātman (self), and direct the enquirer toward it.
- There are more than 200 known Upanishads, but the most influential ones are the 12 principal Upanishads, which date from the 6th to the 3rd century BCE .
- The Upanishads present a vision of an interconnected universe with a single, unifying principle behind the apparent diversity in the cosmos, any articulation of which is called brahman.
- The Upanishads teach that brahman resides in the atman, the unchanging core of the human individual, and that the goal of life is to realize this identity and attain liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth (samsara).
- The Upanishads also introduce concepts such as karma (action and its consequences), dharma (duty or law), and yoga (discipline or practice) as means to achieve this realization .
- The Upanishads are the source of many philosophical and spiritual ideas that have influenced Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and other traditions .
- The Upanishads are also considered as the foundation of Indian culture, literature, art, and science, as they contain insights on ethics, psychology, cosmology, metaphysics, and aesthetics .

# The Mahabharata

The Mahabharata is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of ancient India, along with the Ramayana. It is a long and complex narrative that covers the history, culture, religion, and philosophy of the Indian subcontinent. It is also a story of a dynastic war between two groups of cousins, the Kauravas and the Pandavas, for the throne of Hastinapura, a kingdom in northern India.

Some of the main points to note about the Mahabharata are:

- The Mahabharata is attributed to the sage Vyasa, who is also a major character in the epic. He is said to have dictated the verses to his disciple Ganesha, the elephant-headed god, who wrote them down.
- The Mahabharata consists of 18 books or parvas, each containing several chapters or adhyayas. The total number of verses is about 74,000, making it one of the longest poems in the world. The core story of the war is contained in the sixth book, the Bhishma Parva, which has 117 chapters.
- The Mahabharata is not only a historical or mythical account of the war, but also a source of moral and spiritual guidance for Hindus. It contains many stories, legends, fables, parables, and dialogues that illustrate the principles of dharma, or righteous conduct, and the four goals of human life: dharma (duty), artha (wealth), kama (pleasure), and moksha (liberation).
- The Mahabharata also contains the Bhagavad Gita, one of the most revered scriptures of Hinduism. The Bhagavad Gita is a dialogue between the Pandava prince Arjuna and his charioteer and guide, Lord Krishna, who is an incarnation of the supreme god Vishnu. The Bhagavad Gita teaches the philosophy of karma yoga, or the path of action, and the importance of detachment, devotion, and duty in the face of the inevitable suffering and death in the world.
- The Mahabharata is a rich and diverse literary work that has inspired many adaptations, interpretations, and translations in various languages and media. It has influenced the art, culture, and literature of India and beyond for centuries. It is also a living tradition that is performed and recited in various forms, such as the wayang kulit (shadow puppetry) in Indonesia, the kathakali (dance drama) in Kerala, and the ramlila (dramatic enactment) in northern India.



# Puranas

- Puranas are a vast genre of Indian literature that deal with various topics, especially legends and traditional lore .
- Puranas are considered as one of the most ancient and important sources of Hindu culture, religion, history, and cosmology .
- Puranas are classified as Smriti (remembered) texts, as opposed to Shruti (heard) texts like the Vedas .
- Puranas are composed in Sanskrit verse, mostly in the form of narrative couplets, similar to the epics Mahabharata and Ramayana .
- Puranas are divided into six categories, based on the main deity they glorify: Vishnu, Shiva, Brahma, Devi, Agni, and Skanda .
- Puranas are also classified into Mahapuranas (major Puranas) and Upapuranas (minor Puranas), based on their length and authority .
- Puranas contain various topics, such as creation and dissolution of the universe, genealogies of gods, sages, and kings, descriptions of sacred places, rituals, festivals, and ethical teachings .
- Puranas are not considered as authoritative or binding as the Vedas, but they are widely read and revered by the Hindus for their spiritual and cultural value .
- Puranas reflect the diversity and pluralism of Hinduism, as they present different perspectives and interpretations of the same themes and stories .
- Puranas are also influenced by and influence other literary genres, such as epics, classical poetry, drama, and folk literature .



# Buddhist and Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Pali and Prakrit are two languages that were used to write the religious literature of Buddhism and Jainism, respectively. Pali is an old form of Prakrit that combines several dialects, while Prakrit is a term that refers to any language other than the standard one, Sanskrit.
- The earliest Buddhist works were written in Pali, which was spoken in Magadha and South Bihar. These works include the Tripitaka or the Three Baskets, which consist of the Sutta Pitaka (discourses of the Buddha), the Vinaya Pitaka (rules of monastic discipline), and the Abhidhamma Pitaka (philosophical and psychological analysis of the doctrine).
- The Pali literature also contains commentaries, chronicles, biographies, and other genres. Some of the famous Pali works are the Dhammapada (a collection of verses on ethics and wisdom), the Jataka (stories of the Buddha's previous lives), the Milindapanha (a dialogue between King Milinda and a Buddhist monk), and the Mahavamsa (a historical chronicle of Sri Lanka).
- The Buddhist literature in Sanskrit and Prakrit includes the Mahayana sutras, which expound the doctrines of the Great Vehicle, such as emptiness, compassion, and bodhisattva ideal. Some of the well-known Mahayana sutras are the Prajnaparamita Sutras (on the perfection of wisdom), the Lotus Sutra (on the universal salvation of all beings), the Avatamsaka Sutra (on the interpenetration of all phenomena), and the Lankavatara Sutra (on the nature of mind and reality).
- The Jain literature in Prakrit and Sanskrit comprises the canonical texts or the Agamas, which are divided into two main groups: the Angas (limbs) and the Angabahyas (external to the limbs). The Angas consist of 12 texts that deal with the teachings of Mahavira, the 24th and last Tirthankara (ford-maker) of Jainism, and his immediate disciples. The Angabahyas consist of 34 texts that deal with various topics such as cosmology, ethics, logic, grammar, and biography.
- The Jain literature also includes non-canonical texts, such as commentaries, treatises, stories, and poems. Some of the prominent Jain authors are Haribhadra (8th century CE), who wrote on logic, yoga, and ethics; Hemacandra (12th century CE), who wrote on grammar, history, and philosophy; and Jinasena (9th century CE), who wrote the Adipurana, a biography of the first two Tirthankaras.



# Kautilya's Arthashastra

- Kautilya's Arthashastra is an ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, economics, military strategy, and social organization.
- It is attributed to Kautilya, also known as Chanakya, Vishnugupta, or the Prime Minister of the Mauryan Empire (c. 350-275 BCE).
- It is considered one of the earliest and most comprehensive works on political science and administration in the world.
- It consists of 15 books, 150 chapters, and 180 topics, covering various aspects of governance, such as:
  - The king and his duties, qualities, and training.
  - The council of ministers and their selection, functions, and responsibilities.
  - The civil service and the administration of justice, law, and order.
  - The treasury, revenue, expenditure, and taxation.
  - The army, defense, espionage, and foreign policy.
  - The allies, enemies, and neutrals, and how to deal with them diplomatically or militarily.
  - The state and its population, including the caste system, the status of women, and the welfare of the people.
  - The science of economics, trade, commerce, and industry.
  - The ethics, morals, and values of the ruler and the subjects.
- Kautilya's Arthashastra is based on a pragmatic and realistic approach to politics and administration, without much regard for morality or ethics.
- It advocates the use of any means, fair or foul, to achieve the welfare and security of the state and the king.
- It also emphasizes the importance of knowledge, intelligence, and cunning in dealing with various situations and challenges.
- It is influenced by various sources of Indian thought, such as the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Dharmashastras, and the Buddhist and Jain texts.
- It also reflects the historical and cultural context of the Mauryan period, which was marked by political expansion, economic prosperity, and cultural diversity.
- Kautilya's Arthashastra is a valuable source of information and insight into the ancient Indian polity, society, and economy, and its relevance and applicability to modern times.



# Famous Sanskrit Authors

- Sanskrit is one of the oldest and most influential languages of India and the world. It has a rich and diverse literature that spans various genres, periods and regions.

He is also the author of the Mahabharata, the longest epic poem in the world, which depicts the Kurukshetra war between the Pandavas and the Kauravas. He is admired for his use of chitra kavya (picture poetry), shabda alankara (sound ornamentation) and rasa (sentiment).  
  - **Harsha**: He is the author of Naishadhiya Charitam (The Story of Nala and Damayanti), an epic poem that tells the love story of Nala, the king of Nishadha, and Damayanti, the princess of Vidarbha, and their separation and reunion. He is renowned for his elegance, sweetness and depth of expression.  
  - **Bhasa**: He is one of the earliest and most prolific Sanskrit dramatists, who wrote about thirteen plays, such as Svapnavasavadatta (The Dream of Vasavadatta), Pratijnayaugandharayana (The Pledge of Augandharayana) and Urubhanga (The Shattered Thigh). He is noted for his realism, characterization, humor and tragedy.  
  - **Asvaghosa**: He is a Buddhist poet and philosopher, who composed the Buddhacarita (The Acts of the Buddha), the first Sanskrit biography of the Buddha, and the Saundarananda (The Handsome Nanda), a kavya that depicts the conversion of Nanda, the Buddha's half-brother, to Buddhism. He is also credited with writing two plays: Sariputraprakarana (The Play of Sariputra) and Samajaparikatha (The Discourse on the Assembly). He is acclaimed for his eloquence, wisdom and devotion. 
  - **Bhartrihari**: He is a grammarian, philosopher and poet, who wrote the Vakyapadiya (The Treatise on Sentences and Words), a seminal work on the philosophy of language

# Sangama Literature

Sangama literature is the term used to refer to the ancient Tamil literature that was composed in the Tamilakam region of South India. The word Sangama means a confluence or a gathering, and it is derived from the tradition that there were three Sangamas or academies of poets and scholars that flourished at different periods of time and produced the Sangama literature. The Sangama literature is divided into two main categories: akam (love) and puram (heroism).

## Akam Literature

# Jainism

Jainism is one of the oldest religions of India, with a history of more than 2500 years. It is based on the teachings of 24 spiritual leaders called tirthankaras, who attained enlightenment and liberation from the cycle of rebirth. The last and most famous of these tirthankaras was Mahavira, who lived in the 6th century BCE .

Jainism teaches that every living being has a soul, which is eternal and can achieve liberation by following the path of non-violence, truth, non-stealing, celibacy, and non-attachment. These are the five main vows that every Jain must observe, either partially or fully, depending on their level of commitment .

Jainism also emphasizes the principle of anekantavada, which means that reality is complex and multifaceted, and that different viewpoints can be valid in different contexts. Jainism rejects the idea of absolute truth or authority, and encourages rational inquiry and dialogue .

Jainism is a transtheistic religion, which means that it does not affirm or deny the existence of a supreme being or creator. Instead, it focuses on the self-effort of the individual soul to attain liberation from the bondage of karma, which is the accumulated effect of one's actions, thoughts, and emotions. Jainism believes that the universe is eternal, self-sustaining, and governed by natural laws.

Jainism has a rich and diverse literature, which includes scriptures, commentaries, biographies, stories, and poems. # Six System Indian Philosophy

The six systems of Indian philosophy are Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva Mimamsa and Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta. These are also known as the shad-darshana, or the six views of reality. Each of these systems differs in one way or the other in terms of its concepts, phenomena, laws and dogmas. Each system has its own founder as well. Here is a brief overview of each system:

- **Nyaya**: The founder of Nyaya is Akshapada Gautama and it dated back to 2nd Century BCE. It is a school that deals with logic and epistemology. It aims to provide a method of reasoning to attain valid knowledge and liberation from suffering. It accepts four sources of knowledge: perception, inference, comparison and testimony. It also analyzes the categories of being, such as substance, quality, action, relation, etc. It defends the existence of a self, a God and a moral order.  

- **Vaisheshika**: The founder of Vaisheshika is Kanada and it dated back to 6th Century BCE. It is a school that deals with metaphysics and ontology. It proposes that reality is composed of six categories: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity and inherence. It also postulates that the ultimate constituents of matter are atoms, which combine to form molecules, and that the self and God are distinct substances.  

- **Samkhya**: The founder of Samkhya is Kapila and it dated back to 6th Century BCE. It is a school that deals with cosmology and psychology. It proposes a dualistic theory of reality, consisting of two principles: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter). It explains the evolution of the universe and the diversity of beings from the interaction of these two principles. It also describes the process of liberation from the bondage of prakriti through the discrimination of purusha.  

- **Yoga**: The founder of Yoga is Patanjali and it dated back to 2nd Century BCE. It is a school that deals with ethics and spirituality. It is closely related to Samkhya, but differs in accepting the existence of a personal God, Ishvara, as the highest purusha. It prescribes an eightfold path of discipline and meditation to attain the union of the individual self with the supreme self. It also discusses the various powers and states of consciousness that result from the practice of yoga.  

- **Purva Mimamsa**: The founder of Purva Mimamsa is Jaimini and it dated back to 3rd Century BCE. It is a school that deals with hermeneutics and ritual. It focuses on the interpretation and application of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. It argues that the performance of the Vedic rituals is the means to attain the highest good, which is heaven. It also analyzes the nature and validity of language, meaning and inference.  

- **Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta**: The founder of Vedanta is Badarayana and it dated back to 2nd Century BCE. It is a school that deals with theology and philosophy. It is based on the Upanishads, the philosophical portions of the Vedas. It expounds the doctrine of Brahman, the absolute reality, and its identity or relation with the individual self, or atman. It also discusses the nature and means of liberation, or moksha, from the cycle of rebirth, or samsara. There are various sub-schools of Vedanta, such as Advaita, Vishishtadvaita, Dvaita, etc., that differ in their interpretation of the Upanishadic teachings.

- They offered alternative paths of liberation based on ethical conduct, rational inquiry, and ascetic practices.
- Some of the heterodox sects are:

  - **Ajivika**: Founded by Makkhali Gosala, this sect believed in fatalism, i.e., everything is predetermined by fate and nothing can be changed by human effort. They rejected the notions of free will, karma, and moral responsibility. They practiced extreme asceticism and nudity. They were popular among the lower castes and had royal patronage from Bindusara, the father of Ashoka. They declined after the Mauryan period and survived only in some parts of South India until the 14th century CE .
  - **Unchedvadi**: Also known as Lokayata or Charvaka, this sect was founded by Brihaspati, a mythical sage. They advocated materialism, i.e., only matter is real and there is no soul, God, or life after death. They rejected the notions of karma, rebirth, and liberation. They valued sensory perception as the only source of knowledge and dismissed inference, analogy, and testimony as unreliable. They promoted hedonism, i.e., the pursuit of pleasure as the highest good. They were criticized by the orthodox and other heterodox schools for their lack of morality and spirituality. They had some influence on the Indian intellectual tradition until the medieval period .
  - **Nityavadi**: This sect was founded by Purana Kassapa, a contemporary of the Buddha. They believed in eternalism, i.e., everything is eternal and unchanging. They denied the notions of causation, action, and result. They argued that there is no difference between good and evil, and no consequences for one's deeds. They were opposed by the Buddha and other heterodox teachers for their moral nihilism .
  - **Sandehvadi**: This sect was founded by Sanjaya Belatthiputta, another contemporary of the Buddha. They believed in agnosticism, i.e., nothing can be known for certain. They suspended judgment on all metaphysical and ethical questions. They neither affirmed nor denied the existence of God, soul, karma, rebirth, or liberation. They were criticized by the Buddha and other heterodox teachers for their indecisiveness and skepticism .
  - **Akriyavadi**: This sect was founded by Pakudha Kaccayana, yet another contemporary of the Buddha. They believed in amoralism, i.e., there is no moral value or obligation. They claimed that there are seven eternal and unchanging elements: earth, water, fire, air, pleasure, pain, and soul. They asserted that these elements do not interact with each other and are not affected by any action. They denied the notions of karma, rebirth, and liberation. They were refuted by the Buddha and other heterodox teachers for their irrational and absurd views .



# Bhakti Movement

- The Bhakti movement was a significant religious movement in medieval Hinduism that sought to bring religious reforms to all strata of society by adopting the method of devotion to achieve salvation.
- Bhakti means "devotion" or "love" in Sanskrit, and it refers to the intense emotional attachment and love of a devotee toward a personal god and of the god for the devotee.
- The Bhakti movement preached against the caste system, ritualism, idolatry, and social inequalities, and used the local languages to convey its message to the masses.
- The Bhakti movement originated in South India in the 7th-8th centuries CE, with the Alvars (devotees of Vishnu) and the Nayanars (devotees of Shiva), who composed hymns and songs in praise of their respective deities.
- The Bhakti movement spread to North India in the 12th-13th centuries CE, with the emergence of various sects and schools, such as the Vaishnavism of Ramananda, Ramanuja, and Vallabha, the Shaivism of Basava and Gorakhnath, and the Shaktism of Ramprasad Sen and Chaitanya Mahaprabhu.
- The Bhakti movement also influenced the development of Sikhism, a monotheistic religion founded by Guru Nanak in the 15th century CE, which incorporated elements of both Hinduism and Islam.
- The Bhakti movement had a profound impact on the social, cultural, and literary aspects of Indian society, as it fostered a spirit of tolerance, harmony, and universal brotherhood among the people, and produced a rich and diverse body of literature, music, art, and architecture.
- The Bhakti movement also contributed to the practice of yoga, as it emphasized the importance of meditation, chanting, and surrender to the divine will as means of attaining union with the supreme reality. Bhakti yoga is one of the four main paths of yoga, along with karma yoga (action), jnana yoga (knowledge), and raja yoga (meditation).



# Sufi Movement in India

- Sufism is a mystical branch of Islam that emphasizes the direct experience of God through love, devotion, and ethical conduct.
- Sufism originated in the Middle East and Central Asia in the 8th and 9th centuries and spread to India through the Muslim invasions and migrations in the 12th and 13th centuries.
- Sufis in India established various orders or silsilas, each with its own spiritual leader (sheikh, pir, or murshid), disciples (murids), and successors (khalifahs).
- Some of the prominent silsilas in India were Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi, and Shattari.
- Sufis in India practiced various methods of spiritual training, such as meditation, music, poetry, dance, and service to humanity.
- Sufis in India also played a significant role in the social, cultural, and religious life of the people. They preached the message of love, tolerance, and harmony among different faiths and sects. They also contributed to the development of literature, art, architecture, and music in various languages and styles.
- Sufis in India faced opposition and persecution from some orthodox Muslim rulers and scholars, who considered them as heretics and innovators. They also faced challenges from the rise of the Bhakti movement, which offered a similar path of devotion to God in the Hindu tradition.
- Sufism in India declined in the 18th and 19th centuries due to the political and economic changes brought by the Mughal decline and the British colonial rule. However, Sufism still survives in India as a living tradition, with millions of followers and pilgrims visiting the shrines of the Sufi saints.



# Socio-religious reform movement of 19th century

- The socio-religious reform movement of 19th century was a wave of reforming activities that aimed to remove the social evils and religious superstitions from the Indian society and to revive the ancient glory of Hinduism.
- The main causes of the emergence of the reform movement were:
  - The spread of western education and liberal ideas that exposed the Indians to the rational and scientific outlook of the West and made them question the existing social and religious practices.
  - The contact with the Christian missionaries and the British administration that challenged the Hindu faith and culture and provoked a defensive and reformist response from the Hindus.
  - The influence of the Indian Renaissance that fostered a spirit of nationalism and cultural revival among the Indians and inspired them to reform their society and religion.
  - The social and economic changes that resulted from the British rule, such as the growth of urbanization, industrialization, commerce, transport and communication, that created new classes of educated and progressive Indians who demanded social and religious reforms.
- The main types of the reform movement were:
  - The Brahmo Samaj, founded by Raja Ram Mohan Roy in 1828, that advocated monotheism, abolition of idolatry, caste system, sati, child marriage and polygamy, and promotion of women's education, widow remarriage and social service.
  - The Arya Samaj, founded by Swami Dayanand Saraswati in 1875, that propagated the doctrine of "Back to the Vedas" and rejected the later accretions of Hinduism, such as idolatry, polytheism, rituals, priesthood, animal sacrifice, caste system and untouchability. It also encouraged the dissemination of western scientific knowledge and social reforms, such as women's education, widow remarriage and inter-caste marriage.
  - The Prarthana Samaj, founded by Atmaram Pandurang in 1867, that followed the teachings of Raja Ram Mohan Roy and advocated monotheism, abolition of caste system, child marriage and widowhood, and promotion of women's education, widow remarriage and social service. It also supported the reform of Hindu rituals and ceremonies and the establishment of educational and charitable institutions.
  - The Ramakrishna Mission, founded by Swami Vivekananda in 1897, that propagated the philosophy of Vedanta and the universal religion of humanity. It emphasized the harmony of religions, the service of mankind as the service of God, and the spiritual and moral upliftment of the masses. It also engaged in various social and educational activities, such as running schools, colleges, hospitals, orphanages and relief camps.
  - The Theosophical Society, founded by Madame Blavatsky and Colonel Olcott in 1875, that aimed to promote the study of comparative religion, philosophy and science, and to investigate the hidden mysteries of nature and human potential. It also sought to revive the ancient wisdom of Hinduism and Buddhism and to foster a sense of brotherhood among all people.
  - The Aligarh Movement, founded by Sir Syed Ahmed Khan in 1866, that aimed to reform the Muslim society and religion and to reconcile Islam with modern science and western education. It also advocated the loyalty of the Muslims to the British rule and the promotion of Hindu-Muslim unity. It established the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College, later known as the Aligarh Muslim University, as the center of its educational and cultural activities.

# Astronomy in India

- Astronomy has a long history in the Indian subcontinent, stretching from pre-historic to modern times.
- Some of the earliest roots of Indian astronomy can be dated to the period of Indus Valley civilization or earlier, when people observed the celestial phenomena and developed calendars based on lunar and solar cycles.
- Astronomy later developed as a discipline of Vedanga, or one of the "auxiliary disciplines" associated with the study of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism.
- The Vedanga Jyotisha, attributed to Lagadha, is one of the earliest texts on astronomy in India, dating from around the 6th century BCE. It contains rules for calculating the positions of the sun, moon, and planets, as well as the length of the day, the seasons, and the lunar and solar eclipses.
- Indian astronomy was influenced by Greek astronomy beginning in the 4th century BCE and through the early centuries of the Common Era, for example by the Yavanajataka and the Romaka Siddhanta, Sanskrit translations of Greek texts disseminated from the 2nd century CE.
- The Siddhantas, or astronomical treatises, were composed by various authors from the 5th to the 15th centuries CE, and covered topics such as planetary motions, eclipses, cosmology, and mathematical methods.
- Some of the notable Siddhanta authors include Aryabhata, Varahamihira, Brahmagupta, Bhaskara, and Nilakantha Somayaji.
- Indian astronomers made significant contributions to the fields of trigonometry, algebra, geometry, and arithmetic, and developed methods for solving various astronomical problems.
- They also proposed heliocentric models of the solar system, estimated the size and distance of the earth and the moon, and discovered the precession of the equinoxes.
- Astronomy in India was closely related to astrology, calendar making, and religious rituals, and was patronized by various rulers and institutions.
- The Suryasiddhanta, the most popular work on astronomy in North India, is attributed to divine authorship but seems to have been composed around 800 CE. It adopts the midnight epoch and certain elements from the old Saurasiddhanta, but differs from it in other respects.
- The Indian National Calendar, adopted in 1957, is based on the Saka era and the Suryasiddhanta.
- Astronomy in India continues to thrive in the modern era, with the establishment of various observatories, institutes, and organizations, such as the Indian Institute of Astrophysics, the Indian Space Research Organization, and the Astronomical Society of India.
- Indian astronomers are involved in various research areas, such as solar physics, stellar astronomy, galactic astronomy, extragalactic astronomy, cosmology, and astrobiology.
- India has also participated in several international collaborations and missions, such as the Chandrayaan lunar probes, the Mars Orbiter Mission, the Astrosat satellite, and the Thirty Meter Telescope project.



# Chemistry in India

Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the study of matter, its composition, structure, properties, and reactions. Chemistry has a wide scope and applications in various fields such as medicine, agriculture, industry, environment, and education. Chemistry is also an integral part of the Indian culture and heritage, as it has a long and rich history of development and achievements in this domain.

Some of the main points to note about chemistry in India are:

- Chemistry in India can be traced back to the ancient times, when the Indus Valley or Harappan civilization (3rd millennium BCE) used chemical techniques for metallurgy, pottery, dyeing, cosmetics, and medicine.
- The Vedic period (2nd millennium BCE to 6th century BCE) witnessed the emergence of Ayurveda, the traditional system of medicine that used various minerals, metals, plants, and animal products for healing and wellness. Ayurveda also played an important role in the development of alchemy, the older form of chemistry that aimed at transmuting base metals into gold and discovering the elixir of life .
- The post-Vedic period (6th century BCE to 5th century CE) saw the growth of various schools of philosophy and logic, such as Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Sankhya, and Yoga, that contributed to the understanding of the nature and classification of matter, atoms, elements, and compounds. The concepts of five elements (panchabhuta), six tastes (shadrasa), and three qualities (triguna) were also developed in this period.
- The classical period (5th century CE to 12th century CE) was marked by the flourishing of various branches of science and arts, such as astronomy, mathematics, medicine, literature, and architecture. Chemistry also reached its peak in this period, with the works of eminent scholars such as Nagarjuna, Patanjali, Charaka, Sushruta, Vagbhata, Varahamihira, and Aryabhata. They wrote treatises on alchemy, metallurgy, pharmacy, toxicology, mineralogy, and chemistry, and devised hundreds of devices and apparatus for chemical experiments. They also discovered and synthesized many new substances, such as zinc, mercury, sulfur, arsenic, and alloys, and used them for various purposes, such as medicine, cosmetics, paints, and explosives .
- The medieval period (12th century CE to 18th century CE) witnessed the invasion and colonization of India by various foreign powers, such as the Turks, Mongols, Mughals, Portuguese, Dutch, French, and British. This period also saw the decline of the indigenous science and culture, and the introduction of the Western science and technology. However, some aspects of chemistry, such as alchemy, metallurgy, pharmacy, and dyeing, continued to survive and evolve in this period, with the works of scholars such as Rasarnava, Nityanatha, Chakrapani, and Madhava.
- The modern period (18th century CE onwards) is characterized by the revival and development of chemistry in India, with the influence of the Western science and education. Many Indian chemists, such as Acharya Prafulla Chandra Ray, Sir C.V. Raman, Dr. Homi Bhabha, Dr. Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar, Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam, and Dr. C.N.R. Rao, have made significant contributions to the fields of organic chemistry, physical chemistry, nuclear chemistry, chemical engineering, and nanotechnology. India has also established many institutions, such as the Indian Institute of Science, the Indian Institutes of Technology, the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research, and the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science, to promote research and education in chemistry. India has also hosted and participated in many national and international events, such as the International Year of Chemistry, the Indian Science Congress, and the Royal Society of Chemistry, to celebrate and disseminate the achievements and challenges of chemistry  .

Chemistry in India is thus a rich and diverse field that has a long and glorious past, a vibrant and dynamic present, and a promising and bright future. Chemistry in India is not only a scientific discipline, but also a cultural and social phenomenon that reflects the values, beliefs, and aspirations of the Indian people.



# Mathematics in India

Mathematics in India is the discipline of mathematics as it developed in the Indian subcontinent from ancient to modern times. Some of the main features and achievements of Indian mathematics are:

- The invention of the decimal place-value system, which is the basis of the modern number system. Indian mathematicians used symbols for zero and nine digits, and developed methods of arithmetic, algebra, and geometry based on this system .
- The discovery of many important mathematical concepts and results, such as the concept of zero, negative numbers, fractions, irrational numbers, surds, quadratic equations, trigonometry, sine and cosine functions, the value of pi, the Pythagorean theorem, the area of a triangle, the formula for the sum of a geometric series, and the binomial theorem .
- The development of sophisticated mathematical techniques and texts, such as the Sulba Sutras, which are the earliest extant texts on geometry and mensuration; the Aryabhatiya, which is a treatise on astronomy and mathematics by Aryabhata, one of the most influential Indian mathematicians; the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, which is a work on arithmetic and algebra by Brahmagupta, who introduced the rules for operating with zero and negative numbers; the Lilavati and the Bijaganita, which are two parts of a comprehensive mathematical treatise by Bhaskara II, who solved many problems of algebra, geometry, and trigonometry; and the Pancha-Siddhantika, which is a compendium of five astronomical treatises by Varahamihira, who also contributed to mathematics, astrology, and hydrology  .
- The influence of Indian mathematics on other civilizations, such as the Islamic world, China, and Europe. Indian numerals and mathematics were transmitted to the Islamic world by Arab and Persian scholars, who translated and commented on many Indian mathematical works. Indian mathematics also reached China through Buddhist missionaries and traders, who introduced the decimal system and some algebraic and trigonometric methods. Indian mathematics also influenced Europe through the transmission of Arabic numerals and algebra, which were derived from Indian sources  .

Indian mathematics is a rich and diverse field of study, which reflects the cultural and intellectual achievements of the Indian civilization. It is also a source of inspiration and insight for modern mathematics and science.



# Physics in India

Physics is the branch of science that deals with the nature and properties of matter and energy. Physics in India has a long and rich history, dating back to the ancient times. Here are some points to note about physics in India:

- The Nyaya and Vaisheshika schools of philosophy, which emerged around the 6th century BCE, developed concepts of atomism, causation, inference, logic, and mechanics. They also proposed theories of light, sound, gravity, and motion. These aspects are dealt with in the present day physics, through the laws of motion, gravitation, thermodynamics, wave mechanics, hydrostatics, classical and quantum mechanics, nuclear and atomic physics, and electromagnetic theory.
- The famous mathematician and astronomer Aryabhata, who lived in the 5th century CE, wrote the Aryabhatiya, a treatise on astronomy and mathematics. He gave the value of pi, calculated the circumference of the earth, explained the causes of eclipses, and proposed a heliocentric model of the solar system. He also introduced the concept of zero and the decimal system in India. His work influenced many later Indian and Islamic scholars.
- Another influential astronomer and mathematician was Brahmagupta, who lived in the 7th century CE. He wrote the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, which contains many important results in algebra, geometry, trigonometry, and astronomy. He gave the rules for arithmetic operations with zero and negative numbers, solved quadratic and linear equations, and derived formulas for the area and volume of various geometric figures. He also gave a method for finding the sine of an angle and the inverse sine of a ratio.
- In the 11th century CE, Bhaskara II, also known as Bhaskaracharya, wrote the Siddhanta Shiromani, a comprehensive text on astronomy and mathematics. He gave the correct formula for the length of the year, the diameter of the earth, and the distance between the earth and the moon. He also gave the solutions to various types of indeterminate equations, and developed the concepts of differential calculus and infinitesimals. He also discussed the concepts of gravity, force, acceleration, and free fall.
- In the 14th century CE, Madhava of Sangamagrama, the founder of the Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics, made significant contributions to the fields of calculus, trigonometry, and infinite series. He gave the power series expansions for sine, cosine, tangent, and arctangent functions, and derived the value of pi to 11 decimal places. He also gave the infinite series for the inverse tangent function, which is now known as the Gregory-Leibniz series or the Madhava-Leibniz series. He also gave methods for finding the approximate values of various transcendental numbers, such as pi, e, and square roots.
- In the 16th century CE, Nilakantha Somayaji, a disciple of Madhava, wrote the Tantrasangraha, a treatise on astronomy and mathematics. He improved the heliocentric model of Aryabhata, and gave a correct explanation of the retrograde motion of the planets. He also gave the correct formula for the elliptical orbits of the planets, and derived the value of the astronomical unit, the distance between the earth and the sun. He also gave the infinite series for the sine and cosine functions, and the arctangent function.
- In the 19th century CE, Srinivasa Ramanujan, a self-taught genius, made remarkable contributions to the fields of number theory, algebra, analysis, and combinatorics. He discovered thousands of new formulas and theorems, many of which are still being proved and explored by mathematicians. He also discovered the Ramanujan theta function, the Ramanujan prime, the Ramanujan tau function, the Ramanujan conjecture, the Ramanujan-Soldner constant, the Ramanujan modular equations, and the Ramanujan-Sato series. He also gave a formula for calculating the number of partitions of a positive integer, and a method for finding the approximate value of pi.
- In the 20th century CE, India produced many eminent physicists, such as C.V. Raman, S.N. Bose, Homi Bhabha, Meghnad Saha, Jagadish Chandra Bose, Raja Ramanna, Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, and Abdus Salam. They made significant contributions to the fields of atomic physics



# Agriculture in India

Agriculture is one of the most important sectors of the Indian economy, contributing about 16% of the GDP and employing about 58% of the population. India has a diverse agro-climatic zone, ranging from tropical to temperate, and produces a variety of crops, such as cereals, pulses, oilseeds, fruits, vegetables, spices, sugarcane, cotton, jute, tea, coffee, and rubber. India is also the world's largest producer and consumer of milk, and has a large livestock population, including cattle, buffaloes, goats, sheep, poultry, and fish. 

Some of the major features and challenges of agriculture in India are:

- **Small and marginal farmers**: About 86% of the farmers in India own less than 2 hectares of land, and face problems such as low productivity, lack of access to credit, inputs, markets, and extension services, and vulnerability to climate change and natural disasters.
- **Irrigation and water management**: About 52% of the net sown area in India is rainfed, and depends on the monsoon rainfall, which is often erratic and uneven. Irrigation facilities are inadequate, and water use efficiency is low. Water scarcity, groundwater depletion, and water pollution are some of the major issues affecting agriculture.
- **Soil health and fertility**: Soil degradation, nutrient imbalance, salinity, alkalinity, and erosion are some of the factors that affect soil health and fertility in India. The use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides has also led to environmental and health problems. There is a need to promote organic farming, integrated nutrient management, and soil conservation practices.
- **Crop diversification and value addition**: India has a large potential to diversify its crop production and add value to its agricultural products, such as processing, packaging, branding, and marketing. This can help in increasing farmers' income, reducing wastage, and enhancing food security and nutrition. However, there are constraints such as lack of infrastructure, technology, skills, and policies to support crop diversification and value addition.
- **Agricultural research and innovation**: India has a strong agricultural research system, with institutions such as the Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR), State Agricultural Universities (SAUs), and Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs). However, there is a need to strengthen the linkages between research and extension, and to foster innovation and entrepreneurship in agriculture. There is also a need to adopt new technologies such as biotechnology, nanotechnology, precision agriculture, and digital agriculture.
- **Agricultural policies and programs**: The government of India has launched various policies and programs to support agriculture, such as the Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY), Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY), Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN), National Food Security Mission (NFSM), Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (RKVY), and National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA). However, there are issues such as implementation gaps, fund utilization, monitoring and evaluation, and coordination among different agencies and stakeholders.
- **Agricultural trade and markets**: India is a net exporter of agricultural products, such as rice, wheat, cotton, spices, tea, coffee, and marine products. However, India's share in the global agricultural trade is low, and it faces challenges such as non-tariff barriers, quality standards, and competition from other countries. India also needs to improve its domestic agricultural markets, by removing distortions, enhancing infrastructure, promoting e-NAM, and ensuring fair prices and remunerative markets for farmers.
- **Agricultural education and extension**: India has a large network of agricultural education and extension institutions, such as SAUs, KVKs, Agricultural Technology Management Agencies (ATMAs), and Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs). However, there is a need to improve the quality and relevance of agricultural education and extension, by updating the curriculum, enhancing the skills and competencies of the faculty and staff, and using ICT and mass media for effective dissemination of information and knowledge.
- **Agricultural sustainability and resilience**: India faces the challenge of ensuring agricultural sustainability and resilience, in the face of climate change, natural disasters, pests and diseases, and socio-economic factors. There is a need to adopt climate-smart agriculture



# Medicine in India

- Medicine in India has a long and rich history, dating back to the ancient Vedic texts and influenced by various cultural and religious traditions.
- The most prominent system of traditional medicine in India is Ayurveda, which means "the science of life". Ayurveda is based on the concept of three doshas (vata, pitta, and kapha), which are the fundamental energies that govern the body and mind. Ayurveda aims to balance the doshas and promote health and well-being through diet, lifestyle, herbs, yoga, meditation, and other therapies .
- Another system of traditional medicine in India is Siddha, which originated in Tamil Nadu and is closely related to Ayurveda. Siddha focuses on the use of metals, minerals, and animal products, as well as herbs, to treat diseases and enhance longevity. Siddha practitioners also use alchemy, astrology, and spiritual practices to diagnose and heal patients.
- A third system of traditional medicine in India is Unani, which is derived from the Greek and Arabic medical traditions. Unani is based on the theory of four humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile), which correspond to four elements (air, water, fire, and earth) and four temperaments (sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, and melancholic). Unani aims to restore the equilibrium of the humors and the body's natural faculties through diet, exercise, massage, cupping, and herbal remedies.
- Apart from these systems, there are also other forms of traditional medicine in India, such as Homeopathy, Naturopathy, Yoga, and Tibetan medicine. These forms of medicine have their own principles, methods, and benefits, and are often used in combination with other systems or with modern medicine.
- Modern medicine in India has developed since the colonial period, when Western medicine was introduced by the British and other European powers. Modern medicine in India has made significant advances in fields such as surgery, cardiology, oncology, neurology, and immunology, and has contributed to the global medical research and innovation. Modern medicine in India also faces challenges such as accessibility, affordability, quality, and ethics.
- Medicine in India is a diverse and dynamic field that reflects the country's cultural, historical, and scientific heritage. Medicine in India offers a variety of options and approaches for health care and disease prevention, and also poses some opportunities and challenges for the future.



# Metallurgy in India

- Metallurgy is the science and technology of extracting and processing metals from ores, alloys, or recycled materials.
- Metallurgy in India has a long and rich history, dating back to the 3rd millennium BCE. India has produced and used various metals such as copper, bronze, iron, steel, gold, silver, zinc, lead, tin, etc. for various purposes such as tools, weapons, ornaments, coins, architecture, etc.
- Some of the notable achievements of Indian metallurgy are:

# Music for the notes of the Module 5- Cultural Heritage and Performing Arts in the subject of INDIAN TRADITIONS, CULTURAL AND SOCIETY

- Music is one of the most ancient and diverse forms of intangible cultural heritage in India, reflecting the rich and complex history, geography, and diversity of the country.
- There are two major traditions of Indian classical music: Carnatic music, a style associated with South India, and Hindustani music, a style associated with North India. Both traditions have their roots in the ancient Vedic hymns and the Natyashastra, a treatise on performing arts attributed to Bharata Muni.
- Carnatic music is based on a system of 72 melakartas or parent scales, each of which has a set of seven notes or swaras. Carnatic music also uses a variety of ragas or modes, which are derived from the melakartas by altering one or more swaras. Carnatic music is mainly vocal, with the singer accompanied by instruments such as the veena, violin, flute, mridangam, and ghatam.
- Hindustani music is based on a system of 10 thaats or parent scales, each of which has a set of seven notes or swaras. Hindustani music also uses a variety of ragas or modes, which are derived from the thaats by adding or omitting notes, or by using microtones or shrutis. Hindustani music can be vocal or instrumental, with instruments such as the sitar, sarod, sarangi, bansuri, shehnai, tabla, and harmonium.
- Both Carnatic and Hindustani music follow a structure of alapana, a slow and improvised introduction of the raga, followed by a composition or kriti, which consists of a pallavi or refrain, an anupallavi or second section, and one or more charanams or verses. The composition may also include a niraval, a melodic variation of a line of the pallavi, and a swaraprastara, a rhythmic variation of the swaras of the raga.
- Apart from classical music, India also has a rich and diverse tradition of folk music, which varies from region to region and reflects the local culture, language, and lifestyle of the people. Folk music is often associated with rituals, festive events, oral traditions, or social movements. Some examples of folk music are bhangra, dandiya, lavani, baul, bhajan, qawwali, and ghazal.
- India also has a vibrant and popular tradition of film music, which emerged in the early 20th century with the advent of cinema. Film music is influenced by various genres of Indian and Western music, and often incorporates elements of classical, folk, and modern music. Film music is composed by music directors, who work with lyricists, singers, and musicians to create songs that suit the theme, mood, and genre of the film. Some of the most famous music directors in Indian cinema are Naushad, S.D. Burman, R.D. Burman, A.R. Rahman, and Shankar-Ehsaan-Loy.
- Music is an integral part of the Indian cultural heritage and performing arts, and has contributed to the enrichment and diversity of the global musical landscape. # Theatre

Theatre is a form of performing art that involves actors, stage, audience, and script. Theatre is one of the oldest and most diverse forms of cultural expression in India. It has a rich and varied history that spans from ancient times to the present day. Theatre in India reflects the social, religious, and political aspects of the Indian society and culture.

## Origins of Theatre in India

- The earliest evidence of theatre in India can be traced back to the **Vedas**, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, which contain hymns, rituals, and dialogues that were recited or sung by priests and sages.
- The **Natya Shastra**, a treatise on theatre written by **Bharata Muni** in the **2nd century BCE**, is considered the foundational text of Indian theatre. It describes the theory and practice of various aspects of theatre, such as drama, music, dance, costumes, makeup, stage design, and audience response .
- The **Sanskrit drama**, which flourished from the **1st century BCE** to the **10th century CE**, is the most prominent form of classical theatre in India. It was influenced by the Natya Shastra and the epics of **Ramayana** and **Mahabharata**. It was characterized by elaborate plots, poetic language, stylized gestures, and musical accompaniment. Some of the famous playwrights of Sanskrit drama were **Kalidasa**, **Bhasa**, **Shudraka**, and **Vishakhadatta** .
- The **regional theatre**, which developed from the **11th century CE** onwards, is the diverse and vibrant form of theatre that emerged in different parts of India. It was influenced by the local languages, cultures, folk traditions, and religious movements. It included various genres, such as comedy, tragedy, romance, satire, and historical drama. Some of the prominent forms of regional theatre are **Yakshagana** (Karnataka), **Kathakali** (Kerala), **Koodiyattam** (Kerala), **Bhavai** (Gujarat), **Nautanki** (Uttar Pradesh), **Jatra** (West Bengal), **Tamasha** (Maharashtra), **Bhaona** (Assam), and **Rasleela** (Manipur) .

## Characteristics of Theatre in India

- Theatre in India is a **narrative form**, in which recitation, singing, and dancing are integral elements. It often involves a **sutradhara** or a narrator who introduces the characters, sets the scene, and comments on the action.
- Theatre in India is a **synthesis of various art forms**, such as literature, music, dance, painting, sculpture, and architecture. It encompasses a wide range of styles, techniques, and themes, from realistic to symbolic, from classical to folk, from religious to secular.
- Theatre in India is a **reflection of the society and culture**, in which it is performed. It depicts the values, beliefs, customs, and conflicts of the Indian people. It also responds to the historical, political, and social changes that occur in the country.
- Theatre in India is a **medium of communication and education**, in which the performers and the audience interact and exchange ideas and emotions. It also serves as a tool for social awareness, criticism, and reform.

## Modern Theatre in India

- The modern theatre in India emerged in the **19th century**, under the influence of the **British colonial rule** and the **Western theatre**. It was initially a form of entertainment for the elite and educated classes, who preferred English or European languages and themes.
- The modern theatre in India also witnessed the rise of the **nationalist movement**, which used theatre as a means of expressing the aspirations and struggles of the Indian people. # Drama in Indian Culture

Drama is a form of performing arts that involves the enactment of stories using dialogue, music, gesture and dance. Drama in Indian culture has a long and rich history, dating back to the Vedic times. Drama in Indian culture can be classified into two broad categories: classical and folk.

## Classical Drama

Classical drama in India is based on the principles of the Nātyaśāstra, a treatise on dramaturgy attributed to Bharata Muni, a sage who lived around the 3rd-4th century BCE. The Nātyaśāstra defines drama as a combination of rasa (sentiment), bhava (mood), abhinaya (expression) and vritti (style). The Nātyaśāstra also lays down the rules for the structure, language, music, costumes, stage, audience and performance of drama.

Classical drama in India can be further divided into two types: Sanskrit and Prakrit. Sanskrit drama is the most ancient and prestigious form of classical drama, written in the Sanskrit language and following the conventions of the Nātyaśāstra. Some of the famous Sanskrit dramatists are Kālidāsa, Bhāsa, Śūdraka, Viśākhadatta and Harsha. Some of the famous Sanskrit plays are Abhijñānaśākuntalam, Mālavikāgnimitram, Mṛcchakaṭikā, Mudrārākṣasa and Nāgānanda.

Prakrit drama is a later development of classical drama, written in the Prakrit languages such as Pali, Ardhamagadhi, Sauraseni and Magadhi. Prakrit drama is more realistic, humorous and secular than Sanskrit drama, and deals with the lives of common people. Some of the famous Prakrit dramatists are Hāla, Rājaśekhara, Śrīharṣa and Mahendravarman. Some of the famous Prakrit plays are Sattasaī, Karpūramañjarī, Ratnāvalī and Mattavilāsa.

## Folk Drama

Folk drama in India is a diverse and vibrant form of drama that reflects the regional, linguistic, religious and cultural diversity of the country. Folk drama in India is performed by various communities, castes, tribes and sects, and is influenced by local myths, legends, rituals, festivals and social issues. Folk drama in India is usually performed in open-air venues, such as streets, courtyards, temples, fields and forests, and involves the participation of the audience.

Folk drama in India can be classified into various types, such as Yatra/Jatra, Ramleela, Raasleela, Swang, Nautanki, Dashavatar, Kariyala, Khyal, Tamasha, Bhavai, Yakshagana, Therukoothu, Bhaona, Ankiya Nāt, Chhau, Maach, Bhand Pather, Ras, Bhagavata Mela and Kutiyattam. Each of these types has its own distinctive features, such as language, music, dance, costumes, masks, puppets, props, themes and characters. Some of the famous folk dramatists and performers are Ratan Thiyam, Habib Tanvir, Utpal Dutt, B.V. Karanth, Kanhailal, Raja Paranjape, Shanta Gandhi, Bansi Kaul and Ravi Baswani.



# Painting

Painting is one of the oldest and most diverse forms of artistic expression in India. Painting can be used to depict various themes, such as religious stories, historical events, natural scenes, portraits, abstract concepts, and more. Painting can also reflect the cultural and regional diversity of India, as different styles and techniques emerged in different parts of the country over time.

Some of the main features and types of Indian painting are:

- **Murals**: Murals are large-scale paintings that are executed on the walls or ceilings of solid structures, such as temples, caves, palaces, or forts. Murals are usually done with natural pigments or paints that are applied directly on the surface. Murals can cover a wide range of subjects, such as mythology, epics, legends, royal life, nature, etc. Some of the most famous examples of murals in India are the Ajanta Caves, the Ellora Caves, the Kailashnath Temple, the Brihadeshwara Temple, the Lepakshi Temple, and the Padmanabhapuram Palace.

- **Miniatures**: Miniatures are small-scale paintings that are done on perishable materials, such as paper, cloth, palm leaf, wood, ivory, or metal. Miniatures are usually done with fine brushes and intricate details, and often accompanied by text or calligraphy. Miniatures can be found in manuscripts, albums, scrolls, or as independent artworks. Miniatures can depict various themes, such as religious stories, court scenes, portraits, landscapes, flora and fauna, etc. Some of the most prominent styles of miniatures in India are the Pala, the Jain, the Rajput, the Mughal, the Deccani, the Pahari, and the Company.

- **Paintings on cloth**: Paintings on cloth are artworks that are done on various types of fabrics, such as cotton, silk, jute, or wool. Paintings on cloth can be done with natural or synthetic dyes, paints, or embroidery. Paintings on cloth can be used for various purposes, such as religious rituals, festivals, ceremonies, decoration, or clothing. Paintings on cloth can also showcase various motifs, patterns, designs, and colors that are characteristic of different regions and communities in India. Some of the most well-known forms of paintings on cloth in India are the Kalamkari, the Madhubani, the Warli, the Pattachitra, the Phad, the Thangka, and the Batik.



# Martial Arts Traditions

Martial arts are systems of codified practices and traditions of training for combat. They are used for self-defense, physical fitness, mental and spiritual development, entertainment, and cultural preservation. India has a rich and diverse heritage of martial arts that dates back to ancient times. Some of the most prominent and ancient martial art forms of India are:

- **Mardani Khel**: This is a weapon-based martial art that originated in Maharashtra, especially in Kolhapur. It involves the use of 14 styles of wielding weapons, such as swords, daggers, shields, spears, axes, and lathis. It also incorporates wrestling, grappling, and unarmed combat techniques. Mardani khel was developed by the Maratha warriors as a means of defending their land and honour against invaders. It is still practiced today by some communities in Maharashtra and Karnataka .

- **Silambam**: This is a martial art that uses a bamboo staff as the primary weapon. It originated in Tamil Nadu and has been mentioned in the Sangam literature. It is one of the oldest martial arts in the world and is said to have influenced the development of karate and kung fu. Silambam involves swift and agile movements, strikes, blocks, and feints with the staff. It also includes the use of other weapons, such as knives, swords, and spears, as well as unarmed combat techniques. Silambam is practiced by various communities in South India and Sri Lanka .

- **Kuttu Varisai**: This is an unarmed martial art that originated in Tamil Nadu. It is also known as Varma Kalai or Marma Adi. It is based on the knowledge of vital points or marma on the human body. It involves striking, kicking, locking, and grappling techniques that aim to disable or injure the opponent by attacking the marma points. It also incorporates breathing exercises, meditation, and yoga. Kuttu Varisai is considered a spiritual and scientific art that balances the body, mind, and soul .

- **Thoda**: This is a martial art that involves archery and swordsmanship. It originated in Himachal Pradesh and is practiced by the Khash and Gaddi tribes. It is also known as the dance of the arrows, as it involves rhythmic and graceful movements with the bow and arrow. Thoda is performed as a ritual and a sport during festivals and fairs. It is a symbol of brotherhood, courage, and honour among the tribes .

- **Gatka**: This is a martial art that uses wooden sticks or swords as the main weapons. It originated in Punjab and is associated with the Sikh warriors. It is also known as Shastar Vidya or the science of weapons. It involves swift and circular movements, strikes, blocks, and parries with the sticks or swords. It also includes the use of other weapons, such as daggers, axes, and chakrams, as well as unarmed combat techniques. Gatka is practiced as a form of self-defense, physical fitness, and spiritual discipline by the Sikhs and other communities in North India .

- **Thang Ta**: This is a martial art that uses swords and spears as the main weapons. It originated in Manipur and is also known as Huyen Langlon or the art of sword and spear. It is one of the oldest martial arts in India and is said to have influenced the development of other martial arts in Southeast Asia. Thang Ta involves precise and powerful movements, strikes, blocks, and thrusts with the swords and spears. It also includes the use of other weapons, such as shields, daggers, and bows, as well as unarmed combat techniques. Thang Ta is practiced as a form of self-defense, cultural expression, and spiritual awakening by the Meitei people and other communities in Manipur .

These are some of the martial arts traditions that reflect the diversity and richness of India's cultural heritage and performing arts. They are not only forms of physical training, but also ways of preserving and transmitting the values, history, and identity of the people who practice them. They are also sources of inspiration and innovation for modern martial arts and sports.



# Fairs and Festivals

Fairs and festivals are an integral part of the Indian culture and heritage. They reflect the diversity, creativity, and spirituality of the people of India. Fairs and festivals are celebrated throughout the year, across the length and breadth of the country, by different communities, religions, and regions. They showcase the rich traditions, arts, crafts, cuisines, music, dance, and folklore of India.

Some of the main features of fairs and festivals in India are:

- They are based on the lunar calendar, which follows the phases of the moon and the seasons of the year.
- They are often associated with religious occasions, such as the birth, death, or enlightenment of a deity, a saint, or a guru.
- They are also linked to agricultural cycles, such as the sowing, harvesting, or offering of crops.
- They are marked by various rituals, ceremonies, prayers, offerings, processions, and sacrifices.
- They are attended by millions of people, who travel from far and near to participate in the festivities.
- They are venues for cultural exchange, social bonding, and economic activity.

Some of the most famous fairs and festivals of India are:

- Diwali: The festival of lights, celebrated by Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, and Buddhists, to commemorate the victory of good over evil, and the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya after defeating Ravana.
- Holi: The festival of colors, celebrated by Hindus, to mark the arrival of spring, and the triumph of Lord Vishnu over the demon king Hiranyakashipu and his son Prahlad.
- Dussehra: The festival of victory, celebrated by Hindus, to honor the slaying of the demon king Ravana by Lord Rama, and the killing of the buffalo demon Mahishasura by Goddess Durga.
- Pongal: The harvest festival of South India, celebrated by Tamils, to thank the Sun God, the Earth, and the cattle for their bounty and blessings.
- Ganesh Chaturthi: The birthday of Lord Ganesha, the elephant-headed god of wisdom and success, celebrated by Hindus, with the installation, worship, and immersion of clay idols of Ganesha in water bodies.
- Eid-ul-Fitr: The festival of breaking the fast, celebrated by Muslims, to mark the end of the holy month of Ramadan, with prayers, charity, and feasting.
- Christmas: The festival of the birth of Jesus Christ, celebrated by Christians, with the decoration of Christmas trees, exchange of gifts, and singing of carols.
- Pushkar Fair: The largest camel fair in the world, held in the town of Pushkar in Rajasthan, where thousands of camels, horses, and cattle are traded, and various cultural and religious events are organized.
- Jaisalmer Desert Festival: A three-day extravaganza of music, dance, camel races, turban tying, and other competitions, held in the golden city of Jaisalmer in Rajasthan, to showcase the desert culture and heritage of India.
- Kumbh Mela: The largest religious gathering in the world, held every 12 years at four sacred locations in India, where millions of Hindu pilgrims take a dip in the holy rivers, to wash away their sins and attain salvation.
- Konark Dance Festival: A five-day festival of classical dance and music, held in the backdrop of the magnificent Sun Temple of Konark in Odisha, to celebrate the ancient art forms of India.
- Goa Carnival: A four-day festival of fun, frolic, and festivities, held in the state of Goa, to mark the beginning of Lent, with colorful parades, floats, masks, costumes, and music.

# Indian's Cultural Contribution to the World

India is a country of diverse and rich cultural heritage, with a history of more than 5000 years. India has influenced and been influenced by various civilizations and cultures across the world, through trade, migration, religion, art, literature, science, and technology. Some of the notable contributions of India to the world are:

- India gave the world its first university, Takshashila University, which was established around the 6th century BCE and attracted scholars from various regions and disciplines .
- India gave the world the numeral system, including the concept and symbol of zero, which was developed by Indian mathematicians around the 5th century CE and later transmitted to the Arab world and Europe .
- India gave the world the game of chess, which originated from an ancient Indian game called chaturanga, meaning "four divisions of the military". The game evolved over time and spread to Persia, China, Europe, and other regions .
- India gave the world the use and invention of buttons, which were first used in India as early as 2000 BCE. Buttons were made of seashells, metal, ivory, wood, and other materials, and were used to fasten clothes and ornaments .
- India gave the world the shampoo, which derived from the Sanskrit word champu, meaning "to massage". Shampoo was a herbal mixture of various ingredients, such as gooseberry, soapberries, hibiscus, and other plants, that was used to cleanse and condition the hair .
- India gave the world the cure for leprosy, which was a chronic and infectious disease that affected the skin, nerves, and other organs. The ancient Indian physician Sushruta described the symptoms and treatment of leprosy in his medical treatise Sushruta Samhita, which was written around the 6th century BCE. He prescribed various remedies, such as oil, resin, mercury, and surgical procedures, to treat the disease .
- India gave the world the game of snakes and ladders, which was inspired from an Indian game called mokshapat, meaning "the ladder to salvation". The game was a moral lesson that taught the players about the effects of good and bad deeds on their journey to liberation. The game was later modified and popularized by the British in the 19th century .
- India gave the world the art of yoga, which is a system of physical, mental, and spiritual practices that originated in India around the 5th century BCE. Yoga aims to achieve harmony and balance between the body, mind, and soul, and to attain a state of enlightenment. Yoga has various branches, such as hatha, raja, karma, bhakti, jnana, and kundalini, and has influenced various religions, such as Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism .
- India gave the world the concept of non-violence, which is a principle of peace and harmony that rejects the use of violence and aggression as a means of resolving conflicts. The concept of non-violence was advocated by various Indian leaders and thinkers, such as Mahatma Gandhi, who led the Indian independence movement against the British colonial rule, and inspired many other movements and activists around the world, such as Martin Luther King Jr., Nelson Mandela, Dalai Lama, and Aung San Suu Kyi .
- India gave the world the literature of epics, such as the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, which are among the longest and oldest poems in the world. The epics narrate the stories of gods, kings, heroes, and villains, and explore various themes, such as duty, morality, love, war, and spirituality. The epics have influenced various forms of art, such as drama, dance, music, painting, sculpture, and cinema, and have been translated and adapted into various languages and cultures .



# Indian Cinema

Indian cinema is one of the most diverse and vibrant forms of artistic expression in the world. It encompasses a variety of genres, languages, regions, and styles, reflecting the rich and complex cultural heritage of India. Indian cinema has a long and illustrious history, dating back to the late 19th century, and has influenced and been influenced by other national and global cinema movements. Here are some of the main points to know about Indian cinema:

- **Historical Background**: Indian cinema was introduced by the Lumiere Brothers, who showed six soundless short films in Bombay (now Mumbai) in 1896 . The first Indian film, Raja Harishchandra, a silent mythological drama, was made by Dadasaheb Phalke in 1913 . The first Indian talkie, Alam Ara, was released in 1931 by Ardeshir Irani . The 1930s and 1940s saw the emergence of the studio system, social realism, and the rise of stars like Devika Rani, Ashok Kumar, and K.L. Saigal. The 1950s and 1960s are considered the golden age of Indian cinema, with the emergence of the parallel cinema movement, which focused on realistic and artistic films, and the popularity of musicals, romances, and epics, featuring stars like Raj Kapoor, Nargis, Dilip Kumar, Madhubala, Guru Dutt, Meena Kumari, and Rajesh Khanna . The 1970s and 1980s saw the dominance of the masala genre, which combined action, comedy, drama, and romance, and the rise of the angry young man persona, embodied by Amitabh Bachchan . The 1990s and 2000s witnessed the globalization and diversification of Indian cinema, with the emergence of new genres, themes, and technologies, and the crossover appeal of stars like Shah Rukh Khan, Aamir Khan, Salman Khan, Kajol, Aishwarya Rai, and Hrithik Roshan . The 2010s and 2020s have seen the further expansion and experimentation of Indian cinema, with the growth of regional cinema, independent cinema, digital platforms, and social media, and the recognition of new talents and voices .

- **Cultural and Performing Arts**: Indian cinema is deeply influenced by the rich and diverse cultural and performing arts traditions of India, such as literature, poetry, music, dance, theatre, and folk arts. Indian cinema often adapts stories, themes, and characters from classical and modern literary works, such as the Ramayana, the Mahabharata, the works of Rabindranath Tagore, Premchand, and R.K. Narayan, and the novels of Chetan Bhagat and Arundhati Roy . Indian cinema also incorporates various forms of music, such as classical, folk, devotional, and popular, and showcases the talents of singers, composers, and lyricists, such as Lata Mangeshkar, Kishore Kumar, R.D. Burman, A.R. Rahman, and Gulzar . Indian cinema also features various forms of dance, such as classical, folk, and contemporary, and highlights the skills of dancers, choreographers, and actors, such as Vyjayanthimala, Hema Malini, Madhuri Dixit, Prabhu Deva, and Varun Dhawan . Indian cinema also draws inspiration from various forms of theatre, such as traditional, modern, and street, and showcases the talents of playwrights, directors, and actors, such as Prithviraj Kapoor, Shyam Benegal, Naseeruddin Shah, and Nawazuddin Siddiqui . Indian cinema also reflects the diversity and vibrancy of the folk arts of India, such as puppetry, painting, sculpture, and embroidery, and showcases the creativity and craftsmanship of artists, such as M.F. Husain, Raja Ravi Varma, Nek Chand, and Manish Malhotra .
