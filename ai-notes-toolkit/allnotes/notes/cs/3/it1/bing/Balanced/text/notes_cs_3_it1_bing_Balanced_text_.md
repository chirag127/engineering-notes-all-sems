### State in Ancient India

- The state in ancient India was a social organization with political power that ensured peace, order and happiness.
- The origin of the state in ancient India is not clear, but there are three main theories: social contract theory, divine origin theory and organic theory.
- Social contract theory: This theory holds that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among the people who gave up some of their rights and freedoms to a ruler or a group of rulers in exchange for protection and welfare.
- Divine origin theory: This theory holds that the state was created by God or gods who appointed a king or a dynasty to rule over the people as their representative or deputy.
- Organic theory: This theory holds that the state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the growth and development of human society, like a living organism.
- The types of states in ancient India were mainly monarchy, republic and oligarchy.
- Monarchy: This was the most prevalent type of state in ancient India, where the king was the supreme authority and the source of power for the ministers, governors, village councils and trade guilds . The king was usually hereditary, but sometimes elected or nominated by a council of elders or nobles.
- Republic: This was a type of state where the people or their representatives had a say in the governance and administration of the state. The republics were usually formed by clans, tribes or groups of people who shared a common ancestry, territory or occupation. Some examples of republics in ancient India are the Licchavis, the Mallas, the Shakyas and the Kshudrakas.
- Oligarchy: This was a type of state where the power was concentrated in the hands of a few people or a class of people, such as the nobles, the warriors, the merchants or the priests. The oligarchies were often oppressive and exploitative of the masses. Some examples of oligarchies in ancient India are the Nandas, the Mauryas and the Sungas.



### Evolutionary Theory for the notes of the Module 1- Society State and Polity in India

- The evolutionary theory of the origin of the state is one of the theories that attempts to explain how the state emerged in ancient India.
- According to this theory, the state evolved gradually from the family, clan, tribe, and village to the city-state and the empire.
- The family was the first and the simplest form of human association, based on kinship and blood ties. The family was the basic unit of social, economic, and religious life.
- As the families multiplied and expanded, they formed clans, which were larger groups of people who shared a common ancestor. The clans were more organized and had a leader or a chief who settled disputes and led wars.
- The clans further united into tribes, which were larger and more complex groups of people who shared a common language, culture, and territory. The tribes had a tribal council or a assembly that made decisions and laws for the common good. The tribes also had a king or a chief who was the supreme authority and the protector of the tribe.
- The tribes eventually settled in villages, which were permanent settlements with agriculture and trade. The villages had a village council or a panchayat that managed the local affairs and administered justice. The villages also paid taxes and tribute to the king or the chief of the tribe.
- The villages grew into city-states, which were independent and sovereign political units with a defined territory and a centralized government. The city-states had a complex administration, a legal system, a military, and a bureaucracy. The city-states also had a constitution or a dharma that regulated the rights and duties of the citizens and the rulers.
- The city-states merged into empires, which were large and powerful political entities that ruled over many city-states and regions. The empires had a supreme ruler or a emperor who was the source of all law and authority. The empires also had a council of ministers, a provincial administration, a civil service, and a spy network. The empires also had a common culture, religion, and ideology that unified the diverse peoples and regions under their rule.

- The evolutionary theory of the origin of the state in ancient India is based on the historical and literary sources, such as the Vedas, the Epics, the Dharmashastras, the Arthashastra, and the Buddhist and Jain texts.
- The evolutionary theory of the origin of the state in ancient India is also supported by the archaeological and numismatic evidence, such as the Harappan civilization, the Mauryan empire, the Kushan empire, the Gupta empire, and the Mughal empire.
- The evolutionary theory of the origin of the state in ancient India is not without its limitations and criticisms. Some of the challenges to this theory are:

  - The theory assumes a linear and progressive development of the state, which may not reflect the historical reality of the rise and fall of different states and empires in ancient India.
  - The theory does not account for the diversity and complexity of the political and social structures in ancient India, such as the republics, the oligarchies, the confederations, the feudal states, and the tribal states.
  - The theory does not explain the role and influence of the external factors, such as the invasions, the migrations, the trade, and the cultural contacts, on the origin and evolution of the state in ancient India.
  - The theory does not address the ethical and moral aspects of the state, such as the legitimacy, the justice, the welfare, and the sovereignty, that were important for the ancient Indian thinkers and rulers.



### Force Theory

- Force theory is one of the theories that explain the origin of the state.
- According to this theory, the state emerged as a result of conquest, violence, and coercion by a powerful individual or group over a weaker one.
- The conquerors established their authority and domination over the conquered people and imposed their will and laws on them.
- The state, therefore, is based on force and not on consent or contract.
- Some examples of force theory are the ancient empires of Assyria, Babylon, Persia, Rome, and China, as well as the modern colonial empires of Britain, France, Spain, and Portugal.
- The main criticisms of force theory are that it ignores the role of social contract, consent, and cooperation in the formation of the state, and that it does not explain the diversity and complexity of political systems and cultures in the world.

### Contract Theory

- Contract theory is a branch of political philosophy that studies the origin, nature, and legitimacy of the state and its authority over individuals.
- Contract theory assumes that individuals are rational, self-interested, and free to consent or dissent to any social arrangement.
- Contract theory proposes that the state is the result of a voluntary agreement or contract among individuals who give up some of their natural rights and liberties in exchange for the protection and benefits of a common authority.
- Contract theory can be divided into two main types: classical and modern.
- Classical contract theory is based on the idea of natural law and natural rights, which are derived from human reason and nature. Classical contract theorists include Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau.
- Modern contract theory is based on the idea of hypothetical consent and impartiality, which are derived from rational choice and moral reasoning. Modern contract theorists include John Rawls, Robert Nozick, and David Gauthier.



### Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

- State formation in ancient India is the process of emergence and consolidation of political entities with centralized authority and territorial boundaries in the Indian subcontinent.
- State formation is linked with the growth and spread of agriculture, trade, industry and urbanization, which created and appropriated surplus resources and enabled social differentiation and specialization.
- The stages of state formation in ancient India can be broadly divided into four phases: the Vedic period, the Mahajanapada period, the Mauryan period and the post-Mauryan period.

#### Vedic Period (c. 1500-600 BCE)

- The earliest evidence of state formation in ancient India comes from the Vedic texts, which reflect the socio-political and religious life of the Indo-Aryan people who migrated to the north-western part of the subcontinent from Central Asia.
- The Vedic society was organized into tribes (jana) and clans (vish), which were led by chiefs (raja) and elders (sabha and samiti). The chiefs performed sacrifices (yajna) to secure the favor of gods and ancestors, and also led their people in wars and raids against other tribes.
- The Vedic state was based on kinship, loyalty and personal charisma of the chief, rather than on territorial sovereignty and bureaucracy. The chief did not have absolute power, but was accountable to the assemblies of the people. The Vedic state was also not a unified entity, but a loose confederation of tribes and clans, which often competed and allied with each other.
- The Vedic state gradually expanded its territory and influence by conquering and assimilating the indigenous people, such as the Dasas and Dasyus, who were considered as enemies and inferiors. The Vedic state also developed trade and cultural contacts with other regions, such as the Indus Valley, Mesopotamia and Central Asia.
- The Vedic state underwent significant changes in the later part of the period, as the tribes and clans settled down in the fertile plains of the Ganga and Yamuna rivers, and adopted agriculture as the main occupation. The chief became more powerful and wealthy, and started to claim divine status and hereditary succession. The assemblies of the people lost their importance and became dominated by the priests (brahmins) and the warriors (kshatriyas), who formed the upper classes of the Vedic society. The lower classes consisted of the commoners (vaishyas) and the servants (shudras), who were assigned different occupations and duties according to their birth.
- The later Vedic state also witnessed the emergence of new political institutions, such as the janapada (territorial unit), the rajan (monarch), the sena (army), the danda (punishment), the kosha (treasury) and the amatya (minister). The later Vedic state also developed a more elaborate system of rituals, laws and customs, which were codified in the Vedas, the Brahmanas, the Aranyakas and the Upanishads.

#### Mahajanapada Period (c. 600-300 BCE)

- The Mahajanapada period marks the transition from the tribal polity of the Vedic period to the territorial polity of the Mauryan period. The term mahajanapada means "great realm" or "great people", and refers to the sixteen major political entities that emerged in the northern and central parts of the subcontinent during this period.
- The mahajanapadas were formed by the consolidation and expansion of the janapadas, which were the basic units of political organization in the later Vedic period. The mahajanapadas were characterized by a centralized administration, a standing army, a regular taxation system, a codified law and a defined territory.

Some of them were monarchies, such as Magadha, Kosala, Avanti and Gandhara, while others were republics, such as the Vajji, the Malla, the Shakya and the Kuru. Some of them followed the Vedic religion, while others adopted new religious movements, such as Buddhism and Jainism. Some of them were urbanized and prosperous, while others were rural and backward.

### Kingship in India

- Kingship is a form of political authority that is based on the recognition of a ruler as a sovereign over a territory and a people.
- Kingship in India has a long and diverse history, spanning from the ancient Vedic period to the modern constitutional monarchy.
- Kingship in India was influenced by various religious, cultural, and social factors, such as Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Islam, caste, kinship, and regional diversity.
- Some of the main features of kingship in India are:

  - The king was seen as a protector of the people, the land, and the dharma (cosmic order and moral duty).
  - The king was also seen as a representative of the gods, and often performed rituals and sacrifices to maintain their favor and legitimacy.
  - The king was expected to uphold justice, benevolence, and generosity, and to follow the advice of wise counselors and elders.
  - The king was supported by a network of officials, ministers, nobles, and vassals, who helped him administer his realm and collect taxes and tribute.
  - The king was challenged by various internal and external threats, such as rebellions, invasions, succession disputes, and rival kingdoms.
  - The king was celebrated and praised in various literary and artistic forms, such as epics, inscriptions, coins, and sculptures.

- Some of the examples of kingship in India are:

  - The Mauryan Empire (4th-2nd century BCE), founded by Chandragupta Maurya, was the first pan-Indian empire that unified most of the subcontinent under a centralized administration and a Buddhist patronage.
  - The Gupta Empire (4th-6th century CE), founded by Chandragupta I, was the second pan-Indian empire that ushered in a golden age of culture, science, and art, and a Hindu revivalism.
  - The Chola Empire (9th-13th century CE), founded by Vijayalaya, was a maritime empire that dominated the south and southeast Asia, and was known for its naval prowess, temple architecture, and trade networks.
  - The Delhi Sultanate (13th-16th century CE), founded by Qutb al-Din Aibak, was the first Islamic empire in India that ruled over most of the north and central India, and introduced Persian culture, architecture, and administration.
  - The Mughal Empire (16th-18th century CE), founded by Babur, was the third pan-Indian empire that blended Persian, Turkic, and Indian elements, and was renowned for its artistic, literary, and architectural achievements.
  - The British Raj (1858-1947 CE), established by Queen Victoria, was the colonial rule of India by the British Crown, that brought about political, economic, and social changes, and led to the rise of nationalism and independence movements.
  - The Republic of India (1947-present CE), established by the Indian Constitution, is the largest democracy in the world, that is governed by a parliamentary system, a federal structure, and a multi-party system.

### Administration

- Administration is the process of managing the affairs of an organization or a state by implementing policies, rules and regulations.
- Administration can be classified into two types: public administration and private administration.
- Public administration is the administration of public affairs by the government or its agencies. It involves the delivery of public services, the maintenance of law and order, the collection of taxes, the formulation and execution of public policies, etc.
- Private administration is the administration of private affairs by individuals, groups or organizations. It involves the management of business, finance, education, health, etc.
- Administration can also be classified into three levels: central, state and local.
- Central administration is the administration of the whole country by the central government. It includes the executive, legislative and judicial branches of the government, as well as various ministries, departments and commissions.
- State administration is the administration of a state or a province by the state government. It includes the governor, the chief minister, the state legislature, the state judiciary, as well as various departments and boards.

### Political Ideals in Ancient India

- Ancient India had a rich tradition of political thought and practice that spanned from the Vedic period to the medieval period.
- The political ideals of ancient India were based on the concepts of dharma, rita, varna, ashrama, rajadharma, and samjnana.
- Dharma was the cosmic law that governed the universe and the moral duty of every individual. It was the source of legitimacy and authority for the rulers and the subjects.
- Rita was the cosmic order that maintained the harmony and balance of the natural and social worlds. It was the principle of justice and righteousness that guided the actions of the kings and the people.
- Varna was the social division of the society into four classes: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers). It was based on the functional specialization and the spiritual quality of the individuals.
- Ashrama was the stage of life that prescribed the duties and responsibilities of the individuals according to their age and status. It consisted of four stages: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (retired), and Sannyasa (renunciant).
- Rajadharma was the duty of the king to protect and promote the welfare of the people and uphold the dharma and rita. It was the ideal of kingship that required the king to be virtuous, benevolent, wise, and courageous.
- Samjnana was the collective consciousness of the people and the national mind that represented the democratic spirit and ideals of the society. It was the deity that the king and the people had to respect and obey.

- The political ideals of ancient India were expressed and developed by various thinkers and texts, such as the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Dharmashastras, the Arthashastra, the Mahabharata, the Ramayana, and the Buddhist and Jain texts.
- The political ideals of ancient India influenced the formation and functioning of various political institutions and systems, such as the monarchy, the republics, the assemblies, the councils, the courts, the law, the diplomacy, and the warfare.
- The political ideals of ancient India also reflected the diversity and complexity of the society and the culture, as well as the challenges and changes that occurred over time. They were not static or uniform, but dynamic and adaptable.



### Conditions of the Welfare of Societies

### The Seven Limbs of the State

- The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) are a political concept given by Kautilya in his treatise Arthashastra, which deals with the art of governance and statecraft.
- The seven limbs are: King, Amatya (Bureaucrats), Janapada (Territory), Durga (Fort), Kosa (Treasure), Danda (Coercive Authority), and Mitra (Ally)   .
- The king is the supreme authority and the head of the state. He is responsible for the peace, justice, and stability of the state. He should be virtuous, wise, and benevolent .
- The amatya are the ministers and officials who assist the king in administration and policy-making. They should be loyal, competent, and efficient .
- The janapada is the territory or the land of the state. It should be fertile, prosperous, and well-populated. It should also be well-protected from external and internal threats .
- The durga is the fort or the capital of the state. It should be strategically located, well-fortified, and well-supplied. It should also be the center of culture, commerce, and learning .
- The kosa is the treasury or the wealth of the state. It should be sufficient, well-managed, and well-distributed. It should also be used for the welfare of the people and the defense of the state .
- The danda is the coercive authority or the military of the state. It should be strong, disciplined, and loyal. It should also be used for the protection of the state and the enforcement of the law  .
- The mitra is the ally or the friend of the state. It should be reliable, supportive, and cooperative. It should also be used for the mutual benefit and security of the state .

- These seven limbs are interdependent and interrelated. They should work in harmony and balance for the welfare and prosperity of the state  .



### Society in Ancient India

- The society in ancient India was based on the concept of **varna**, which was a four-fold division of people into four classes: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers). 
- The society was also organized into **ashramas**, which were four stages of life that each individual was expected to follow: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (retired), and Sannyasa (renunciant).
- The society was governed by the rules of **dharma**, which was the moral and religious duty of each person according to their varna and ashrama. Dharma also included the performance of **samskaras**, which were rites of passage that marked the important events in one's life, such as birth, initiation, marriage, and death.
- The society was structured around the institution of **family**, which was the basic unit of social life. The family was usually patriarchal, with the father or the eldest male as the head. The family was also patrilineal, with the inheritance and succession passing through the male line. The family was also extended, with several generations living together under one roof.
- The society had different norms and practices regarding **marriage**, which was considered a sacred and lifelong bond between a man and a woman. The marriage was usually arranged by the parents or elders, based on the compatibility of the horoscopes, caste, and social status of the bride and groom. The marriage was also monogamous, with polygamy being allowed only for the upper classes and under certain circumstances. The marriage was also endogamous, with inter-caste and inter-religious marriages being prohibited or discouraged.
- The society had a varied and changing position of **women**, who enjoyed different degrees of freedom and rights depending on the period, region, and class. In general, women were subordinate to men in the patriarchal and patrilineal society, and had to follow the norms of **pativrata**, which was the ideal of a faithful and devoted wife. Women were also expected to observe the customs of **sati**, which was the self-immolation of a widow on her husband's funeral pyre, and **purdah**, which was the seclusion and veiling of women from public view. However, women also had some opportunities for education, religious participation, artistic expression, and social service, especially in the early and medieval periods.



### Purusārtha

- Purusārtha is a Sanskrit term that means "the object or aim of human life" .
- It is a key concept in Hinduism that refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life .
- The four purusārthas are: dharma, artha, kāma, and mokṣa .
- Dharma means "duty, righteousness, or moral law" . It is the ethical and social obligation of a person according to their stage and role in life .
- Artha means "wealth, prosperity, or material success" . It is the pursuit of economic and worldly well-being in a lawful and ethical manner .
- Kāma means "desire, pleasure, or enjoyment" . It is the fulfillment of sensual and aesthetic desires in a moderate and lawful way .
- Mokṣa means "liberation, release, or emancipation" . It is the ultimate goal of human life that involves the realization of one's true nature and the liberation from the cycle of birth and death .
- The four purusārthas are not mutually exclusive, but rather interdependent and complementary . They provide a framework for living a balanced, meaningful, and fulfilling life at the deepest and most holistic level .
- The concept of purusārtha reflects the diversity and complexity of human aspirations and values in Hinduism . It also shows the importance of individual choice and responsibility in pursuing one's goals .



### Varnāshrama System

- The Varnāshrama system is a concept of social and spiritual organization in Hinduism that consists of four varnas (classes) and four ashramas (stages of life).
- The four varnas are:
  - Brahmanas: the priests, teachers, and intellectuals who perform religious rituals and study the Vedas.
  - Kshatriyas: the warriors, rulers, and administrators who protect the society and uphold justice.
  - Vaishyas: the merchants, farmers, and artisans who produce and trade goods and services.
  - Sudras: the laborers, servants, and workers who serve the other three varnas.
- The four ashramas are:
  - Brahmacharya: the student stage, in which one learns the scriptures and the duties of one's varna under a guru (teacher).
  - Grihastha: the householder stage, in which one marries, raises a family, and contributes to the society.
  - Vanaprastha: the forest dweller stage, in which one retires from worldly affairs and prepares for spiritual liberation.
  - Sannyasa: the renunciate stage, in which one renounces all attachments and seeks the highest truth.
- The Varnāshrama system is based on the idea that each person has a natural inclination and aptitude for a certain type of work and a certain stage of life, and that by following one's dharma (duty) according to one's varna and ashrama, one can achieve harmony, happiness, and moksha (liberation).
- The Varnāshrama system is also a way of categorizing the diversity and complexity of human society and providing a framework for co-operation and mutual support among different groups and individuals.
- The Varnāshrama system is not a rigid or fixed system, but a flexible and dynamic one that can adapt to changing times and circumstances. It is not based on birth or caste, but on one's qualities and actions. It is not a system of oppression or discrimination, but a system of respect and dignity.



### Āshrama or the Stages of Life

- Āshrama (Sanskrit: आश्रम) is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras.
- The four āshramas are: Brahmacharya (student), Gṛhastha (householder), Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller), and Sannyasa (renunciate)  .
- The Āshrama system is one facet of the Dharma concept in Hinduism  .
- The Āshrama system is believed to have been prevalent since the 5th century B.C.E. in Hindu society.
- The Āshrama system aims to guide the individual through four stages of spiritual development, each with its own duties, responsibilities, and goals  .

#### Brahmacharya: The Celibate Student

- Brahmacharya is a period of formal education lasting until around age 25, during which the student learns about the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, as well as the skills and values necessary for life.
- Brahmacharya is marked by chastity, devotion, and obedience to one’s teacher (guru) .
- Brahmacharya is considered the foundation of the other three stages, as it prepares the student for the moral and spiritual challenges of life .

#### Gṛhastha: The Householder

- Gṛhastha is the stage of the householder, requiring marriage, the begetting of children, sustaining one’s family and contributing to society.
- Gṛhastha is the longest and most important stage, as it involves fulfilling one’s duties (dharma) as a spouse, parent, and citizen .
- Gṛhastha is also the stage where one can enjoy the pleasures (kāma) and wealth (artha) of the world, within the limits of morality and law .

#### Vanaprastha: The Forest Dweller

- Vanaprastha is the stage of the forest dweller, or the retired hermit, which begins around the age of 50, or after one’s children are grown up and settled.
- Vanaprastha is a time of gradual withdrawal from the worldly attachments and responsibilities, and a shift of focus to spiritual pursuits and self-realization .
- Vanaprastha is often marked by a symbolic ritual of renunciation, such as giving up one’s possessions, shaving one’s head, or wearing saffron robes .
- Vanaprastha is a stage of transition between the active life of the householder and the detached life of the renunciate .

#### Sannyasa: The Renunciate

- Sannyasa is the stage of the renunciate, or the ascetic, who renounces all worldly ties and possessions, and lives a life of celibacy, austerity, and meditation.
- Sannyasa is the final and most difficult stage, as it involves giving up one’s identity, family, caste, and social status, and seeking liberation (moksha) from the cycle of birth and death (samsara) .
- Sannyasa is not obligatory for all Hindus, and only a few choose to take this radical step of renunciation .
- Sannyasa is considered the highest and most noble stage, as it represents the ultimate goal of Hindu spirituality .



### Marriage

Marriage is a social and legal institution that regulates the relationship between two individuals and their families. Marriage is considered to be a sacred bond in many cultures and religions, especially in India. Marriage in India has the following characteristics:

Depending on your religion, you may also need to follow the specific personal laws or opt for a civil marriage under the Special Marriage Act.

### Four-class Classification for the notes of the Module 1- Society State and Polity in India in the subject of INDIAN TRADITIONS, CULTURAL AND SOCIETY

- The four-class classification, also known as the varna system, is a social hierarchy that divides the Hindu society into four main categories: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers).
- The origin of the varna system is traced back to the Rig Veda, one of the oldest Hindu scriptures, where the four classes are said to have emerged from the body of the cosmic being Purusha: the Brahmins from his mouth, the Kshatriyas from his arms, the Vaishyas from his thighs, and the Shudras from his feet.
- The varna system is based on the concept of dharma, or duty, which assigns different roles and responsibilities to each class according to their nature and abilities. The Brahmins are expected to perform rituals, study the scriptures, and teach others. The Kshatriyas are expected to protect the society, uphold justice, and fight wars. The Vaishyas are expected to engage in trade, agriculture, and commerce. The Shudras are expected to serve the other three classes and perform menial tasks.
- The varna system is also linked to the concept of karma, or action, which determines the quality of one's life and rebirth. According to the Hindu belief in reincarnation, the soul transmigrates from one body to another after death, depending on the actions performed in the previous life. The varna system provides a framework for ethical conduct and spiritual progress, as one can attain a higher or lower status in the next life by following or violating one's dharma.
- The varna system has been criticized for creating social inequality, discrimination, and oppression, especially for the Shudras and the outcastes, who are considered to be outside the four-class system and are subjected to various forms of exclusion and stigma. The outcastes, also known as the untouchables or the Dalits, are those who perform the most polluting and degrading occupations, such as scavenging, leather work, and cremation. They are often denied access to education, public facilities, and religious places, and face violence and harassment from the upper classes.
- The varna system has also been challenged by various social movements, religious reformers, and political leaders, who have advocated for the abolition of caste discrimination and the promotion of social justice and human rights. Some of the prominent figures who have opposed the varna system are Buddha, Mahavira, Guru Nanak, Raja Ram Mohan Roy, Mahatma Gandhi, B.R. Ambedkar, and Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.

### Evolution of script and languages in India

- India has a rich and diverse linguistic history, with over 1,600 languages and dialects spoken in the subcontinent.

- The next major script in India is Brahmi, which emerged around the 3rd century BCE and is considered the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts. Brahmi was used to write various languages, including Prakrit, Sanskrit, Pali, and Tamil.
- Brahmi evolved into different regional variants over time, such as Kharosthi, Gupta, Nagari, Siddham, Sharada, Grantha, and many others. Some of these scripts are still in use today, while others have become extinct or obsolete .
- The modern Indian scripts are derived from Brahmi or its descendants, and can be classified into four main groups: Devanagari and its related scripts (such as Gujarati, Marathi, Nepali, etc.), Bengali-Assamese and its related scripts (such as Oriya, Manipuri, etc.), Southern scripts (such as Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, etc.), and Eastern scripts (such as Tibetan, Sinhala, etc.) .

The scripts also show the development of various styles, such as cursive, calligraphic, and ornamental .
- The linguistic history of India is not static, but dynamic and ongoing. There are still many languages and scripts that are endangered, undocumented, or hidden from the mainstream. There are also efforts to revive, preserve, and promote the linguistic heritage of India.



### Harappan Script

- Harappan script is the name given to the symbols used by the Indus Valley Civilization, which flourished in the northwest of India and Pakistan from about 2500 to 1900 BCE.
- Harappan script is also known as Indus script, as it was discovered during the excavation of Harappan city.
- Harappan script consists of about 400 signs, which are mostly pictographic or ideographic, representing objects, animals, plants, or abstract concepts .
- Harappan script has not been deciphered yet, as there are no bilingual inscriptions or known underlying language to compare it with .
- Harappan script is found on various types of artifacts, such as seals, pottery, copper tablets, tools, and ivory .
- Harappan script is usually written from right to left, sometimes in a boustrophedon style (alternating direction of lines), and occasionally in a spiral or circular pattern .
- Harappan script is considered to be a logo-syllabic writing system, where some symbols express ideas or words while others represent sounds.
- Harappan script is the oldest known script of India and one of the earliest writing systems in the world .
- Harappan script is a source of mystery and fascination for scholars and enthusiasts, who have proposed various theories and interpretations of its meaning and origin.



### Brahmi Script

- Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script.
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi .
- Brahmi script appeared as a fully developed script in the third century BCE and was used for writing various languages, such as Sanskrit, Prakrit, Pali, and others .
- Brahmi script is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations. For example, the Lalitavistara Sūtra lists 64 scripts, with Brahmi script starting the list.
- Brahmi script was already divided into regional variants at the time of the earliest surviving epigraphy around the third century BCE. Cursives of the Brahmi script began to diversify further from around the fifth century CE and continued to give rise to new scripts throughout the Middle Ages.
- Brahmi script is thought to have been modelled on the Aramaic or Phoenician alphabets, or possibly derived from the Indus or Harappa script .
- Brahmi script consists of 33 to 40 consonants and 9 to 16 vowels, depending on the variant . It is written from left to right and has no case distinction . It uses diacritical marks to indicate vowels and other features, such as aspiration, nasalization, and gemination .
- Brahmi script has a decimal system of numerals, with separate symbols for 1 to 9, 10 to 90, 100, and 1000 . It also has symbols for fractions and weights and measures .
- Brahmi script is considered a sacred script by some Hindu and Buddhist traditions, and is used for writing mantras and religious texts . It is also regarded as a source of artistic and cultural heritage by many communities in India and beyond .



### The Vedas

- The Vedas are the oldest and most authoritative scriptures of Hinduism, the religion of India. They are composed in an ancient form of Sanskrit, the language of classical Indian civilization. The word Veda means "knowledge" or "vision" and refers to the divine wisdom revealed to the ancient sages or rishis .
- The Vedas consist of four collections of hymns, rituals, mantras, and philosophical treatises, each known as a Veda. They are: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda, and the Atharvaveda . Each Veda has four sections: the Samhitas (the core texts), the Brahmanas (commentaries on the rituals), the Aranyakas (forest texts for ascetics), and the Upanishads (mystical and philosophical teachings) .
- The Vedas are considered to be of divine origin and eternal, as they are not attributed to any human author. They are believed to have been revealed to the rishis through a process of shruti (hearing) and smriti (remembering). The rishis then transmitted the Vedas orally to their disciples, who preserved them through memorization and recitation for generations  .
- The Vedas are the source of the dharma, the moral and religious law that governs the life of Hindus. They prescribe the duties, rites, and obligations of the four varnas (social classes) and the four ashramas (stages of life). They also contain the cosmogonic myths, the stories of gods and goddesses, the ethical principles, and the metaphysical doctrines of Hinduism  .
- The Vedas are also the foundation of the six schools of orthodox Hindu philosophy, which seek to interpret and systematize the teachings of the Vedas. They are: the Mimamsa (ritual exegesis), the Vedanta (end of the Vedas), the Nyaya (logic), the Vaisheshika (atomism), the Samkhya (enumeration), and the Yoga (discipline) .
- The Vedas are the most revered and influential texts in the history of Indian culture, literature, and spirituality. They have inspired countless poets, thinkers, saints, and reformers, and have shaped the identity and values of the Hindu civilization  .



### The Upanishads

- The Upanishads are ancient Hindu scriptures that explore the nature of reality, the self, and the supreme being.
- The word Upanishad means "to sit down closely", implying the intimate dialogue between a teacher and a student.
- The Upanishads are part of the Vedas, the sacred texts of Hinduism, but they are considered the end or the essence of the Vedas (Vedanta).
- There are more than 200 Upanishads, but only 13 are considered the principal or the classical ones. They are: Isa, Kena, Katha, Prasna, Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Chandogya, Brhadaranyaka, Svetasvatara, Kausitaki, and Maitri.
- The Upanishads date from the 8th to the 6th century BCE, but some of them may be later additions or interpolations.
- The Upanishads present a vision of an interconnected universe with a single, unifying principle behind the apparent diversity in the cosmos, called Brahman.
- The Upanishads also teach that Brahman resides in the Atman, the unchanging core of the human individual, and that the goal of life is to realize this identity and attain liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth (samsara).
- The Upanishads use various methods and metaphors to convey their teachings, such as stories, dialogues, analogies, paradoxes, and aphorisms.
- The Upanishads have influenced various schools of Hindu philosophy, such as Advaita (non-dualism), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism), and Dvaita (dualism), as well as other religious traditions, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and the New Age movement.



### The Ramayana

- The Ramayana is a Hindu epic about the life of the legendary Prince Rama of Kosala    .
- Rama is the virtuous, wise, and powerful son of King Dasaratha, who wants him to succeed him as king  .
- Rama marries the beautiful Sita, the princess of Mithila, after lifting and stringing the bow of Siva, a divine weapon  .
- Rama is exiled from his homeland for 14 years by his stepmother Kaikeyi, who wants her son Bharata to be king instead   .
- Rama, Sita, and his loyal brother Lakshmana go to the forest, where they encounter many sages, friends, and foes   .
- Ravana, the demon king of Lanka, abducts Sita with the help of his sister Surpanakha and his uncle Maricha   .
- Rama and Lakshmana search for Sita, aided by the monkey king Sugriva and his general Hanuman   .
- Rama and his allies build a bridge to Lanka and wage a war against Ravana and his army   .
- Rama kills Ravana with the help of Vibhishana, Ravana's brother who defects to Rama's side   .
- Rama rescues Sita, but doubts her chastity and asks her to prove her purity by undergoing a fire test   .
- Sita passes the test, but Rama still banishes her to the forest when he hears a rumor that his subjects doubt her fidelity   .
- Sita gives birth to twin sons, Lava and Kusha, in the hermitage of sage Valmiki, who teaches them the Ramayana   .
- Rama performs a horse sacrifice to assert his sovereignty, but the horse is captured by Lava and Kusha, who challenge Rama's army   .
- Rama recognizes his sons and reunites with Sita, who asks the earth to swallow her as a final proof of her innocence   .
- Rama returns to Ayodhya and rules as a righteous king, until he departs to his heavenly abode along with his brothers   .



### The Mahabharata

- The Mahabharata is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of ancient India, the other being the Ramayana .
- It is a long and complex narrative that tells the story of the struggle for sovereignty between two groups of cousins, the Kauravas and the Pandavas, who belong to the Bharata dynasty  .
- The main plot of the Mahabharata revolves around the Kurukshetra War, a fierce and bloody conflict that lasts for 18 days and involves various kingdoms, gods, sages, and supernatural beings .
- The Mahabharata also contains many sub-stories, legends, myths, and philosophical discourses that explore various aspects of Hinduism, such as dharma (moral law), karma (action and consequence), moksha (liberation), and bhakti (devotion)  .
- The Mahabharata is considered to be an important source of information on the development of Hinduism between 400 BCE and 200 CE, and is revered by Hindus as both a sacred text and a historical account.
- The Mahabharata is divided into 18 parvas (books) and has over 74,000 verses, making it one of the longest poems in the world  .
- The Mahabharata is traditionally attributed to the sage Vyasa, who is also a major character in the epic. However, it is widely accepted that the Mahabharata is the result of multiple authors and centuries of oral transmission and redaction  .
- The Mahabharata has influenced many aspects of Indian culture, such as literature, art, music, dance, theatre, and cinema. It has also inspired numerous adaptations, translations, and interpretations in various languages and media  .



### Puranas

- Puranas are a vast genre of Indian literature that deal with various topics, especially legends and traditional lore .
- Puranas are considered as one of the most ancient sources of Hinduism and are regarded as the fifth Veda.
- Puranas are composed in Sanskrit verse and date from the 4th century BCE to about 1000 CE.
- Puranas are written in a narrative style, similar to the epics Mahabharata and Ramayana.
- Puranas are divided into six categories, based on the main deity they glorify: Vishnu, Shiva, Brahma, Shakti, Agni, and Surya.
- Puranas contain various stories, myths, legends, genealogies, cosmology, rituals, festivals, temples, geography, and history of India .
- Puranas are also known for their symbolic and allegorical interpretations of the events and characters they describe.
- Puranas are not considered as authoritative or infallible by the orthodox schools of Hinduism, but are revered as sacred and inspirational by the devotees .
- Puranas are a rich source of cultural and religious information for the study of Indian traditions, society, and history.



### Buddhist and Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit are three languages that were used to write the religious and philosophical texts of Buddhism and Jainism in ancient India.
- Pali is a term that refers to an archaic or old form of Prakrit that combines several existing dialects. It was spoken in Magadha and South Bihar, and was the language of the earliest Buddhist works.
- Prakrit is a term that refers to any language other than the standard one, Sanskrit. It was spoken by the common people and was used to write various forms of literature, such as drama, poetry, epics, and inscriptions. It was also the language of the Jain scriptures, which were written in Ardhamagadhi, a Prakrit dialect .
- Sanskrit is the classical and literary language of India, derived from the Vedic language. It was used to write the Vedas, the Upanishads, the epics, the Puranas, and many other works of Hinduism. It was also used by some Buddhist and Jain scholars to write commentaries, treatises, and philosophical works.

- The Buddhist literature in Pali consists of three main collections: the Vinaya Pitaka, the Sutta Pitaka, and the Abhidhamma Pitaka. These are collectively known as the Tipitaka or the Pali Canon, and contain the teachings of the Buddha and his disciples  .
  - The Vinaya Pitaka contains the rules and regulations for the monastic order, as well as stories of the Buddha and his disciples.
  - The Sutta Pitaka contains the discourses and sermons of the Buddha and his disciples on various topics, such as ethics, meditation, wisdom, and cosmology. ### Kautilya’s Arthashastra

- Kautilya’s Arthashastra is an ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, economics, military strategy, and social organization.
- It is attributed to Kautilya, also known as Chanakya, Vishnugupta, or the Prime Minister of the Mauryan Empire under Chandragupta Maurya (c. 321-c.297 BCE).

- The Arthashastra is considered one of the earliest and most comprehensive works on political science, and it reflects the political, social, and cultural conditions of ancient India.
- The Arthashastra advocates a pragmatic and ruthless approach to statecraft, based on the principles of dharma (duty), artha (wealth), and kama (pleasure).

- The Arthashastra is a valuable source of information on the history, geography, economy, society, and culture of ancient India, as well as the relations between India and its neighbors.

### Sangama Literature

- Sangama literature is the oldest literature to have been composed in the Tamilakam, the ancient region of South India that corresponds to the modern states of Tamil Nadu and Kerala .
- The word Sangama means "assembly" or "academy" and refers to three legendary gatherings of Tamil poets and scholars that lasted for several centuries and produced a vast body of literary works  .
- The first Sangama was said to be held at Then Madurai, the second at Kapalapuram, and the third at Madurai. The exact dates and locations of these Sangamas are uncertain, but they are generally believed to span from the 3rd century BCE to the 3rd century CE .
- Sangama literature consists of poems, grammar, epics and Tirukkural, a collection of ethical maxims. The poems are divided into two main categories: akam (love) and puram (heroism). Akam poems deal with various aspects of love, such as courtship, separation, union, and infidelity. Puram poems deal with various aspects of heroism, such as war, valor, generosity, and praise of kings and their deeds  .
- Sangama literature is remarkable for its secular and realistic portrayal of human emotions, social life, and natural scenery. It also reflects the cultural diversity and richness of the Tamilakam, which was influenced by various religions, such as Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Greek and Roman mythology  .
- Sangama literature had fallen into oblivion for much of the second millennium of the common era, but was preserved by and rediscovered in the monasteries of Hinduism, particularly those related to Shaivism near Kumbakonam, by colonial-era scholars in the late nineteenth century. It is now regarded as one of the finest and oldest examples of classical Tamil literature and a valuable source of historical and cultural information .

## Module 3- Indian Religion, Philosophy, and Practices

- India is a diverse and pluralistic country with many religions and philosophies that have influenced its culture and society.
- The major religions in India are Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, Islam, and Christianity. There are also smaller groups of Jews, Zoroastrians, and Bahá'ís.
- Hinduism is the oldest and largest religion in India, with about 80% of the population identifying as Hindus. Hinduism is a complex and diverse tradition that encompasses many schools of thought, sects, and practices. Hinduism is based on the Vedas, the ancient scriptures that contain hymns, rituals, and philosophy. Hinduism believes in the concepts of karma, dharma, reincarnation, and moksha (liberation from the cycle of birth and death).
- Buddhism is a religion and philosophy that originated in India in the 6th century BCE by Siddhartha Gautama, who became known as the Buddha (the enlightened one). Buddhism teaches the Four Noble Truths, which are the reality of suffering, the cause of suffering, the cessation of suffering, and the path to the cessation of suffering. The path consists of the Eightfold Path, which are ethical and mental disciplines that lead to wisdom and compassion. Buddhism also believes in the concepts of karma, rebirth, and nirvana (the state of peace and freedom from suffering).
- Jainism is a religion and philosophy that originated in India in the 6th century BCE by Mahavira, who is considered the last of the 24 Tirthankaras (ford-makers or teachers). Jainism teaches the principle of ahimsa (non-violence) towards all living beings, and the practice of asceticism and self-control. Jainism also believes in the concepts of karma, reincarnation, and liberation, but rejects the authority of the Vedas and the existence of a supreme creator god.
- Sikhism is a religion and philosophy that originated in the Punjab region of India in the 15th century CE by Guru Nanak, who is the first of the 10 Gurus (spiritual teachers). Sikhism is based on the teachings of the Gurus, which are compiled in the Guru Granth Sahib, the holy scripture of the Sikhs. Sikhism teaches the belief in one God, who is formless and omnipresent, and the equality of all human beings. Sikhism also emphasizes the values of service, justice, and social responsibility.
- Islam is a religion and philosophy that originated in Arabia in the 7th century CE by Muhammad, who is regarded as the final prophet of God. Islam is based on the Quran, the holy book of the Muslims, and the Sunnah, the example and teachings of Muhammad. Islam teaches the belief in one God, who is the creator and sustainer of the universe, and the submission to his will. Islam also prescribes the five pillars of faith, which are the declaration of faith, prayer, charity, fasting, and pilgrimage.
- Christianity is a religion and philosophy that originated in Palestine in the 1st century CE by Jesus Christ, who is believed to be the son of God and the savior of humanity. Christianity is based on the Bible, the holy book of the Christians, which consists of the Old Testament and the New Testament. Christianity teaches the belief in one God, who is a trinity of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, and the salvation by grace through faith in Jesus Christ. Christianity also follows the teachings and commandments of Jesus Christ, such as love, forgiveness, and service.



### Pre-Vedic and Vedic Religion

- Pre-Vedic religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the people who lived in the Indus Valley or Harappan civilization, which flourished from about 2500 to 1500 BCE.
- Pre-Vedic religion was characterized by the worship of both male and female aspects of nature, such as trees, animals, rivers, mountains, and fertility symbols.
- Pre-Vedic religion also involved the use of seals, terracotta figurines, fire altars, and ritual baths, which suggest a complex and sophisticated culture.
- Vedic religion refers to the religion of the Indo-European-speaking peoples who entered northwestern India from Persia around 1500 BCE and composed the four Vedas, the oldest sacred texts of Hinduism.
- Vedic religion was based on the worship of many gods, such as Indra, Varuna, Agni, Surya, and Rudra, who represented natural forces and cosmic phenomena.
- Vedic religion also involved the performance of sacrifices, rituals, and hymns, which were meant to appease the gods and secure their blessings for prosperity, protection, and happiness.
- Vedic religion evolved over time and gave rise to various schools of thought, such as the Upanishads, the Brahmanas, the Aranyakas, and the Sutras, which explored the nature of reality, the self, and the supreme being.



### Buddhism

- Buddhism is an Indian religion or philosophy that developed from the teachings of the Buddha (Sanskrit: "Awakened One"), a teacher who lived in northern India between the mid-6th and mid-4th centuries BCE .
- Buddhism is also known as Buddha Dharma and Dharmavinaya, which means "doctrines and disciplines" in Sanskrit.
- The followers of Buddhism are called Buddhists, who referred to themselves as Sakyan-s or Sakyabhiksu in ancient India.
- The main goal of Buddhism is to attain nirvana, which is the cessation of suffering and the cycle of rebirth .
- Buddhism is based on the Four Noble Truths, which are:
  - The truth of suffering (dukkha): All conditioned existence is unsatisfactory and subject to pain, sorrow, and impermanence.
  - The truth of the origin of suffering (samudaya): The cause of suffering is craving (tanha) and ignorance (avidya) of the true nature of reality.
  - The truth of the cessation of suffering (nirodha): The end of suffering is possible by eliminating craving and ignorance.
  - The truth of the path to the cessation of suffering (magga): The way to end suffering is to follow the Noble Eightfold Path, which consists of right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration.
- Buddhism is divided into three main branches: Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana .
  - Theravada (the "Way of the Elders") is the oldest and most conservative form of Buddhism, which follows the Pali Canon, the earliest collection of Buddhist scriptures. Theravada emphasizes the individual attainment of nirvana through meditation and ethical conduct.
  - Mahayana (the "Great Vehicle") is the more expansive and diverse form of Buddhism, which accepts other scriptures and teachings besides the Pali Canon. Mahayana emphasizes the universal compassion and the bodhisattva ideal, which is the aspiration to attain buddhahood for the benefit of all sentient beings.
  - Vajrayana (the "Diamond Vehicle") is the esoteric and tantric form of Buddhism, which incorporates rituals, mantras, and visualizations as means of transforming the mind and body. Vajrayana claims to offer a faster and more direct path to enlightenment than the other branches.
- Buddhism has spread to various regions and cultures, such as Tibet, China, Japan, Southeast Asia, and the West, and has influenced art, literature, philosophy, and social movements .



### Jainism

- Jainism is an ancient Indian religion that teaches a path to spiritual purity and enlightenment through disciplined nonviolence (ahimsa) to all living creatures .
- Jainism is also a transtheistic religion, meaning that it does not affirm or deny the existence of a supreme being, but rather considers the universe to be eternal and self-sustaining.
- The name Jainism derives from the Sanskrit verb ji, "to conquer", which refers to the ascetic battle that Jain renunciants (monks and nuns) must fight against the passions and bodily senses to gain omniscience and purity of soul.
- The followers of Jainism are called Jains, and they believe that every soul (jiva) is potentially divine and can attain liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth (samsara) by following the teachings of the tirthankaras, or "ford-makers", who are the enlightened beings who have shown the way .
- The most recent and 24th tirthankara in this time cycle was Mahavira, who lived in the 6th century BCE and is considered the founder of Jainism as we know it today .
- The three main pillars of Jainism are ahimsa (non-violence), anekantavada (non-absolutism), and aparigraha (asceticism).
  - Ahimsa means avoiding harm to any living being, not only in action, but also in speech and thought. Jains practice strict vegetarianism, avoid killing insects, and wear masks or cloths over their mouths to prevent inhaling microorganisms .
  - Anekantavada means recognizing the multiplicity of perspectives and the relativity of truth. Jains avoid dogmatism and intolerance, and seek to understand different points of view through dialogue and debate .
  - Aparigraha means renouncing attachment to worldly possessions and pleasures, and leading a simple and contented life. Jains practice voluntary poverty, celibacy, fasting, and meditation, and donate to charitable causes .
- Jainism has two main sects: Digambara and Svetambara, which differ mainly in their monastic practices and doctrines. Digambara monks go naked, while Svetambara monks wear white robes. Digambara monks also do not accept that women can attain liberation, while Svetambara monks do .
- Jainism has a rich and diverse literature, consisting of scriptures, commentaries, stories, biographies, and philosophical works. Some of the most important texts are the Agamas, the canonical scriptures of Jainism, which contain the teachings of the tirthankaras and their disciples .
- Jainism has influenced and been influenced by other Indian religions, especially Hinduism and Buddhism. Jainism shares many concepts and practices with Hinduism, such as karma, dharma, and ahimsa, but rejects the authority of the Vedas, the caste system, and the worship of gods. Jainism also shares some similarities with Buddhism, such as the rejection of a creator god, the emphasis on nonviolence, and the goal of liberation, but differs in its view of the soul, the role of the tirthankaras, and the nature of reality .
- Jainism is a minority religion in India, with about 4.5 million adherents, or 0.4% of the population. Most Jains live in the states of Maharashtra, Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Karnataka. Jainism has also spread to other countries, such as the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Kenya, through migration and conversion .



### Six System Indian Philosophy

### Other Heterodox Sects

- Heterodox sects are those that do not accept the authority of the Vedas and the Brahmanical tradition.
- They emerged in the 6th century BCE as a reaction to the social, economic and religious changes in India.
- They challenged the caste system, the ritual sacrifices, the priestly domination and the metaphysical speculation of the orthodox schools.
- They offered alternative paths of salvation based on ethical conduct, rational inquiry and personal experience.
- Some of the heterodox sects are:

  - **Ajivika**: A fatalistic sect that believed in the doctrine of niyati (determinism) and denied the role of free will, karma and rebirth. They followed a strict ascetic lifestyle and practiced extreme forms of self-mortification. They were founded by Makkhali Gosala, a contemporary of Mahavira and Buddha .
  - **Unchedvadi**: A materialistic sect that rejected the existence of soul, God, afterlife and moral values. They advocated the pursuit of sensual pleasures and worldly happiness. They were also known as Lokayata or Charvaka, and their main exponent was Brihaspati.

  - **Sandehvadi**: An agnostic sect that suspended judgment on all metaphysical and religious questions and doubted the validity of knowledge and perception. They were also called Ajnana or Ahetuvada, and their chief proponent was Sanjaya Belatthiputta.
  - **Akriyavadi**: An amoralist sect that denied the existence of karma and its consequences, and the efficacy of action and morality. They were also called Akiriyavada or Antinomianism, and their leader was Pakudha Kaccayana.



### Bhakti Movement

- The Bhakti movement originated in South India in the 7th-8th centuries CE with the Alvars (devotees of Vishnu) and the Nayanars (devotees of Shiva) and spread to North India in the 12th-13th centuries CE with the Sant poets such as Ramananda, Kabir, Nanak, and Tulsidas.

### Sufi Movement

- Sufism is a mystical and ascetic form of Islam that emphasizes the direct experience of God through love, devotion, and self-purification.
- Sufism originated in the Middle East and Central Asia in the early centuries of Islam, and spread to India through the travels and teachings of various Sufi saints or sheikhs.

  - Suhrawardi order: Founded by Sheikh Shihabuddin Suhrawardi in the 12th century, it is a more orthodox and conservative Sufi order that follows the Sharia law strictly and emphasizes the role of reason and logic in the path of God. It also advocates the concept of wahdat al-wujud (unity of being), which holds that God is the only reality and everything else is His manifestation. Some of the famous Suhrawardi saints are Sheikh Bahauddin Zakariya, Sheikh Ruknuddin Razi, and Sheikh Hamiduddin Nagori.
  - Qadiri order: Founded by Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani in the 12th century, it is a Sufi order that focuses on the cultivation of moral and spiritual qualities, such as sincerity, humility, patience, and gratitude. Some of the famous Qadiri saints are Sheikh Nizamuddin Auliya, Sheikh Nasiruddin Chiragh Dehlavi, and Sheikh Abdul Haq Muhaddis Dehlavi.
  - Naqshbandi order: Founded by Sheikh Baha-ud-Din Naqshband in the 14th century, it is a Sufi order that stresses the importance of silent meditation (zikr-i-khafi) and the observance of the Sunnah (the practices of the Prophet Muhammad). It also teaches the concept of wahdat al-adyan (unity of religions), which holds that all religions are valid paths to God and should be respected. Some of the famous Naqshbandi saints are Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi, Sheikh Mujaddid Alf Sani, and Sheikh Shah Waliullah.

  - Seal carving: The Harappans carved distinctive seals, usually made of steatite, a soft stone, with various motifs and inscriptions. The seals were used to stamp clay on trade goods, to identify property, and possibly to serve as amulets or tokens of authority. The motifs on the seals include animals, such as bulls, elephants, rhinoceroses, tigers, and unicorns, as well as human and divine figures. The inscriptions are in a script that has not been deciphered yet.
  - Pottery: The Harappans produced a variety of pottery, both plain and painted, for domestic and ritual purposes. The pottery was made of fine clay, well-fired, and often decorated with geometric patterns, floral motifs, or animal figures. The pottery shapes include dishes, bowls, jars, vases, goblets, and spouted vessels.
  - Jewelry: The Harappans crafted jewelry from materials such as gold, silver, copper, bronze, ivory, shell, bone, and beads of stone, glass, or faience.   - Urban planning: The Harappans built well-planned cities, with a grid layout, fortified walls, and gateways. The cities had a citadel, where the public buildings and religious structures were located, and a lower town, where the residential and commercial areas were situated. ## Module 5- Cultural Heritage and Performing Arts

- Performing arts are forms of art that use the body, voice, or objects to convey artistic expression, such as music, dance, theatre, opera, mime, puppetry, circus arts, and performance art.
- Performing arts are often performed in front of an audience, but may also be performed for a specific purpose, such as ritual, education, or social change.

- Performing arts can also contribute to the safeguarding and transmission of cultural heritage, as they can help to preserve, document, and revitalize the oral traditions, languages, skills, and knowledge that are associated with them.

  - Wayang puppet theatre, a form of storytelling that uses flat leather puppets, which is widely practiced in Indonesia and other parts of Southeast Asia.
  - Tango, a social dance and musical genre that originated in the Rio de la Plata basin, which is a symbol of cultural identity and social integration for Argentina and Uruguay.

### Indian Architecture

- Indian architecture is rooted in the history, culture, and religion of India. It reflects the diversity and richness of Indian civilization and its interactions with other cultures over time.
- Indian architecture can be classified into several styles and traditions, such as Hindu temple architecture, Indo-Islamic architecture, South Indian architecture, Indo-Saracenic architecture, and modern architecture.
- Hindu temple architecture is characterized by the use of stone, brick, or wood to create elaborate structures with towers, domes, halls, and sanctuaries. The temples are often decorated with sculptures, carvings, and paintings depicting gods, goddesses, and mythological scenes. The temples are also aligned with the cardinal directions and follow the principles of vastu shastra, the ancient science of architecture.
- Indo-Islamic architecture is the result of the fusion of Islamic and Indian elements, especially during the medieval period. It includes the styles of Rajput architecture, Mughal architecture, and Deccan architecture. Rajput architecture is known for its forts, palaces, and temples with ornate facades, balconies, and arches. Mughal architecture is famous for its monuments, such as the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort, and the Jama Masjid, which feature domes, minarets, and geometric patterns. Deccan architecture is influenced by Persian and Turkish styles, and showcases the use of stucco, plaster, and glazed tiles.
- South Indian architecture is distinguished by its Dravidian style, which originated in the Tamil region. It features pyramid-shaped towers, called gopurams, that rise above the entrances of the temples. The temples are also surrounded by walls and courtyards, and contain pillared halls, called mandapas, and shrines, called vimanas. The temples are adorned with sculptures, paintings, and inscriptions that depict the history and legends of the region.
- Indo-Saracenic architecture is a hybrid style that emerged during the British colonial rule in India. It combines elements of Indian, Islamic, and Gothic architecture, and was used to construct public buildings, such as railway stations, museums, and universities. It is characterized by the use of domes, arches, spires, and turrets, and the incorporation of motifs, such as lotus, peacock, and elephant.
- Modern architecture in India is influenced by the global trends and movements, such as Art Deco, Bauhaus, and International Style. It also reflects the social and political changes that occurred after India's independence in 1947. Some of the prominent modern architects in India are Le Corbusier, who designed the city of Chandigarh, Charles Correa, who designed the Gandhi Smarak Sangrahalaya, and B.V. Doshi, who designed the Indian Institute of Management.



### Engineering and Architecture in Ancient India

- Engineering and architecture in ancient India was known as **Sthapatya-Kala**, which means the art of constructing something.
- Ancient Indian architects and engineers derived principles of construction and architecture based on experience, observation of natural phenomena and social and cultural aspects of India.
- Ancient India witnessed two urban revolutions: the first one in the **Harappan civilization** (2500-1900 BCE) and the second one in the **Mauryan and Kushan empires** (6th century BCE-3rd century CE).
- The Harappan civilization is known for its **urban planning**, **drainage systems**, **granaries**, **dockyards**, **seals**, **weights and measures** and **script**.
- The Mauryan and Kushan empires are known for their **stone pillars**, **stupas**, **rock-cut caves**, **sculptures** and **temples** .
- Ancient India also developed two distinct styles of **Hindu temple architecture**: the **Nagara style** in North India and the **Dravida style** in South India.
- The Nagara style features a **shikhara** (spire) over the **garbhagriha** (sanctum) and a **mandapa** (hall) in front of it.
- The Dravida style features a **vimana** (tower) over the **garbhagriha** and a **gopuram** (gateway) at the entrance.
- Some of the most remarkable examples of ancient Indian engineering and architecture are the **Kailasa temple** at Ellora, the **Brihadeshwara temple** at Thanjavur, the **Konark Sun temple**, the **Khajuraho temples**, the **Ajanta and Ellora caves**, the **Sanchi stupa**, the **Qutub Minar**, the **Iron pillar of Delhi** and the **Stepwells of Gujarat and Rajasthan** .

### Seals for the notes of the Module 5- Cultural Heritage and Performing Arts in the subject of INDIAN TRADITIONS, CULTURAL AND SOCIETY

- Seals are small objects that have an engraved design on one side and are used to make an impression on a soft material, such as clay or wax, to authenticate documents, seal containers, or indicate ownership.
- Seals have been used in various cultures and civilizations across the world, including India, where they have a long and rich history.
- Seals of ancient India give us useful information about the civilization of the Indus Valley, which flourished from about 2500 to 1900 BCE in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent. Seals were used in ancient times for trade, administration, and ritual purposes.
- The seals of the Indus Valley are mostly rectangular, circular, or cylindrical in shape, and are made of steatite, a soft stone that can be easily carved. They have an engraved design on one side, usually featuring an animal, a plant, or a geometric pattern, and a short inscription in an undeciphered script on the other side .
- The animals depicted on the Indus seals include buffaloes, rhinoceroses, elephants, tigers, bulls, and unicorns (that is, animals with a single horn). Some of these animals are native to the region, while others may have been imported from other lands. The animals are always shown in profile and sometimes standing alongside feeding troughs. The animals may represent the power, wealth, or status of the seal owners, or may have a symbolic or religious meaning .
- The plants depicted on the Indus seals include trees, such as the pipal tree, which is considered sacred in Hinduism and Buddhism, and flowers, such as the lotus, which is a symbol of purity and enlightenment. The plants may also have a symbolic or religious meaning, or may indicate the fertility and prosperity of the land .
- The geometric patterns depicted on the Indus seals include circles, squares, triangles, and swastikas. The swastika is an ancient symbol of good luck and auspiciousness in many cultures, including India. The geometric patterns may have a mathematical or astronomical significance, or may represent abstract concepts or deities .
- The inscriptions on the Indus seals are written in a script that has not been deciphered yet, despite many attempts by scholars and linguists. The script consists of about 400 signs, some of which resemble pictograms, while others are more abstract. The inscriptions are usually very short, consisting of one to five signs, and are read from right to left. The inscriptions may indicate the name, title, or profession of the seal owners, or may have a religious or magical function .
- The Indus seals were used to make impressions on clay tablets, sealings, or tags, which were attached to goods, documents, or containers. The impressions served as a mark of identity, authority, or ownership, and also as a protection against tampering or theft. The seals may have also been used as amulets or talismans, to ward off evil or bring good fortune .
- The Indus seals are a valuable source of information about the culture, society, economy, and religion of the Indus Valley civilization, which remains largely mysterious and enigmatic. The seals reflect the artistic skill, technological innovation, and cultural diversity of the people who made and used them. The seals also raise many questions and challenges for researchers who try to decipher their meaning and significance .



### Coins

Coins are a form of money that have been used in India since ancient times. Coins reflect the cultural, political, economic and religious aspects of Indian society. Coins can be classified into different periods based on their origin, design, metal and inscription.

- Prehistoric and Bronze Age: The earliest form of money in India was cowry shells, which were used as a medium of exchange in the Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1750 BCE). Cowry shells were also used in other parts of Asia and Africa. Some metal objects, such as copper rings, axes and knives, were also used as money in some regions of India.
- Iron Age and Early Historic Period: The first coins in India were punch-marked coins, which were made of silver and had symbols or marks punched on them. These coins were issued by various kingdoms and republics (janapadas) in northern India from the 6th century BCE to the 1st century CE. Some of the common symbols on these coins were sun, moon, elephant, bull, lion, tree, etc. These coins were influenced by the Persian and Greek coinage that circulated in the northwest of India.
- Classical Period: The classical period of Indian coinage saw the emergence of various dynasties and empires that issued coins in different metals, shapes, sizes and styles. Some of the prominent coin-issuing powers of this period were the Mauryans, the Indo-Greeks, the Sakas, the Kushans, the Guptas, the Pallavas, the Chalukyas, the Rashtrakutas, the Pandyas, the Cholas, etc. These coins had images of kings, gods, goddesses, animals, plants, etc. and inscriptions in various languages and scripts, such as Brahmi, Kharoshthi, Greek, Prakrit, Sanskrit, Tamil, etc. These coins also showed the influence of foreign cultures, such as Hellenistic, Roman, Iranian, etc.
- Medieval Period: The medieval period of Indian coinage witnessed the arrival of new invaders and rulers, such as the Arabs, the Turks, the Mongols, the Afghans, the Mughals, the Marathas, the Sikhs, etc. These rulers introduced new types of coins, such as the dirham, the dinar, the tanka, the rupee, the mohur, etc. These coins were made of gold, silver, copper, lead, etc. and had inscriptions in Arabic, Persian, Nagari, Gurmukhi, etc. These coins also displayed the religious and artistic preferences of the rulers, such as the Islamic kalima, the Hindu shlokas, the Sikh gurbani, the floral motifs, the calligraphy, etc.
- Colonial and Modern Period: The colonial and modern period of Indian coinage saw the domination of the European powers, such as the Portuguese, the Dutch, the French, the British, etc. These powers issued coins in their own names and currencies, such as the cruzado, the guilder, the livre, the pound, etc. These coins were made of gold, silver, copper, nickel, etc. and had inscriptions in English, Portuguese, Dutch, French, etc. These coins also bore the portraits of the European monarchs, the coats of arms, the dates, etc. After India gained independence in 1947, the Indian government issued coins in the name of the Republic of India, with the national emblem, the Ashoka lion capital, the denomination, the year, etc. These coins were made of various metals, such as stainless steel, aluminium, brass, etc. and had inscriptions in Hindi and English. These coins also featured various symbols of India's culture, heritage and diversity, such as the lotus, the banyan tree, the peacock, the rhinoceros, the lion, the tiger, etc.

### Puppetry

Puppetry is an ancient art form of storytelling that involves manipulating puppets to perform a narrative. Puppetry is a part of the cultural heritage and performing arts of India, as it reflects the social, religious, and historical aspects of the country. Puppetry in India can be classified into four main types, based on the material and technique of the puppets:

- **String puppets**: These are puppets that are controlled by strings attached to their limbs and head. The strings are manipulated by the puppeteer from above, who also narrates the story. The most popular form of string puppetry in India is Kathputli, which originated in Rajasthan. Kathputli puppets are made of wood and cloth, and depict characters from folk tales, epics, and legends. Kathputli shows are accompanied by music and songs. Other forms of string puppetry in India include Kundhei in Odisha, Gombeyatta in Karnataka, and Bommalattam in Tamil Nadu.
- **Shadow puppets**: These are flat puppets that are made of leather, paper, or cardboard, and are cut out in the shape of the characters. The puppets are held against a translucent screen, and a light source behind them creates their shadows on the screen. The puppeteer manipulates the puppets with rods or wires, and also provides the voice and sound effects. The most common form of shadow puppetry in India is Tholu Bommalata, which originated in Andhra Pradesh. Tholu Bommalata puppets are made of leather and are painted with bright colors. They depict scenes from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata. Other forms of shadow puppetry in India include Togalu Gombeyatta in Karnataka, Ravanachhaya in Odisha, and Chamadyache Bahulya in Maharashtra.
- **Rod puppets**: These are puppets that are controlled by rods attached to their limbs and head. The rods are held by the puppeteer from below, who also narrates the story. The puppets are usually made of wood, cloth, or clay, and are dressed in elaborate costumes and accessories. The most prominent form of rod puppetry in India is Putul Nautch, which originated in West Bengal. Putul Nautch puppets are made of wood and are carved with intricate details. They depict characters from the Puranas, the Ramayana, and the Mahabharata. Putul Nautch shows are accompanied by music and songs. Other forms of rod puppetry in India include Yampuri in Bihar, and Sakhi Kundhei in Odisha.
- **Glove puppets**: These are puppets that are worn on the hands of the puppeteer, who also narrates the story. The puppets are usually made of cloth, leather, or paper, and have a hollow head and a cloth body. The puppeteer inserts his fingers into the head and the arms of the puppet, and moves them to create expressions and gestures. The most popular form of glove puppetry in India is Pavakoothu, which originated in Kerala. Pavakoothu puppets are made of leather and are painted with natural colors. They depict scenes from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata. Pavakoothu shows are performed in temples and festivals, and are accompanied by music and songs. Other forms of glove puppetry in India include Chamadyache Bahulya in Maharashtra, and Kundhei Nach in Odisha.

### Music

Music is an integral part of Indian culture and society. It reflects the diversity and richness of India's heritage, traditions, religions, languages and regions. Music has been used for various purposes, such as expressing devotion, celebrating festivals, performing rituals, entertaining audiences, communicating emotions, and creating art. Music has also influenced and been influenced by other forms of art, such as dance, drama, poetry, and painting.

- Music in India is classified into two broad categories: classical and folk. Classical music is further divided into two main traditions: Hindustani (North Indian) and Carnatic (South Indian). Folk music is more regional and diverse, reflecting the local cultures, languages, and lifestyles of the people. Folk music includes genres such as bhajans (devotional songs), qawwalis (Sufi songs), bhangra (Punjabi dance music), lavani (Maharashtrian dance music), and baul (Bengali mystic songs).

- Music in India is also a form of social and cultural expression and identity. Music reflects the diversity and unity of India's people, who belong to different castes, classes, genders, ethnicities, and regions. Music also serves as a medium of communication and dialogue, as well as a source of resistance and protest, among various social and political groups and movements. For example, music has been used to promote nationalism, patriotism, and freedom during the colonial and post-colonial periods, to assert the rights and dignity of the oppressed and marginalized communities, such as the Dalits and the tribals, to challenge the norms and stereotypes of the dominant and mainstream cultures, such as the Bollywood and the pop music industries, and to raise awareness and action on various issues, such as environmentalism, feminism, and human rights.



### Theatre

- Theatre is one of the most ancient forms of art and expression in India, dating back to the Vedic period (1500-500 BCE).
- Theatre in India began as a narrative form, in which recitation, singing and dancing were integral elements. The encompassing of all forms of literature and fine arts into its physical presentation and the emphasis on narrative elements made the classical Indian drama theatrical right from the beginning.
- The earliest evidence of theatre in India is found in the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, which contain hymns, rituals and dialogues that were performed by priests and sages. The Natya Shastra, a treatise on dramaturgy attributed to Bharata Muni, is the most comprehensive and authoritative source of classical Indian theatre. It covers various aspects of theatre, such as the origin, purpose, structure, genres, styles, techniques, costumes, makeup, music, dance, acting, directing and criticism of theatre.
- The classical Indian theatre was mainly divided into two types: Sanskrit theatre and folk theatre. Sanskrit theatre was based on the literary works composed in Sanskrit, the ancient language of India. It followed the rules and conventions laid down by the Natya Shastra and was patronized by the royal courts and the elite classes. The most famous playwrights of Sanskrit theatre were Kalidasa, Bhasa, Shudraka, Vishakhadatta and Harsha. Some of the popular Sanskrit plays are Abhijnana Shakuntalam, Malavikagnimitram, Mrichchhakatika, Mudrarakshasa and Ratnavali.
- Folk theatre was based on the local languages, cultures and traditions of different regions and communities of India. It was more flexible, spontaneous and diverse than Sanskrit theatre and was performed by the common people for the common people. Folk theatre incorporated various elements of music, dance, puppetry, masks, acrobatics and magic. Some of the prominent forms of folk theatre are Jatra (Bengal), Tamasha (Maharashtra), Nautanki (Uttar Pradesh), Bhavai (Gujarat), Yakshagana (Karnataka), Koodiyattam (Kerala) and Therukoothu (Tamil Nadu).
- The modern theatre in India emerged during the colonial period, under the influence of the Western theatre brought by the British. It was mainly performed in English, Hindi and Hinglish (a mix of Hindi and English) and addressed the social, political and cultural issues of the contemporary times. Some of the pioneers of modern Indian theatre were Rabindranath Tagore, Bal Gandharva, Prithviraj Kapoor, Habib Tanvir, Vijay Tendulkar, Girish Karnad, Badal Sircar and Mahesh Dattani. Some of the notable modern Indian plays are Ghashiram Kotwal, Tughlaq, Hayavadana, Evam Indrajit and Dance Like a Man.
- Theatre in India is not only a form of entertainment, but also a medium of education, communication and social change. It reflects the diversity, richness and vitality of the Indian culture and society. It also showcases the creativity, innovation and experimentation of the Indian theatre artists. Theatre in India is a living and evolving tradition that continues to inspire and influence the audiences across the world.



### Drama in Indian Culture and Society

- Drama is one of the oldest and most popular forms of performing arts in India. It has a rich and diverse history that spans from ancient times to the present day.
- According to the Natyashastra, a classical treatise on drama attributed to Bharata Muni, drama was a divine gift to humans from the gods. The Natyashastra lays down the principles and techniques of drama, such as the types of plays, the structure of plot, the modes of expression, the styles of acting, the use of music, costumes, and stagecraft.
- Drama in India reflects the social, cultural, religious, and political realities of the people. It also serves as a medium of education, entertainment, and social criticism. Drama in India has been influenced by various regional, linguistic, and religious traditions, as well as by foreign influences, such as Persian, Greek, and British.
- Some of the famous traditional dramas or theatres of India are:

  - Sanskrit drama: The classical form of drama that flourished from the 2nd century BCE to the 10th century CE. Some of the renowned playwrights of Sanskrit drama are Kalidasa, Bhasa, Shudraka, Vishakhadatta, and Harsha. Some of the famous plays of Sanskrit drama are Abhijnanasakuntalam, Malavikagnimitram, Mrichchhakatika, Mudrarakshasa, and Ratnavali.
  - Folk theatre: The rural or village theatre that is performed during various social and religious occasions. Folk theatre is characterized by its use of local languages, dialects, songs, dances, masks, and props. Folk theatre is also influenced by the regional and ethnic cultures of the people. Some of the popular forms of folk theatre are Nautanki, Tamasha, Jatra, Bhavai, Yakshagana, and Bhand Pather.
  - Parsi theatre: The modern form of theatre that emerged in the late 19th century with the patronage of the Parsi community. Parsi theatre was influenced by the European and Urdu theatre and introduced elements such as realism, melodrama, comedy, and musicals. Parsi theatre also popularized the use of proscenium stages, curtains, lights, and scenery. Some of the famous playwrights and actors of Parsi theatre are Agha Hashr Kashmiri, Kaikhushru Kabraji, Sohrab Modi, and Prithviraj Kapoor.
  - Modern theatre: The contemporary form of theatre that developed after the Indian independence in 1947. Modern theatre is marked by its experimentation, innovation, and diversity of themes, styles, and forms. Modern theatre also reflects the social and political issues and movements of the post-colonial era. Some of the prominent playwrights and directors of modern theatre are Rabindranath Tagore, Mohan Rakesh, Vijay Tendulkar, Girish Karnad, Badal Sircar, and Satyadev Dubey.



### Painting

- Painting is one of the oldest and most diverse forms of artistic expression in India.
- Indian paintings can be broadly classified as murals, miniatures and paintings on cloth.
- Murals are large works executed on the walls of solid structures, such as temples, caves and palaces. They depict religious, mythological, historical or secular themes. Some of the famous examples of murals are the Ajanta Caves, the Kailashnath temple, the Brihadeshwara temple and the Mattancherry Palace.
- Miniatures are small-scale paintings done on perishable materials such as paper, cloth, palm leaf, wood or ivory. They are usually made for books, albums, manuscripts or court records. They reflect the styles and influences of different regions, dynasties and religions. Some of the prominent schools of miniature painting are the Pala, the Mughal, the Rajput, the Deccan and the Pahari.
- Paintings on cloth are also known as patachitra, which means picture on cloth. They are usually made of cotton or silk and painted with natural or synthetic dyes. They often depict stories from Hindu epics, folk tales, local deities or festivals. They are used for religious, educational or entertainment purposes. Some of the well-known forms of paintings on cloth are the Kalighat, the Madhubani, the Warli, the Kalamkari and the Thangka.
- Indian paintings have a long history and tradition that spans from the prehistoric times to the modern era. They reflect the cultural, social, religious and political diversity of the Indian subcontinent. They also show the influence of foreign invaders, traders and colonizers, such as the Greeks, the Persians, the Arabs, the Turks, the Mongols, the Europeans and the British.
- Indian paintings are a rich source of information and inspiration for the study of Indian traditions, culture and society. They reveal the values, beliefs, customs, practices, aesthetics, skills and creativity of the Indian people. They also express the emotions, thoughts, aspirations and challenges of the Indian artists and their patrons.



### Martial Arts Traditions

- Martial arts are systems of codified practices and traditions of training for combat. They are used for self-defense, physical fitness, mental and spiritual development, entertainment, and cultural preservation.
- India has an ancient and diverse tradition of martial arts. Some of the most prominent and popular styles are:
  - **Kalaripayattu**: Originated in Kerala in the 13th century. It is considered the oldest martial art in the world and the mother of all martial arts. It involves strikes, kicks, grappling, weaponry, and healing methods. It is based on the concept of vital points (marmas) on the human body.   
  - **Gatka**: Originated in Punjab and is associated with the Sikh community. It is a weapon-based martial art that uses swords, daggers, sticks, and shields. It was developed as a means of self-defense against the Mughal invaders. It is also a sport and a cultural performance.  
  - **Lathi Khela**: Originated in North India and is popular in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, and West Bengal. It is a martial art that uses a bamboo staff (lathi) as a weapon. It also involves kicks, punches, and wrestling techniques. It is practiced by farmers and villagers as a form of exercise and entertainment.  
  - **Silambam**: Originated in Tamil Nadu and is one of the oldest martial arts in India. It is a stick-fighting martial art that also uses other weapons such as knives, swords, spears, and fire pots. It is influenced by Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism. It is known for its fast and fluid movements.   
  - **Thang-ta and Sarit Sarak**: Originated in Manipur and are the two main aspects of the martial art known as Huyen Langlon. Thang-ta means sword and spear and involves the use of various weapons and unarmed combat. Sarit Sarak means fighting while escaping and involves the use of acrobatic and evasive maneuvers. It is influenced by the Meitei culture and religion.   
  - **Mardaani Khel**: Originated in Maharashtra and is a weapon-based martial art that uses swords, spears, axes, and daggers. It was developed by the Maratha warriors as a means of guerrilla warfare against the Mughal and British armies. It is also known as Maratha fencing or dandpatta.  
  - **Inbuan Wrestling**: Originated in Mizoram and is a form of folk wrestling that is popular among the Mizo people. It is a test of strength, skill, and endurance. It involves grappling, throwing, and pinning the opponent. It has strict rules that prohibit kicking, punching, biting, and holding the opponent's hair or clothes.  
  - **Kushti**: Originated in North India and is one of the most popular martial arts in India. It is a form of traditional wrestling that combines Malla Yuddha, a local sport, and Varzesh-e-Bastani, a Persian sport. It involves grappling, striking, and submission techniques. It is practiced in a clay or dirt pit called an akhara.   
- These martial arts reflect the rich and diverse cultural heritage and performing arts of India. They are not only methods of fighting, but also ways of living, learning, and expressing. They have influenced and been influenced by other forms of art, such as dance, music, theater, and literature. They have also contributed to the development of other martial arts around the world, such as karate and kung-fu.



### Fairs and Festivals

- Fairs and festivals are an integral part of the Indian culture and heritage. They reflect the diversity, creativity, and spirituality of the people of India.
- Fairs and festivals are celebrated for various reasons, such as religious, seasonal, historical, cultural, artistic, or social. They provide an opportunity for people to express their faith, joy, gratitude, and solidarity.
- Some of the common features of fairs and festivals are:

  - They involve rituals, prayers, offerings, processions, and ceremonies that are specific to the occasion and the region.
  - They showcase the traditional arts, crafts, music, dance, and cuisine of the local communities.
  - They attract large crowds of devotees, pilgrims, tourists, and traders who participate in the festivities and exchange goods and services.
  - They promote social harmony, cultural exchange, and national integration by bringing together people from different backgrounds, regions, and religions.

- Some of the major fairs and festivals of India are:

  - Diwali: The festival of lights that marks the victory of good over evil and the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya after defeating Ravana. It is celebrated by lighting lamps, candles, and fireworks, exchanging gifts and sweets, and worshipping Goddess Lakshmi and Lord Ganesha.
  - Holi: The festival of colors that celebrates the arrival of spring and the triumph of love over hatred. It is celebrated by throwing colored powder and water on each other, playing with water guns and balloons, and singing and dancing to the tunes of folk songs.
  - Dussehra: The festival that commemorates the victory of Lord Rama over Ravana and the victory of Goddess Durga over Mahishasura. It is celebrated by burning effigies of Ravana, Kumbhakarna, and Meghnad, watching Ramleela plays, and worshipping Goddess Durga and her nine forms.
  - Pongal: The harvest festival of South India that thanks the Sun God, the Earth, and the cattle for their bounty and blessings. It is celebrated by cooking and offering rice, milk, and jaggery to the Sun God, decorating the houses and courtyards with rangoli and flowers, and enjoying bullfights and boat races.
  - Ganesh Chaturthi: The festival that celebrates the birthday of Lord Ganesha, the elephant-headed god of wisdom and success. It is celebrated by installing and worshipping idols of Ganesha, chanting his names and mantras, and immersing the idols in water bodies after the festivities.
  - Eid-ul-Fitr: The festival that marks the end of the holy month of Ramadan, during which Muslims fast from dawn to dusk. It is celebrated by offering prayers at mosques, visiting relatives and friends, exchanging greetings and gifts, and feasting on delicacies like biryani, sevai, and sheer khurma.
  - Christmas: The festival that celebrates the birth of Jesus Christ, the son of God and the savior of mankind. It is celebrated by decorating the houses and churches with lights, stars, and Christmas trees, singing carols and hymns, exchanging gifts and cards, and attending the midnight mass.
  - Rath Yatra: The festival that celebrates the annual journey of Lord Jagannath, his brother Balabhadra, and his sister Subhadra from their temple in Puri to their aunt's temple in Gundicha. It is celebrated by pulling the huge and colorful chariots of the deities along the streets, accompanied by thousands of devotees, musicians, and dancers.
  - Vasant Panchami: The festival that welcomes the spring season and worships Goddess Saraswati, the goddess of learning, music, and arts. It is celebrated by wearing yellow clothes, offering yellow flowers and sweets to the goddess, and flying kites in the sky.
  - Ram Navami: The festival that celebrates the birth of Lord Rama, the seventh incarnation of Lord Vishnu and the hero of the epic Ramayana. It is celebrated by reciting the Ramayana, performing rituals and puja, and taking out processions of Lord Rama, Sita, Lakshmana, and Hanuman.

### Indian's Cultural Contribution to the World

- India is one of the oldest and most diverse civilizations in the world, with a rich and varied heritage of art, literature, philosophy, religion, science, and technology.
- India has given many gifts to the world that have influenced and enriched various aspects of human culture and civilization. Some of these contributions are:

  - India gave the world its first university, Takshashila University, which was established around the 6th century BCE and attracted scholars from all over Asia. It offered courses in various disciplines such as grammar, logic, medicine, astronomy, and mathematics .
  - India gave the world the numeral zero, which is essential for mathematics and science. The concept of zero and the decimal system were developed by Indian mathematicians such as Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, and Bhaskara in the 5th to 7th centuries CE .
  - India gave the world the game of chess, which originated from an ancient Indian game called chaturanga, meaning "four divisions". The game was played on an 8x8 board with pieces representing the king, the elephant, the horse, the chariot, and the foot soldiers .
  - India gave the world the use and invention of buttons, which were first used in India as early as 2000 BCE. Buttons were made of seashells, metal, ivory, wood, and bone, and were used to fasten clothes and ornaments .
  - India gave the world the shampoo, which originated from the Sanskrit word champu, meaning "to massage". Shampoo was a herbal mixture of soapberries, gooseberries, and other ingredients that was used to cleanse and condition the hair and scalp .
  - India gave the world the cure for leprosy, which was a chronic and disfiguring disease that affected the skin and nerves. The ancient Indian physician Sushruta described the symptoms and treatment of leprosy in his medical treatise Sushruta Samhita, which was written around the 6th century BCE. He prescribed the use of oil, alkaline, and surgical procedures to treat the disease .
  - India gave the world the game of snakes and ladders, which was inspired from an Indian game called mokshapat, meaning "the ladder to salvation". The game was a moral lesson that taught the effects of good and bad deeds on one's destiny. The ladders represented virtues and the snakes represented vices .
  - India gave the world the concept of non-violence, which was advocated by the Indian spiritual leader Mahatma Gandhi, who led the Indian independence movement against the British colonial rule. Gandhi's philosophy of non-violence, or ahimsa, inspired many other movements and leaders around the world, such as Martin Luther King Jr., Nelson Mandela, and Dalai Lama .
  - India gave the world the practice of yoga, which is a system of physical, mental, and spiritual exercises that originated in India around the 5th century BCE. Yoga is derived from the Sanskrit word yuj, meaning "to unite". Yoga aims to harmonize the body, mind, and soul, and to achieve a state of liberation or enlightenment .
  - India gave the world the art of meditation, which is a technique of focusing one's attention and awareness on a single object, thought, or sensation. Meditation is a part of many Indian traditions, such as Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. Meditation has many benefits for the health and well-being of the practitioner, such as reducing stress, improving concentration, and enhancing creativity .
  - India gave the world the diversity of languages, which reflects the richness and complexity of Indian culture and history. India has 22 official languages and hundreds of regional and local languages, belonging to four major language families: Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Austroasiatic, and Tibeto-Burman. India is also the birthplace of four major world religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism, which have influenced the culture and philosophy of many other regions and countries .

### Indian Cinema

- Indian cinema is one of the largest and most diverse film industries in the world, producing films in various languages, genres, and formats.
- Indian cinema has a rich history that dates back to the early 20th century, when the first silent films were made by pioneers like Dadasaheb Phalke, Hiralal Sen, and Raja Harishchandra.
- Indian cinema has been influenced by various cultural, social, political, and economic factors, such as the colonial rule, the independence movement, the partition, the nation-building, the regional identities, the globalization, and the digital revolution.
- Indian cinema has also influenced and been influenced by other film industries and cultures, such as Hollywood, European cinema, Asian cinema, and Middle Eastern cinema.
- Indian cinema has produced some of the most acclaimed and popular filmmakers, actors, and films in the world, such as Satyajit Ray, Raj Kapoor, Guru Dutt, Amitabh Bachchan, A.R. Rahman, Mira Nair, Aamir Khan, and Slumdog Millionaire.
- Indian cinema has also contributed to the development and promotion of various forms of performing arts, such as music, dance, theatre, and puppetry, which are often integrated into the films as songs, dances, and drama.
- Indian cinema can be broadly classified into two categories: mainstream cinema and parallel cinema. Mainstream cinema refers to the commercial and popular films that cater to the mass audience and follow certain conventions and formulas. Parallel cinema refers to the alternative and independent films that challenge the mainstream norms and explore various themes and styles.
- Indian cinema can also be divided into various regional cinemas, such as Hindi cinema (also known as Bollywood), Tamil cinema (also known as Kollywood), Telugu cinema (also known as Tollywood), Malayalam cinema (also known as Mollywood), Bengali cinema, Marathi cinema, Kannada cinema, and others. Each regional cinema has its own distinctive features, traditions, and audiences.
- Indian cinema has faced various challenges and opportunities in the contemporary era, such as the rise of multiplexes, the emergence of new media platforms, the competition from foreign films, the censorship and piracy issues, the social and environmental impacts, and the recognition and awards from international festivals and institutions.
