

# Indian Traditions, Culture and Society

India is a diverse and ancient country with a rich and varied heritage. India has been influenced by many different civilizations, religions, languages, and cultures over the centuries, resulting in a unique and complex mosaic of traditions and customs. Some of the main aspects of Indian culture and society are:

- **Arts and Language**: India has a long and rich history of artistic expression, ranging from classical music and dance, to painting and sculpture, to literature and poetry. India is also home to many languages, with 22 official languages and hundreds of regional dialects. The most widely spoken languages are Hindi, English, Bengali, Telugu, Marathi, Tamil, Urdu, and Gujarati. The ancient Indian classical language, Sanskrit, was one of the earliest Indo-European languages to be developed and is still used for religious and scholarly purposes.

- **Philosophy and Religion**: India is the birthplace of four major world religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. Hinduism is the oldest and most dominant religion in India, with about 80% of the population identifying as Hindus. Hinduism is a diverse and polytheistic faith that encompasses various sects, beliefs, practices, and rituals. Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism are offshoots of Hinduism that emerged in the 6th and 5th centuries BCE, and emphasize non-violence, compassion, and liberation from the cycle of rebirth. India also has a significant Muslim population, about 14%, as well as smaller communities of Christians, Zoroastrians, Jews, and others. India is known for its religious tolerance and pluralism, as well as its philosophical contributions to ethics, logic, metaphysics, and epistemology.

- **Food and Drink**: The cuisine of India varies considerably across its various regions and ethnic communities. However, some common elements include the use of spices, herbs, grains, legumes, vegetables, fruits, dairy products, and meat (except for beef, which is forbidden for Hindus and Sikhs). Indian food is typically cooked in oil or ghee (clarified butter) and served with flatbreads, such as roti, naan, or paratha, or rice. Some of the most popular dishes include dal (lentil soup), curry (spicy stew), biryani (rice with meat or vegetables), tandoori (marinated and roasted meat or vegetables), samosa (fried pastry with savory filling), chaat (snack food), and sweets, such as laddu, jalebi, and halwa. India also has a rich and diverse tradition of beverages, such as tea, coffee, lassi (yogurt drink), nimbu pani (lemonade), and alcoholic drinks, such as toddy, feni, and bhang.

- **Society and Culture**: India is a multi-ethnic, multi-lingual, and multi-religious society, with a population of about 1.3 billion people. India is also a federal parliamentary republic, with a central government and 28 states and 8 union territories, each with its own legislature and administration. India has a complex and hierarchical social structure, based on the ancient system of varna (caste) and jati (sub-caste), which divides people into four main categories: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers). There is also a fifth category of people, known as Dalits or untouchables, who are considered outside the caste system and face discrimination and oppression. However, the caste system is gradually losing its relevance and influence in modern India, especially in urban areas and among the educated and affluent classes. India also has a strong and vibrant civil society, with many non-governmental organizations, social movements, media outlets, and cultural institutions that promote democracy, human rights, social justice, and diversity   .

# State in Ancient India

- The state in ancient India was a social organization with political power that ensured peace, order and happiness.
- The origin of the state in ancient India is not clear, but there are three main theories: social contract theory, divine origin theory and organic theory.
- Social contract theory: This theory holds that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among the people who gave up some of their rights and freedoms to a ruler or a group of rulers in exchange for protection and welfare.
- Divine origin theory: This theory holds that the state was created by God or gods who appointed a king or a dynasty to rule over the people as their representatives or agents.
- Organic theory: This theory holds that the state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the growth and development of human society, which needed a central authority to regulate its affairs and maintain harmony.
- The types of states in ancient India were loosely categorized into monarchy, republic and oligarchy.
- Monarchy: This was the most prevalent type of state in ancient India, where the king was the supreme ruler who derived his power from his lineage, charisma, military prowess or divine sanction. The king was assisted by ministers, provincial governors, village councils, town councils and trade guilds, who were all subordinate to him.
- Republic: This was a type of state where the people or their representatives had a say in the governance and administration of the state. The republics were usually formed by clans, tribes or groups of people who shared a common ancestry, territory or occupation. The republics had assemblies or councils where the members discussed and decided on matters of common interest.
- Oligarchy: This was a type of state where the power was concentrated in the hands of a few people or a class of people who dominated the rest of the society. The oligarchs were usually wealthy landowners, merchants or warriors who controlled the resources and the military of the state. The oligarchs often exploited the masses and oppressed the weaker sections of the society.

# Evolutionary Theory

- Evolutionary theory is a perspective that explains the origin and development of human societies and cultures based on the principles of biological evolution.
- Evolutionary theory assumes that human societies and cultures are the products of natural selection, adaptation, and inheritance, and that they change over time in response to environmental and historical factors.
- Evolutionary theory also implies that human societies and cultures can be classified into different stages or types according to their level of complexity, organization, and technology.
- Evolutionary theory was influenced by the works of Charles Darwin, Herbert Spencer, Lewis Henry Morgan, and Edward Burnett Tylor, among others, who applied the concepts of evolution, survival of the fittest, and social progress to the study of human history and culture.
- Evolutionary theory was dominant in the 19th and early 20th centuries, but was criticized and challenged by later anthropologists and sociologists, who pointed out its ethnocentric, deterministic, and teleological biases, and its lack of empirical evidence and historical specificity.
- Evolutionary theory was revived and modified in the second half of the 20th century by scholars such as Julian Steward, Leslie White, Marshall Sahlins, and Elman Service, who developed new approaches such as cultural ecology, neoevolutionism, and cultural materialism, which emphasized the role of environment, technology, and economy in shaping human societies and cultures.
- Evolutionary theory is still relevant and influential in the 21st century, as it provides a framework for understanding the diversity and complexity of human societies and cultures, and for addressing the challenges and opportunities of globalization, development, and sustainability.

# Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

- State formation is the process of emergence and consolidation of political authority and institutions in a given territory.
- State formation in ancient India was influenced by various factors, such as geography, ecology, culture, religion, economy, and warfare.
- According to some scholars, state formation in ancient India can be divided into four stages: the Vedic period, the Mahajanapada period, the Mauryan period, and the post-Mauryan period.

## The Vedic Period

- The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) is the earliest phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the arrival and settlement of the Indo-Aryan tribes in the northwestern and northern regions of the subcontinent.
- The Vedic society was organized into tribes (jana) and clans (vish), which were led by chiefs (raja) and elders (sabha and samiti). The chiefs performed religious and military functions and received tributes from their subjects.
- The Vedic states were mainly pastoral and nomadic, relying on cattle rearing and agriculture for their livelihood. They also engaged in trade and warfare with other tribes and indigenous people.
- The Vedic states were not centralized or territorial, but rather based on kinship and loyalty. They did not have a fixed capital, a standing army, a bureaucracy, or a codified law. They followed the customs and traditions of their ancestors, as recorded in the Vedas and other sacred texts.

## The Mahajanapada Period

- The Mahajanapada period (c. 600-300 BCE) is the second phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the emergence and expansion of sixteen large and powerful states (mahajanapadas) in the northern and central regions of the subcontinent.
- The Mahajanapada states were more urbanized, commercialized, and militarized than the Vedic states. They had permanent capitals, professional armies, administrative officials, and legal codes. They also minted coins, built roads, and developed art and literature.
- The Mahajanapada states were mainly agrarian and dependent on the surplus production of the peasants. They also engaged in trade and diplomacy with other states and foreign powers, such as Persia and Greece.
- The Mahajanapada states were diverse and heterogeneous in their political, social, and religious systems. Some were monarchies, some were oligarchies, and some were republics. Some followed the Vedic religion, some followed heterodox sects, such as Buddhism and Jainism, and some followed local cults and practices.

## The Mauryan Period

- The Mauryan period (c. 321-185 BCE) is the third phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the establishment and decline of the first pan-Indian empire under the Mauryan dynasty.
- The Mauryan empire was founded by Chandragupta Maurya, who defeated the Nanda dynasty and the Greek invaders. He was succeeded by his son Bindusara and his grandson Ashoka, who expanded and consolidated the empire to its greatest extent.
- The Mauryan empire was the most centralized, organized, and bureaucratic state in ancient India. It had a capital at Pataliputra, a large and well-trained army, a complex administration, and a uniform law. It also had a network of spies, emissaries, and governors, who maintained the imperial control and communication.
- The Mauryan empire was also the most diverse and tolerant state in ancient India. It embraced and patronized various religions, cultures, and peoples, such as Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, Greeks, and Iranians. It also promoted welfare, education, and morality, as evident from the edicts of Ashoka.

## The Post-Mauryan Period

- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185-300 CE) is the fourth and final phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the fragmentation and decentralization of the Mauryan empire and the rise and fall of various regional and foreign powers.
- The post-Mauryan period witnessed the emergence of several indigenous dynasties, such as the Sungas, the Kanvas, the Satavahanas, the Kushanas, the Guptas, and the Vakatakas, who ruled over different parts of the subcontinent. They were often in conflict and competition with each other, as well as with the external invaders, such as the Sakas, the Parthians, and the Huns.

# Political Ideals in Ancient India

- Ancient India had a rich and diverse political culture that spanned from the Vedic period (c. 1500-500 BCE) to the medieval period (c. 1200-1700 CE).
- The political ideals of ancient India were based on various sources, such as the Vedas, the Upanishads, the epics, the Dharmashastras, the Arthashastra, the Buddhist and Jain texts, and the inscriptions and edicts of various rulers.
- Some of the common political ideals that emerged in ancient India were:
  - Dharma: The concept of dharma, or moral duty, was the foundation of political legitimacy and social order in ancient India. Dharma was seen as the cosmic law that governed the universe and the human society. Dharma also defined the roles and responsibilities of different classes and groups in the society, such as the varnas (castes), the ashramas (stages of life), and the jatis (sub-castes). Dharma also prescribed the ethical conduct and the duties of the rulers and the subjects. The rulers were expected to uphold and protect dharma by following the principles of rajadharma, or the dharma of kingship, which included justice, benevolence, protection, welfare, and sacrifice. The subjects were expected to obey and support the rulers who followed dharma, and to resist and overthrow the rulers who violated dharma.
  - Rta: The concept of rta, or cosmic order, was closely related to dharma. Rta was the principle that maintained the harmony and balance of the natural and the social world. Rta was also the source of truth, law, and justice. The rulers and the subjects had to follow rta to ensure the prosperity and stability of the society. Rta also implied the participation and consultation of the people in the political affairs. The Rig Veda, the oldest of the Vedas, mentions the concept of samjnana, or the collective consciousness of the people, as a deity and a source of political authority. The Rig Veda also mentions the assemblies of the people, such as the sabha and the samiti, where the people could voice their opinions and elect their leaders.
  - Artha: The concept of artha, or material wealth, was another important political ideal in ancient India. Artha was seen as the means to achieve the four goals of human life, namely dharma, artha, kama (pleasure), and moksha (liberation). Artha also referred to the science of politics, or the art of governance, that aimed at the acquisition and maintenance of power and wealth. The most influential treatise on artha was the Arthashastra, attributed to Kautilya, the minister of the Mauryan emperor Chandragupta Maurya (c. 321-297 BCE). The Arthashastra advocated a pragmatic and ruthless approach to politics, based on the principles of niti (policy), shakti (power), and yoga (skill). The Arthashastra also prescribed the methods of diplomacy, espionage, warfare, taxation, administration, and law and order, to ensure the security and prosperity of the state.
  - Ahimsa: The concept of ahimsa, or non-violence, was another prominent political ideal in ancient India, especially among the heterodox traditions of Buddhism and Jainism. Ahimsa was seen as the highest virtue and the supreme dharma, that transcended the distinctions of caste, creed, and nationality. Ahimsa also implied compassion, tolerance, and respect for all living beings. The proponents of ahimsa advocated the renunciation of violence, war, and animal sacrifice, and the promotion of peace, harmony, and welfare. Some of the rulers who embraced ahimsa were Ashoka (c. 268-232 BCE), the Mauryan emperor who converted to Buddhism after witnessing the horrors of the Kalinga war, and Harshavardhana (c. 606-647 CE), the king of Thanesar and Kannauj, who patronized both Buddhism and Jainism.

# The Seven Limbs of the State

- The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) are a political concept given by Kautilya in his treatise Arthashastra, which deals with the art of governance and statecraft.
- According to Kautilya, the seven limbs of the state are: 
  - King: The king is the supreme authority and the head of the state. He is responsible for the peace, justice and stability of the state. He should be virtuous, wise, courageous and benevolent. He should also be well-versed in the four sciences of politics, economics, ethics and warfare.
  - Amatya: The amatya are the bureaucrats or ministers who assist the king in the administration of the state. They should be loyal, efficient, honest and competent. They should also be well-trained in the six branches of knowledge: law, diplomacy, military, finance, agriculture and trade.
  - Janapada: The janapada is the territory or population of the state. It should be fertile, prosperous, well-defended and loyal to the king. It should also be diverse and harmonious, with different castes, classes, religions and occupations.
  - Durga: The durga is the fort or the capital of the state. It should be strategically located, well-fortified and well-supplied. It should also be the center of culture, education and commerce.
  - Kosa: The kosa is the treasury or the wealth of the state. It should be sufficient, well-managed and well-spent. It should also be derived from fair and legitimate sources, such as taxes, trade, mines and spoils of war.
  - Danda: The danda is the coercive authority or the military of the state. It should be strong, disciplined and loyal. It should also be well-equipped, well-trained and well-led.
  - Mitra: The mitra is the ally or the friend of the state. It should be reliable, supportive and beneficial. It should also be chosen wisely, based on the six-fold policy of peace, war, neutrality, alliance, march and division.
- Kautilya believed that the seven limbs of the state are interdependent and interrelated, and that the success or failure of the state depends on the balance and harmony of these limbs. He also provided detailed guidelines and advice on how to maintain, improve and protect each limb of the state.



# Society in Ancient India

- Society in ancient India was based on the concept of **varna**, which was a four-fold division of people into four classes: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers). 
- The varna system was not rigid or hereditary, but based on one's qualities, actions, and duties. It also allowed for mobility and intermixing among the different classes.  
- Another aspect of society in ancient India was the **ashrama**, which was a four-stage division of life for each individual: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (retiree), and Sannyasa (renunciant). 
- The ashrama system was meant to guide one's spiritual and moral development, as well as one's social and economic responsibilities. It also provided a balance between the worldly and the spiritual pursuits.

- The Vedas are composed in Vedic Sanskrit, the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the oldest scriptures of Hinduism.
- The Vedas are divided into four collections: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda.
- The Rigveda is the oldest and most important of the Vedas, containing 1,028 hymns to various gods and natural phenomena.
- The Yajurveda contains the formulas and rituals for performing sacrifices and ceremonies.
- The Samaveda consists of melodies and chants for the priests who sing during the sacrifices.
- The Atharvaveda contains spells, charms, and magical incantations for various purposes.
- The Vedas also have six supplementary texts called the Vedangas, which deal with grammar, phonetics, astronomy, ritual, etymology, and metrics.
- The Vedas are considered to be revealed by the gods to the sages, who transmitted them orally to their disciples for generations.
- The Vedas are the source of authority for the Hindu dharma, or the eternal order of life.
- The Vedas have regulated the social, legal, domestic and religious customs of Hindus up to the present day.
- The Vedas are also the basis of many philosophical and spiritual traditions within Hinduism, such as the Upanishads, the Vedanta, and the Yoga.

# The Upanishads

- The Upanishads are a collection of 108 texts that form the final part of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism.
- The Upanishads explore the nature of the self (Atman) and its relation to the ultimate reality (Brahman), which is the source and essence of everything.
- The Upanishads teach that the self is not the body, mind, or senses, but a pure and eternal consciousness that transcends the physical world.
- The Upanishads also teach that Brahman is not a personal god, but a universal and impersonal principle that pervades all existence and manifests as various gods and beings.
- The Upanishads emphasize the importance of knowledge (jnana) over ritual (karma) as the means to attain liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth (samsara).
- The Upanishads contain various methods of inquiry, meditation, and ethical conduct to help the seeker realize the identity of the self and Brahman, and experience the bliss and peace of liberation.
- The Upanishads are the foundation of the philosophical school of Vedanta, which has influenced many other traditions such as Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and Neo-Vedanta.

The Ramayana is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of ancient India, along with the Mahabharata. The Ramayana consists of seven books and 24,000 verses. # The Mahabharata

The Mahabharata is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of ancient India, along with the Ramayana. It is a long and complex narrative that covers the history, culture, religion, and philosophy of the Indian subcontinent. It is also a story of a dynastic struggle for the throne of Hastinapura, a kingdom ruled by the Kuru clan.

The main plot of the Mahabharata can be summarized as follows:

- The Kuru clan is divided into two branches: the Kauravas, led by Dhritarashtra, the blind and eldest son of the previous king; and the Pandavas, led by Pandu, the younger brother of Dhritarashtra. Dhritarashtra has 100 sons, while Pandu has five sons, born from his two wives Kunti and Madri, with the help of various gods.
- Pandu dies after being cursed by a sage for killing a deer while hunting. His sons, known as the Pandavas, are raised by Kunti along with the Kauravas in Hastinapura. The Pandavas are Yudhishthira, the eldest and the most virtuous; Bhima, the strongest and the most loyal; Arjuna, the most skilled archer and warrior; Nakula and Sahadeva, the twins and the youngest.
- The Kauravas, especially the eldest Duryodhana, are jealous and hostile towards the Pandavas, and try to harm them in various ways. They plot to kill them by burning them alive in a house of lac, but the Pandavas escape with the help of their cousin Vidura and their uncle Krishna, who is an incarnation of the god Vishnu.
- The Pandavas go into exile and disguise themselves as Brahmins. They travel across India and have many adventures and encounters with various sages, kings, demons, and gods. They also participate in a swayamvara, a contest for the hand of a princess, where Arjuna wins the beautiful Draupadi as his wife. However, due to a misunderstanding, Draupadi becomes the common wife of all the five Pandavas, as per the custom of polyandry among the Panchalas, her father's kingdom.
- The Pandavas return to Hastinapura and claim their share of the kingdom. Dhritarashtra reluctantly agrees to give them half of the land, known as Khandavaprastha, which is barren and infested with snakes. However, with the help of Krishna, the Pandavas transform it into a prosperous and beautiful city, called Indraprastha. They also perform the Rajasuya sacrifice, a ritual that establishes their supremacy over other kings.
- Duryodhana, who is humiliated and enraged by the success and glory of the Pandavas, invites them to a game of dice, where he cheats with the help of his uncle Shakuni, a master of gambling. He wins everything from the Pandavas, including their wealth, kingdom, and Draupadi. He then orders his brother Dushasana to drag Draupadi to the court and disrobe her in front of everyone. However, Krishna miraculously saves her honor by providing her with endless garments. Draupadi vows to not tie her hair until she washes it with the blood of Dushasana and Duryodhana.
- The Pandavas are exiled for 13 years, with the condition that they must spend the last year in disguise, and if they are recognized, they must repeat the exile. They again wander across India and have more adventures and encounters. During this time, Arjuna obtains divine weapons from various gods, including Shiva, Indra, and Varuna. He also marries Subhadra, the sister of Krishna, and begets a son named Abhimanyu. The other Pandavas also have other wives and sons during their exile.
- After the 13 years are over, the Pandavas ask for their kingdom back, but Duryodhana refuses to give them even a needlepoint of land. A war becomes inevitable, and both sides gather their allies and armies. The war is fought on the plain of Kurukshetra, near Delhi, and lasts for 18 days. Krishna becomes the charioteer and advisor of Arjuna, and delivers the Bhagavad Gita, a sacred scripture that contains the essence of Hindu philosophy and ethics, before the war begins.

# Puranas

- Puranas are a vast genre of Indian literature that deal with various topics, especially legends and traditional lore .
- Puranas are considered as one of the most ancient and important sources of Hindu culture, religion, history, and cosmology .
- Puranas are classified as Smriti (remembered) texts, as opposed to Shruti (heard) texts like the Vedas .
- Puranas are composed in Sanskrit verse, mostly in the form of narrative couplets, similar to the epics Mahabharata and Ramayana .
- Puranas are divided into two categories: Mahapuranas (major Puranas) and Upapuranas (minor Puranas) .
- There are 18 Mahapuranas, each focusing on a different aspect or manifestation of the Supreme Being, such as Vishnu, Shiva, or Shakti .
- There are also 18 Upapuranas, which are usually shorter and less authoritative than the Mahapuranas, and deal with various topics such as sects, regions, festivals, or sacred places .
- Puranas are not considered as authoritative or binding as the Vedas, but they are widely read and revered by the Hindus for their spiritual and moral guidance, as well as their cultural and historical information .
- Puranas are also known for their symbolic and allegorical interpretations of the events and characters, which often have multiple layers of meaning and significance .
- Puranas are dynamic and evolving texts, which reflect the changes and developments in the Hindu society and thought over the centuries .



# Buddhist and Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit are three languages that were used to write the religious and philosophical texts of Buddhism and Jainism in ancient India.
- Pali is a term that refers to an archaic or old form of Prakrit that combines several existing dialects. It was spoken in Magadha and South Bihar, and was the language of the earliest Buddhist works.
- Prakrit is a term that refers to any language other than the standard one, Sanskrit. It was spoken by the common people and used for various literary genres such as drama, poetry, epics and inscriptions. It was also the language of the Jain scriptures, which were written in Ardhamagadhi, a Prakrit dialect .
- Sanskrit is the classical and literary language of India, derived from the Vedic language. It was used for writing the later Buddhist and Jain texts, as well as the Hindu scriptures, epics, and scientific works.

## Buddhist Literature

- The Buddhist literature can be divided into two main categories: the canonical literature and the non-canonical literature.
- The canonical literature consists of the Tripitaka or the Three Baskets, which are the collections of the teachings of the Buddha and his disciples. They are:
  - The Vinaya Pitaka, which contains the rules and regulations for the monastic order.
  - The Sutta Pitaka, which contains the discourses and dialogues of the Buddha and his followers on various topics such as ethics, meditation, philosophy, and cosmology.
  - The Abhidhamma Pitaka, which contains the systematic and analytical exposition of the Buddhist doctrine and psychology.
- The canonical literature was originally transmitted orally, and was written down in Pali in the first century BCE in Sri Lanka. Later, it was translated into various languages such as Sanskrit, Chinese, Tibetan, and others  .
- The non-canonical literature consists of the works that were written after the canonical literature, and are not considered as authoritative by all the Buddhist schools. They include:
  - The commentaries and sub-commentaries on the canonical texts, which explain and interpret the teachings of the Buddha and his disciples. They were written in Pali, Sanskrit, and other languages by various scholars and monks.
  - The Jataka tales, which are stories of the previous lives of the Buddha and his associates, illustrating the moral and spiritual lessons of the Buddhist path. They were written in Pali and other languages, and are popular among the lay followers of Buddhism.
  - The Mahayana sutras, which are the scriptures of the Mahayana or the Great Vehicle school of Buddhism, which emerged around the first century CE. They emphasize the concepts of bodhisattva, emptiness, compassion, and skillful means. They were written in Sanskrit and other languages, and are revered by the Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions of Buddhism  .

# Kautilya's Arthashastra

- Kautilya's Arthashastra is an ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, economics, military strategy, and social organization .
- It is attributed to Kautilya, also known as Chanakya, Vishnugupta, or the Machiavelli of India, who was a scholar at Taxila University and the prime minister of the Mauryan Empire under Chandragupta Maurya  .
- The Arthashastra was written around the 4th century BCE, but was lost for centuries until it was rediscovered by Rudrapatna Shamasastry in 1905 CE .
- The Arthashastra consists of 15 books, 150 chapters, and 180 topics, covering various aspects of governance, administration, diplomacy, law, trade, agriculture, mining, taxation, welfare, defense, espionage, and war  .
- The Arthashastra is considered a masterpiece of political realism, pragmatism, and utilitarianism, as it advocates for the use of any means necessary to achieve the welfare and security of the state, without regard for ethical or moral considerations  .
- The Arthashastra also shows compassion for the poor, the slaves, and the women, and provides guidelines for the protection of the environment, the preservation of culture, and the promotion of education and science  .
- The Arthashastra is relevant for the modern world, as it offers insights into the principles and practices of statecraft, economics, and diplomacy, and provides a comprehensive and systematic analysis of the various factors that influence the success and failure of a state  .

- **Vyasa**: He is the legendary sage and compiler of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. He is also the author of the Mahabharata, another epic that depicts the Kurukshetra war and the moral dilemmas of the Pandavas and the Kauravas. He is also said to have written the Puranas, the Bhagavad Gita, the Brahma Sutras and many other texts.

# Telugu Literature

# Kannada Literature

- Kannada literature is one of the oldest and richest in India. It has a history of more than 1500 years and has produced many eminent writers, poets, and scholars.
- Kannada literature can be broadly divided into three periods: classical, medieval, and modern.
- The classical period (9th to 14th century) is marked by the emergence of the champu style, which combines prose and verse, and the development of various genres such as kavya (poetry), prabandha (narrative), and shastra (treatise). Some of the notable works of this period are Pampa's Adipurana and Vikramarjuna Vijaya, Ranna's Gadayuddha and Ajitanatha Purana, and Janna's Yashodhara Charite.
- The medieval period (14th to 18th century) is characterized by the growth of bhakti (devotional) literature, which expressed the religious and social sentiments of the masses. The vachana (sayings) and kirtana (songs) of the Veerashaiva saints, such as Basavanna, Akka Mahadevi, and Allama Prabhu, and the dasara pada (songs) of the Haridasa saints, such as Purandara Dasa and Kanaka Dasa, are the major examples of this genre. The medieval period also witnessed the rise of the navya (new) style, which introduced innovations in form and content, and the emergence of the dalita (oppressed) literature, which voiced the concerns of the marginalized sections of the society. Some of the prominent works of this period are Kumara Vyasa's Bharata, Lakshmisha's Jaimini Bharata, Sarvajna's Tripadi, and Ratnakaravarni's Bharatesha Vaibhava.
- The modern period (19th century onwards) is marked by the influence of Western literature and the social reform movements. The novel, the short story, the essay, and the drama became the dominant forms of expression. The modern period also saw the development of various literary movements, such as navodaya (renaissance), navya (modernist), bandaya (protest), dalita (oppressed), and spandana (responsive). Some of the notable works of this period are B.M. Srikantaiah's Kannada Sahitya Charitre, Kuvempu's Shri Ramayana Darshanam, Masti Venkatesha Iyengar's Chikaveera Rajendra, Shivarama Karanth's Chomana Dudi, U.R. # Sangama Literature

- Sangama literature is the oldest literature to have been composed in the Tamilakam, the ancient region of South India that corresponds to the modern states of Tamil Nadu and Kerala .
- The word Sangama means "college" or "assembly" and refers to the three literary academies that were established at different periods and places in Tamilakam to foster and patronize Tamil poetry .
- The first Sangama was at Then Madurai (Southern Madurai), the second at Kapalapuram (City of the Goddess), and the third at Madurai (the present-day city in Tamil Nadu). The duration and dates of these Sangamas are uncertain and disputed, but some scholars suggest that they spanned from the 3rd century BCE to the 3rd century CE .
- The Sangama literature consists of poems, grammar, epics and Tirukkural, a collection of ethical aphorisms. The poems are divided into two main categories: akam (love) and puram (heroism) .
- The akam poems deal with various aspects of love, such as courtship, separation, union, and infidelity. They are set in different landscapes or tinai, each of which symbolizes a particular mood or emotion. The five tinai are kurinji (mountainous region), mullai (forest), marutam (agricultural land), neytal (coastal area), and palai (desert) .
- The puram poems deal with the praise of kings, their wars, victories, generosity, and virtues. They also describe the life and culture of the ancient Tamils, such as their customs, festivals, arts, crafts, and trade. The puram poems are classified into ten types, such as patirruppattu (decads of lineage), porunararruppadai (guides to the poets), and perumparrapadal (great songs of war) .
- The Sangama literature is considered to be the source of authentic information about the ancient Tamil society, history, and culture. It also showcases the richness and diversity of the Tamil language and literature, and its influence on other languages and literatures of India and Southeast Asia  .

# Persian and Urdu

- Persian and Urdu are two languages that have influenced the literary and cultural traditions of India, especially in the northern and central regions.
- Persian was the official language of the Mughal Empire, which ruled over most of India from the 16th to the 18th centuries. Persian was also the language of court, administration, literature, and art in many regional kingdoms and sultanates that emerged after the decline of the Mughals.
- Urdu is a language that developed from the interaction of Persian, Arabic, and local Indian languages, such as Hindi, Punjabi, and Sindhi. Urdu is written in a modified form of the Arabic script, while Hindi is written in the Devanagari script. Urdu and Hindi are mutually intelligible in their spoken forms, but differ in their literary and religious vocabularies.
- Persian and Urdu have contributed to the richness and diversity of Indian literature, culture, tradition, and practices in various ways, such as:

  - Persian and Urdu poets, such as Amir Khusrau, Mirza Ghalib, Allama Iqbal, and Faiz Ahmed Faiz, have composed works of poetry that express the themes of love, mysticism, nationalism, and social justice. Their poetry has inspired generations of Indian writers and artists, and has been translated into many Indian and foreign languages.
  - Persian and Urdu have also influenced the genres and forms of Indian literature, such as the ghazal, the qawwali, the masnavi, the dastan, and the rekhta. These are forms of lyrical, musical, narrative, and hybrid expressions that combine elements of poetry, prose, and oral storytelling.

# Pre-Vedic and Vedic Religion

- Pre-Vedic religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the people who lived in the Indus Valley or Harappan civilization, which flourished from about 2500 to 1500 BCE.
- Vedic religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the Indo-Aryan peoples who entered northwestern India from Persia around 1500 BCE and composed the four Vedas, the sacred texts of Hinduism .

## Pre-Vedic Religion

- The Pre-Vedic religion was based on the worship of natural forces and fertility symbols, such as the Mother Goddess, the bull, the pipal tree, and the swastika.
- The Pre-Vedic religion also involved ritual bathing, fire altars, animal sacrifice, and the use of seals and amulets for protection and identification.
- The Pre-Vedic religion did not have a priestly class, a caste system, or a concept of reincarnation.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was influenced by the cultures of Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Central Asia, and may have had some common elements with the ancient Iranian religion of Zoroastrianism.

## Vedic Religion

- The Vedic religion was based on the worship of many gods, such as Indra, Varuna, Agni, Surya, and Rudra, who represented different aspects of nature, such as thunder, rain, fire, sun, and storm .
- The Vedic religion also involved hymns, prayers, rituals, sacrifices, and offerings to the gods, performed by a priestly class of Brahmins, who had access to the Vedas and the knowledge of the sacred language of Sanskrit .
- The Vedic religion developed a caste system, dividing the society into four main groups: Brahmins (priests), Kshatriyas (warriors), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers) .
- The Vedic religion also introduced the concept of reincarnation, the cycle of birth and death, which was determined by one's karma, the moral law of cause and effect .
- The Vedic religion was influenced by the cultures of the Indus Valley, the Dravidians, and the aboriginal tribes of India, and may have had some common elements with the ancient Greek and Roman religions  .



# Buddhism

Buddhism is an Indian religion or philosophy that developed from the teachings of the Buddha (Sanskrit: "Awakened One"), a teacher who lived in northern India between the mid-6th and mid-4th centuries BCE . Buddhism is one of the major world religions, with over 500 million followers across Asia and beyond.

Some of the main features of Buddhism are:

- Buddhism is based on the Four Noble Truths, which are: the truth of suffering (dukkha), the truth of the cause of suffering (samudaya), the truth of the end of suffering (nirhodha), and the truth of the path that leads to the end of suffering (magga) .
- Buddhism teaches the Noble Eightfold Path, which is a set of ethical and mental guidelines for achieving nirvana, the ultimate goal of liberation from the cycle of rebirth (samsara) .

Theravada is the oldest and most conservative form of Buddhism, which emphasizes the individual's effort to attain enlightenment. Mahayana is the largest and most diverse form of Buddhism, which emphasizes the compassion and wisdom of bodhisattvas, beings who postpone their own nirvana to help others. Vajrayana is the most esoteric and ritualistic form of Buddhism, which uses tantric practices and symbols to achieve a rapid transformation of the mind and body .
- Buddhism has a rich and varied tradition of scriptures, literature, art, architecture, philosophy, ethics, meditation, and social action. # Jainism

Jainism is one of the oldest religions of India, with a history of more than 2500 years. It is based on the teachings of 24 spiritual leaders called tirthankaras, who attained enlightenment and liberation from the cycle of rebirth. The most recent tirthankara was Mahavira, who lived in the 6th century BCE.

Some of the main features of Jainism are:

- It teaches a path of spiritual purity and enlightenment through disciplined nonviolence (ahimsa) to all living beings, including animals and plants. Ahimsa is the highest ethical principle in Jainism and the basis of its moral code.
- It believes in the existence of an eternal soul (jiva) in every living being, which can be liberated from the bondage of karma (the accumulated effects of actions) by following the three jewels of right knowledge, right faith, and right conduct. Jainism rejects the idea of a supreme creator, ruler, or judge of the universe, and considers the soul to be self-sufficient and responsible for its own destiny.
- It advocates a non-absolutist (anekantavada) approach to reality, which acknowledges the multiplicity of perspectives and the relativity of truth. Jainism encourages dialogue and tolerance among different viewpoints and respects the diversity of life forms and beliefs.
- It emphasizes a simple and ascetic (aparigraha) lifestyle, which involves renouncing worldly attachments, possessions, and pleasures, and practicing self-restraint, meditation, and compassion. Jainism also prescribes a strict vegetarian diet and various rules of conduct to minimize harm to the environment and other beings.

Jainism has a rich and diverse literature, which includes scriptures, commentaries, biographies, stories, and poems. Some of the most important texts are the Agamas, the canonical scriptures of the Jains, which contain the teachings of the tirthankaras and their disciples. Other texts include the Kalpa Sutra, which narrates the lives of the tirthankaras, the Tattvartha Sutra, which summarizes the main doctrines of Jainism, and the Bhagavati Sutra, which contains dialogues and stories on various topics.

Jainism has two main sects, the Digambaras and the Svetambaras, which differ in some aspects of doctrine, practice, and tradition. The Digambaras, meaning "sky-clad", believe that the tirthankaras and the monks should be naked, as a symbol of detachment and purity. They also believe that women cannot attain liberation in this life and need to be reborn as men. The Svetambaras, meaning "white-clad", believe that the tirthankaras and the monks can wear white clothes, as a mark of modesty and dignity. They also believe that women can attain liberation in this life and have equal spiritual potential as men.

Jainism has a significant influence on the culture, art, architecture, and philosophy of India and other regions. It has contributed to the development of nonviolence, vegetarianism, environmentalism, and human rights movements. It has also inspired many prominent figures, such as Mahatma Gandhi, Rabindranath Tagore, and Albert Einstein. Today, Jainism has about 4 to 5 million followers, mostly in India, but also in other parts of the world.



# Six System Indian Philosophy

The six systems of Indian philosophy are Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva Mimamsa and Vedanta. They are also known as shad-darshana, meaning six views or perspectives. Each system has its own founder, texts, concepts and methods of inquiry. They represent different aspects of the intellectual and spiritual traditions of Hinduism.

## Nyaya
- Nyaya is the school of logic and epistemology, founded by Akshapada Gautama in the 2nd century BCE.
- Nyaya means rule or method, and it aims to provide a rational and systematic way of acquiring valid knowledge and avoiding error.
- Nyaya accepts four sources of knowledge: perception, inference, comparison and testimony.
- Nyaya also develops a theory of categories, causation, argumentation, fallacies, syllogism and debate.
- Nyaya is often paired with Vaisheshika, as they share many concepts and principles.

## Vaisheshika
- Vaisheshika is the school of atomism and naturalism, founded by Kanada Kashyapa in the 6th century BCE.
- Vaisheshika means distinction or particularity, and it aims to explain the nature and diversity of reality in terms of atoms and their combinations.
- Vaisheshika accepts six categories of existence: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity and inherence.
- Vaisheshika also proposes a theory of atomism, which holds that all physical objects are composed of indivisible and eternal atoms of earth, water, fire and air.
- Vaisheshika is often paired with Nyaya, as they share many concepts and principles.

## Samkhya
- Samkhya is the school of dualism and analysis, founded by Kapila Muni in the 6th century BCE.
- Samkhya means enumeration or discrimination, and it aims to provide a rational and empirical account of the origin and evolution of the universe and the human condition.
- Samkhya accepts two fundamental realities: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter), which are eternal, independent and opposite in nature.
- Samkhya also proposes a theory of evolution, which holds that prakriti manifests itself into various forms of existence through the interaction of three qualities: sattva (purity), rajas (activity) and tamas (inertia).
- Samkhya is often paired with Yoga, as they share many concepts and principles.

## Yoga
- Yoga is the school of discipline and practice, founded by Patanjali in the 2nd century BCE.
- Yoga means union or connection, and it aims to provide a practical and experiential way of attaining liberation and self-realization.
- Yoga accepts the dualism of Samkhya, but adds a third reality: Ishvara (God), who is the supreme purusha and the source of all knowledge and guidance.
- Yoga also prescribes a system of eight limbs or steps of practice: yama (restraints), niyama (observances), asana (postures), pranayama (breath control), pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation) and samadhi (absorption).
- Yoga is often paired with Samkhya, as they share many concepts and principles.

- Purva Mimamsa is the school of ritual and exegesis, founded by Jaimini in the 3rd century BCE.
- Purva Mimamsa means earlier or first investigation, and it aims to provide a systematic and authoritative interpretation of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism.
- Purva Mimamsa accepts the Vedas as the eternal and infallible source of knowledge and duty, and rejects any other authority or revelation.

# Shankaracharya

- Shankaracharya is a title given to the heads of monastic institutions (mathas) in the Advaita Vedanta tradition of Hinduism. The title means "teacher of the way of Shankara", referring to the 8th-century philosopher and theologian Adi Shankara, who is considered the founder of the Advaita Vedanta school and the reviver of Hinduism in India.  
- Adi Shankara was born in a Brahmin family in Kaladi, Kerala, around the 8th century CE. He was a prodigy who mastered the Vedas and the Upanishads at a young age. He renounced the worldly life and became a wandering monk, seeking the ultimate truth of reality. He traveled across India, engaging in debates with scholars of various schools of thought, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Mimamsa, Nyaya, and Samkhya. He established his doctrine of Advaita Vedanta, which asserts that the supreme reality (Brahman) is one, without a second, and that the individual self (Atman) is identical with Brahman. He also taught that the phenomenal world of multiplicity and diversity is an illusion (Maya) caused by ignorance (Avidya) of the true nature of reality. He wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras, and the Bhagavad Gita, as well as original works such as the Vivekachudamani and the Upadesasahasri. He also composed devotional hymns to various deities, such as Shiva, Vishnu, and Devi. 
- Adi Shankara established four mathas in the four corners of India, namely, Sringeri in the south, Dwarka in the west, Puri in the east, and Badrinath in the north. He appointed four of his disciples as the heads of these mathas, and gave them the authority to ordain new monks and teach the Advaita Vedanta doctrine. He also divided the Hindu scriptures into two categories: the Prasthanatrayi, consisting of the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras, and the Bhagavad Gita, which are the authoritative sources of Vedanta; and the Smriti, consisting of the Puranas, the Itihasas, the Dharmashastras, and the Agamas, which are the supplementary sources of Hindu law and practice. He also organized the Hindu society into four varnas (castes) and four ashramas (stages of life), and prescribed the duties and rituals for each of them. 
- Adi Shankara is widely regarded as one of the greatest philosophers and teachers of Hinduism, and his influence is evident in various aspects of Hindu culture, such as art, literature, music, and spirituality. He is also revered as a saint and an incarnation of Shiva by his followers. His teachings have been interpreted and elaborated by various sub-schools and commentators of Advaita Vedanta, such as Mandana Mishra, Sureshvara, Padmapada, Prakasatman, Vachaspati Mishra, and Madhusudana Sarasvati. He is also respected and admired by other schools of Hindu philosophy, such as Vishishtadvaita, Dvaita, and Bhakti, as well as by other religions, such as Buddhism, Sikhism, and Jainism.



# Various Philosophical Doctrines in Indian Religion, Philosophy, and Practices

- Indian philosophy is the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. 
- Indian philosophy is concerned with various philosophical problems, such as the nature of the world, the nature of reality, logic, the nature of knowledge, ethics, and the philosophy of religion. 
- Indian philosophy can be divided into two main categories: orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) systems. 
- Orthodox systems are those that accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism. Unorthodox systems are those that reject the Vedas or do not rely on them. 
- The six orthodox systems of Indian philosophy are: Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva-Mimamsa, and Vedanta. 
- Nyaya is the school of logic and epistemology. It deals with the methods of reasoning, the sources of knowledge, and the criteria of validity. 
- Vaisheshika is the school of atomism and naturalism. It deals with the classification of reality into categories, such as substance, quality, action, relation, and universals. It also postulates the existence of atoms as the ultimate constituents of matter. 
- Samkhya is the school of dualism and analysis. It deals with the distinction between the two fundamental principles of reality: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter). It also explains the evolution of the world from prakriti and the liberation of purusha from the bondage of prakriti. 
- Yoga is the school of discipline and practice. It deals with the methods of controlling the body and the mind to achieve the union of the individual self (atman) with the supreme self (brahman). It also prescribes the eightfold path of yoga, consisting of ethical rules, physical exercises, breath control, sensory withdrawal, concentration, meditation, and absorption. 
- Purva-Mimamsa is the school of ritual and exegesis. It deals with the interpretation and application of the Vedic texts, especially the sacrificial rituals. It also defends the validity and eternal nature of the Vedas and the doctrine of karma (the law of moral causation). 
- Vedanta is the school of metaphysics and spirituality. It deals with the nature and relation of brahman, the ultimate reality, and atman, the individual soul. It also expounds the various doctrines of Vedanta, such as non-dualism (advaita), qualified non-dualism (vishishtadvaita), and dualism (dvaita). 
- The two unorthodox systems of Indian philosophy are: Buddhism and Jainism. 
- Buddhism is the religion and philosophy founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, in the 6th century BCE. It deals with the four noble truths, the eightfold path, the concept of nirvana, the doctrine of no-self (anatman), and the theory of dependent origination (pratityasamutpada). 
- Jainism is the religion and philosophy founded by Mahavira, the Jina, in the 6th century BCE. It deals with the principles of non-violence (ahimsa), non-absolutism (anekantavada), and non-possessiveness (aparigraha). It also teaches the concept of jiva (soul), ajiva (non-soul), and the cycle of rebirth (samsara).

# Other Heterodox Sects

- Heterodox sects are those that do not accept the authority of the Vedas and the Brahmanical rituals and practices.
- They emerged in the 6th century BCE as a reaction to the rigid and complex Vedic religion, the social inequalities and the materialistic tendencies of the society.
- They advocated a more ethical and rational approach to life, based on the principles of karma, rebirth, liberation and non-violence.
- Some of the prominent heterodox sects are:

  - **Ajivika**: Founded by Makkhali Gosala, this sect believed in fatalism, that is, everything is predetermined by fate and human actions have no effect on the outcome. They also denied the existence of free will, soul, God and moral values. They practiced extreme asceticism and renunciation of worldly pleasures. They were supported by some rulers, such as Bindusara, the father of Ashoka.  
  - **Lokayata**: Also known as Charvaka or Brihaspatya, this sect was founded by Brihaspati, a mythical sage. This sect believed in materialism, that is, only matter is real and there is no soul, God, afterlife or moral law. They rejected the concepts of karma, rebirth and liberation. They advocated a hedonistic lifestyle, that is, pursuit of sensual pleasures and enjoyment of the present. They also criticized the Vedas, the Brahmins and the rituals. They were opposed by most of the orthodox and other heterodox sects.  
  - **Nityavada**: This sect was founded by Purana Kassapa, a contemporary of Buddha and Mahavira. This sect believed in eternalism, that is, everything is eternal and unchanging. They also denied the existence of causation, karma, rebirth and liberation. They argued that there is no difference between good and evil, and that human actions have no consequences. They were also known as Akriyavada or Amoralism.  
  - **Sandehavada**: This sect was founded by Ajita Kesakambali, another contemporary of Buddha and Mahavira. This sect believed in agnosticism, that is, nothing can be known for certain. They also denied the existence of soul, God, afterlife, karma, rebirth and liberation. They argued that there is no evidence for any of these concepts and that they are mere speculations. They were also known as Sasvata or Nihilism.



# Bhakti Movement

- The Bhakti movement was a significant religious movement in medieval Hinduism that sought to bring religious reforms to all strata of society by adopting the method of devotion to achieve salvation.
- The term bhakti means "devotion" in Sanskrit and refers to the intense emotional attachment and love of a devotee toward a personal god and of the god for the devotee.
- The Bhakti movement preached against the caste system, ritualism, idolatry, and social inequalities and used the local languages to convey its message to the masses.
- The Bhakti movement originated in South India in the 7th-8th centuries CE with the Alvars (devotees of Vishnu) and the Nayanars (devotees of Shiva) and spread to other parts of India by the 12th-13th centuries CE.
- The Bhakti movement was influenced by various factors, such as the teachings of the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, the Puranas, the Sufi mystics, and the regional cultures and traditions.
- The Bhakti movement produced many saints, poets, and reformers, such as Ramanuja, Basava, Ramananda, Kabir, Nanak, Chaitanya, Mirabai, Tulsidas, Surdas, and Tukaram, who enriched the literature and art of India with their compositions and teachings.
- The Bhakti movement also led to the development of various sects and schools of Hinduism, such as the Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism, Sikhism, and the Bhakti Yoga .
- The Bhakti movement had a lasting impact on the social, cultural, and religious life of India, as it fostered a spirit of tolerance, harmony, and universal brotherhood among the people.



# Sufi Movement in India

- Sufism is a mystical and spiritual form of Islam that emphasizes personal experience of God, love, and service to humanity .
- Sufism originated in Persia and Central Asia in the 8th and 9th centuries as a reaction to the growing materialism and orthodoxy of the Islamic caliphate .
- Sufis are followers of various orders or silsilas, each with a spiritual leader or pir who guides the disciples or murids in their quest for God .
- Sufism reached India in the 12th and 13th centuries through the invasions of the Turks and the Mongols, and the migrations of the Persian and Central Asian Sufis .
- Sufism spread to different parts of India and developed various regional and linguistic expressions, such as the Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi, and Shattari silsilas .
- Sufism had a significant impact on the religious, social, and cultural life of India. Some of the contributions of Sufism are:

  - Sufism promoted a tolerant and inclusive form of Islam that respected the diversity of beliefs and practices in India  .
  - Sufism fostered a spirit of harmony and dialogue between Muslims and Hindus, and influenced the development of the Bhakti movement and the Sikh faith  .
  - Sufism enriched the literary and artistic traditions of India, especially in the fields of poetry, music, architecture, and calligraphy  .
  - Sufism inspired many social and political movements and leaders, such as the Mughal emperor Akbar, the rebel prince Dara Shikoh, and the anti-colonial activist Maulana Azad  .

- Sufism continues to be a living and vibrant tradition in India, with millions of devotees visiting the shrines of the Sufi saints and participating in their festivals and rituals  .



# Socio-religious reform movement of 19th century

- The socio-religious reform movement of 19th century was a wave of reforming activities that aimed to remove the social evils and religious superstitions from the Indian society and to revive the ancient glory of Hinduism .
- The main causes of the emergence of the reform movement were:
  - The spread of western education and liberal ideas that exposed the Indians to the rational and scientific outlook of the West .
  - The contact with the Christian missionaries and the British administration that challenged the traditional beliefs and practices of the Indians and made them aware of the need for social change .
  - The rise of nationalism and the sense of pride in the Indian culture and heritage that inspired the Indians to reform their own society and religion .

# Fairs and Festivals

- Fairs and festivals are an integral part of the Indian culture and heritage. They reflect the diversity, creativity, and spirituality of the people of India. They also provide an opportunity for social interaction, entertainment, and economic activity.
- Fairs and festivals in India can be classified into different categories based on their origin, purpose, theme, and region. Some of the common categories are:

  - **Religious festivals:** These are the festivals that are associated with the faith and beliefs of various religions and sects in India. They are celebrated to commemorate the birth, death, or enlightenment of deities, saints, prophets, or gurus. They also mark the seasons, cycles, or events of nature that are considered sacred by the followers of a particular religion. Some examples of religious festivals are Diwali, Dussehra, Holi, Eid-ul-Fitr, Christmas, Buddha Purnima, Guru Nanak Jayanti, etc. 
  - **Harvest festivals:** These are the festivals that are celebrated to express gratitude to the nature and the farmers for the bountiful crops and food. They also signify the end of the agricultural season and the beginning of a new one. They are usually celebrated in the months of January or February, when the crops are ready to be harvested. Some examples of harvest festivals are Pongal, Makar Sankranti, Lohri, Bihu, etc.  
  - **Cultural festivals:** These are the festivals that showcase the rich and diverse art forms, traditions, and customs of India. They include dance, music, theatre, literature, crafts, and cuisine. They are organized to promote and preserve the cultural heritage of India and to provide a platform for the artists and performers to showcase their talents. Some examples of cultural festivals are Khajuraho Dance Festival, Konark Dance Festival, Jaipur Literature Festival, Surajkund Crafts Fair, etc.  
  - **Regional festivals:** These are the festivals that are specific to a particular state, region, or community in India. They reflect the local history, culture, and identity of the people. They are celebrated with unique rituals, customs, and practices that are different from the rest of the country. Some examples of regional festivals are Onam, Ganesh Chaturthi, Navratri, Durga Puja, Hornbill Festival, etc.  
  - **National festivals:** These are the festivals that are celebrated by the whole nation to commemorate the historical events, personalities, or achievements that are of national importance. They are also celebrated to instill a sense of patriotism, unity, and pride among the citizens. Some examples of national festivals are Republic Day, Independence Day, Gandhi Jayanti, etc. 

- Fairs and festivals in India have a significant impact on the social, economic, and environmental aspects of the country. They provide an opportunity for people to interact, exchange, and learn from each other. They also generate employment, income, and tourism for the local people and the government. They also create awareness and appreciation for the natural resources and the environment. They also foster a sense of harmony, tolerance, and diversity among the people of different religions, regions, and cultures.

# Indian's Cultural Contribution to the World

India is a country of diverse and rich cultural heritage, with a history of more than 5000 years. India has influenced and contributed to the world in various fields, such as art, literature, philosophy, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, architecture, music, dance, and games. Some of the notable contributions of India to the world are:

- India gave the world its first university, Takshashila University, which was established around 700 BCE and had more than 10,000 students from different countries .
- India gave the world the numeral system, which is the basis of modern mathematics and science. The concept of zero, decimal system, and place value system were developed in India .
- India gave the world the game of chess, which originated from an ancient Indian game called chaturanga, meaning four divisions of the army .
- India gave the world the use and invention of buttons, which were first used in India as ornaments and seals around 2000 BCE .
- India gave the world the shampoo, which derived from the Sanskrit word champu, meaning to massage or knead. The practice of washing hair with herbs and oils was introduced by Indian traders to Europe in the 18th century .
- India gave the world the cure for leprosy, which was discovered by an Indian physician named Sushruta in the 6th century BCE. He also performed the first plastic surgery and cataract surgery in the world .
- India gave the world the game of snakes and ladders, which was inspired by an Indian game called mokshapat, meaning the ladder to salvation. The game was used to teach moral values and karma to children .
- India gave the world the art of yoga, which is a system of physical, mental, and spiritual practices that originated in India around 5000 years ago. Yoga is widely practiced around the world for its health and wellness benefits .
- India gave the world the philosophy of non-violence, which was advocated by Mahatma Gandhi, the leader of the Indian independence movement. His principles of ahimsa (non-violence) and satyagraha (truth force) inspired many global leaders and movements, such as Martin Luther King Jr., Nelson Mandela, and the civil rights movement .
- India gave the world the literature of epics, such as the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, which are among the longest and oldest poems in the world. These epics contain stories of heroism, morality, and spirituality that have influenced many cultures and religions .
- India gave the world the architecture of temples, monuments, and sculptures, such as the Taj Mahal, the Khajuraho temples, the Ajanta and Ellora caves, and the Konark Sun Temple. These structures showcase the artistic and engineering skills of Indian craftsmen and reflect the diversity of Indian culture and religion .
- India gave the world the music of ragas, which are melodic modes that form the basis of Indian classical music. Indian music has influenced many genres of music, such as jazz, rock, pop, and fusion .
- India gave the world the dance of bharatanatyam, which is one of the oldest and most refined forms of classical dance in the world. Bharatanatyam is a graceful and expressive dance that combines movement, gesture, and emotion to convey a story or a theme .
- India gave the world the concept of democracy, which was practiced in ancient India as early as the 6th century BCE. The republics of Vaishali, Magadha, and Kosala had elected assemblies and councils that governed their affairs .
- India gave the world the science of ayurveda, which is a system of holistic medicine that originated in India around 3000 years ago. Ayurveda uses natural herbs, diet, and lifestyle to balance the body, mind, and spirit .

# Indian Cinema

Indian cinema is one of the most diverse and vibrant forms of artistic expression in the world. It encompasses a variety of genres, languages, styles, and themes, reflecting the rich and complex history and culture of India. Here are some of the main points to know about Indian cinema:

- Indian cinema has a long history that dates back to the late 19th century, when the first public screening of a movie took place in Bombay (now Mumbai) in 1896. The movie belonged to the Lumiere brothers, who were pioneers of cinema in France.
- The father of Indian cinema is considered to be Dadasaheb Phalke, who released the first ever full-length feature film, Raja Harishchandra, in 1913. The silent film was a commercial success and inspired many others to make films in India.
- The first Indian talkie, or sound film, was Alam Ara, directed by Ardeshir Irani and released in 1931. The film was a musical drama that introduced the concept of songs and dialogues in Indian cinema.
- Indian cinema is not a monolithic entity, but rather a collection of regional film industries that produce films in various languages, such as Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Malayalam, Kannada, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, and others. Each regional industry has its own distinctive features, such as themes, stars, genres, and styles.
- Bollywood is the most popular and influential of the regional film industries in India. It is based in Mumbai and produces films mainly in Hindi. Bollywood is known for its masala films, which are a blend of action, comedy, romance, drama, and musical elements. Bollywood films have a wide viewership and fanbase throughout South Asia and have spread internationally, especially among the Indian diaspora .
- Indian cinema has also produced many critically acclaimed and internationally recognized films that belong to the parallel cinema or art cinema movement. These films are characterized by their realism, social commentary, experimentation, and artistic vision. Some of the prominent directors of this movement are Satyajit Ray, Ritwik Ghatak, Mrinal Sen, Shyam Benegal, Adoor Gopalakrishnan, and Mani Ratnam .
- Indian cinema has also been influenced by and has influenced other film industries around the world, such as Hollywood, European cinema, and Asian cinema. Indian cinema has borrowed and adapted elements from various genres, such as westerns, thrillers, horror, sci-fi, and fantasy. Indian cinema has also inspired filmmakers from other countries, such as Danny Boyle, Quentin Tarantino, Ang Lee, and Mira Nair .
- Indian cinema is a dynamic and evolving form of art that reflects the changing social, political, economic, and cultural realities of India. Indian cinema has also contributed to the preservation and promotion of India's cultural heritage and performing arts, such as classical music, dance, theater, and folk traditions .
