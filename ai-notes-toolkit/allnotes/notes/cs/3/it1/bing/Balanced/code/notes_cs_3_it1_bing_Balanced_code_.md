

# Indian Traditions, Culture and Society

- India is a diverse country with a rich and varied heritage of languages, religions, arts, cuisines, philosophies, and customs.
- India's culture and traditions are influenced by its ancient history, geography, and various invasions and migrations over the centuries.
- Some of the main aspects of Indian culture and traditions are:

  - **Arts and Language**: India has a long and distinguished tradition of literature, music, dance, architecture, painting, and sculpture. India has 22 official languages and hundreds of regional dialects. The most ancient and influential language is Sanskrit, which is the source of many Indo-European languages and the language of Hindu scriptures and classical literature. India also has a rich oral tradition of folktales, legends, and epics.
  - **Philosophy and Religion**: India is the birthplace of four major world religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. Hinduism is the oldest and most dominant religion in India, with about 80% of the population following it. Hinduism is a diverse and complex faith that encompasses various sects, schools, and practices. Buddhism and Jainism are offshoots of Hinduism that emphasize non-violence, compassion, and liberation from the cycle of rebirth. Sikhism is a monotheistic religion that was founded by Guru Nanak in the 15th century and combines elements of Hinduism and Islam. India also has a significant minority of Muslims, Christians, Parsis, Jews, and other faiths.
  - **Food and Drink**: Indian cuisine is diverse and regional, reflecting the variety of climates, cultures, and ingredients in the country. Indian food is known for its use of spices, herbs, grains, legumes, vegetables, fruits, dairy products, and meats. Some of the common dishes are dal (lentil soup), roti (flatbread), rice, curry, biryani (rice with meat and spices), dosa (rice and lentil crepe), idli (steamed rice cake), samosa (fried pastry with savory filling), chaat (snack food), and sweets. Indian drinks include tea, coffee, lassi (yogurt drink), and alcoholic beverages such as arrack (distilled palm wine), toddy (fermented palm sap), and feni (cashew or coconut liquor).
  - **Society and Customs**: Indian society is hierarchical and patriarchal, with a strong emphasis on family, kinship, caste, and community. India has a population of about 1.3 billion people, belonging to various ethnic, linguistic, religious, and regional groups. India follows a system of joint family, where several generations live together under one roof. Marriage is usually arranged by the parents and elders, and divorce is rare. Caste is a hereditary social class that determines one's occupation, status, and access to resources. India has four main castes: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers). There is also a fifth group of Dalits (untouchables), who are considered outside the caste system and face discrimination and oppression. India also has various tribal and indigenous communities, who have their own distinct cultures and traditions. India has many festivals and celebrations, such as Diwali (festival of lights), Holi (festival of colors), Dussehra (victory of good over evil), Eid (end of Ramadan), Christmas, and Gandhi Jayanti (birth anniversary of Mahatma Gandhi). India also has many customs and rituals, such as greeting with a namaste (folding hands), wearing a bindi (dot on the forehead), applying henna (temporary tattoo), wearing a sari (traditional dress for women), and practicing yoga (physical and mental discipline).

### State in Ancient India

- The state in ancient India was a social organization with political power that ensured peace, order and happiness.
- The origin of the state in ancient India is not clear, but there are three main theories: social contract theory, divine origin theory and organic theory.
- Social contract theory: This theory holds that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among the people who gave up some of their rights and freedoms to a ruler or a group of rulers in exchange for protection and welfare.
- Divine origin theory: This theory holds that the state was created by God or gods who appointed a king or a dynasty to rule over the people as their representative or agent.
- Organic theory: This theory holds that the state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the growth and development of human society, and that it evolved from the family, clan, tribe and village communities.
- The types of states in ancient India were loosely categorized into monarchy, republic and oligarchy.
- Monarchy: This was the most prevalent type of state in ancient India, where the king was the supreme authority and the source of power for the ministers, governors, officials and other functionaries . The king was assisted by a council of ministers, a general assembly of the people, and a judicial system. The king was expected to follow the dharma (righteousness) and protect the varnas (social classes) and ashramas (stages of life).
- Republic: This was a type of state where the people elected their own rulers or representatives from among themselves, and where the power was shared by a number of clans, tribes or guilds. The republics were also known as sanghas, ganas, janapadas or rashtras. The republics had their own assemblies, laws, coins and armies. Some examples of republics in ancient India are the Licchavis, the Mallas, the Shakas and the Yaudheyas.
- Oligarchy: This was a type of state where the power was concentrated in the hands of a few wealthy or influential people, such as nobles, merchants or priests. The oligarchies were also known as shrenis, pugas or nigamas. The oligarchies had their own rules, regulations and customs, and often controlled trade and commerce. Some examples of oligarchies in ancient India are the Nandas, the Mauryas and the Guptas.

### Evolutionary Theory

- Evolutionary theory is a perspective that explains social change and development in terms of biological and cultural evolution.
- Evolutionary theory assumes that societies evolve from simple to complex, from homogeneous to heterogeneous, and from primitive to civilized over time.
- Evolutionary theory also assumes that there is a linear progression of stages or phases that societies go through, such as savagery, barbarism, and civilization.
- Evolutionary theory was influenced by the ideas of Charles Darwin, Herbert Spencer, Lewis Henry Morgan, and Edward Burnett Tylor, among others.
- Evolutionary theory was popular in the 19th and early 20th centuries, but was criticized for being ethnocentric, deterministic, and teleological.
- Evolutionary theory was challenged by other perspectives, such as historical particularism, functionalism, structuralism, and Marxism, that emphasized the diversity, complexity, and dynamism of social phenomena.
- Evolutionary theory has been revived and modified in recent decades by scholars who use concepts such as cultural evolution, sociobiology, evolutionary psychology, and memetics to explain social change and development.



# Force Theory of Origin of State

- Force theory of origin of state is a theory that explains the emergence of the state as a result of the conquest and domination of one group of people over another by the use of physical force  .
- According to this theory, the state originated due to the need for security, order and protection from external and internal threats, which led the strong and powerful to subjugate the weak and vulnerable and establish their authority over them  .
- The theory is based on the assumption that war is the natural condition of human society and that the state is the product of war and violence  .
- The theory is also known as the conquest theory or the might-is-right theory, as it implies that the state is founded on the principle of force and coercion, and that the legitimacy and sovereignty of the state derive from the power and will of the conqueror  .
- The theory is supported by some historical examples of state formation, such as the ancient empires of Egypt, Mesopotamia, China, Rome and Greece, which were built by the expansion and annexation of territories and peoples by military force  .
- The theory is criticized by many scholars and thinkers for being simplistic, unrealistic, unethical and inaccurate, as it ignores the role of other factors and processes in the origin and development of the state, such as social contract, divine right, natural law, evolution, culture, religion, economy, law, consent, cooperation and democracy    .

### Contract Theory

- Contract theory is a branch of political philosophy that studies the origin, nature and legitimacy of the state and its authority over the people.
- Contract theory assumes that the state is the result of a voluntary agreement or contract among rational individuals who consent to give up some of their natural rights and freedoms in exchange for the protection and benefits provided by the state.
- Contract theory also explores the conditions and terms of the contract, the rights and duties of the parties involved, the limits and scope of the state's power, and the possibility of dissolving or revising the contract.
- Contract theory is influenced by the historical and social context of its proponents, who often reflect on the problems and challenges faced by their own societies and times.
- Some of the major contract theorists are Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Immanuel Kant, John Rawls and Robert Nozick.

- Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679) was an English philosopher who wrote Leviathan (1651) in the aftermath of the English Civil War. He argued that in the state of nature, human life was "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short" due to the constant fear of violence and death. He proposed that people entered into a social contract with an absolute sovereign who had the power to enforce the law and maintain peace and order. He believed that the sovereign's authority was derived from the consent of the people, who gave up all their rights except the right to self-preservation. He also claimed that the contract was irrevocable and that the people had no right to rebel against the sovereign.
- John Locke (1632-1704) was an English philosopher who wrote Two Treatises of Government (1689) in response to the Glorious Revolution of 1688. He argued that in the state of nature, people had natural rights to life, liberty and property, which were derived from God and natural law. He proposed that people entered into a social contract with a limited government that had the duty to protect their natural rights and to act according to the consent of the governed. He believed that the government's authority was based on the trust of the people, who retained the right to resist or overthrow the government if it violated their rights or acted tyrannically.
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712-1778) was a French philosopher who wrote The Social Contract (1762) in the context of the Enlightenment and the French Revolution. He argued that in the state of nature, people were free, equal and happy, but they were corrupted by the development of civilization and private property. He proposed that people entered into a social contract with a democratic government that represented the general will of the people, which was the source of legitimacy and sovereignty. He believed that the government's authority was based on the participation and consent of the people, who had the right to revise or dissolve the contract if it failed to express the general will or to promote the common good.



### Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

- State formation is the process of emergence and consolidation of political authority and institutions in a given territory.
- State formation in ancient India was influenced by various factors, such as geography, ecology, culture, religion, economy, and warfare.
- According to some scholars, state formation in ancient India can be divided into four stages: the Vedic period, the Mahajanapada period, the Mauryan period, and the post-Mauryan period.

#### The Vedic Period

- The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) is the earliest phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the arrival and settlement of the Indo-Aryan tribes in the northwestern and northern regions of the subcontinent.
- The Vedic society was organized into tribes (jana), clans (vish), and families (kula), each with its own chief (raja) and assembly (sabha or samiti).
- The Vedic state was based on kinship, ritual, and sacrifice, and had a limited territorial scope and administrative capacity.
- The Vedic state was also characterized by frequent conflicts and alliances among the tribes, as well as interactions with the indigenous people (dasas or dasyus).

#### The Mahajanapada Period

- The Mahajanapada period (c. 600-300 BCE) is the second stage of state formation in ancient India, marked by the emergence and expansion of sixteen large territorial states (mahajanapadas) in the northern and central regions of the subcontinent.
- The Mahajanapada states were more centralized and complex than the Vedic states, and had a more diverse and stratified society, comprising of four varnas (classes) and numerous jatis (castes).
- The Mahajanapada states were also influenced by various religious and philosophical movements, such as Buddhism, Jainism, and Upanishads, which challenged the authority and legitimacy of the Vedic religion and the brahmanical order.
- The Mahajanapada states were also engaged in intense competition and warfare for resources and supremacy, which led to the rise and fall of several dynasties and kingdoms, such as the Nandas, the Shishunagas, the Magadhas, the Kosala, the Vatsa, and the Avanti.

#### The Mauryan Period

- The Mauryan period (c. 321-185 BCE) is the third stage of state formation in ancient India, marked by the establishment and consolidation of the first pan-Indian empire by Chandragupta Maurya and his successors, especially Ashoka.
- The Mauryan empire was the largest and most powerful state in ancient India, and extended from Afghanistan to Bengal, and from Kashmir to Karnataka.
- The Mauryan empire was also the most centralized and bureaucratic state in ancient India, and had a well-developed administration, taxation, judiciary, military, and intelligence system, as well as a network of roads, trade, and communication.
- The Mauryan empire was also known for its cultural and religious diversity and tolerance, and its patronage of various schools of art, architecture, literature, and philosophy, such as the Mauryan art, the Ashokan edicts, the Arthashastra, and the Sanchi stupa.

#### The Post-Mauryan Period

- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE-300 CE) is the fourth and final stage of state formation in ancient India, marked by the decline and disintegration of the Mauryan empire, and the emergence and coexistence of several regional and foreign powers in different parts of the subcontinent.
- The post-Mauryan period witnessed the rise and fall of various dynasties and kingdoms, such as the Sungas, the Kanvas, the Satavahanas, the Kushanas, the Shakas, the Indo-Greeks, the Indo-Parthians, the Indo-Scythians, and the Guptas.
- The post-Mauryan period also witnessed the development and diffusion of various cultural and religious traditions, such as the Mahayana Buddhism, the Hinayana Buddhism, the Ajivikas, the Lokayatas, the Vaisheshikas, the Nyayas, the Sankhyas, the Yogas, the Mimamsas, the Vedantas, the Jainism, the Shaivism, the Vaishnavism, and the Shaktism.
- The post-Mauryan period also witnessed the flourishing of various forms of art, architecture



### Kingship in Ancient India

- Kingship in ancient India was a **sovereignty over a territory** by a king who functioned as its **protector**, a role which involved both **secular and religious power**.
- Ancient Indians believed that kingship arose out of **military requirement**. A king must be a **capable general** whose leadership is recognized by all.
- The meaning and significance of kingship changed dramatically between the **Vedic and Later Vedic period**, and underwent further development under the times of the **Jain and Buddhist rulers**.
- The Vedic literature has some speculations about the **origin of kingship**. Some sources suggest that kingship was **divinely ordained**, while others imply that it was a **human invention** or a **social contract**.
- The Vedic king was primarily a **ritual leader** who performed sacrifices and maintained the **cosmic order**. He was also a **warrior** who defended his people and expanded his territory.
- The Later Vedic period saw the emergence of **monarchical states** and the decline of **tribal republics**. The king became more **powerful** and **autonomous**, and acquired a **divine status**. He was assisted by a **council of ministers** and a **general assembly** of the people.
- The Jain and Buddhist texts challenged the **Brahmanical theory of kingship** and advocated for a **moral and ethical** basis of governance. They also recognized the **diversity** and **complexity** of the political landscape in ancient India, which included **empires, kingdoms, republics, and chiefdoms**.
- Some of the famous kings and emperors of ancient India were **Ajatasatru, Chandragupta Maurya, Ashoka, Samudragupta, Harshavardhana, Rajaraja Chola, and Krishnadevaraya**. They are remembered for their **military conquests, administrative reforms, cultural achievements, and religious patronage**.

### The Seven Limbs of the State

- The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) are a political concept given by Kautilya in his treatise Arthashastra, which deals with the art of governance and statecraft .
- The seven limbs are: 
  - King (swami): The ruler who is the head of the state and responsible for its peace, justice and stability . The king should be virtuous, intelligent, courageous and benevolent.
  - Amatya (amatra): The bureaucrats or ministers who assist the king in administration and policy-making . The amatya should be loyal, efficient, honest and competent.
  - Janapada (janapada): The territory or population of the state, which provides the resources and manpower for the state . The janapada should be fertile, prosperous, loyal and diverse.
  - Durga (durga): The fort or capital of the state, which serves as the center of power and defense . The durga should be well-fortified, well-located, well-supplied and well-guarded.
  - Kosa (kosa): The treasury or wealth of the state, which funds the state activities and welfare . The kosa should be abundant, well-managed, well-spent and well-audited.
  - Danda (danda): The coercive authority or military of the state, which enforces the law and protects the state from external and internal threats  . The danda should be strong, disciplined, well-trained and well-equipped.
  - Mitra (mitra): The ally or friend of the state, who supports the state in times of need and shares common interests . The mitra should be reliable, trustworthy, powerful and friendly.
- The seven limbs of the state are interdependent and interrelated, and the state should maintain a balance among them for its stability and prosperity  .
- The seven limbs of the state reflect the ancient Indian political thought and practice, and have influenced the later political thinkers and rulers in India and beyond .



### Society in Ancient India

- The ancient Indian society was based on the **varna system**, a four-fold classification of the people into four classes: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers). 
- The varna system was not rigid or hereditary, but based on one's qualities, actions, and duties. It also allowed for mobility and intermixing among the classes.  
- The ancient Indian society also followed the **ashrama system**, a four-fold division of the life of each individual into four stages: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (retiree), and Sannyasa (renunciant).  
- The ashrama system was meant to guide one's spiritual and moral development, as well as one's social and economic responsibilities. Each stage had its own rules, goals, and duties.  
- The ancient Indian society also practiced **sanskaras** or sacraments, which were rites of passage that marked the important events and transitions in one's life, such as birth, education, marriage, death, etc.  
- The sanskaras were meant to purify, sanctify, and elevate one's soul, as well as to integrate one into the social and religious community. There were 16 major sanskaras, but the number and nature of them varied according to different traditions and regions.

They are found in states like Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Orissa, and Kerala. Glove puppets are usually made of cloth, leather, or paper, and are decorated with bright colors and costumes. ### Theatre

- Theatre is one of the most ancient forms of art and expression in India, dating back to the Vedic period (1500-500 BCE).
- Theatre in India began as a narrative form, in which recitation, singing and dancing were integral elements. The encompassing of all forms of literature and fine arts into its physical presentation and the emphasis on narrative elements made the classical Indian drama theatrical right from the beginning.
- The earliest evidence of theatre in India is found in the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, which contain hymns, rituals and dialogues that were performed by priests and sages. The Natya Shastra, a treatise on dramaturgy attributed to Bharata Muni, is the most comprehensive and authoritative source of classical Indian theatre. It covers various aspects of theatre, such as the origin, purpose, structure, genres, styles, techniques, costumes, makeup, music, dance, acting, directing and criticism of theatre .
- The classical Indian theatre was mainly divided into two types: Sanskrit theatre and folk theatre. Sanskrit theatre was based on the literary works of Sanskrit poets and playwrights, such as Kalidasa, Bhasa, Shudraka, Vishakhadatta and Harsha. Sanskrit theatre followed the rules and conventions of the Natya Shastra and was performed in royal courts and temples. The most popular genres of Sanskrit theatre were nataka (heroic drama), prakarana (social drama), bhana (monologue), vyayoga (minor heroic drama), samavakara (mixed drama), dima (farce), anka (episodic drama) and utsrishtikanka (fragmentary drama) .
- Folk theatre was based on the local traditions, myths, legends and stories of different regions and communities of India. Folk theatre was more flexible, spontaneous and diverse than Sanskrit theatre and was performed in open spaces, such as streets, fields, courtyards and village squares. Folk theatre used various languages, dialects, costumes, props, masks, puppets, music and dance to create a lively and colorful spectacle. Some of the prominent forms of folk theatre are jatra (Bengal), tamasha (Maharashtra), nautanki (Uttar Pradesh), bhavai (Gujarat), therukoothu (Tamil Nadu), yakshagana (Karnataka), koodiyattam (Kerala) and bhand pather (Kashmir) .
- The modern theatre in India emerged during the colonial period, under the influence of the Western theatre and the social and political movements of the time. The modern theatre in India comprises mainly of English, Hindi and Hinglish (comprising of a mix of Hindi and English dialogues) plays. English theatre was brought to India during the British rule and was watched mostly by art connoisseurs of the rich, upper class. Some of the notable English playwrights and theatre groups in India are Rabindranath Tagore, Sri Aurobindo, Harindranath Chattopadhyay, Ebrahim Alkazi, Prithvi Theatre, Indian People's Theatre Association and Theatre of the Absurd. Hindi theatre was inspired by the nationalist and socialist movements and the revival of the folk theatre. Some of the prominent Hindi playwrights and theatre groups in India are Mohan Rakesh, Vijay Tendulkar, Badal Sircar, Girish Karnad, Habib Tanvir, Nandikar, Rangmandal and Rangashankara. Hinglish theatre is a contemporary and popular form of theatre that blends Hindi and English languages and cultures and appeals to the urban and cosmopolitan audiences. Some of the famous Hinglish playwrights and theatre groups in India are Mahesh Dattani, Dinesh Thakur, Lillete Dubey, Feroz Khan, Akvarious Productions and Rage Productions.
- Theatre in India is not only a form of entertainment, but also a medium of education, awareness, social change and cultural heritage. Theatre in India reflects the diversity, richness and complexity of the Indian society and its history, values, beliefs, traditions and aspirations. Theatre in India is a living and evolving art form that continues to experiment, innovate and adapt to the changing times and contexts  .

  - **Ganesh Chaturthi**: The festival of celebrating the birthday of Lord Ganesha, the elephant-headed god of wisdom and success, celebrated in August or September. The festival is especially popular in Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka and Telangana.
  - **Pongal**: The harvest festival of Tamil Nadu, celebrated in January, to thank the Sun God, the Earth and the cattle for their bounty. People cook and offer pongal, a sweet rice dish, to the Sun God, decorate their houses with kolam, and celebrate with bull-taming, folk dances and music.
  - **Pushkar Fair**: The largest camel fair in the world, held in November, in the holy town of Pushkar in Rajasthan. Thousands of camels, horses and cattle are traded, decorated and raced, along with cultural and religious activities, such as folk music, dance, puppet shows, camel beauty contests, and bathing in the sacred Pushkar lake .
  - **Konark Dance Festival**: A classical dance festival, held in December, in the backdrop of the magnificent Sun Temple in Konark, Odisha. Renowned dancers from various styles, such as Odissi, Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Kuchipudi, Manipuri and Mohiniattam, perform and enchant the audience with their grace and skill .
  - **Goa Carnival**: A colourful and lively festival, held in February or March, before the Lent season, in Goa. The festival is a legacy of the Portuguese rule and features parades, floats, masks, costumes, music, dance, and street parties. The highlight of the festival is the crowning of King Momo, who declares the festivities open .



### Current developments in Arts and Culture in India

- India has a rich and diverse cultural heritage that includes various forms of art, music, dance, theatre, literature, architecture, and festivals.
- India is also home to 37 world heritage sites, two thriving film industries, and many smaller ones, and a vibrant folk and tribal culture.
- Some of the current developments in arts and culture in India are:

  - Three Indian cities, Thrissur and Nilambur in Kerala and Warangal in Telangana, have joined the UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities, which aims to promote lifelong learning and social inclusion through culture and education.
  - India has nominated the Hoysala Temples of Karnataka, which are examples of the unique Hoysala style of architecture and sculpture, for inscription in the World Heritage List.
  - India has successfully retrieved a 10th-century Yogini statue, which was stolen from a temple in Bihar and smuggled to France, after a legal battle of 24 years.
  - India has witnessed a surge in contemporary art, which explores new trends, themes, mediums, and expressions, reflecting the changing socio-political and cultural realities of the country.
  - India has launched several initiatives to preserve and promote its intangible cultural heritage, such as the Dharohar project, which showcases the customs and traditions of the Indian Revenue Service, and the Devayatanam project, which documents the temple architecture of India.
  - India has celebrated its diversity and creativity through various events and festivals, such as the Republic Day Parade, which featured cultural tableaux from different states and union territories, and the Sydney WorldPride, which showcased the LGBT community and culture of India .



### Indian's Cultural Contribution to the World

India is a country of diverse and rich cultural heritage, which has influenced many aspects of the world. Some of the major contributions of India to the world are:

- India gave the world its first university, Takshashila University, which was established around 700 BCE and had more than 10,000 students from different countries .
- India gave the world the numeral zero, which is essential for mathematics and science. The concept of zero was developed by Indian mathematicians around the 5th century CE .
- The game of chess originated in India, where it was called chaturanga, meaning four divisions of the army. Chess was later introduced to Persia and then to Europe .
- Indians were the first ones to use and invent buttons, which were made of seashells and used to fasten clothes. Buttons were used in India as early as 2000 BCE .
- Shampoo originated from India, where it was derived from the Sanskrit word champu, meaning to massage. Shampoo was made of herbs and natural ingredients and was used to cleanse and condition hair .
- India gave the cure for leprosy, which was a dreaded disease in ancient times. The Indian physician Sushruta described the symptoms and treatment of leprosy in his medical treatise Sushruta Samhita, which was written around the 6th century BCE .
- Snakes and ladders was inspired from an Indian game called Mokshapat, which was a moral lesson on karma and destiny. The game was invented by the 13th century poet and saint Gyandev .
- India has a rich tradition of literature, music, dance, theatre, and art, which have influenced many cultures around the world. Some of the famous examples are the Vedas, the Ramayana, the Mahabharata, the Bhagavad Gita, the Upanishads, the Panchatantra, the Kamasutra, the Natyashastra, the Ragas, the Kathakali, the Bharatanatyam, the Rangoli, the Madhubani, and the Taj Mahal  .
- India has also contributed to the fields of philosophy, religion, spirituality, yoga, meditation, astronomy, astrology, mathematics, algebra, geometry, trigonometry, calculus, decimal system, binary system, metallurgy, medicine, surgery, ayurveda, plastic surgery, cataract surgery, inoculation, vaccination, martial arts, and many more .

These are some of the examples of India's cultural contribution to the world, which show the richness and diversity of Indian civilization. India has been a source of inspiration and learning for many people and nations, and continues to do so in the present times.



### Indian Cinema

Indian cinema is one of the largest and most diverse film industries in the world, producing films in various languages, genres, and styles. Indian cinema has a rich and complex history that spans over a century, reflecting the social, cultural, and political changes of the nation. Here are some of the main points to know about Indian cinema:

- The beginning of Indian cinema dates back to 1896, when the first public screening of a movie by the Lumiere brothers took place in Bombay (now Mumbai) . The father of Indian cinema, Dadasaheb Phalke, released the first ever full-length feature film, Raja Harishchandra, in 1913. The silent film was a commercial success and inspired many filmmakers to follow suit .
- The advent of sound in cinema marked a new era for Indian cinema, as the first talkie, Alam Ara, by Ardeshir Irani, was screened in Bombay in 1931. The film was a musical drama that featured seven songs and attracted huge crowds. The talkies also gave rise to the growth of regional films, as different languages and dialects could be used to cater to different audiences .
- The 1930s and 1940s saw the emergence of some of the most influential filmmakers and actors of Indian cinema, such as V. Shantaram, Prithviraj Kapoor, Devika Rani, and K.L. Saigal. The films of this period dealt with various themes, such as social realism, nationalism, romance, and mythology. Some of the notable films of this era include Achhut Kanya (1936), Sant Tukaram (1936), Kismet (1943), and Mother India (1957) .
- The 1950s and 1960s are considered the golden age of Indian cinema, as some of the most acclaimed and popular films were made during this time. The films of this period showcased the artistic and technical excellence of Indian cinema, as well as the diversity of genres and styles. Some of the prominent filmmakers and actors of this era include Satyajit Ray, Guru Dutt, Raj Kapoor, Bimal Roy, Dilip Kumar, Nargis, Madhubala, and Meena Kumari. Some of the landmark films of this era include Pather Panchali (1955), Pyaasa (1957), Mughal-e-Azam (1960), and Guide (1965) .
- The 1970s and 1980s witnessed the rise of the masala film, a genre that combined action, comedy, romance, drama, and music in a formulaic and entertaining manner. The masala film was popularized by filmmakers such as Manmohan Desai, Prakash Mehra, and Ramesh Sippy, and actors such as Amitabh Bachchan, Dharmendra, Hema Malini, and Rekha. Some of the iconic films of this genre include Sholay (1975), Amar Akbar Anthony (1977), and Mr. India (1987) .
- The 1990s and 2000s saw the emergence of new trends and challenges in Indian cinema, as the industry faced competition from television, video, and foreign films. The films of this period explored new themes, such as urban life, globalization, diaspora, and terrorism. The films also experimented with new genres, such as parallel cinema, art cinema, and new wave cinema. Some of the notable filmmakers and actors of this era include Adoor Gopalakrishnan, Shyam Benegal, Aamir Khan, Shah Rukh Khan, Salman Khan, Madhuri Dixit, and Kajol. Some of the popular films of this era include Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge (1995), Lagaan (2001), Dil Chahta Hai (2001), and Rang De Basanti (2006) .

The films of this period have explored various genres, such as biopic, thriller, horror, and sci-fi. The films have also addressed various issues, such as gender, caste, religion, and politics.
