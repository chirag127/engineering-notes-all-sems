- The quality of the air-interface is affected by several factors, including distance from the base station, obstacles in the path of the signal, and interference from other devices.
- The cellular concept is the basis of the air-interface. It divides a geographic area into cells, each of which is served by a base station. This allows for efficient use of radio spectrum and enables devices to move between cells without losing connection to the network.
- The Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) is a standard for the air-interface that is widely used in mobile networks around the world. It uses a combination of time division multiple access (TDMA) and frequency division multiple access (FDMA) to divide the air-interface into multiple channels, allowing multiple devices to use the same frequency band at the same time without interfering with each other.
- GSM supports voice and data communication, including text messaging and internet access.
- GSM also includes features such as call forwarding, caller ID, and roaming, which allow users to stay connected even when they are outside of their home network.

Understanding the air-interface and the cellular concept is crucial for mobile computing, as it enables devices to connect to the network and communicate with other devices. ### Location Management in Mobile Computing

Location management is an essential aspect of mobile computing, as it enables mobile devices to maintain their connectivity while moving. In this context, HLR-VLR, hierarchical, and handoffs are key concepts that help manage the location of mobile devices. Here are some important points to understand about these concepts:

1. HLR-VLR: Home Location Register (HLR) and Visitor Location Register (VLR) are two databases that store information about mobile devices and their current location. HLR stores the permanent information of a mobile device, including its home location and subscription information. VLR stores the temporary information of a mobile device, including its current location and roaming status.

2. Hierarchical Location Management: This approach divides the network into smaller areas, such as cells, and assigns each area a unique identity. When a mobile device moves from one area to another, it updates its location information in the HLR-VLR databases. This approach helps reduce the signaling overhead and improves the efficiency of location management.

3. Handoffs: When a mobile device moves from one cell to another, it needs to transfer its ongoing communication to the new cell seamlessly. This process is called a handoff or a handover. There are two types of handoffs – soft handoff and hard handoff. In soft handoff, the mobile device maintains a connection with both the old and new cells, while in hard handoff, the mobile device terminates the connection with the old cell before establishing a connection with the new cell.

4. Cellular Concept: The cellular concept is a fundamental principle of wireless telephony, where the coverage area is divided into small cells, and each cell is served by a base station. This approach helps increase the capacity of the network and reduce interference.

5. GSM: Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) is a standard for digital cellular networks that uses the hierarchical location management approach. It is used by over 80% of the global mobile market and supports voice and data services.

Understanding location management in mobile computing is crucial for designing efficient and reliable mobile networks. These concepts play a significant role in maintaining the connectivity of mobile devices and ensuring seamless communication.



### Channel Allocation in Cellular Systems

In cellular systems, mobile devices communicate with a network of base stations to establish and maintain connections. Channel allocation is the process of assigning communication channels to these connections. 

Here are some key points on channel allocation in cellular systems:

- Channel allocation is necessary to avoid interference between mobile devices using the same frequency band.
- Frequency reuse is a technique used to increase the capacity of cellular systems. It involves dividing a geographic area into cells and reusing the same frequency band in different cells that are sufficiently far apart.
- Channel allocation can be static or dynamic. In static channel allocation, channels are permanently assigned to cells, while in dynamic channel allocation, channels are assigned on demand based on the current traffic load.
- There are several algorithms for dynamic channel allocation, including fixed channel assignment, dynamic channel assignment, and hybrid channel assignment. Each algorithm has its advantages and disadvantages in terms of efficiency and complexity.
- In the Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM), which is one of the most widely used cellular standards, channel allocation is based on a combination of frequency hopping and time division multiple access (TDMA). This allows multiple users to share the same frequency band by dividing it into time slots.
- The GSM system uses a hierarchical structure of cells, with larger cells covering a larger geographic area and smaller cells used in densely populated areas to increase capacity. Channel allocation is performed separately for each cell, with the goal of maximizing the number of users that can be supported while maintaining good call quality.

Overall, channel allocation is a critical component of cellular systems that enables efficient and reliable communication between mobile devices and the network.



### CDMA

CDMA stands for Code Division Multiple Access. It is a wireless communication technology that allows multiple users to share the same frequency band by assigning a unique code to each user. Here are some key points to know about CDMA:

- CDMA uses spread spectrum technology to transmit data over the airwaves.
- Unlike other wireless technologies like GSM, CDMA does not use time slots to allocate channels to users. Instead, the data is spread over a wide frequency band using a unique code for each user.
- CDMA provides better call quality and higher data speeds compared to other wireless technologies.
- CDMA is used by many cellular networks around the world, including Verizon Wireless and Sprint in the United States.
- CDMA is also used for other wireless applications such as satellite communication and wireless LANs.

Overall, CDMA is an important technology that has revolutionized wireless communication. Its unique approach to sharing wireless spectrum has made it a popular choice for cellular networks and other wireless applications.



### GPRS

GPRS (General Packet Radio Service) is a packet-switched data service that provides data rates up to 115 kbps. It is an extension of the GSM cellular network and is used for mobile data transfer.

Some important points about GPRS are:

- GPRS is a packet-switched technology, which means that data is transmitted in small packets instead of a continuous stream.
- GPRS uses the same radio spectrum as GSM, but it allocates radio resources only when data needs to be transmitted.
- GPRS is always on and does not require a dial-up connection, which means that users can be online all the time without having to connect and disconnect.
- GPRS can be used for a variety of applications, such as email, web browsing, and file transfer.
- GPRS provides a higher data transfer rate than GSM, which allows for faster data transfer and better multimedia support.
- GPRS is more cost-effective than circuit-switched data services, as users are charged only for the amount of data they transmit.

Overall, GPRS is an important technology in the evolution of mobile computing, as it enables faster and more efficient data transfer over cellular networks.

   - The MAC layer of the 802.11 standard uses the Distributed Coordination Function (DCF) protocol for contention-based access.
   - The 802.11 standard also includes optional contention-free access protocols, such as the Point Coordination Function (PCF).
   
3. Bluetooth
   - Bluetooth is a wireless technology used for short-range communication between devices.
   - Bluetooth uses a frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) technique to avoid interference with other wireless devices.

- Mobile IP is based on the concept of a home network and a foreign network.
- The home network is where the mobile device is originally registered and has its IP address.
- The foreign network is the network where the mobile device is currently located and wants to access the Internet.
- When a mobile device moves from its home network to a foreign network, it registers with a foreign agent in the foreign network.
- The foreign agent then forwards all packets to the home agent in the home network, which forwards them to the mobile device.
- When a mobile device moves back to its home network, it de-registers from the foreign agent and resumes communication with its home agent.
- Mobile IP is an important protocol for mobile computing as it allows mobile devices to stay connected to the Internet while on the move.

In conclusion, Mobile IP is a key protocol for mobile computing that enables mobile devices to maintain their IP address while moving between different networks. ## Unit 5 - Ad Hoc networks, localization, MAC issues, Routing protocols, global state routing (GSR)

In this unit, we will cover several important topics related to ad hoc networks, including localization, MAC issues, routing protocols, and global state routing (GSR). Here are some key points to keep in mind:

- Ad hoc networks are a type of wireless network that don't rely on a fixed infrastructure. Instead, devices in an ad hoc network communicate directly with one another, forming a self-organizing network. This makes ad hoc networks useful in situations where a fixed infrastructure is not available or practical, such as in disaster zones or military operations.
- Localization is the process of determining the physical location of a device in an ad hoc network. This can be challenging in a wireless environment where signals can be affected by interference and obstacles. Various localization techniques have been developed, including triangulation, which uses the angle of arrival of signals to determine a device's location, and fingerprinting, which uses a database of signal patterns to match a device's signal to a known location.
- MAC (Media Access Control) issues are a common problem in ad hoc networks. MAC protocols determine how devices access the wireless medium and avoid collisions when multiple devices are transmitting at the same time. However, in an ad hoc network, devices may not be able to hear each other's transmissions, leading to collisions and reduced network performance. Various MAC protocols have been developed to address this issue, including the Distributed Coordination Function (DCF) and the Enhanced Distributed Channel Access (EDCA) protocol.
- Routing protocols are used to determine the best path for data to travel through the network. In an ad hoc network, routing can be challenging because the network topology can change rapidly as devices move around. Various routing protocols have been developed for ad hoc networks, including reactive protocols, which only search for a route when data needs to be sent, and proactive protocols, which maintain a routing table for all devices in the network.
- Global state routing (GSR) is a routing protocol that uses a global view of the network to determine the best path for data to travel. GSR can be useful in situations where the network topology is complex and changes frequently. However, GSR requires a lot of communication overhead to maintain the global view of the network, so it may not be practical in all situations.

By understanding these concepts and protocols, you will be better equipped to design and deploy ad hoc networks for a variety of applications.



### Destination sequenced distance vector routing (DSDV)

Destination sequenced distance vector routing (DSDV) is a proactive routing protocol used in mobile ad hoc networks (MANETs). It is based on the classical Bellman-Ford algorithm with some modifications.

DSDV maintains a routing table at each node, which contains information about the destination, the next hop, and the distance to the destination. 

Here are some key features of DSDV:

- **Sequence numbers:** DSDV uses sequence numbers to ensure that the most recent routing information is used. Each time a node updates its routing table, it increments the sequence number for that destination. When a neighboring node receives an update with a higher sequence number, it discards the old information.

- **Periodic updates:** DSDV sends periodic updates to ensure that all nodes have the most up-to-date routing information. The frequency of updates can be configured based on the network conditions.

- **Route maintenance:** DSDV uses a route maintenance mechanism to detect link failures and update routing tables accordingly. When a link fails, the affected nodes broadcast this information to their neighbors, which then update their routing tables.

- **Loop prevention:** DSDV uses a technique called "count-to-infinity" to prevent routing loops. When a node receives information about a failed link, it sets the distance to that destination to infinity. This information is propagated through the network, and eventually all nodes converge on the correct routing information.

DSDV has some limitations, including:

- **Routing overhead:** DSDV generates a significant amount of routing overhead due to the periodic updates. This can consume a significant amount of bandwidth, especially in large networks.

- **Slow convergence:** DSDV can be slow to converge after a network topology change. This can result in suboptimal routes being used until the routing tables have fully converged.

- **Static topology:** DSDV assumes a static network topology, which can be a limitation in dynamic environments. If nodes are constantly moving or new nodes are joining the network, DSDV may not be the best routing protocol to use.

Overall, DSDV is a simple and efficient routing protocol that is well-suited for small to medium-sized MANETs with relatively static topologies. However, it may not be the best choice for larger or more dynamic networks.



### Dynamic Source Routing (DSR)

Dynamic Source Routing (DSR) is a routing protocol used in ad hoc networks. It is a reactive protocol that means it only establishes a route when it is needed. Here are some key points about DSR:

- DSR is a source routing protocol, meaning the entire route is included in the packet header.
- Each node in the network maintains a cache of recently used routes to reduce overhead.
- DSR is resilient to topology changes because the source node can dynamically change the route it takes based on the network conditions.
- It is useful in environments where nodes are mobile and the network topology is constantly changing.
- DSR is not suitable for networks with high traffic because the overhead of maintaining the route cache can become significant.

### Advantages of DSR

- DSR is highly adaptable to the changing network conditions and can dynamically establish routes.
- It is relatively simple to implement because the entire route is included in the packet header, which reduces the amount of routing overhead.
- DSR is scalable because it can handle large networks without the need for a centralized control.

### Disadvantages of DSR

- DSR can suffer from high overhead in large networks, where the route cache becomes too large.
- It is not suitable for networks with high traffic because the overhead of maintaining the route cache can become significant.
- DSR is vulnerable to attacks such as packet spoofing and replay attacks because the entire route is included in the packet header.

In conclusion, DSR is a useful routing protocol in ad hoc networks because it is adaptable to changing network conditions and can dynamically establish routes. However, it is not suitable for high traffic networks and is vulnerable to attacks such as packet spoofing and replay attacks.



### Ad Hoc on demand distance vector routing (AODV)

Ad Hoc On-demand Distance Vector (AODV) is a routing protocol used in ad hoc networks to find the shortest path between nodes. It is based on distance vector routing (DVR) and only establishes routes when needed. Here are some key points about AODV:

- AODV is a reactive routing protocol that only establishes routes when needed, reducing the overhead of route discovery.
- It uses sequence numbers to avoid the formation of loops and ensure that the most recent route is used.
- AODV supports both unicast and multicast routing.
- It is suitable for both small and large networks.
- AODV is designed to work in networks with high mobility, where nodes may frequently join and leave the network.
- The protocol has low processing and memory requirements, making it ideal for resource-constrained devices.
- AODV supports both symmetric and asymmetric links.
- The protocol uses a routing table to store the routing information for each node in the network.
- AODV can support multiple routes between nodes, allowing for redundancy and load balancing.
- AODV has been widely used in various applications, such as military networks, disaster response, and sensor networks.

Overall, AODV is a reliable and efficient routing protocol for ad hoc networks that can handle the challenges of mobility and limited resources. Its reactive nature and support for multiple routes make it a popular choice for many applications.



### Temporary Ordered Routing Algorithm (TORA)

- TORA is a distributed routing protocol used in mobile ad hoc networks (MANETs).
- It is a reactive protocol, which means that it only establishes routes when needed.
- TORA is based on the concept of using height as a metric for routing.
- Nodes in the network are assigned a height value, which indicates their position in the network.
- The height value is used to determine the shortest path to a destination node.
- TORA uses three types of packets: query, update, and control.
- When a node needs to send a packet to a destination node, it broadcasts a query packet.
- The destination node responds with an update packet containing its height value.
- The node then broadcasts a control packet to inform the other nodes of the new route.
- TORA is capable of handling network partitions and is resilient to node failures.
- It is also able to adapt to changes in network topology and can quickly establish new routes when needed.
- TORA has been shown to perform well in dense networks with high mobility.
- However, it can suffer from high overhead and can be inefficient in sparse networks.
- TORA is a useful protocol for MANETs and provides a reliable and efficient way of establishing routes in dynamic networks.
