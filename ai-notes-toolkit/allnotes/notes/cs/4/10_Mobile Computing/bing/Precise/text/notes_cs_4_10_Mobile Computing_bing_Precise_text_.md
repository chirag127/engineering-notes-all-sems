### Location Management: HLR-VLR, Hierarchical, Handoffs

Location management is a key aspect of mobile computing, as it allows the network to keep track of the location of mobile devices. This is necessary for the delivery of services such as voice calls and data transmission.

- **HLR-VLR:** The Home Location Register (HLR) and Visitor Location Register (VLR) are two databases used in location management. The HLR stores information about the subscribers of a mobile network, including their current location. The VLR, on the other hand, is a temporary database that stores information about the subscribers currently located in a particular area.

- **Hierarchical:** Location management can be implemented in a hierarchical manner, where the network is divided into multiple levels of hierarchy. At the top level, there is a central database that stores information about the location of all mobile devices. At lower levels, there are regional databases that store information about the location of mobile devices within a particular region.

- **Handoffs:** Handoffs refer to the process of transferring an ongoing call or data session from one base station to another as the mobile device moves between the coverage areas of different base stations. This is necessary to maintain the continuity of the call or data session. Handoffs can be classified into two types: hard handoffs and soft handoffs. In a hard handoff, the connection to the old base station is terminated before the connection to the new base station is established. In a soft handoff, the connection to the old base station is maintained until the connection to the new base station is established.




### Channel Allocation in Cellular Systems

Channel allocation in cellular systems refers to the process of assigning available communication channels to the users in a cellular network. This is an important aspect of mobile computing as it ensures efficient use of the limited frequency spectrum available for wireless communication.

There are two main approaches to channel allocation in cellular systems: fixed channel allocation (FCA) and dynamic channel allocation (DCA).

1. **Fixed Channel Allocation (FCA)**: In FCA, each cell is assigned a predetermined set of channels that are used exclusively by that cell. This approach is simple to implement but can result in inefficient use of the available channels, especially in areas with varying traffic patterns.

2. **Dynamic Channel Allocation (DCA)**: In DCA, channels are not permanently assigned to cells but are allocated dynamically based on the current traffic demand. This approach can result in more efficient use of the available channels but requires more complex algorithms and real-time monitoring of traffic patterns.

Both FCA and DCA can be further divided into different schemes, such as borrowing, channel locking, and hybrid channel allocation, which combine the advantages of both FCA and DCA.

In summary, channel allocation in cellular systems is a crucial aspect of mobile computing that ensures efficient use of the limited frequency spectrum available for wireless communication. There are different approaches to channel allocation, each with its own advantages and disadvantages, and the choice of approach depends on the specific requirements of the cellular network.

### Multiple Access Protocols

Multiple access protocols are used in wireless networking to allow multiple devices to share the same communication channel. These protocols are necessary to prevent collisions and ensure that data is transmitted efficiently.

There are several types of multiple access protocols, including:

1. **Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA):** This protocol assigns a unique frequency band to each user or device. Each user transmits their data on their assigned frequency, preventing interference with other users.

2. **Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA):** This protocol divides the communication channel into time slots. Each user is assigned a specific time slot during which they can transmit their data. This prevents collisions and ensures that each user has an equal opportunity to transmit their data.

3. **Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA):** This protocol assigns a unique code to each user or device. Each user's data is spread across the entire frequency band using their unique code. This allows multiple users to transmit their data simultaneously without interfering with each other.

4. **Carrier Sense Multiple Access (CSMA):** This protocol allows users to listen to the communication channel before transmitting their data. If the channel is busy, the user will wait until it is free before transmitting their data. This helps to prevent collisions and improve the efficiency of the network.

These are some of the most common multiple access protocols used in wireless networking. Each protocol has its own advantages and disadvantages, and the choice of protocol will depend on the specific requirements of the network. ### TCP over Wireless

TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) is a widely used transport layer protocol that provides reliable, connection-oriented communication between two devices. TCP is designed to work over wired networks, where packet loss is mainly due to congestion. However, when TCP is used over wireless networks, the performance of the protocol can be significantly degraded due to the characteristics of wireless networks, such as high bit error rates, frequent disconnections, and handoffs.

Here are some points to consider when using TCP over wireless networks:

1. **Packet Loss**: In wireless networks, packet loss can occur due to various reasons such as interference, fading, and handoffs. TCP interprets packet loss as a sign of congestion and reduces its sending rate, which can result in unnecessary throughput degradation.

2. **Disconnections**: Wireless networks are prone to frequent disconnections due to mobility, interference, and limited coverage. When a disconnection occurs, TCP times out and enters the retransmission phase, which can result in long delays and reduced throughput.

3. **Handoffs**: When a mobile device moves from one base station to another, a handoff occurs. During a handoff, the connection may be temporarily lost, which can result in packet loss and delay. TCP may interpret this as congestion and reduce its sending rate, resulting in reduced throughput.

To address these issues, several enhancements to TCP have been proposed for use over wireless networks. These enhancements include:

1. **Snoop Protocol**: The snoop protocol is a link-layer protocol that caches packets at the base station and retransmits lost packets locally, without involving the sender. This can reduce the delay and improve the throughput of TCP over wireless networks.

2. **Indirect TCP**: Indirect TCP (I-TCP) splits the TCP connection into two separate connections: one between the mobile device and the base station, and another between the base station and the destination. The base station acts as a proxy and handles the wireless part of the connection, while the destination handles the wired part. This can improve the performance of TCP over wireless networks by isolating the wireless part of the connection.

3. **Mobile TCP**: Mobile TCP (M-TCP) is an enhancement to TCP that detects disconnections and handoffs and freezes the TCP state until the connection is re-established. This can prevent unnecessary retransmissions and improve the performance of TCP over wireless networks.

These are some of the key points to consider when using TCP over wireless networks. By understanding the challenges and using appropriate enhancements, the performance of TCP over wireless networks can be significantly improved.

### Mobile IP

Mobile IP is a protocol that allows mobile devices to move between different networks while maintaining a permanent IP address. It is an extension of the Internet Protocol (IP) designed to enable the mobility of devices. Here are some key points to note about Mobile IP:

1. Mobile IP is used to maintain internet connectivity while a device moves between different networks.
2. It allows a device to keep the same IP address even when it moves to a different network.
3. Mobile IP uses two addresses: a home address and a care-of address.
4. The home address is the permanent IP address of the device, while the care-of address is the temporary address assigned to the device when it moves to a new network.
5. Mobile IP uses a home agent and a foreign agent to facilitate communication between the mobile device and the network.
6. The home agent is responsible for forwarding packets to the mobile device's care-of address, while the foreign agent is responsible for delivering the packets to the mobile device.
7. Mobile IP is an important protocol for enabling seamless internet connectivity for mobile devices.




### WAP: Architecture

WAP (Wireless Application Protocol) is a technical standard for accessing information over a mobile wireless network. It is an open international standard for applications that use wireless communication. The WAP architecture is based on a layered model, where each layer has a specific function.

1. **Wireless Application Environment (WAE):** This is the topmost layer of the WAP architecture. It provides a consistent interface for application developers to create content and applications that can be accessed by different types of wireless devices.

2. **Wireless Session Protocol (WSP):** This layer provides a reliable session service between the application and the client. It is responsible for establishing, maintaining, and terminating sessions between the client and the server.

3. **Wireless Transaction Protocol (WTP):** This layer provides a lightweight transaction-oriented protocol for the exchange of data between the client and the server. It is responsible for ensuring the reliable delivery of data.

4. **Wireless Transport Layer Security (WTLS):** This layer provides security services for the communication between the client and the server. It is responsible for ensuring the confidentiality, integrity, and authentication of the data being exchanged.

5. **Wireless Datagram Protocol (WDP):** This is the lowest layer of the WAP architecture. It provides a common interface for the upper layers to access different types of wireless networks, such as GSM, CDMA, and TDMA.

The WAP architecture is designed to be scalable and extensible, allowing for the addition of new services and features as the technology evolves. It provides a framework for the development of wireless applications and services that can be accessed by a wide range of wireless devices.

## Unit 5 - Ad Hoc networks, localization, MAC issues, Routing protocols, global state routing (GSR)

- **Ad Hoc networks** are decentralized wireless networks that do not rely on pre-existing infrastructure such as routers or access points. Instead, nodes in the network communicate directly with each other and cooperate to route data packets.

- **Localization** refers to the process of determining the physical location of nodes in a wireless network. This can be achieved through various techniques such as triangulation, time of arrival, and received signal strength.

- **MAC (Medium Access Control) issues** arise in wireless networks due to the shared nature of the wireless medium. To avoid collisions and ensure efficient use of the medium, various MAC protocols have been developed, such as CSMA/CA and TDMA.

- **Routing protocols** are used to determine the best path for data packets to travel from the source to the destination in a network. In ad hoc networks, routing protocols must be able to handle the dynamic nature of the network, where nodes can join, leave, or move at any time.

- **Global State Routing (GSR)** is a type of routing protocol that maintains global information about the network topology. Each node maintains a complete view of the network and uses this information to make routing decisions. GSR can be more efficient in stable networks, but may not perform well in highly dynamic networks.



### Destination sequenced distance vector routing (DSDV)

- Destination sequenced distance vector routing (DSDV) is a table-driven routing scheme for ad hoc mobile networks based on the Bellman–Ford algorithm.
- It was developed by C. Perkins and P. Bhagwat in 1994.
- The main contribution of the algorithm was to solve the routing loop problem.
- DSDV is adapted from the conventional Routing Information Protocol (RIP) to ad hoc networks routing.
- It adds a new attribute, sequence number, to each route table entry of the conventional RIP.
- Using the newly added sequence number, the mobile nodes can distinguish stale route information from the new and thus prevent the formation of routing loops.
- Each entry in the routing table contains a sequence number.
- DSDV routing protocol is a pro-active, table-driven routing protocol for MANETs.
- It uses the hop count as metric in route selection.




### Dynamic Source Routing (DSR)

- The Dynamic Source Routing protocol (DSR) is a simple and efficient routing protocol designed specifically for use in multi-hop wireless ad hoc networks of mobile nodes.
- DSR allows the network to be completely self-organizing and self-configuring, without the need for any existing network infrastructure or administration.
- DSR is a routing protocol for wireless mesh networks.
- It is similar to AODV in that it forms a route on-demand when a transmitting node requests one.
- However, it uses source routing instead of relying on the routing table at each intermediate device.
- DSR is an on-demand protocol designed to restrict the bandwidth consumed by control packets in ad hoc wireless networks by eliminating the periodic table-update messages required in the table-driven approach.



### Ad Hoc On-Demand Distance Vector Routing (AODV)

Ad Hoc On-Demand Distance Vector Routing (AODV) is a routing protocol designed for ad hoc mobile networks. It is capable of both unicast and multicast routing. AODV is an on-demand algorithm, meaning that it builds routes between nodes only as desired by source nodes. It maintains these routes as long as they are needed by the sources.

Some key features of AODV include:
- **Route discovery:** AODV uses a broadcast route discovery mechanism to find a route to the destination node. The source node broadcasts a route request (RREQ) packet to its neighbors, which then forward the request to their neighbors, and so on, until the destination node is reached or an intermediate node with a fresh enough route to the destination is found.
- **Route maintenance:** AODV uses route error (RERR) messages to notify the source node of a broken link. When a node detects a link break, it generates a RERR message and sends it to the source node. The source node can then initiate a new route discovery process to find a new route to the destination.
- **Sequence numbers:** AODV uses sequence numbers to ensure the freshness of routes. Each node maintains a sequence number for itself and for each destination it has a route to. The sequence number is incremented whenever a node detects a change in the topology of the network.
- **Hop-by-hop routing:** AODV uses hop-by-hop routing, meaning that each node along the route forwards packets to the next hop, rather than the source node specifying the entire route in the packet header.

AODV is well suited for ad hoc networks with a large number of nodes and high mobility, where the network topology changes frequently. It has low overhead and can quickly adapt to changes in the network topology. However, it may not perform as well in networks with low mobility or in networks with a small number of nodes.



### Temporary Ordered Routing Algorithm (TORA)

Temporary Ordered Routing Algorithm (TORA) is a distributed routing protocol designed for multi-hop mobile ad hoc networks. It is a highly adaptive, efficient, and scalable protocol that can be used for both proactive and reactive routing. Some of the key features of TORA are:

1. TORA uses a "link reversal" algorithm to establish routes between nodes in the network.
2. It is a source-initiated, on-demand routing protocol, meaning that routes are only established when they are needed by the source node.
3. TORA uses a height metric to determine the direction of data flow in the network. Each node in the network is assigned a height value, and data flows from nodes with higher heights to nodes with lower heights.
4. TORA can quickly adapt to changes in the network topology, such as the addition or removal of nodes or links.
5. It is capable of handling multiple routes between a source and destination, providing increased robustness and fault tolerance.

In summary, TORA is a highly adaptive and efficient routing protocol for mobile ad hoc networks, capable of quickly adapting to changes in the network topology and providing multiple routes between nodes. It is a valuable tool for use in dynamic, rapidly changing network environments.



### QoS in Ad Hoc Networks

Quality of Service (QoS) refers to the ability of a network to provide improved service to certain network traffic. In the context of Ad Hoc Networks, QoS is important because these networks are often used for real-time applications such as voice and video, which require a certain level of service to function properly.

Some of the challenges in providing QoS in Ad Hoc Networks include:

1. **Dynamic topology:** The topology of an Ad Hoc Network can change frequently due to the mobility of nodes. This makes it difficult to establish and maintain QoS routes.

2. **Limited resources:** Ad Hoc Networks often have limited resources such as bandwidth and power. This makes it challenging to provide QoS guarantees.

3. **Interference:** Ad Hoc Networks are often subject to interference from other wireless devices. This can affect the ability to provide QoS.

To address these challenges, various QoS mechanisms have been proposed for Ad Hoc Networks. These include:

1. **QoS routing protocols:** These protocols aim to establish routes that meet the QoS requirements of the traffic. Examples include QoS-AODV and QoS-DSR.

2. **MAC layer mechanisms:** These mechanisms aim to provide QoS at the MAC layer by prioritizing traffic and managing access to the wireless medium. Examples include IEEE 802.11e.

3. **Cross-layer mechanisms:** These mechanisms aim to provide QoS by coordinating the operation of different layers of the protocol stack. Examples include QoS-aware power control and QoS-aware routing.

Overall, providing QoS in Ad Hoc Networks is a challenging problem that requires the use of multiple mechanisms at different layers of the protocol stack. Further research is needed to develop more effective solutions to this problem.
