- The air-interface is a critical component of any mobile network, and ongoing research and development efforts are focused on improving its performance and capabilities.



### Channel Structure

- In mobile computing, the channel structure refers to the way in which the available frequency spectrum is divided and allocated to different users or devices.
- The channel structure is an important aspect of wireless telephony and cellular networks, as it determines the capacity and performance of the network.

- In the Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM), the channel structure is based on a combination of frequency division multiple access (FDMA) and time division multiple access (TDMA). This means that the available frequency spectrum is divided into multiple channels, and each channel is further divided into time slots.
- The channel structure in GSM is designed to support a large number of users while minimizing interference and maximizing spectral efficiency.

### Location Management: HLR-VLR, Hierarchical, Handoffs

Location management is a key component of mobile computing, allowing the network to track the location of mobile devices and facilitate communication. There are several methods for location management, including the use of Home Location Register (HLR) and Visitor Location Register (VLR), hierarchical location management, and handoffs.

- **HLR-VLR:** The HLR is a central database that contains information about the subscribers of a mobile network, including their current location. The VLR is a local database that temporarily stores information about the subscribers currently located in its coverage area. When a mobile device moves from one location to another, the VLR of the new location communicates with the HLR to update the subscriber's location information.

- **Hierarchical Location Management:** This method organizes the network into a hierarchy of location areas, with each level responsible for tracking the location of mobile devices within its coverage area. When a mobile device moves from one location area to another, the location update is performed at the appropriate level of the hierarchy.

- **Handoffs:** A handoff occurs when a mobile device moves from one cell to another and the call is transferred from one base station to another. This process is necessary to maintain the continuity of the call as the mobile device moves through the network. Handoff algorithms are used to determine when and how the handoff should occur.

These are some of the key concepts related to location management in mobile computing. Understanding these concepts is important for understanding the overall functioning of mobile networks and the issues involved in mobile computing.



### Channel Allocation in Cellular Systems

Channel allocation in cellular systems refers to the process of assigning available communication channels to the users in a cellular network. This is an important aspect of mobile computing as it ensures efficient use of the limited frequency spectrum available for wireless communication.

There are two main approaches to channel allocation in cellular systems: fixed channel allocation (FCA) and dynamic channel allocation (DCA).

1. **Fixed Channel Allocation (FCA):** In FCA, each cell is assigned a predetermined set of channels that are used exclusively by that cell. This approach is simple to implement but can result in inefficient use of the available channels, particularly in areas where the traffic demand varies significantly over time.

2. **Dynamic Channel Allocation (DCA):** In DCA, channels are not permanently assigned to cells but are allocated dynamically based on the current traffic demand. This approach can result in more efficient use of the available channels but requires more complex algorithms and real-time monitoring of the traffic demand.

Both FCA and DCA can be further divided into different schemes, such as borrowing, channel locking, and hybrid channel allocation, which combine the advantages of both FCA and DCA.

In summary, channel allocation in cellular systems is a crucial aspect of mobile computing that ensures efficient use of the limited frequency spectrum available for wireless communication. There are two main approaches to channel allocation, fixed channel allocation and dynamic channel allocation, each with its own advantages and disadvantages.

### Multiple Access Protocols

Multiple access protocols are used in wireless networking to coordinate the access of multiple devices to a shared communication medium. These protocols are necessary to prevent collisions and ensure that data is transmitted efficiently and reliably.

There are several types of multiple access protocols, including:

1. **Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA):** This protocol assigns a unique frequency band to each device, allowing them to transmit data simultaneously without interference.

2. **Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA):** This protocol divides time into slots and assigns each device a unique time slot for transmission. This allows multiple devices to share the same frequency band without interference.

3. **Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA):** This protocol assigns a unique code to each device, allowing them to transmit data simultaneously on the same frequency band. The receiver uses the code to separate the signals from different devices.

4. **Carrier Sense Multiple Access (CSMA):** This protocol requires devices to listen to the communication medium before transmitting data. If the medium is busy, the device waits until it is free before transmitting.

In the context of wireless LANs, the IEEE 802.11 standard specifies the use of CSMA/CA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance) as the multiple access protocol. This protocol is similar to CSMA, but includes additional mechanisms to avoid collisions.

Bluetooth, on the other hand, uses a combination of TDMA and FHSS (Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum) as its multiple access protocol. This allows multiple Bluetooth devices to communicate simultaneously without interference.

In summary, multiple access protocols are essential for efficient and reliable wireless communication. Different protocols are used in different wireless technologies, depending on their specific requirements and characteristics.



### TCP over Wireless

TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) is a reliable, connection-oriented protocol that is widely used in wired networks. However, when used over wireless networks, TCP faces several challenges due to the unique characteristics of wireless networks.

1. **Packet Loss:** In wired networks, packet loss is mainly caused by network congestion. However, in wireless networks, packet loss can also occur due to factors such as signal fading, interference, and handoffs. TCP interprets all packet loss as a sign of congestion and responds by reducing its sending rate, which can result in unnecessary throughput degradation in wireless networks.

2. **Variable Bandwidth:** Wireless networks often have variable bandwidth due to factors such as changing signal strength and interference. TCP's congestion control mechanism is designed for networks with relatively stable bandwidth and may not perform well in the presence of large bandwidth variations.

3. **High Bit Error Rate:** Wireless networks often have a higher bit error rate than wired networks due to factors such as signal fading and interference. This can result in a higher rate of packet loss and retransmissions, which can reduce TCP's performance.

4. **Mobility:** In wireless networks, nodes may move frequently, resulting in changes in network topology and routing paths. This can cause packet loss and delay, which can affect TCP's performance.

To address these challenges, several modifications to TCP have been proposed for use in wireless networks. These include:

1. **Explicit Loss Notification:** In this approach, the network provides explicit feedback to the sender about the cause of packet loss (e.g., congestion or wireless link error). This allows the sender to distinguish between congestion and non-congestion related packet loss and adjust its sending rate accordingly.

2. **Split TCP:** In this approach, the end-to-end TCP connection is split into multiple connections, with each connection responsible for a different segment of the end-to-end path. This allows the TCP sender to adapt its sending rate to the characteristics of each segment of the path.

3. **TCP with Adaptive Retransmission:** In this approach, the TCP sender adapts its retransmission timeout value based on the characteristics of the wireless link. This can reduce the number of unnecessary retransmissions and improve TCP's performance.

These are some of the challenges and proposed solutions for using TCP over wireless networks. Further study and research is needed to fully understand and address the challenges of using TCP in wireless environments.

### Mobile IP

Mobile IP is a protocol that allows mobile devices to move from one network to another while maintaining their IP address. It is an extension of the Internet Protocol (IP) and is designed to enable mobile computing devices to seamlessly roam among different IP networks.

Here are some key points to note about Mobile IP:

1. Mobile IP is used to maintain a stable IP address for a mobile device, even as it moves between different networks.
2. The protocol uses two IP addresses: a home address and a care-of address. The home address is the permanent IP address of the mobile device, while the care-of address is the temporary IP address assigned to the device when it is connected to a foreign network.
3. Mobile IP uses a home agent and a foreign agent to facilitate communication between the mobile device and the network. The home agent is responsible for forwarding packets from the home network to the mobile device, while the foreign agent is responsible for forwarding packets from the foreign network to the mobile device.
4. Mobile IP is transparent to applications and higher-level protocols, meaning that they do not need to be modified to work with Mobile IP.
5. Mobile IP is not limited to wireless networks and can be used with any type of IP network.




### WAP: Architecture

Wireless Application Protocol (WAP) is a technical standard for accessing information over a mobile wireless network. The WAP architecture is based on a layered model, where each layer is responsible for a specific function in the protocol stack.

1. **Wireless Datagram Protocol (WDP)**: This layer is responsible for adapting the transport layer of the underlying wireless network to present a consistent data format to the higher layers of the WAP protocol stack.

2. **Wireless Transaction Protocol (WTP)**: This layer provides a lightweight transaction-oriented protocol suitable for implementation in thin clients.

3. **Wireless Session Protocol (WSP)**: This layer provides the upper-level application layer of WAP with a consistent interface for two session services.

4. **Wireless Application Environment (WAE)**: This layer provides a standard environment for developing and executing wireless applications.

The WAP architecture is designed to be scalable, allowing for the addition of new services and features as the technology evolves. It also provides a high level of security through the use of encryption and authentication mechanisms.

### Destination Sequenced Distance Vector Routing (DSDV)

Destination Sequenced Distance Vector Routing (DSDV) is a proactive routing protocol for ad hoc networks. It is based on the Bellman-Ford algorithm and was developed to solve the routing loop problem. In DSDV, each node maintains a routing table that contains the shortest distance and the first node on the shortest path to every other node in the network. The routing table is updated periodically to maintain the most up-to-date routing information.

Some key features of DSDV are:
- Proactive: DSDV is a proactive routing protocol, meaning that it maintains routing information for all nodes in the network at all times.
- Loop-free: DSDV uses sequence numbers to ensure that the routes are loop-free.
- Periodic updates: The routing tables are updated periodically to maintain the most up-to-date routing information.
- Bellman-Ford algorithm: DSDV is based on the Bellman-Ford algorithm, which is used to find the shortest path between nodes.

DSDV is suitable for small networks with low mobility, as the periodic updates can generate a large amount of control overhead in large, highly mobile networks. In such scenarios, reactive routing protocols such as AODV or DSR may be more suitable.

### Dynamic Source Routing (DSR)

Dynamic Source Routing (DSR) is a routing protocol for wireless mesh networks and mobile ad hoc networks (MANETs). It is used to find a route between two nodes in the network. Here are some key points to note about DSR:

1. DSR is based on the concept of source routing, where the sender of a packet determines the complete sequence of nodes through which the packet must pass.
2. The protocol is composed of two main mechanisms: Route Discovery and Route Maintenance.
3. Route Discovery is used to find a route between the source and destination nodes. The source node broadcasts a Route Request (RREQ) packet to its neighbors, which is then forwarded until it reaches the destination node or a node that has a route to the destination.
4. The destination node or the intermediate node with a route to the destination sends a Route Reply (RREP) packet back to the source node along the reverse path.

### QoS in Ad Hoc Networks

Quality of Service (QoS) refers to the ability of a network to provide improved service to certain network traffic. In the context of Ad Hoc Networks, QoS is important because it can help ensure that critical data is transmitted with minimal delay and packet loss.

Some of the challenges in providing QoS in Ad Hoc Networks include:

1. **Dynamic topology:** The topology of an Ad Hoc Network can change frequently due to the mobility of nodes. This can make it difficult to establish and maintain QoS routes.

2. **Limited resources:** Ad Hoc Networks often have limited bandwidth and power resources. This can make it challenging to provide QoS guarantees, especially in scenarios with high traffic demands.

3. **Interference:** Wireless transmissions in Ad Hoc Networks can interfere with each other, leading to packet loss and reduced network performance. This can make it difficult to provide QoS guarantees.

Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges and provide QoS in Ad Hoc Networks. These include:

1. **QoS-aware routing protocols:** These protocols aim to establish routes that meet the QoS requirements of different traffic flows. Examples include QoS-AODV and QoS-DSR.

2. **Resource reservation:** This approach involves reserving resources such as bandwidth and power to ensure that QoS guarantees can be met.

3. **Traffic prioritization:** This approach involves prioritizing traffic based on its QoS requirements. For example, real-time traffic such as voice and video may be given higher priority than non-real-time traffic such as file transfers.

4. **Admission control:** This approach involves controlling the admission of new traffic flows into the network to ensure that QoS guarantees can be met for existing flows.

In summary, providing QoS in Ad Hoc Networks is challenging due to the dynamic nature of the network and limited resources. However, several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges and provide improved service to critical network traffic. These include QoS-aware routing protocols, resource reservation, traffic prioritization, and admission control.
