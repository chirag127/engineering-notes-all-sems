### Channel Structure

In the context of wireless telephony and cellular networks, the channel structure refers to the way in which the available frequency spectrum is divided and allocated for communication between the mobile devices and the base stations.

1. **Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA):** This method divides the available frequency spectrum into multiple channels, each with a specific frequency range. Each user is assigned a unique channel for communication.

2. **Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA):** This method divides the available time into multiple time slots. Each user is assigned a unique time slot for communication, allowing multiple users to share the same frequency channel.

3. **Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA):** This method assigns a unique code to each user, allowing multiple users to share the same frequency channel simultaneously. The signals from different users are separated using the assigned codes.

These are the three main methods used for channel allocation in cellular networks. Each method has its own advantages and disadvantages, and the choice of method depends on various factors such as the number of users, the available frequency spectrum, and the desired level of security.



# Location Management: HLR-VLR, Hierarchical, Handoffs

Location management is a key aspect of mobile computing, as it allows the network to keep track of the location of mobile devices and route calls and data to them. There are several techniques used for location management, including HLR-VLR, hierarchical, and handoffs.

- **HLR-VLR**: HLR (Home Location Register) and VLR (Visitor Location Register) are databases used in cellular networks to keep track of the location of mobile devices. The HLR contains information about the subscribers of the network, including their current location, while the VLR temporarily stores information about roaming subscribers in the area it serves.

- **Hierarchical**: Hierarchical location management is a technique that organizes the network into a hierarchy of location areas, with each level responsible for tracking the location of mobile devices within its area. This can reduce the signaling overhead and improve the scalability of the network.

- **Handoffs**: Handoffs are the process of transferring an ongoing call or data session from one base station to another as the mobile device moves between the coverage areas of the two stations. This allows the call or data session to continue uninterrupted as the mobile device moves.

These techniques are used in wireless telephony, including the cellular concept and the Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM), to provide efficient and reliable location management for mobile devices. They are an important part of the overall architecture of mobile computing systems.



# Channel Allocation in Cellular Systems

Channel allocation in cellular systems refers to the process of assigning available communication channels to users within a cellular network. This is an important aspect of mobile computing, as it ensures efficient use of the limited frequency spectrum available for wireless communication.

There are several methods for channel allocation in cellular systems, including:

1. Fixed Channel Allocation (FCA): In this method, each cell is assigned a predetermined set of channels that are used exclusively within that cell. This method is simple to implement, but can result in inefficient use of the available channels.

2. Dynamic Channel Allocation (DCA): In this method, channels are assigned to cells on an as-needed basis. This allows for more efficient use of the available channels, but requires more complex algorithms and management.

3. Hybrid Channel Allocation: This method combines elements of both FCA and DCA, allowing for both predetermined channel assignments and dynamic allocation of channels as needed.

In the context of wireless telephony, the cellular concept refers to the division of a geographic area into smaller cells, each served by its own base station. This allows for more efficient use of the available frequency spectrum, as the same channels can be reused in different cells, provided they are separated by a sufficient distance to avoid interference.

The Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) is a widely used standard for mobile telephony. It employs a combination of FCA and DCA for channel allocation, with channels being assigned to cells based on traffic demand and interference considerations.

In summary, channel allocation in cellular systems is an important aspect of mobile computing, allowing for efficient use of the available frequency spectrum for wireless communication. Various methods, including FCA, DCA, and hybrid approaches, can be used to allocate channels within a cellular network. The cellular concept and standards such as GSM employ these methods to provide efficient and reliable mobile communication services.

# Multiple Access Protocols

Multiple access protocols are used in wireless networking to allow multiple devices to share the same communication channel. These protocols are necessary to ensure that data transmission is efficient and to avoid collisions or interference between devices.

There are several types of multiple access protocols, including:

1. **Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA):** This protocol assigns a unique frequency band to each device, allowing them to transmit data simultaneously without interference.

2. **Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA):** This protocol divides the communication channel into time slots and assigns each device a unique time slot for data transmission.

3. **Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA):** This protocol assigns a unique code to each device, allowing them to transmit data simultaneously on the same frequency band without interference.

4. **Carrier Sense Multiple Access (CSMA):** This protocol allows devices to listen to the communication channel before transmitting data to avoid collisions.

These protocols are used in various wireless networking technologies, including IEEE 802.11 (Wi-Fi) and Bluetooth.

In the context of wireless LANs, the IEEE 802.11 standard specifies the use of CSMA/CA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance) as the multiple access protocol. This protocol is designed to avoid collisions by using a "listen before talk" approach, where devices listen to the communication channel before transmitting data.

Bluetooth, on the other hand, uses a combination of TDMA and FHSS (Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum) as its multiple access protocol. This allows multiple devices to share the same frequency band by hopping between different frequencies in a predetermined pattern.

In summary, multiple access protocols are essential for efficient data transmission in wireless networking. Different technologies use different protocols, and it is important to understand the differences and how they work to effectively design and implement wireless networks.



# TCP over Wireless

TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) is a protocol that enables the scalability of the internet through its flow control mechanisms. It is used for web browsing, which is expected to be the dominant application in wireless data networks .

Wireless networks, such as 3G networks, exhibit high delays and high error rates. TCP assumes a relatively reliable underlying network where most packet losses are due to congestion. However, in a wireless network, packet losses will occur more often due to unreliable wireless links than due to congestion .

To deal with packet losses due to fading, shadowing, and contention, TCP optimization for wireless networks should preferably maintain TCP end-to-end semantics with minimal dependence on intermediate nodes. The development of advanced 3G networks and services makes it necessary to find a way of improving TCP's efficiency and resource utilization .

One solution is to split the TCP connection at the wireless interface, namely, the base station, which in turn uses some other reliable connection to connect to the destination. This solution is referred to as split-connection .

The desired attributes of a solution for TCP over wireless networks are that it must maintain TCP’s end-to-end semantics, meaning that a packet is acknowledged only after being received by the final destination. Additionally, modifications must be local, meaning that only the Base Station (BS) and Mobile Host (MH) are in the control of the wireless service provider. It is not possible to change all locations that the MH visits .

### Mobile IP

Mobile IP is a protocol that allows mobile devices to maintain their internet connection while moving between different networks. It is a standard developed by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) and is defined in RFC 5944.

Here are some key points to remember about Mobile IP:

1. Mobile IP is designed to enable seamless roaming of mobile devices across different IP networks.
2. It allows a mobile device to use the same IP address, regardless of its location, and to maintain ongoing connections while moving between networks.
3. Mobile IP uses two addresses: a home address and a care-of address. The home address is the permanent IP address of the mobile device, while the care-of address is the temporary address assigned to the device while it is connected to a foreign network.
4. Mobile IP uses a home agent and a foreign agent to facilitate communication between the mobile device and the network. The home agent is located on the home network, while the foreign agent is located on the foreign network.
5. When a mobile device moves to a new network, it registers its new care-of address with its home agent. The home agent then forwards any packets addressed to the mobile device's home address to its care-of address.
6. Mobile IP can be used with both IPv4 and IPv6 networks.




# WAP: Architecture

Wireless Application Protocol (WAP) is a technical standard for accessing information over a mobile wireless network. It is a protocol designed for micro-browsers and it enables the creation of web applications for mobile devices.

The WAP architecture is based on a layered model, where each layer is responsible for a specific function. The layers are:

1. **Wireless Application Environment (WAE):** This layer is responsible for providing the content and user interface for WAP applications. It includes the Wireless Markup Language (WML), which is used to create the content and layout of WAP pages.

2. **Wireless Session Protocol (WSP):** This layer is responsible for providing a reliable session service between the client and the server. It includes features such as connection suspension and reconnection, which are important for mobile devices that may lose connectivity.

3. **Wireless Transaction Protocol (WTP):** This layer is responsible for providing a lightweight transaction-oriented service. It is used to support reliable request/response transactions between the client and the server.

4. **Wireless Transport Layer Security (WTLS):** This layer is responsible for providing security services such as authentication, data integrity, and confidentiality. It is based on the Transport Layer Security (TLS) protocol.

5. **Wireless Datagram Protocol (WDP):** This layer is responsible for providing a common interface for different wireless data bearers such as SMS, CSD, and GPRS. It allows WAP to be bearer-independent.

The WAP architecture is designed to be scalable and extensible, allowing for the addition of new services and features as needed. It provides a framework for the development of mobile web applications that can be accessed by a wide range of devices.

### Protocol Stack

A protocol stack is a set of network protocol layers that work together to provide network communication. Each layer in the stack provides a specific function and communicates with the layers above and below it. The protocol stack for wireless networking typically includes the following layers:

1. **Physical Layer:** This layer is responsible for transmitting and receiving data over the wireless medium. It defines the characteristics of the wireless signal, such as frequency, modulation, and power.

2. **Data Link Layer:** This layer is responsible for providing reliable data transmission over the wireless medium. It includes the Media Access Control (MAC) sublayer, which controls access to the wireless medium, and the Logical Link Control (LLC) sublayer, which provides error control and flow control.

3. **Network Layer:** This layer is responsible for routing data between devices on the network. It uses protocols such as the Internet Protocol (IP) to route data between devices.

4. **Transport Layer:** This layer is responsible for providing end-to-end communication between applications on different devices. It uses protocols such as the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) to provide reliable data transmission and the User Datagram Protocol (UDP) to provide fast, but unreliable, data transmission.

5. **Application Layer:** This layer is responsible for providing application-specific services, such as file transfer, email, and web browsing. It includes protocols such as the Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) and the File Transfer Protocol (FTP).

In wireless networking, the IEEE 802.11 standard defines the specifications for the physical and data link layers, while the other layers are typically defined by the Internet Protocol Suite (TCP/IP). Bluetooth, on the other hand, is a short-range wireless technology that uses a different protocol stack, with its own specifications for the physical, data link, and application layers.

# Destination Sequenced Distance Vector Routing (DSDV)

Destination Sequenced Distance Vector Routing (DSDV) is a proactive routing protocol for ad hoc networks. It is based on the Bellman-Ford algorithm and was developed to solve the routing loop problem. In DSDV, each node maintains a routing table that contains the shortest distance and the first node on the shortest path to every other node in the network. The routing table is updated periodically to maintain the most recent route information.

Some key features of DSDV are:
- Each node maintains a routing table that contains the shortest distance and the first node on the shortest path to every other node in the network.
- The routing table is updated periodically to maintain the most recent route information.
- Each entry in the routing table is marked with a sequence number that is originated by the destination node.
- The sequence numbers are used to distinguish stale routes from new ones and to prevent routing loops.
- Each node transmits updates containing its routing table information to its neighbors.
- The updates are both time-driven and event-driven.
- If there is a change in the network topology, the affected nodes transmit updates immediately, instead of waiting for the next periodic update.

DSDV is a simple and efficient routing protocol for ad hoc networks. However, it has some limitations, such as the overhead of periodic updates and the slow convergence in the face of network topology changes. These limitations have led to the development of other routing protocols for ad hoc networks, such as Ad hoc On-Demand Distance Vector (AODV) and Dynamic Source Routing (DSR).



### Dynamic Source Routing (DSR)

Dynamic Source Routing (DSR) is a routing protocol designed specifically for use in multi-hop wireless ad hoc networks of mobile nodes. It is simple and efficient, allowing the network to be completely self-organizing and self-configuring, without the need for any existing network infrastructure or administration  .

- DSR is considered one of the discrete and predominant routing protocols for Mobile Ad Hoc Networks (MANETs) .
- DSR finds the path from the source node to the destination node only when the source starts the process of route discovery .
- DSR is similar to AODV in that it forms a route on-demand when a transmitting node requests one .
- However, DSR uses source routing instead of relying on the routing table at each intermediate device .



# Ad Hoc on demand distance vector routing (AODV)

Ad Hoc on demand distance vector routing (AODV) is a routing protocol for ad hoc mobile networks. It is part of Unit 5 - Ad Hoc networks, localization, MAC issues, Routing protocols, global state routing (GSR), in the subject of Mobile Computing. Here are some key points to note about AODV:

1. AODV is an on-demand routing protocol, meaning that routes are established only when needed.
2. It uses a destination sequence number to ensure loop-free and up-to-date routes.
3. Route discovery in AODV is done through the use of Route Request (RREQ) and Route Reply (RREP) messages.
4. When a node needs to establish a route to a destination, it broadcasts a RREQ message to its neighbors.
5. The RREQ message is propagated through the network until it reaches the destination or a node with a fresh enough route to the destination.
6. The destination or the intermediate node with a fresh enough route then sends a RREP message back to the source, establishing the route.
7. Route maintenance is done through the use of Route Error (RERR) messages and HELLO messages.
8. If a link break is detected, a RERR message is sent to the affected upstream nodes to inform them of the link break.
9. HELLO messages are used to detect link breaks between neighbors.
10. AODV has low overhead and is scalable to large networks.

### QoS in Ad Hoc Networks

Quality of Service (QoS) refers to the ability of a network to provide improved service to certain network traffic. In the context of Ad Hoc Networks, QoS is important because it can help ensure that critical data is transmitted with minimal delay and packet loss.

Some of the challenges in providing QoS in Ad Hoc Networks include:

1. **Dynamic topology:** The topology of an Ad Hoc Network can change frequently due to the mobility of nodes. This can make it difficult to establish and maintain QoS routes.

2. **Limited resources:** Ad Hoc Networks often have limited bandwidth and power resources. This can make it challenging to provide QoS guarantees, especially in high traffic scenarios.

3. **Interference:** Wireless transmissions in Ad Hoc Networks can interfere with each other, leading to packet loss and reduced network performance. This can make it difficult to provide QoS guarantees.

Several approaches have been proposed to provide QoS in Ad Hoc Networks, including:

1. **QoS routing protocols:** These protocols aim to establish routes that meet the QoS requirements of different traffic flows. Examples include QoS-AODV and QoS-DSR.

2. **MAC layer solutions:** These solutions aim to provide QoS at the MAC layer by prioritizing certain traffic flows. Examples include IEEE 802.11e and Blackburst.

3. **Cross-layer approaches:** These approaches aim to provide QoS by coordinating the operation of different layers of the network stack. Examples include CACP and QoS-OLSR.

Overall, providing QoS in Ad Hoc Networks is a challenging problem that requires a combination of approaches at different layers of the network stack. Further research is needed to develop effective solutions that can provide QoS guarantees in dynamic and resource-constrained Ad Hoc Networks.
