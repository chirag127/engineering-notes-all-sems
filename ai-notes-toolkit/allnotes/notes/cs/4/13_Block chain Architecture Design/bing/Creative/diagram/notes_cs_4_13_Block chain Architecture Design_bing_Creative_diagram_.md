

## Unit 1 - Introduction to Blockchain

- A blockchain is a **digital ledger** that stores data of any kind across a network of computers .
- A blockchain can record information about **cryptocurrency transactions**, **NFT ownership** or **DeFi smart contracts**.
- A blockchain is **decentralized**, meaning that no single entity controls or owns the data .
- A blockchain is **distributed**, meaning that the data is replicated and synchronized among all the nodes in the network .
- A blockchain is **public**, meaning that anyone can access and verify the data .
- A blockchain is **secure**, meaning that the data is protected by **cryptographic hashes** and **consensus algorithms** that prevent tampering or fraud .
- A blockchain is **immutable**, meaning that the data cannot be altered or deleted once it is recorded .
- A blockchain consists of **blocks** that are linked together by **chains** .
- Each block contains a **cryptographic hash** of the previous block, a **timestamp**, and **transaction data** .
- A blockchain is updated by **adding new blocks** to the chain, which requires the **validation** of the network participants .
- A blockchain is a **system** in which a record of transactions made in **bitcoin** or another **cryptocurrency** is maintained across several computers that are linked in a **peer-to-peer network** .
- A blockchain is a **technology** that enables the creation of **decentralized applications** (DApps) that can run on the network without intermediaries or censorship .
- A blockchain is a **revolutionary** innovation that has the potential to transform various industries and sectors, such as **finance**, **supply chain**, **healthcare**, **gaming**, **art**, and more .

### Consensus

- Consensus is the process of reaching agreement among a group of participants on a shared state of data or a common decision.
- Consensus is essential for blockchain systems, as they are distributed, decentralized, and trustless networks that need to maintain consistency and validity of transactions and data.
- Consensus algorithms are the rules and mechanisms that enable the participants of a blockchain network to reach consensus, despite the possibility of failures, errors, or malicious attacks.
- Consensus algorithms can be classified into two broad categories: **permissionless** and **permissioned**.
  - Permissionless consensus algorithms are used in public blockchains, where anyone can join and participate in the network. They are designed to be resilient to censorship, collusion, and Sybil attacks, but they often sacrifice scalability and efficiency. Examples of permissionless consensus algorithms are **Proof-of-Work (PoW)**, **Proof-of-Stake (PoS)**, and **Delegated Proof-of-Stake (DPoS)**.
  - Permissioned consensus algorithms are used in private or consortium blockchains, where the participants are known and authorized by a central authority or a group of trusted entities. They are designed to achieve high performance, scalability, and finality, but they often sacrifice decentralization and security. Examples of permissioned consensus algorithms are **Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT)**, **Raft**, and **Tendermint**.
- Consensus algorithms can also be classified based on the type of **fault tolerance** they provide, which is the ability to withstand failures or errors in the network.
  - **Crash fault tolerance (CFT)** is the ability to tolerate nodes that stop functioning or crash, but do not behave maliciously. CFT algorithms can tolerate up to **n/2** faulty nodes, where **n** is the total number of nodes in the network. Examples of CFT algorithms are **Raft** and **Paxos**.
  - **Byzantine fault tolerance (BFT)** is the ability to tolerate nodes that behave arbitrarily or maliciously, such as sending conflicting or invalid messages. BFT algorithms can tolerate up to **n/3** faulty nodes, where **n** is the total number of nodes in the network. Examples of BFT algorithms are **PBFT**, **Tendermint**, and **HotStuff**.
  - **Nakamoto consensus** is a term coined by Vitalik Buterin to describe the probabilistic and asynchronous consensus achieved by PoW-based blockchains, such as Bitcoin and Ethereum. Nakamoto consensus relies on the longest chain rule, where the chain with the most accumulated proof-of-work is considered the valid one. Nakamoto consensus can tolerate up to **n/2** faulty nodes, where **n** is the total number of nodes in the network, but it does not guarantee finality, as forks or reorganizations can occur.

### Privacy for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction to Blockchain in the subject of Block chain Architecture Design

- Privacy is the ability to control the access and disclosure of personal or sensitive information.
- Privacy in blockchains is rather complicated as it contradicts with some highly praised properties of blockchain such as immutability.
- Immutability is considered a cornerstone of blockchains’ security and, therefore, an indisputable property according to which transactional blockchain data cannot be edited nor deleted.
- However, immutability also poses a challenge for privacy, as it means that once data is recorded on the blockchain, it cannot be erased or modified, even if it contains personal or confidential information.
- Moreover, public blockchains, which are available to any node that wishes to download the network, offer little privacy, as everyone can access the history of transactions and trace the identities and activities of the participants.
- Therefore, privacy in blockchains requires the use of various techniques and mechanisms to protect the data and the identities of the users, while preserving the integrity and transparency of the system.
- Some of the techniques and mechanisms for privacy in blockchains are:

  - Cryptography: A key aspect of privacy in blockchains is the use of private and public keys. Blockchain systems use asymmetric cryptography to secure transactions between users. In these systems, each user has a public and private key. These keys are random strings of numbers and are cryptographically related. The public key is used to identify the user and to verify the authenticity of the transactions. The private key is used to sign the transactions and to decrypt the data. The private key should be kept secret and never shared with anyone, as it is the only way to access the user's funds and data. Cryptography also enables the use of other techniques such as encryption, hashing, digital signatures, zero-knowledge proofs, and homomorphic encryption, which can enhance the privacy and security of the data and the transactions on the blockchain.
  - Permissioned blockchains: Private blockchains (or permissioned blockchains) are different from public blockchains, as they are not open to anyone, but only to a selected group of participants who have been authorized to join the network. Permissioned blockchains can offer more privacy, as they can restrict the access and the visibility of the data and the transactions to the authorized nodes. Permissioned blockchains can also implement different levels of privacy, such as public, private, or consortium, depending on the degree of control and trust among the participants.
  - Data minimization: Data minimization is the principle of collecting and storing only the minimum amount of data that is necessary for the purpose of the system. Data minimization can help to reduce the privacy risks and the storage costs of the blockchain, as it can limit the exposure and the retention of the data. Data minimization can be achieved by using techniques such as pseudonymization, anonymization, aggregation, or deletion of the data.
  - Accountability: Accountability is the principle of being responsible and answerable for the actions and the consequences of the system. Accountability can help to balance privacy with transparency and trust, as it can ensure that the participants of the blockchain are compliant with the rules and the regulations of the system, and that they can be held liable for any misconduct or misuse of the data. Accountability can be achieved by using techniques such as identity verification, audit trails, smart contracts, or governance models.



### Blockchain Architecture and Design

Blockchain is a distributed ledger technology that enables peer-to-peer transactions without intermediaries. Blockchain architecture and design are the key aspects of building a blockchain system that meets the business objectives, security requirements, and performance standards. 

Some of the main components of blockchain architecture and design are:

- **Nodes**: Nodes are the users or computers that participate in the blockchain network. They can have different roles and permissions, such as validators, miners, or clients. Nodes store a copy of the blockchain ledger and communicate with each other to reach consensus on the validity of transactions.
- **Blocks**: Blocks are the data structures that store a set of transactions and other metadata, such as the block hash, the previous block hash, the timestamp, and the nonce. Blocks are linked together by hashes, forming a chain of blocks that is immutable and verifiable.
- **Transactions**: Transactions are the records or information that are exchanged on the blockchain network. Transactions can represent anything of value, such as money, assets, contracts, or votes. Transactions are signed by the sender's private key and verified by the receiver's public key.
- **Consensus**: Consensus is the mechanism that ensures that all nodes agree on the state of the blockchain ledger. Consensus can be achieved by different algorithms, such as proof-of-work, proof-of-stake, or proof-of-authority. Consensus ensures that the blockchain is secure, consistent, and decentralized.
- **Smart contracts**: Smart contracts are self-executing programs that run on the blockchain and enforce the rules and logic of transactions. Smart contracts can automate business processes, reduce costs, and increase efficiency. Smart contracts can be written in various languages, such as Solidity, Vyper, or Chaincode.

Blockchain architecture and design can vary depending on the use case, the type of blockchain, and the desired features. Some of the common types of blockchain are:

- **Public blockchain**: A public blockchain is open and permissionless, meaning that anyone can join the network, read the ledger, and write transactions. Public blockchains are transparent, decentralized, and censorship-resistant, but they also face scalability and privacy challenges. Examples of public blockchains are Bitcoin, Ethereum, and Litecoin.
- **Private blockchain**: A private blockchain is closed and permissioned, meaning that only authorized entities can join the network, read the ledger, and write transactions. Private blockchains are more efficient, secure, and compliant, but they also sacrifice some degree of decentralization and trustlessness. Examples of private blockchains are Hyperledger Fabric, Corda, and Quorum.
- **Consortium blockchain**: A consortium blockchain is a hybrid between public and private blockchains, meaning that only a group of pre-selected entities can join the network, read the ledger, and write transactions. Consortium blockchains are more democratic, collaborative, and interoperable, but they also require more coordination and governance. Examples of consortium blockchains are R3, IBM Blockchain, and Binance Chain.



### Basic crypto primitives

Cryptographic primitives are the low-level algorithms that are used to build cryptographic protocols for a strong secured network. They are the basic building blocks of the cryptosystem. The programmers develop new cryptographic algorithms with the help of cryptographic primitives .

Some of the common cryptographic primitives are:

- **One-way hash functions**: These are mathematical functions that map an arbitrary-length input to a fixed-length output, such that it is easy to compute the output given the input, but hard to find the input given the output, or to find two inputs that map to the same output. One-way hash functions are used for integrity verification, digital signatures, proof-of-work, and hash pointers  . Examples of one-way hash functions are SHA-256, SHA-512, and Ethash.
- **Symmetric key cryptography**: This is a type of encryption where the same secret key is used to encrypt and decrypt the data. Symmetric key cryptography is used for confidentiality, authentication, and encryption  . Examples of symmetric key algorithms are AES, DES, and RC4.
- **Asymmetric key cryptography**: This is also known as public key cryptography. It is a type of encryption where two different keys are used: a public key that can be shared with anyone, and a private key that is kept secret. The public key can be used to encrypt data that can only be decrypted by the private key, or to verify signatures that can only be generated by the private key. Asymmetric key cryptography is used for confidentiality, authentication, digital signatures, and key exchange  . Examples of asymmetric key algorithms are RSA, ECC, and ElGamal.
- **Randomized algorithms**: These are algorithms that use random numbers or bits to produce different outputs for the same input. Randomized algorithms are used for encryption, digital signatures, zero-knowledge proofs, and secure multiparty computation . Examples of randomized algorithms are Blum-Blum-Shub, RSA-OAEP, and Fiat-Shamir.



### Hash

- A hash is a mathematical function that takes any input and produces a fixed-length output, usually represented as a hexadecimal string.
- A hash function has the following properties:
  - It is deterministic, meaning that the same input always produces the same output.
  - It is one-way, meaning that it is easy to compute the output from the input, but hard to find the input from the output.
  - It is collision-resistant, meaning that it is hard to find two different inputs that produce the same output.
- A hash function can be used to verify the integrity of data, by comparing the hash of the original data with the hash of the received data. If they match, the data is likely to be authentic and unaltered.
- A hash function can also be used to identify and index data, by using the hash as a unique identifier or a key for a data structure.
- A hash function can also be used to create a digital fingerprint of data, by using the hash as a representation of the data's content or features.
- A hash function can also be used to create a proof of work, by requiring the hash of a data block to satisfy a certain condition, such as having a certain number of leading zeros. This makes it hard to generate a valid hash, but easy to verify it.
- A hash function can also be used to create a hash chain, by using the hash of a previous data block as part of the input for the next data block. This creates a link between the blocks, and makes it hard to modify or replace any block without breaking the chain.

### Basic consensus mechanisms

- A consensus mechanism is any method used to achieve agreement, trust, and security across a decentralized computer network .
- Consensus mechanisms play an essential part of securing information by encrypting it and using automated group verification .
- Consensus mechanisms are the methodologies used to protect networks from malicious behaviour and hacking attacks.
- In the context of blockchains and cryptocurrencies, some of the most prevalent consensus mechanisms are:
  - Proof-of-work (PoW): A system that requires network validators (or miners) to solve complex mathematical puzzles in order to create new blocks and verify transactions. The first validator to solve the puzzle gets rewarded with newly minted coins and transaction fees. PoW is used by Bitcoin, Ethereum, and many other cryptocurrencies  .
  - Proof-of-stake (PoS): A system that selects network validators based on the amount of coins they stake (or lock up) in the network. The more coins a validator stakes, the higher their chance of being chosen to create new blocks and verify transactions. Validators get rewarded with transaction fees, but not with newly minted coins. PoS is used by Cardano, Polkadot, and Ethereum 2.0  .
  - Proof-of-authority (PoA): A system that assigns network validators based on their reputation and identity. Validators are trusted entities that have been vetted by the network and have to follow certain rules and standards. Validators get rewarded with transaction fees, but not with newly minted coins. PoA is used by some private or permissioned blockchains, such as VeChain and POA Network.
  - Proof-of-space (PoSpace): A system that requires network validators (or farmers) to allocate a certain amount of disk space to store data in order to create new blocks and verify transactions. The more disk space a validator allocates, the higher their chance of being chosen to create new blocks and verify transactions. Validators get rewarded with newly minted coins and transaction fees. PoSpace is used by Chia and Filecoin.
  - Proof-of-burn (PoB): A system that requires network validators to destroy (or burn) a certain amount of coins in order to create new blocks and verify transactions. The more coins a validator burns, the higher their chance of being chosen to create new blocks and verify transactions. Validators get rewarded with newly minted coins and transaction fees. PoB is used by Slimcoin and Counterparty.



## Unit 2 - Consensus

- Consensus is the process of reaching agreement among a group of participants on a common state or value.
- Consensus is essential for distributed systems that need to coordinate their actions and ensure consistency and reliability.
- Consensus can be achieved by various algorithms or protocols, such as Paxos, Raft, Byzantine Fault Tolerance, Proof of Work, Proof of Stake, etc.
- Consensus algorithms or protocols have different properties and trade-offs, such as fault tolerance, availability, latency, scalability, security, etc.
- Consensus algorithms or protocols can be classified into two categories: leader-based and leaderless.
  - Leader-based consensus algorithms or protocols elect a leader or a set of leaders to propose and commit values, and the other participants follow the leader(s).
  - Leaderless consensus algorithms or protocols allow any participant to propose and commit values, and the participants reach agreement through some form of voting or validation.
- Consensus algorithms or protocols can also be classified into two categories: deterministic and probabilistic.
  - Deterministic consensus algorithms or protocols guarantee that the participants will eventually agree on the same value with certainty, as long as some assumptions are met, such as a majority of honest participants, bounded network delays, etc.
  - Probabilistic consensus algorithms or protocols guarantee that the participants will agree on the same value with high probability, but not with certainty, as there may be some scenarios where the participants diverge or disagree, such as network partitions, malicious attacks, etc.

- For example, a blockchain that prioritizes decentralization and security may have low scalability, as it requires every node to validate every transaction, which limits the throughput and increases the latency.

### Consensus protocols for Permissioned Blockchains

- A consensus protocol enables all the parties of the blockchain network to come to a common agreement (consensus) on the present data state of the ledger .
- In a permissioned blockchain, all the participating nodes are known and chosen, but consensus is still required because we can’t assume that every node is trustworthy .
- Choosing the right consensus protocol for permissioned blockchain depends on factors like the extent of decentralization required, the level of trust among the participants, the performance and scalability of the network, and the security and fault tolerance of the system .
- Some of the common consensus protocols for permissioned blockchains are:

  - **Proof of Authority (PoA)**: This protocol assigns a set of validators who have the authority to validate transactions and create new blocks. The validators are selected based on their reputation and identity, and they are incentivized to act honestly and maintain the network integrity. PoA is suitable for private or consortium blockchains where the validators are known and trusted entities. PoA offers high speed, low cost, and energy efficiency, but it sacrifices some degree of decentralization and censorship resistance.
  - **Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT)**: This protocol is based on the Byzantine Generals' Problem, which is a classic problem of achieving consensus among distributed nodes that may be faulty or malicious. PBFT requires that each node in the network broadcasts its proposed value to all other nodes, and then collects and verifies the responses from a majority of nodes. If the majority agrees on the same value, then the consensus is reached and the value is committed to the ledger. PBFT can tolerate up to one-third of faulty nodes, and it provides finality and consistency of the ledger. PBFT is suitable for small to medium-sized networks that require high performance and security, but it has scalability limitations due to the high communication overhead.
  - **Raft**: This protocol is a simplified version of PBFT that uses a leader-follower model to achieve consensus. Raft elects a leader node among the network, and the leader is responsible for proposing new blocks and replicating them to the followers. The followers accept the leader's proposals and acknowledge them. If the leader fails or becomes unresponsive, a new leader is elected through a randomized timer mechanism. Raft ensures that the ledger is always in a consistent state, and it provides fast and deterministic finality. Raft is suitable for networks that do not require high fault tolerance or adversarial resistance, but it offers simplicity and efficiency.
  - **Tendermint**: This protocol is a hybrid of PBFT and PoS that uses a rotating leader and a voting process to achieve consensus. Tendermint divides the consensus process into two phases: propose and pre-commit. In the propose phase, a leader node is randomly selected and proposes a new block. In the pre-commit phase, the other nodes vote on the proposed block, and if more than two-thirds of the nodes agree, the block is committed to the ledger. If the leader is faulty or the block is invalid, the leader is changed and the process is repeated. Tendermint can tolerate up to one-third of faulty nodes, and it provides instant finality and high performance. Tendermint is suitable for networks that require a balance of decentralization, security, and scalability.

### Decomposing the consensus process for the notes of the Unit 4 - Hyperledger Fabric (A) in the subject of Block chain Architecture Design

- Consensus in Hyperledger Fabric is a process where the nodes in the network provide a guaranteed ordering of the transactions and validate those blocks of transactions that need to be committed to the ledger.
- Consensus in Hyperledger Fabric must ensure the following properties in the network:
  - Agreement: All honest nodes must agree on the same order of transactions and the same state of the ledger.
  - Validity: Only valid transactions that satisfy the endorsement policy and other rules must be committed to the ledger.
  - Integrity: No node can tamper with or forge a transaction or a block.
  - Finality: Once a transaction is committed to the ledger, it cannot be reversed or changed.
- Consensus in Hyperledger Fabric is broken out into three phases: Endorsement, Ordering, and Validation .
  - Endorsement: This phase is driven by policy (m out of n signatures) upon which participants endorse a transaction. The endorsing peers execute the transaction proposal and produce a signed response, called an endorsement. The client collects the endorsements and creates a transaction envelope to be sent to the ordering service .
  - Ordering: This phase will get the endorsed transaction and agrees to the order to be committed to the ledger. The ordering service is a cluster of nodes that provides a fault-tolerant, atomic broadcast of transactions to all the peers in the network. The ordering service batches the transactions into blocks and delivers them to the peers .
  - Validation: This phase will validate the transactions and ensure that they satisfy the endorsement policy and other rules. The validation is performed by the committing peers, which check the signatures, the read-write sets, and the version numbers of the transactions. The committing peers also mark any invalid transactions as such and do not update the state of the ledger with them. The valid transactions are then appended to the ledger .
- Hyperledger Fabric follows a modular approach wherein different consensus techniques can be plugged in as per the requirement. Currently, Hyperledger Fabric uses Solo and Kafka to reach consensus, which requires a node to validate a batch of transactions and add them as a new block to the blockchain.
  - Solo: This is a simple consensus mechanism that is used for development and testing purposes. It involves a single ordering node that delivers the blocks to the peers. It does not provide any fault tolerance or scalability.
  - Kafka: This is a production-ready consensus mechanism that is based on Apache Kafka, a distributed messaging system. It involves a set of ordering nodes that form a cluster and use the Kafka protocol to order the transactions. It provides crash fault tolerance and scalability, but not Byzantine fault tolerance.

### Visibility

- Visibility refers to the ability of different participants in a blockchain network to see and verify the transactions and data stored on the ledger.
- Visibility is an important aspect of blockchain design, as it affects the security, privacy, scalability, and performance of the system.
- Visibility can be classified into two types: **public** and **private**.
- Public visibility means that anyone can access and view the transactions and data on the ledger, without needing any permission or authentication. This is the case for most public blockchains, such as Bitcoin and Ethereum.
- Private visibility means that only authorized participants can access and view the transactions and data on the ledger, based on their roles and permissions. This is the case for most private or permissioned blockchains, such as Hyperledger Fabric and Corda.
- Visibility can also be customized and fine-tuned according to the specific needs and requirements of the use case and the stakeholders involved. For example, some transactions or data can be encrypted, hashed, or masked to protect sensitive information, while still allowing verification and auditability. Some transactions or data can be shared selectively with certain parties, based on their identities or attributes. Some transactions or data can be aggregated or anonymized to preserve privacy, while still providing useful insights and statistics.
- Visibility can be achieved and implemented using various techniques and mechanisms, such as cryptography, consensus protocols, smart contracts, access control policies, channels, and zero-knowledge proofs. These techniques and mechanisms can be combined and integrated to create different levels and modes of visibility, such as full, partial, conditional, or zero visibility.

- Hash functions have the following properties :

  - They are **one-way**, meaning that it is easy to compute the output from the input, but hard to find the input from the output.

### Privacy and Security on Blockchain

- Privacy and security are two important aspects of blockchain technology that affect its adoption and use cases.
- Privacy refers to the ability of users to control their own data and identity on the blockchain, and to prevent unauthorized access or disclosure of their information.
- Security refers to the ability of the blockchain to resist attacks and ensure the integrity, availability, and authenticity of the data and transactions on the network.
- Some of the key features and challenges of privacy and security on blockchain are:

  - **Public and private keys**: Blockchain systems use asymmetric cryptography to secure transactions between users. Each user has a public and private key, which are mathematically linked but not identical. The public key is used to identify the user and verify their signature, while the private key is used to sign and encrypt the transactions. The private key should be kept secret and protected by the user, as losing or compromising it can result in the loss of funds or identity.  
  - **Pseudonymity**: Blockchain transactions are pseudonymous, meaning that users are identified by their public keys or addresses, which are not directly linked to their real identities. This provides a degree of privacy and anonymity for the users, as they can transact without revealing their personal information. However, pseudonymity is not the same as anonymity, as transactions are still recorded and visible on the public ledger, and can be traced and analyzed by third parties. Moreover, some blockchain applications may require users to provide additional information or verification, such as KYC (know your customer) or AML (anti-money laundering) regulations.  
  - **Data privacy**: Blockchain data is stored and replicated across multiple nodes on the network, which increases its availability and resilience. However, this also means that the data is exposed to anyone who can access the network, which may pose privacy risks for sensitive or confidential information. Some blockchain systems use encryption or hashing techniques to protect the data, but these may not be sufficient or compatible with the blockchain design or functionality. For example, encryption may not prevent unauthorized access to the metadata or the transaction patterns, and hashing may not prevent data tampering or deletion. Therefore, data privacy on blockchain requires careful consideration of the trade-offs between transparency and confidentiality, and the use of appropriate mechanisms and protocols.  
  - **Secure communication**: Blockchain transactions are transmitted and validated by the nodes on the network, which may involve different types of communication channels and protocols. These channels and protocols should be secure and reliable, to prevent attacks such as eavesdropping, interception, modification, or denial of service. Some of the security measures that can be used to enhance the communication on blockchain include encryption, authentication, authorization, and consensus algorithms.  
  - **Smart contract security**: Smart contracts are self-executing programs that run on the blockchain and enforce the rules and logic of the transactions. Smart contracts can enable automation, efficiency, and trustless interaction on the blockchain, but they can also introduce security vulnerabilities and risks. Smart contracts are immutable and irreversible, which means that any errors or bugs in the code cannot be easily fixed or updated, and can result in losses or damages for the users. Moreover, smart contracts can be exploited or manipulated by malicious actors, who can exploit the flaws or loopholes in the code or the logic. Therefore, smart contract security requires careful design, testing, auditing, and verification of the code and the functionality.  

- Privacy and security on blockchain are not static or absolute, but rather dynamic and relative, depending on the context, the purpose, and the perspective of the users and the stakeholders. Different blockchain systems may have different levels and types of privacy and security, depending on their design, architecture, and governance. Moreover, privacy and security on blockchain may evolve and change over time, as new technologies, standards, and regulations emerge and influence the blockchain ecosystem. Therefore, privacy and security on blockchain require constant evaluation, adaptation, and innovation, to balance the needs and interests of the users and the society.
