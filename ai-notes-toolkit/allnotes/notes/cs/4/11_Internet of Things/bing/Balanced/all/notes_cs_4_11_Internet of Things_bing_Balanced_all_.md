

## Unit 1 - Internet of Things (IoT)

- The Internet of Things (IoT) is a network of physical objects that are embedded with sensors, actuators, and other devices that can communicate and exchange data over the Internet.
- The IoT enables various applications and services that can improve the efficiency, convenience, and quality of life for individuals and organizations.
- Some examples of IoT devices are smart home appliances, wearable devices, industrial machines, environmental sensors, medical devices, and vehicles.
- The IoT architecture consists of four main layers: the device layer, the network layer, the middleware layer, and the application layer.
  - The device layer includes the physical objects that are connected to the IoT network, such as sensors, actuators, and gateways.
  - The network layer provides the communication infrastructure and protocols for data transmission and routing among the IoT devices and the cloud or edge servers.
  - The middleware layer provides the software platforms and services that enable data processing, storage, management, and analysis for the IoT applications.
  - The application layer includes the end-user applications and services that utilize the data and functionalities provided by the IoT devices and the middleware layer.
- The IoT faces various challenges and issues, such as security, privacy, scalability, interoperability, reliability, and energy efficiency.
  - Security refers to the protection of the IoT devices and data from unauthorized access, modification, or destruction.
  - Privacy refers to the protection of the personal and sensitive information of the IoT users and owners from unauthorized disclosure or misuse.
  - Scalability refers to the ability of the IoT system to handle the increasing number and diversity of the IoT devices and data.
  - Interoperability refers to the ability of the IoT devices and platforms to communicate and cooperate with each other, regardless of their different standards, protocols, or vendors.
  - Reliability refers to the ability of the IoT system to function correctly and consistently, even in the presence of faults, failures, or disruptions.
  - Energy efficiency refers to the ability of the IoT system to minimize the energy consumption and waste of the IoT devices and the network.

# Architectural View of Internet of Things (IoT)

- Internet of Things (IoT) is a paradigm in which objects equipped with sensors, actuators, and processors communicate with each other to serve a meaningful purpose.
- IoT architecture is a complicated organization comprising many structural elements working in tandem. Their concerted efforts ensure the coordinated performance of any kind of IoT system, be it for industrial, business or personal use.
- IoT architecture can be broken down into four layers:
  - Device layer: This layer consists of the physical objects or things that are connected to the internet and can sense, actuate, and/or compute data. Examples are sensors, actuators, microcontrollers, RFID tags, etc.
  - Network layer: This layer provides the communication infrastructure and protocols for data transmission between the devices and the cloud. Examples are Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, LoRaWAN, MQTT, CoAP, etc.
  - Cloud layer: This layer provides the storage, processing, and analysis of the data collected from the devices. It also hosts the applications and services that enable the IoT functionality. Examples are cloud platforms, databases, analytics tools, machine learning models, etc.
  - Application layer: This layer provides the user interface and interaction for the IoT system. It allows the users to access, monitor, and control the devices and the data. Examples are web browsers, mobile apps, dashboards, voice assistants, etc.
- Depending on the application areas and requirements of IoT, different architectures may be proposed with different numbers and names of layers. For example, a three-layer architecture consists of perception, network, and application layers, while a five-layer architecture consists of perception, transport, processing, application, and business layers.



# Technology behind IoT

The Internet of Things (IoT) is the concept of connecting any device that has an on/off switch to the Internet and to other connected devices. IoT devices can collect and share data about their usage and the environment around them, enabling various applications and services. Some examples of IoT devices are smart home systems, wearable devices, smart meters, industrial sensors, and connected vehicles.

To enable IoT, several technologies are involved, such as:

- **Hardware**: IoT devices require hardware components such as processors, sensors, actuators, memory, and communication modules. Depending on the device's functionality and power consumption, different types of hardware can be used, such as CPUs, MCUs, GPUs, security chips, and FPGA.
- **Connectivity**: IoT devices need to communicate with each other and/or a central area, such as a cloud platform or an edge gateway. Different types of connectivity technologies can be used, such as GSM cellular networks, Wi-Fi, Ethernet, Bluetooth, Zigbee, LoRa, eSIM, mesh networks, 5G, and Wi-Fi 6  .
- **Software**: IoT devices need software to run the hardware, process the data, and provide the user interface. Software can include operating systems, firmware, drivers, middleware, applications, and cloud services. Software can also enable edge computing, which refers to the technology used to make smart devices do more than just send or receive data to their IoT platform. It increases the computing power at the edges of an IoT network, reducing communication latency and improving response time .
- **Data analytics**: IoT devices generate a large amount of data that can be analyzed to provide insights, predictions, and recommendations. Data analytics can include data collection, storage, processing, visualization, and machine learning. Data analytics can be performed on the device, on the edge, or on the cloud, depending on the data volume, complexity, and latency requirements .



# Sources of the IoT

The Internet of Things (IoT) is the network of physical objects that are embedded with sensors, software, and other technologies for the purpose of connecting and exchanging data with other devices and systems over the internet. The sources of the IoT can be classified into different categories based on the type, location, and purpose of the devices and data. Some of the common sources of the IoT are:

- **Sensors**: Sensors are devices that measure physical quantities such as temperature, pressure, humidity, flow, motion, sound, light, etc. and convert them into electrical signals that can be transmitted and processed by other devices. Sensors are widely used in various industries and domains such as manufacturing, agriculture, healthcare, transportation, environmental monitoring, smart cities, etc. Sensors are one of the most important and abundant sources of IoT data .
- **Actuators**: Actuators are devices that perform physical actions such as opening or closing a valve, turning on or off a light, moving a robot arm, etc. based on the commands or signals received from other devices. Actuators are often used in conjunction with sensors to create feedback loops and control systems. Actuators are also used in various industries and domains such as manufacturing, agriculture, healthcare, transportation, smart homes, etc.
- **RFID tags and readers**: RFID (Radio Frequency Identification) tags are small electronic devices that store and transmit information using radio waves. RFID tags can be attached to objects or living beings to identify and track them. RFID readers are devices that can read the information stored in RFID tags using radio waves. RFID tags and readers are used in various applications such as inventory management, supply chain management, asset tracking, access control, etc.
- **Cameras and video analytics**: Cameras are devices that capture images or videos of the surrounding environment. Video analytics are software tools that can process and analyze the images or videos captured by cameras to extract useful information such as faces, objects, activities, events, etc. Cameras and video analytics are used in various applications such as security, surveillance, traffic management, crowd analysis, facial recognition, etc.
- **Smartphones and wearable devices**: Smartphones and wearable devices are personal devices that have various sensors, actuators, and communication capabilities. Smartphones and wearable devices can collect and transmit data about the user's location, activity, health, preferences, etc. Smartphones and wearable devices can also interact with other IoT devices and systems using various protocols and applications. Smartphones and wearable devices are used in various applications such as fitness, wellness, entertainment, social networking, etc.
- **Smart meters and smart grids**: Smart meters are devices that measure and record the consumption of electricity, gas, water, etc. and communicate with the utility provider or the user. Smart grids are networks of smart meters and other devices that can monitor and control the generation, transmission, and distribution of electricity, gas, water, etc. Smart meters and smart grids are used in various applications such as energy management, demand response, load balancing, etc.
- **Cloud computing platforms and services**: Cloud computing platforms and services are online platforms and services that provide various resources and capabilities such as storage, processing, analytics, machine learning, artificial intelligence, etc. for IoT devices and systems. Cloud computing platforms and services can enable IoT devices and systems to store, access, process, and analyze large amounts of data, as well as to perform complex tasks and functions. Cloud computing platforms and services are used in various applications such as data management, data visualization, data mining, data fusion, etc.

# IoT/M2M systems layers and design standardization

- IoT/M2M systems are composed of devices, networks, platforms, and applications that enable communication and data exchange between machines and humans.
- IoT/M2M systems can be divided into three major domains: the application layer, the service layer, and the network layer.
- The application layer is the domain where the end-user interacts with the IoT/M2M system through various applications, such as smart home, smart city, smart health, etc.
- The service layer is the domain where the IoT/M2M system provides common functionalities and services, such as device management, data management, security, discovery, etc.
- The network layer is the domain where the IoT/M2M system connects the devices and the platforms using various communication technologies, such as cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, LoRa, etc.
- IoT/M2M systems require standardization to ensure interoperability, scalability, security, and reliability among different devices, platforms, and applications.
- One of the leading standardization initiatives for IoT/M2M systems is oneM2M, which is a global partnership project involving 14 standards development organizations (SDOs), such as ETSI, TIA, TTC, etc.
- oneM2M defines a common service layer architecture that can be implemented on any device, platform, or application, and that can support any network technology.
- oneM2M also defines a set of APIs and protocols that enable communication and data exchange between the service layer and the application layer, and between the service layer and the network layer.
- oneM2M aims to provide a horizontal and vertical integration of IoT/M2M systems across various domains and industries, such as smart cities, smart grids, smart agriculture, etc.
- Other standardization initiatives for IoT/M2M systems include OGC, which focuses on geospatial information and sensor web, IEEE, which covers various aspects of IoT/M2M technologies, such as security, privacy, edge computing, etc., and IETF, which develops protocols and architectures for the Internet, such as CoAP, MQTT, 6LoWPAN, etc.



# Communication Technologies for IoT

Communication technologies are the methods and protocols that enable the exchange of data between IoT devices and the internet. Communication technologies can be classified into two categories: wireless and wired. Wireless communication technologies use radio waves or other electromagnetic signals to transmit data without physical connections. Wired communication technologies use cables or wires to connect devices and transfer data.

Some of the wireless communication technologies for IoT are:

- Bluetooth: A short-range IoT communication protocol that uses low-power radio waves to connect devices within a range of 10 meters. Bluetooth is widely used for personal area networks (PANs) and wearable devices. Bluetooth has different versions, such as Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), which is optimized for low-power and low-data-rate applications. 
- Zigbee: A low-power IoT communication protocol that uses mesh networking to connect devices within a range of 100 meters. Zigbee is mainly used for industrial and home automation applications, such as smart lighting, smart metering, and security systems. Zigbee operates in the 2.4 GHz frequency band and supports data rates up to 250 kbps. 
- Z-Wave: A low-power IoT communication protocol that uses mesh networking to connect devices within a range of 30 meters. Z-Wave is similar to Zigbee, but operates in the sub-GHz frequency band and supports data rates up to 100 kbps. Z-Wave is mainly used for home automation applications, such as smart locks, thermostats, and sensors. 
- Wi-Fi: A widely used IoT communication protocol that uses high-power radio waves to connect devices within a range of 100 meters. Wi-Fi is mainly used for local area networks (LANs) and internet access. Wi-Fi operates in the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz frequency bands and supports data rates up to 1 Gbps. Wi-Fi has different standards, such as Wi-Fi 6, which is designed for high-density and low-latency applications. 
- Cellular: A long-range IoT communication protocol that uses cellular networks to connect devices across large distances. Cellular is mainly used for wide area networks (WANs) and mobile internet access. Cellular operates in various frequency bands and supports data rates up to 10 Gbps. Cellular has different generations, such as 5G, which is the latest and fastest cellular technology for IoT. 
- NFC: A very short-range IoT communication protocol that uses magnetic fields to connect devices within a range of 10 centimeters. NFC is mainly used for contactless payment, identification, and data exchange. NFC operates in the 13.56 MHz frequency band and supports data rates up to 424 kbps. 
- LoRaWAN: A low-power IoT communication protocol that uses long-range radio waves to connect devices within a range of 10 kilometers. LoRaWAN is mainly used for low-data-rate and battery-powered applications, such as smart agriculture, smart city, and environmental monitoring. LoRaWAN operates in the sub-GHz frequency band and supports data rates up to 50 kbps.

- Ethernet: A widely used IoT communication protocol that uses twisted-pair cables or optical fibers to connect devices within a range of 100 meters. Ethernet is mainly used for LANs and internet access. Ethernet supports data rates up to 100 Gbps. Ethernet has different standards, such as Ethernet 2.0, which is the most common Ethernet protocol for IoT. 
- USB: A widely used IoT communication protocol that uses serial cables to connect devices within a range of 5 meters. USB is mainly used for data transfer, power supply, and device control. USB supports data rates up to 20 Gbps. USB has different versions, such as USB 3.2, which is the latest and fastest USB protocol for IoT.

# Actuators for IoT

- An actuator is a device that converts energy into motion. It does this by taking an electrical signal and combining it with an energy source. In an IoT system, the actuator can act on data collected by sensors to create an outcome as determined by the chosen settings of the user.
- Actuators enable a physical action based on data that originates with one or more sensors. The conversion of sensor data to activity follows this sequence:
  - Sensors detect an event in the physical environment.
  - The sensor data is transmitted to a gateway or cloud platform for processing and analysis.
  - The gateway or cloud platform sends a command to the actuator based on the sensor data and the predefined logic or rules.
  - The actuator executes the command by converting energy into movement.
- There are different types of actuators for IoT, depending on the energy source, the movement type, and the application. Some of the common types are :
  - Servo Motors: A servomotor is a rotary actuator or linear actuator that allows for precise control of angular or linear position, velocity and acceleration. Servo motors are widely used in robotics, automation, and industrial applications.
  - Stepper Motors: Stepper motors are DC motors that move in discrete steps. Each step corresponds to a specific angle of rotation. Stepper motors are used for precise positioning and speed control, such as in 3D printers, CNC machines, and cameras.
  - DC Motors (Continuous Rotation Motors): DC motors are two wire (power & ground), continuous rotation motors. They can vary their speed and direction by changing the polarity and voltage of the input. DC motors are used for applications that require high torque and speed, such as in electric vehicles, fans, and pumps.
  - Linear Actuators: Linear actuators are devices that produce linear motion by converting electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, or mechanical energy. They can be used for lifting, pushing, pulling, or adjusting objects, such as in doors, windows, valves, and beds.
  - Thermal/Magnetic Actuators: These are actuated by thermal or mechanical energy. Shape Memory Alloys (SMAs) or Magnetic Shape Memory Alloys (MSMAs) are materials that can change their shape when heated or exposed to a magnetic field. They can be used for applications that require low power consumption, high force, and fast response, such as in medical devices, aerospace, and biotechnology.
  - Mechanical Actuators: A mechanical actuator executes movement by converting rotary motion into linear motion. It uses gears, screws, levers, or belts to achieve the desired motion. Mechanical actuators are simple, reliable, and cost-effective, but they have limitations in speed, accuracy, and efficiency. They can be used for applications that require large force and displacement, such as in clamps, presses, and brakes.
  - Soft Actuators: Soft actuators are made of flexible or elastic materials that can deform or bend when stimulated by electrical, pneumatic, or chemical signals. They can mimic the movements of natural muscles and organs, and can adapt to complex and dynamic environments. They can be used for applications that require softness, compliance, and biocompatibility, such as in wearable devices, soft robots, and artificial organs.
  - Light Activated Actuators: Light activated actuators are devices that respond to light stimuli by changing their shape, color, or transparency. They can be made of photochromic, photovoltaic, or photomechanical materials that can absorb, generate, or convert light energy. They can be used for applications that require remote control, wireless communication, or optical modulation, such as in smart windows, solar cells, and optical switches.



# Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) Technology

- Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) technology uses radio waves to identify people or objects without physical contact or line of sight  .
- An RFID system consists of a tag, a reader and a database  .
  - A tag is a wireless device that contains an integrated circuit (IC) and an antenna. The tag stores information such as a unique identifier or other data. The tag can be passive (no battery) or active (battery-powered)  .
  - A reader is a device that has one or more antennas that emit radio waves and receive signals from the tag. The reader can be fixed or mobile, and can communicate with one or more tags at a time   .
  - A database is a system that stores the information associated with the tags, such as the tag ID, the object name, the location, the status, etc. The database can be accessed by the reader or other devices via a network  .
- RFID technology has many applications in various domains, such as supply chain management, inventory control, asset tracking, access control, identification, authentication, security, etc.     .
- RFID technology has some advantages and disadvantages over other identification technologies, such as barcodes, optical character recognition (OCR), biometrics, etc.  .
  - Advantages include:
    - No line of sight or physical contact required
    - High data capacity and fast data transfer
    - Long read range and multiple tag reading
    - High durability and reusability
    - Low maintenance and operational costs
  - Disadvantages include:
    - Interference from metal, liquids or other radio sources
    - Security and privacy risks from unauthorized reading or cloning
    - Standardization and compatibility issues among different systems
    - High initial investment and tag costs
    - Environmental and health concerns from radio emissions



# Wireless Sensor Networks

- Wireless sensor networks (WSNs) are networks of spatially dispersed and dedicated sensors that monitor and record the physical conditions of the environment and forward the collected data to a central location .
- WSNs can measure environmental conditions such as temperature, sound, pollution levels, humidity and wind.
- WSNs have the power of distributed communication, computing, and sensing features. They are characterized as infrastructure less, fault tolerant and self-organizing networks which provide opportunities for low-cost, easy-to-apply, rapid and flexible installations in an environment for various applications.
- A WSN consists of a set of connected tiny sensor nodes, which communicate with each other and exchange information and data. A sensor node could behave both as data originator and data router .
- A WSN also consists of one or more sink nodes (also called base stations) that collect data from sensors and forward it to a remote server or a user.
- The components of a WSN are:
  - Sensors: Sensors in WSN are used to capture the environmental variables and which is used for data acquisition. Sensor signals are converted into digital signals by an analog-to-digital converter (ADC).
  - Radio Nodes: Radio nodes are used to receive the data produced by the sensors and send it to the WLAN access point. It consists of a microcontroller, a transceiver, and a battery.
  - WLAN Access Point: WLAN access point is used to connect the radio nodes to the internet or a local network. It acts as a gateway between the WSN and the external world.
  - Server: Server is used to store, process, and analyze the data collected by the WSN. It can also provide feedback or control commands to the WSN.

# Embedded Computing Basics

- An embedded system is a special-purpose computer system designed to perform a dedicated function.
- Unlike a general-purpose computer, such as a personal computer, an embedded system performs one or a few pre-defined tasks, usually with very specific requirements, and often includes task-specific hardware and mechanical parts not usually found in a general-purpose computer.
- Examples of embedded systems are an watch, washing machine, microwave oven, digital camera, etc.
- Embedded systems are typically characterized by the following features  :
  - Performs specific task: Embedded systems perform some specific function or tasks.
  - Low Cost: The price of an embedded system is not so expensive.
  - Time Specific: It performs the tasks within a certain time frame.
  - Low Power: Embedded Systems don’t consume much power and can run on batteries.
  - Small Size: Embedded Systems are usually small in size and can be easily integrated with other devices.
  - Reliable: Embedded Systems are designed to work under harsh conditions and are less prone to errors or failures.
  - Real-Time: Embedded Systems have to respond to events or inputs in real-time, without any delay or latency.
- Embedded systems can be classified into four types based on their connectivity and mobility:
  - Standalone embedded systems: These systems operate independently and do not require any network connection. They have their own user interface and memory. Examples are calculators, digital watches, etc.
  - Network embedded systems: These systems are connected to a network and can communicate with other devices or systems. They rely on protocols such as TCP/IP, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, etc. Examples are routers, smart home devices, etc.
  - Mobile embedded systems: These systems are portable and can move from one place to another. They have limited memory and battery life. Examples are smartphones, tablets, laptops, etc.
  - Real-time embedded systems: These systems have to meet strict timing constraints and provide deterministic results. They can be further divided into hard real-time systems and soft real-time systems. Examples are airbags, pacemakers, etc.
- Embedded systems require software and hardware components to function properly. The software component is also known as the firmware, which is the program that runs on the embedded system. The hardware component consists of the microcontroller, which is the brain of the embedded system, and the peripherals, which are the devices that interact with the external environment .
- The development of embedded systems involves several steps, such as requirement analysis, design, implementation, testing, debugging, and deployment. The development environment consists of various tools, such as compilers, assemblers, linkers, debuggers, simulators, etc.

# Wireless Medium Access Issues for the Notes of the Unit 4 - Network & Communication Aspects in IoT in the Subject of Internet of Things

- Wireless medium access is the process of coordinating the transmission and reception of data among multiple devices that share a common wireless channel.
- Wireless medium access is challenging in IoT because of the following issues:
  - **Interference**: IoT devices may operate in the same frequency band as other wireless technologies, such as WiFi, Bluetooth, ZigBee, etc. This may cause interference and degrade the performance of IoT devices. For example, IEEE 802.15.4-compliant IoT devices operate in the unlicensed 2.4 GHz band and are subject to interference caused by high-powered IEEE 802.11-compliant WiFi users.
  - **Energy efficiency**: IoT devices are often battery-powered and have limited energy resources. Therefore, wireless medium access should minimize the energy consumption of IoT devices and prolong their lifetime. For example, IoT devices may use duty cycling techniques to switch between active and sleep modes, or use wireless energy harvesting techniques to replenish their energy from the environment.
  - **Scalability**: IoT networks may consist of a large number of devices with diverse capabilities and requirements. Wireless medium access should be able to support the scalability of IoT networks and adapt to the dynamic changes in the network topology and traffic patterns. For example, IoT devices may use clustering techniques to form groups of devices with a common leader, or use content-centric networking techniques to deliver data based on the content name rather than the device address.
  - **Latency**: IoT applications may have strict latency requirements for the delivery of data. # Survey routing protocols for the notes of the Unit 4 - Network & Communication aspects in IoT in the subject of Internet of Things

- Routing protocols are the rules that determine how data packets are forwarded from one node to another in a network.
- Routing protocols for Internet of Things (IoT) are designed to meet the specific requirements and challenges of IoT networks, such as low power consumption, scalability, mobility, heterogeneity, and security.
- Routing protocols for IoT can be classified into different categories based on various criteria, such as network topology, routing strategy, data flow direction, and security mechanism.
- Some of the common categories of routing protocols for IoT are:

  - Flat routing protocols: These protocols do not have any hierarchy or structure in the network. All nodes have the same role and responsibility. Examples of flat routing protocols are Ad hoc On-Demand Distance Vector (AODV), Dynamic Source Routing (DSR), and Optimized Link State Routing (OLSR).
  - Hierarchical routing protocols: These protocols organize the network into clusters or groups of nodes, where each cluster has a leader or a coordinator. The leader is responsible for collecting and aggregating data from the cluster members and forwarding it to the sink or the base station. Examples of hierarchical routing protocols are Low-Energy Adaptive Clustering Hierarchy (LEACH), Threshold-sensitive Energy Efficient sensor Network (TEEN), and Hybrid Energy-Efficient Distributed clustering (HEED).
  - Location-based routing protocols: These protocols use the geographic information of the nodes to make routing decisions. The nodes can obtain their location information from Global Positioning System (GPS) or other localization techniques. Examples of location-based routing protocols are Geographic Routing Protocol (GRP), Greedy Perimeter Stateless Routing (GPSR), and Distance Routing Effect Algorithm for Mobility (DREAM).
  - Data-centric routing protocols: These protocols use the content or the attributes of the data packets to route them to the appropriate nodes or destinations. The nodes can query the network for the data they are interested in, and the nodes that have the matching data can reply. Examples of data-centric routing protocols are Sensor Protocols for Information via Negotiation (SPIN), Directed Diffusion, and Rumor Routing.
  - Multipath routing protocols: These protocols establish multiple paths between the source and the destination nodes to increase the reliability, load balancing, and fault tolerance of the network. Examples of multipath routing protocols are Split Multipath Routing (SMR), Ad hoc On-demand Multipath Distance Vector (AOMDV), and Braided Multipath Routing (BMR).
  - QoS-aware routing protocols: These protocols consider the Quality of Service (QoS) requirements of the data packets, such as delay, bandwidth, energy, and reliability, and route them accordingly. Examples of QoS-aware routing protocols are Sequential Assignment Routing (SAR), Energy Aware Routing (EAR), and SPEED.
  - Security-aware routing protocols: These protocols provide security mechanisms to protect the data packets and the network from various attacks, such as eavesdropping, modification, replay, spoofing, and denial of service. Examples of security-aware routing protocols are Secure Efficient Ad hoc Distance vector routing protocol (SEAD), Secure Routing Protocol (SRP), and Secure Position Aided Ad hoc Routing (SPAAR).

- Routing protocols for IoT are still an active area of research and development, as new challenges and opportunities emerge with the advancement of IoT technologies and applications. Some of the open issues and future directions are:

  - Energy efficiency: Routing protocols for IoT should minimize the energy consumption of the nodes and prolong the network lifetime, as most of the IoT devices are battery-powered and have limited energy resources.
  - Scalability: Routing protocols for IoT should be able to handle the large number of nodes and data traffic in the network, as IoT networks are expected to grow exponentially in the future.
  - Mobility: Routing protocols for IoT should be able to cope with the dynamic topology and frequent link changes caused by the mobility of the nodes and the environment, as IoT networks are often deployed in mobile and unpredictable scenarios.
  - Heterogeneity: Routing protocols for IoT should be able to support the diversity of the nodes and the data in the network, as IoT networks are composed of different types of devices and applications with different capabilities and requirements.
  - Security: Routing protocols for IoT should provide security measures to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the data and the network, as IoT networks are vulnerable to various attacks and threats.



# Sensor deployment and node discovery in IoT

Sensor deployment and node discovery are two important aspects of network and communication in IoT. Sensor deployment refers to the process of placing sensor nodes in the environment to collect data and perform tasks. Node discovery refers to the process of identifying and locating sensor nodes in the network.

## Sensor deployment

Sensor deployment can be done in various ways depending on the application, environment, and objectives. Some of the common methods are:

- Random deployment: Sensor nodes are scattered randomly in the area of interest without any prior knowledge or planning. This method is suitable for large-scale and dynamic environments where precise node placement is not feasible or necessary. However, random deployment may result in uneven node distribution, coverage holes, and connectivity issues.
- Deterministic deployment: Sensor nodes are placed in predefined locations according to a specific pattern or algorithm. This method is suitable for small-scale and static environments where precise node placement is required or beneficial. However, deterministic deployment may require more resources, time, and coordination than random deployment.
- Hybrid deployment: Sensor nodes are placed in a combination of random and deterministic ways to balance the trade-offs between the two methods. For example, some nodes may be placed randomly to cover a large area, while some nodes may be placed deterministically to achieve certain objectives or constraints.

## Node discovery

Node discovery is the process of finding and identifying sensor nodes in the network. Node discovery can be done in various ways depending on the network topology, communication protocol, and security requirements. Some of the common methods are:

- Broadcast-based discovery: Sensor nodes broadcast their presence and information to their neighbors periodically or on demand. This method is simple and efficient, but may cause network congestion and interference if the broadcast frequency is too high or the network size is too large.
- Query-based discovery: Sensor nodes respond to queries from other nodes or a central authority that request their presence and information. This method is flexible and scalable, but may incur more communication overhead and delay than broadcast-based discovery.
- Location-based discovery: Sensor nodes use their location information (such as GPS coordinates or relative distances) to discover and locate other nodes in the network. This method is accurate and robust, but may require additional hardware and software support for location estimation and verification.

# Additions in Arduino

- Addition is one of the four primary arithmetic operations in Arduino.
- The operator + (plus) operates on two operands to produce the sum.
- The syntax for addition is: `sum = operand1 + operand2;`
- The operands can be variables or constants of any numeric data type, such as int, float, double, byte, short, or long.
- The sum variable must be of the same or larger data type as the operands, otherwise the result may be truncated or incorrect.

```c
int a = 2; // declare and initialize a variable of type int
int b = 7; // declare and initialize another variable of type int
int sum; // declare a variable of type int to store the sum
sum = a + b; // perform the addition and assign the result to sum
```

- The syntax for compound addition is: `variable += value;`
- This is equivalent to: `variable = variable + value;`
- The variable and the value must be of the same or compatible data types, otherwise the result may be truncated or incorrect.
- For example, to increment a variable by one on the Arduino, we can write:

```c
int x = 10; // declare and initialize a variable of type int
x += 1; // add one to x and assign the result to x
```

# Security Challenges for the notes of the Unit 6 - Challenges in IoT in the subject of Internet of Things

- Internet of Things (IoT) is the network of physical objects embedded with sensors, software, and other technologies that enable them to communicate and interact with other devices, systems, and humans over the internet.
- IoT security is the protection of IoT devices and data from unauthorized access, manipulation, or harm.
- IoT security faces many challenges due to the diversity, complexity, and scale of IoT systems, as well as the lack of standardization, regulation, and awareness among users and developers.
- Some of the common security challenges in IoT are:

  - **Lack of secure boot process**: A secure boot process ensures that only authorized and verified firmware or software can run on an IoT device. Without a secure boot process, an IoT device can be compromised by malicious code that alters its functionality or behavior.
  - **Exposed communication ports**: Communication ports are the interfaces that allow IoT devices to send and receive data over the internet or other networks. Exposed communication ports can be exploited by attackers to gain access to the device or its data, or to launch denial-of-service attacks that disrupt the device's operation.
  - **Lack of encryption**: Encryption is the process of transforming data into an unreadable form that can only be decrypted by authorized parties. Encryption protects the confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity of data in transit and at rest. Without encryption, IoT data can be intercepted, modified, or stolen by attackers.
  - **Weak default passwords**: Default passwords are the pre-set passwords that come with IoT devices when they are manufactured or installed. Weak default passwords are easy to guess or crack by attackers, who can then take control of the device or its data. Users should always change the default passwords to strong and unique ones.
  - **Lots of viruses and malware**: Viruses and malware are malicious programs that can infect IoT devices and cause various damages, such as stealing data, spying on users, disrupting services, or launching attacks on other devices or networks. IoT devices are vulnerable to viruses and malware due to their limited processing power, memory, and security features, as well as their frequent connection to the internet.
  - **Legacy operating systems**: Legacy operating systems are outdated or unsupported software platforms that run on IoT devices. Legacy operating systems may have known vulnerabilities that can be exploited by attackers, or may not be compatible with newer security updates or protocols. IoT devices should always run on the latest and supported operating systems.
  - **Insecure web interface**: Web interface is the graphical user interface that allows users to access and manage IoT devices through a web browser. Insecure web interface can expose IoT devices to various attacks, such as cross-site scripting, SQL injection, or brute-force login. Web interface should be secured with encryption, authentication, and input validation.
  - **Lack of visibility into device security**: Visibility into device security refers to the ability to monitor and assess the security status and performance of IoT devices. Lack of visibility into device security can prevent users and developers from detecting and responding to security incidents, or from applying security best practices and policies. IoT devices should provide clear and timely feedback on their security state and events.
  - **Lack of standardization**: Standardization is the process of establishing and following common rules, guidelines, and specifications for IoT devices and systems. Standardization can facilitate interoperability, compatibility, and security among different IoT devices and platforms. Lack of standardization can lead to inconsistencies, conflicts, and vulnerabilities in IoT systems.
  - **Lack of awareness**: Awareness is the level of knowledge and understanding of IoT security among users and developers. Awareness can influence the behavior and attitude of users and developers towards IoT security, such as their willingness to adopt security measures, report security issues, or comply with security regulations. Lack of awareness can result in negligence, ignorance, or indifference towards IoT security.

# Designing of smart street lights in smart city

- Smart street lights are street lights that can be remotely controlled, monitored and managed by a central system or a network of sensors and devices.
- Smart street lights can provide various benefits for a smart city, such as:
  - Energy saving: Smart street lights can adjust their brightness and color temperature according to the ambient light, traffic, weather and time of the day, reducing the energy consumption and carbon footprint of the city.
  - Cost saving: Smart street lights can reduce the maintenance and operational costs of the city by detecting and reporting faults, optimizing the lighting schedule and performance, and extending the lifespan of the lamps.
  - Safety and security: Smart street lights can enhance the safety and security of the city by providing adequate and uniform illumination for pedestrians, cyclists and drivers, deterring crime and vandalism, and supporting emergency services and law enforcement.
  - Data and connectivity: Smart street lights can collect and transmit various data about the city, such as air quality, noise, traffic, weather and parking, enabling data-driven decision making and planning for the city. Smart street lights can also serve as a backbone for the smart city network, providing wireless connectivity and power for other smart city devices and applications, such as cameras, sensors, chargers and displays.
- The design of smart street lights in a smart city involves the following steps:
  - Define the objectives and requirements of the smart street lighting system, such as the expected outcomes, performance indicators, budget, timeline and stakeholders.
  - Select the appropriate technology and components for the smart street lighting system, such as the type of lamps, controllers, sensors, communication protocols and network infrastructure.
  - Design the layout and configuration of the smart street lighting system, such as the number, location, spacing and orientation of the street lights, the wiring and cabling, and the integration with other smart city systems and services.
  - Implement and test the smart street lighting system, such as the installation, commissioning, calibration and verification of the street lights and the network, and the evaluation of the system functionality and performance.
  - Operate and maintain the smart street lighting system, such as the monitoring, management and optimization of the street lights and the network, the detection and resolution of faults and issues, and the upgrade and replacement of the components.
