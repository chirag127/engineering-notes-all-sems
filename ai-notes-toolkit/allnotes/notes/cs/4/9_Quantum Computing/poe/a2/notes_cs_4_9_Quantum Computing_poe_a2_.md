### Quantum algorithms for the notes of the Unit 2 - Quantum Computation in the subject of Quantum Computing.

1. Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm:
-Takes as input an oracle function f that is either constant (always 0 or 1) or balanced (output 0 and 1 with equal probability).
-The goal is to determine whether the function is constant or balanced in a single query to the oracle.
-It uses an interferometer with one input qubit and one output qubit. If the output qubit ends up in the $\lvert + \rangle$ state, the function is constant. If it ends up in the $\lvert -\rangle$ state, the function is balanced.

2. Simon's algorithm:
-Takes as input a black-box that computes a function $f: \{0,1\}^n → \{0,1\}^n$ such that $f(x) = x^2 \mod N$ for some secret $N$.
-The goal is to determine the period $r = \gcd(N, 2^n − 1)$.
-It uses a quantum Fourier transform on $n$ qubits to solve the problem in a single invocation of the black-box function. The period can then be extracted via classical post-processing.

5. There are other interpretations such as the ensemble interpretation and quantum Bayesianism that provide alternate explanations for the process of quantum measurement. The measurement problem is still an open question in quantum mechanics with no widely agreed upon resolution.

### Simulation of Quantum Systems

- Quantum systems can be simulated on a classical computer to study their behavior and dynamics.
- However, the computational resources required to simulate quantum systems grow exponentially with the number of quantum particles. This is known as the quantum complexity barrier.
- There are various methods to simulate quantum systems:
-- The exact simulation that follows the time evolution of the quantum state. This becomes infeasible for systems with more than a few quantum particles due to the exponential growth of required resources.
-- Approximate and variational methods that provide an approximate solution to the time evolution with less computational cost but some loss of accuracy. Examples include the variational quantum eigensolver.
-- Simulation of the time evolution of observables instead of the quantum state. This can be more efficient but depends on the observable being measured.
- Quantum simulation is a promising application of quantum computers as they can overcome the quantum complexity barrier and efficiently simulate quantum systems. This could enable the study of quantum systems that are intractable for classical computers.

### Phase estimation for the notes of the Unit 2 - Quantum Computation in the subject of Quantum Computing.

* Phase estimation is a quantum algorithm which allows us to estimate the phase of a unitary operator. It can be used to approximately solve the ordered search problem and also provides the basic steps for Shor's algorithm.
* The phase estimation algorithm works by applying controlled-U gates to prepare an eigenstate of U^(2^k) and then performing measurements to get an estimate of the phase. By repeating this process for increasing k, we can get progressively more accurate estimates of the phase.
* The phase estimation algorithm takes three inputs - unitary operator U, integer k, and δ. It outputs an estimate ψ of the phase φ modulo 2π/δ. The probability of success and error decrease exponentially in k. For larger values of k, we get more accurate estimates of the phase.
* The quantum circuit for phase estimation involves applying k controlled-U^(2^j) gates for j = 0 to k-1 and then performing measurements in the computational basis. This allows us to get a probabilistic estimate of the 2^jth binary digit of the phase's integer part. By concatenating the estimates, we get an estimate of the full phase.
* The time complexity of the phase estimation algorithm is O(k log(1/δ)) as it involves applying O(k) controlled-U gates and O(k log(1/δ)) measurements. The space complexity is O(k) as we need O(k) qubits. Phase estimation is a key subroutine in Shor's algorithm for quantum factoring.

### Speeding up the solution of NP – complete problems

1. NP-complete problems: Problems whose solutions cannot be verified in polynomial time. Ex: Travelling salesman problem, Boolean satisfiability problem, etc.
2. Quantum computers can speed up the solution of NP-complete problems using quantum algorithms like Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and Quantum Adiabatic Optimization (QAO).
3. In QAOA, the solution is approximated by preparing a quantum state that encodes candidate solutions and applying alternate sequences of quantum gate and driving Hamiltonian. The final measurement gives an approximate solution.
4. In QAO, the system is allowed to evolve gradually under a time-dependent Hamiltonian. The ground state of the final Hamiltonian encodes the solution. The adiabatic theorem ensures that if the evolution is sufficiently slow, the system remains in the ground state.
5. Though quantum algorithms do not provide guaranteed solutions, they can provide candidate solutions that can be further refined using classical algorithms to obtain the exact solution. This results in a speed up compared to solely using classical algorithms.
6. The resource requirements of these quantum algorithms are also quite low in terms of qubits and quantum gates, making them more feasible for near-term quantum computers.

### Distance Measures for Quantum information

1. Fidelity - It is a measure of how close two quantum states are. It is defined as the absolute value of the inner product of the two states. It takes values between 0 and 1. Higher the fidelity, closer the two states are.
2. Trace distance - It is the sum of the absolute values of the differences between the corresponding matrix elements of the two states. It is a true metric and satisfies all the properties of a distance measure. It is easier to calculate than fidelity but is a cruder measure of distance between two states.
3. Bures distance - It is a geometric distance between two states and is defined as the square root of 1 minus the fidelity between the states. It satisfies the properties of a true metric and is a smoother measure of distance than trace distance.
4. Relative entropy - It is a measure of distinguishability between two states. It is defined as the difference between the von Neumann entropies of the two states. It is a measure of how much additional work is required to distinguish two states and is asymmetric in the two states. It does not satisfy all properties of a true metric.

## Unit 5 - Quantum Error Correction

1. Error correction is necessary for building a quantum computer. Quantum error correction is a method of encoding quantum information in a redundant way so that these errors can be detected and corrected.

2. The most common types of errors in a quantum computer are bit-flip errors, where a qubit's state is flipped, and phase-flip errors, where the relative phase of a qubit's state is flipped. 3. There are many quantum error correction codes that have been developed, including the Steane code, the Bacon-Shor code, and surface codes. 4. While there has been a lot of theoretical work on quantum error correction, demonstrating it experimentally with multiple physical qubits remains difficult. 3. The entropy of an isolated system always increases over time. This is also known as the Second Law of Thermodynamics. The disorder or uncertainty of an isolated system will always increase if left alone.
4. The entropy of a system depends on the probabilities of its microstates. The more uniform the probability distribution of the microstates, the higher the entropy. If all microstates are equally probable, the entropy is maximum.
5. The entropy of a composite system is the sum of entropies of its constituent subsystems if they are independent. For dependent subsystems, the total entropy is less than the sum of individual entropies.
