

# Soft Skills

Soft skills are the personal attributes and abilities that enable you to interact effectively and harmoniously with other people in the workplace and beyond. They are often contrasted with hard skills, which are the technical or professional skills that you need to perform a specific job or task  .

Some examples of soft skills are:

- Communication: The ability to express yourself clearly and confidently, listen actively, and adapt your message to your audience   .
- Teamwork: The ability to collaborate and cooperate with others, respect different opinions and perspectives, and contribute to a common goal   .
- Problem-solving: The ability to identify, analyze, and resolve issues, using creativity, logic, and critical thinking   .
- Time management: The ability to prioritize tasks, manage deadlines, and work efficiently and productively   .
- Critical thinking: The ability to evaluate information, arguments, and assumptions, and draw valid conclusions based on evidence and reasoning   .
- Decision-making: The ability to make sound and timely choices, considering the available options, risks, and consequences   .
- Organizational: The ability to plan, coordinate, and execute tasks, projects, and activities, using appropriate tools and resources   .
- Stress management: The ability to cope with pressure, challenges, and uncertainty, and maintain a positive and resilient attitude  .
- Adaptability: The ability to adjust to changing situations, expectations, and demands, and learn from new experiences   .
- Conflict management: The ability to handle and resolve disagreements, disputes, and complaints, using diplomacy, empathy, and negotiation  .

Soft skills are important for your career success, as they can help you:

- Build rapport and trust with your colleagues, clients, and customers  .
- Enhance your performance and productivity  .
- Demonstrate your professionalism and work ethic  .
- Show your leadership and initiative  .
- Improve your creativity and innovation  .
- Increase your satisfaction and motivation  .

Soft skills can be developed and improved through various methods, such as:

- Self-assessment and reflection  .
- Feedback and coaching  .
- Training and education  .
- Practice and experience  .

Soft skills can also be showcased in your resume, cover letter, and interview, by using specific examples, stories, and achievements that demonstrate how you have applied them in your previous or current roles .

Soft skills are essential for any job or career, as they can help you stand out from the crowd, work well with others, and achieve your goals. By developing and enhancing your soft skills, you can increase your value and potential as an employee and a person   .

### 1. Students will be enabled to understand the correct usage of grammar.

- Grammar is the set of rules and conventions that govern how words and sentences are structured and used in a language.
- Understanding grammar helps students to communicate clearly, accurately, and effectively in both spoken and written forms.
- To understand the correct usage of grammar, students need to learn and practice the following aspects of grammar:
  - Parts of speech: the categories of words based on their function and meaning, such as nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions, and interjections.
  - Sentence structure: the way words are arranged and grouped to form sentences, such as subject, predicate, object, complement, modifier, clause, and phrase.
  - Punctuation: the marks and symbols that indicate how sentences and words are divided and connected, such as periods, commas, semicolons, colons, apostrophes, quotation marks, parentheses, dashes, and hyphens.
  - Capitalization: the use of uppercase letters to indicate the beginning of sentences, proper nouns, titles, acronyms, and abbreviations.
  - Spelling: the correct way of writing words according to the standard rules and conventions of a language.
  - Tense: the form of verbs that indicate the time and aspect of an action or state, such as present, past, future, simple, continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous.
  - Voice: the form of verbs that indicate the relationship between the subject and the action or state, such as active and passive.
  - Mood: the form of verbs that indicate the attitude or mode of expression of the speaker or writer, such as indicative, imperative, subjunctive, and conditional.
  - Agreement: the correspondence between words in terms of number, person, gender, and case, such as subject-verb agreement and pronoun-antecedent agreement.
  - Modality: the use of words and phrases that express the degree of certainty, possibility, necessity, obligation, permission, and ability, such as modal verbs, adverbs, and adjectives.
  - Cohesion: the use of words and devices that link and connect sentences and paragraphs, such as pronouns, determiners, conjunctions, transitions, and reference words.
  - Coherence: the use of logical and consistent organization and development of ideas and information in sentences and paragraphs, such as topic sentences, supporting details, examples, and conclusions.

- Subject-verb agreement is the rule that the verb must agree in number and person with the subject of a sentence. For example:
  - He **runs** every day. (singular subject, singular verb)
  - They **run** every day. (plural subject, plural verb)
- Pronoun-antecedent agreement is the rule that the pronoun must agree in number, gender and person with its antecedent, which is the word or phrase that the pronoun refers to. For example:
  - John likes his bike. (singular antecedent, singular pronoun)
  - The students like their bikes. (plural antecedent, plural pronoun)
- Parallelism is the rule that the words or phrases in a list or a series must have the same grammatical form and function. For example:
  - She likes to read, write and paint. (all verbs in the same form)
  - He is smart, handsome and funny. (all adjectives in the same form)
- Modifier placement is the rule that the modifier, which is a word or phrase that adds detail or description to another word or phrase, must be placed as close as possible to the word or phrase it modifies to avoid confusion or ambiguity. For example:
  - He saw a dog with a telescope. (ambiguous: who has the telescope?)
  - He saw with a telescope a dog. (clear: he has the telescope)
- Punctuation is the use of symbols such as commas, periods, semicolons, etc. to separate or clarify the meaning of words, phrases or sentences. For example:
  - Let's eat, Grandma. (comma indicates a pause and a direct address)
  - Let's eat Grandma. (no comma indicates a cannibalistic intention)

### Transformation of Sentences: Simple, Compound and Complex

- A sentence is a group of words that expresses a complete thought. A sentence can be classified into different types based on its structure and meaning.
- A simple sentence is a sentence that has only one independent clause. An independent clause is a group of words that can stand alone as a sentence. For example, "She sings well."
- A compound sentence is a sentence that has two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon (;). For example, "She sings well and plays the guitar."
- A complex sentence is a sentence that has one independent clause and one or more dependent clauses. A dependent clause is a group of words that cannot stand alone as a sentence and depends on the independent clause for its meaning. A dependent clause is usually introduced by a subordinating conjunction (such as because, although, when, etc.) or a relative pronoun (such as who, which, that, etc.). For example, "She sings well because she practices every day."
- A sentence can be transformed from one type to another by adding or removing words, changing the word order, or changing the punctuation. For example, the simple sentence "She sings well." can be transformed into a compound sentence by adding another independent clause: "She sings well and plays the guitar." It can also be transformed into a complex sentence by adding a dependent clause: "She sings well because she practices every day."

### Subject-verb agreement

- Subject-verb agreement is the rule that the verb in a sentence must agree with the subject in number (singular or plural) and person (first, second, or third). For example, "He sings well." (singular subject, singular verb) "They sing well." (plural subject, plural verb)
- Some common rules for subject-verb agreement are:
  - The verb agrees with the subject, not the complement. For example, "The singer is a woman." (singular subject, singular verb) "The singers are women." (plural subject, plural verb)
  - The verb agrees with the subject, not the modifier. For example, "The singer with the guitar sings well." (singular subject, singular verb) "The singers with the guitars sing well." (plural subject, plural verb)
  - The verb agrees with the subject closest to it when the subject is compound or collective. For example, "The singer and the guitarist sing well." (plural subject, plural verb) "The band sings well." (singular subject, singular verb)
  - The verb agrees with the subject in inverted sentences. For example, "There is a singer on the stage." (singular subject, singular verb) "There are singers on the stage." (plural subject, plural verb)
  - The verb agrees with the subject in questions. For example, "Does he sing well?" (singular subject, singular verb) "Do they sing well?" (plural subject, plural verb)

### Prefix and Suffix

- A prefix is a word part that is added to the beginning of a word to change its meaning. For example, the prefix un- means not, so adding un- to the word happy makes unhappy, which means not happy.
- A suffix is a word part that is added to the end of a word to change its meaning. For example, the suffix -ful means full of, so adding -ful to the word joy makes joyful, which means full of joy.
- Some common prefixes and suffixes are:
  - Prefixes: un-, re-, dis-, in-, im-, il-, ir-, non-, pre-, post-, anti-, bi-, tri-, quad-, multi-, etc.
  - Suffixes: -ful, -less, -able, -ible, -er, -or, -ist, -ism, -ness, -ment, -tion, -sion, -ly, -y, -ed, -ing, -en, -s, -es, etc.

### Antonyms, Synonyms, Homophones

- Antonyms are words that have opposite meanings. For example, hot and cold, big and small, happy and sad, etc.
- Synonyms are words that have similar meanings. For example, happy and glad, big and large, fast and quick, etc.
- Homophones are words that sound the same but have different meanings and spellings. For example, see and sea, hear and here, write and right, etc.



### Homonyms, New word Formation, Select word power

- Homonyms are words that have the same spelling and pronunciation but different meanings. For example, bat can mean a flying mammal or a wooden stick used in sports.
- New word formation is the process of creating new words from existing words or parts of words. There are different ways of forming new words, such as:
  - Affixation: adding prefixes or suffixes to a word. For example, un + happy = unhappy, happy + ness = happiness.
  - Compounding: combining two or more words to form a new word. For example, black + board = blackboard, sun + flower = sunflower.
  - Blending: merging parts of two words to form a new word. For example, smoke + fog = smog, breakfast + lunch = brunch.
  - Clipping: shortening a word by deleting some syllables. For example, advertisement = ad, telephone = phone.
  - Acronym: forming a word from the initial letters of a phrase. For example, NASA = National Aeronautics and Space Administration, AIDS = Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome.
  - Conversion: changing the word class of a word without changing its form. For example, run (verb) = run (noun), green (adjective) = green (noun).
- Select word power is the ability to choose the most appropriate and effective words for a given context and purpose. It involves knowing the meaning, usage, connotation, and register of words. For example, to describe a person who is very thin, one can use different words depending on the tone and intention of the speaker or writer, such as:
  - Skinny: informal, neutral or slightly negative
  - Slim: formal, positive or neutral
  - Gaunt: formal, negative or sympathetic
  - Emaciated: formal, very negative or sympathetic

### Active Listening: Meaning and Art of Listening, Traits of a Good Listener

- Active listening is a skill that involves paying full attention to the speaker, understanding their message, and responding appropriately.
- Active listening is different from passive listening, which is simply hearing what the speaker says without engaging with them or showing interest.
- Active listening is important for effective communication, building rapport, resolving conflicts, and learning new information.
- Active listening requires the following steps:
  - Prepare to listen: Clear your mind of distractions, avoid interruptions, and focus on the speaker.
  - Show interest: Use verbal and non-verbal cues to indicate that you are listening, such as nodding, smiling, eye contact, and paraphrasing.
  - Listen attentively: Pay attention to the speaker's words, tone, body language, and emotions. Try to understand their perspective and feelings.
  - Ask questions: Clarify any doubts, confusion, or assumptions by asking open-ended, probing, or reflective questions. Avoid interrupting, judging, or giving advice.
  - Provide feedback: Summarize the main points, express your understanding, and acknowledge the speaker's feelings. Validate their message and show empathy.

### Tools and Methods: Inductive Deductive; Exposition; Linear; Interrupted; Spatial

- **Inductive and Deductive Methods**: These are two types of reasoning that are used to draw conclusions from evidence or premises. Inductive reasoning moves from specific observations to broad generalizations, while deductive reasoning moves from general rules to specific conclusions .
  - For example, an inductive reasoning might be: "All the swans I have seen are white, therefore all swans are white." A deductive reasoning might be: "All mammals have hair, a whale is a mammal, therefore a whale has hair."
  - Inductive reasoning is often used to develop theories or hypotheses based on data, while deductive reasoning is often used to test existing theories or hypotheses using logic .
- **Exposition Method**: This is a method of writing or speaking that explains, informs, or describes something. Exposition can be used to present facts, definitions, examples, or arguments in a clear and organized way.
  - For example, an exposition method might be: "The solar system consists of eight planets that orbit the sun, which is a star. The planets are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Each planet has its own characteristics, such as size, distance from the sun, atmosphere, and moons."
- **Linear Method**: This is a method of writing or speaking that follows a logical and sequential order, from the beginning to the end. Linear method can be used to present a process, a timeline, a cause and effect, or a problem and solution in a straightforward way.
  - For example, a linear method might be: "To bake a cake, you need to follow these steps: preheat the oven, mix the ingredients, pour the batter into a pan, bake for 25 minutes, and let it cool."
- **Interrupted Method**: This is a method of writing or speaking that breaks the flow of the main idea with digressions, flashbacks, or side comments. Interrupted method can be used to create suspense, humor, contrast, or perspective in a creative way.
  - For example, an interrupted method might be: "I was walking down the street when I saw a strange sight. But before I tell you what it was, let me give you some background information. You see, I have always been fascinated by..."
- **Spatial Method**: This is a method of writing or speaking that describes the physical arrangement or location of something. Spatial method can be used to create a vivid picture, a map, or a comparison in a descriptive way.
  - For example, a spatial method might be: "My room is small but cozy. On the left wall, there is a bookshelf full of novels and magazines. - Group communication is the exchange of messages among three or more people who share a common goal, identity, or interest.

- Modes of speech delivery can be categorized into four main types: impromptu, extemporaneous, manuscript, and memorized.
- Impromptu speech delivery is the spontaneous and unrehearsed presentation of oral communication, usually in response to a question, situation, or occasion.
- Extemporaneous speech delivery is the planned and prepared presentation of oral communication, usually with the aid of notes or an outline, but without reading from a script.
- Manuscript speech delivery is the verbatim and precise presentation of oral communication, usually by reading from a written text.

The 4 A’s are a framework for managing stress in different situations. They are:

- **Avoid**: This means avoiding unnecessary stressors that are not worth your time or energy. For example, you can avoid stress by:
  - Saying no to extra commitments that you cannot handle.
  - Limiting your exposure to negative people or media.
  - Avoiding topics that trigger arguments or conflicts.
- **Alter**: This means changing the way you communicate or behave in stressful situations. For example, you can alter stress by:
  - Expressing your feelings and needs clearly and respectfully.
  - Being willing to compromise and negotiate with others.
  - Managing your time and priorities effectively.
- **Access**: This means seeking support and resources that can help you cope with stress. For example, you can access stress relief by:
  - Talking to a trusted friend, family member, or counselor.
  - Joining a support group or community that shares your concerns or goals.
  - Seeking professional help or advice when needed.
- **Adapt**: This means changing your attitude and perspective towards stress. For example, you can adapt to stress by:
  - Reframing the situation in a more positive or realistic way.
  - Focusing on the things you can control and letting go of the things you can't.
  - Practicing gratitude and optimism.

### Management

Management is the process of planning, organizing, leading, and controlling the resources and activities of an organization to achieve its goals and objectives. Management can be applied to different levels (such as individual, team, department, or organization), domains (such as finance, marketing, human resources, or operations), and functions (such as planning, organizing, leading, or controlling).

Some of the main topics in management are:

- **Management functions**: These are the basic tasks that managers perform to ensure the effective and efficient functioning of an organization. They include planning (setting goals and strategies), organizing (arranging and allocating resources), leading (motivating and influencing others), and controlling (monitoring and evaluating performance).
- **Management skills**: These are the abilities and competencies that managers need to perform their functions effectively. They include technical skills (knowledge and expertise in a specific field), human skills (ability to work with and communicate with others), and conceptual skills (ability to think analytically and creatively).
- **Management roles**: These are the specific behaviors and actions that managers perform in their positions. They include interpersonal roles (such as figurehead, leader, or liaison), informational roles (such as monitor, disseminator, or spokesperson), and decisional roles (such as entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, or negotiator).
- **Management styles**: These are the different ways that managers approach their functions, roles, and skills. They can vary depending on the personality, values, and preferences of the manager, as well as the situation, context, and culture of the organization. Some of the common management styles are autocratic (high control and low involvement), democratic (high involvement and low control), consultative (high involvement and moderate control), and laissez-faire (low involvement and low control).
- **Management theories**: These are the different perspectives and frameworks that explain and guide the practice of management. They can be classified into classical theories (such as scientific management, administrative management, or bureaucratic management), behavioral theories (such as human relations, motivation, or leadership), contingency theories (such as situational, systems, or contingency), and modern theories (such as learning, knowledge, or innovation).
