### Structure of Prokaryotic Cell

- Prokaryotic cells are single-celled organisms that lack a nucleus surrounded by a membrane.
- The prokaryotic cell structure includes:
- Cell wall: It is made up of peptidoglycan and provides shape and rigidity to the cell.
- Cytoplasm: It is a gel-like substance inside the cell that contains various cell components.
- Ribosomes: They are organelles responsible for protein synthesis. Ribosomes are smaller in size in prokaryotes compared to eukaryotes.
- DNA: The genetic material in prokaryotes is a single, circular chromosome located in the cytoplasm.
- Plasmids: They are small, circular, extra-chromosomal DNA that are not essential for the cell. They help in the horizontal transfer of genes between bacteria.
- Flagella: They are hair-like structures that help in locomotion. Some prokaryotes may have flagella.
- Pili: They are hair-like appendages that help in adhesion to surfaces and exchange of genetic material.

Prokaryotes are classified into bacteria and archaea. Bacteria are further classified into gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria based on the cell wall structure. The prokaryotic cell structure is simpler than the eukaryotic cell structure as prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles. The simple structure allows for faster growth and reproduction in prokaryotes compared to eukaryotes.

### Eukaryotic Cells

- Eukaryotic cells are cells that contain a nucleus enclosed within membranes.
- The nucleus houses the cell's DNA and is the control center of the cell.
- Eukaryotic cells are typically larger than prokaryotic cells and have additional intracellular components called organelles that carry out specific functions.
- The major organelles in eukaryotic cells include:
    - Nucleus: Contains DNA and controls cellular activities
    - Mitochondria: Produce cellular energy through respiration
    - Endoplasmic reticulum: Network of folded membranes involved in protein transport and synthesis
    - Ribosomes: Organelles that assemble proteins based on mRNA sequences
    - Golgi apparatus: Packages and transports proteins for use inside and outside the cell
    - Vacuoles: Storage organelles that hold waste products and intracellular fluids
- Examples of eukaryotic cells include plant cells, animal cells, and fungi. Plant and animal cells share some organelles but differ in others, such as the presence of a cell wall and chloroplasts in plant cells.
- The organization and compartmentalization of eukaryotic cells allows for greater complexity and efficiency in carrying out cellular processes compared to prokaryotic cells. The separation of activities into organelles prevents unwanted chemical interactions and allows for tighter regulation of cell functions.

### Plant Cells

- Plant cells are eukaryotic cells surrounded by a cell wall made up of cellulose.
- The cell wall provides shape and rigidity to the plant cell. It allows the cell to withstand hypotonic environments without bursting.
- A large central vacuole is present in plant cells which helps in storage of nutrients, maintain turgor pressure and remove waste materials.
- Chloroplasts are present in plant cells which carry out photosynthesis. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll which absorbs sunlight to produce food for the plant.
- Mitochondria are present for cellular respiration to produce energy for cell activities.
- Other organelles like endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes, Golgi bodies are also present to carry out various cell functions.
- A gel-like substance called cytoplasm fills the space between various organelles. It contains enzymes and other molecules required for cellular processes.
- Unlike animal cells, plant cells do not have centrioles, lysosomes and cilia or flagella.
- The shape of plant cells is usually irregular due to the rigid cell wall. They can be square, rectangular, cylindrical or flat in shape.

### Structure and function of cell membrane

- Cell membrane is the outermost covering of the cell that separates the contents of the cell from its external environment.
- It is a lipid bilayer with embedded proteins that controls what enters and exits the cell.
- The main components of cell membrane are phospholipids, proteins and carbohydrates.
- Phospholipids are arranged in a bilayer with their hydrophilic heads facing outwards and hydrophobic tails facing inwards. This structure is fluid and flexible.
- Membrane proteins have various functions like channel proteins that help in transport of molecules, carrier proteins that help in facilitated diffusion, receptor proteins that help in cell signaling, glycoproteins that help in cell recognition.
- The carbohydrates present on the outer surface of cell membrane help in cell recognition and adhesion.
- The fluid nature of cell membrane helps in various cellular processes like endocytosis, exocytosis, cell signaling, etc. while the protein components help in selective permeability.
- Thus, cell membrane is a essential structure of cells that controls the internal environment of the cell and helps in cellular communications.

8. Cytoskeleton: Network of filaments that maintains cell shape, enables movement, and transports materials. Includes microtubules, actin filaments, and intermediate filaments.
9. Cell Membrane: Outer layer that controls what enters and exits the cell. Made of a lipid bilayer with embedded proteins.

### Basics of cell signaling

- Cell signaling refers to the process by which cells communicate with each other and respond to the surroundings. It is crucial for the proper functioning and coordination of cells.
- Cell signaling involves the transmission of molecular signals from one cell to another via direct contact or through secreted molecules. These signals are received and converted into cellular responses via signal transduction pathways.
- The basic steps in cell signaling are:
	1. Signal reception: The signaling molecules bind to receptor proteins on the cell surface or within the cell.
	2. Signal transduction: The signal is transduced into the cell through a signaling pathway. This often involves a cascade of biochemical reactions inside the cell.
	3. Cellular response: The signal is amplified and triggers a cellular response, such as changes in metabolism, gene expression, or cell division.
- There are several types of signals involved in cell signaling:
	- Molecular signals: Includes neurotransmitters, hormones, cytokines, growth factors, etc.
	- Adhesion molecules: Facilitate cell-cell contact. For example, integrins.
	- Extracellular matrix: Provides mechanical and biochemical support to cells. Various molecules in the matrix can signal to cells.

### Structure and use of compound microscope

- A compound microscope is used to magnify objects/samples to view the minute details.
- It consists of two lenses - objective lens and eyepiece lens.
- The objective lens is near the object and has shorter focal length. It produces an enlarged and inverted image.
- The eyepiece lens is like a simple microscope through which we view the image formed by the objective lens. It acts as a magnifier and produces a virtual and erect enlarged image.
- The total magnification of the object is the product of magnifications of objective lens and eyepiece lens.
- The resolving power of microscope depends on the wavelength of light and numerical aperture of the objective lens. Higher the numerical aperture, higher is the resolving power.
- The compound microscope requires proper illumination of the object. Mirrors are used to reflect light from external source to illuminate the object.
- The microscope has a stage to place the object/sample. Coarse and fine adjustments are provided to focus the object.
- Different types of objective lenses like scanning (4X), low power (10X), high power (40X) and oil immersion (100X) are used based on the magnification required. Higher the power of objective lens, higher is the magnification and lesser is the field of view.
- The microscope needs proper care and handling. The lenses should be cleaned gently and stored properly to avoid damage.

### Tissues in animal and plants

**Animal tissues:**

-   Epithelial tissue: Lines the outer surfaces of organs and blood vessels, involved in secretion and absorption.
-   Connective tissue: Binds and supports other tissues, transports substances. Includes blood, bone, cartilage, fat, etc.
-   Muscular tissue: Involved in movement, contractions. Includes skeletal, smooth and cardiac muscle tissue.
-   Nervous tissue: Transmits signals between different parts of the body, coordinates responses to stimuli. Includes neurons, neuroglia, etc.

**Plant tissues:**

-   Meristematic tissue: Found in growth regions, involved in continuous division and growth. Includes apical meristem, lateral meristem, etc.
-   Permanent tissue: Formed from mature meristematic tissue, carry out most plant functions. Includes simple permanent tissue (parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma) and complex permanent tissue (xylem, phloem).
-   Parenchyma: Thin walled, involved in storage, photosynthesis and healing.
-   Collenchyma: Irregular thickened angles, provides mechanical support growing regions.
-   Sclerenchyma: Thick lignified walls, provides strength and support. Includes fibres and sclereids.
-   Xylem: Conducts water and minerals upward. Includes tracheids and vessel elements.
-   Phloem: Transports organic nutrients throughout the plant. Includes sieve tube elements and companion cells.

### Fruit

- Fruit is the mature ovary of a flowering plant containing the seeds.
- It protects the seeds and aids in their dispersal.
- Based on origin, fruits are classified into:
-- True fruits: Develop from the ovary after fertilization like mango, apple.

-- Parthenocarpic fruits: Develop without fertilization like bananas, oranges.

-- Fleshy fruits: Have fleshy pulp like mango, papaya, banana.
-- Dry fruits: Lack fleshy pulp like pulses, nuts, grains.

-- Simple fruits: Develop from a single carpel or syncarpous gynoecium.

-- Multiple fruits: Develop from a single inflorescence with accessory parts like pineapple, fig.

-- Protects the seeds: The fruit wall protects the seeds from damage, predation, and harsh conditions.
-- Aids dispersal: The fruit facilitates dispersion of seeds through various means like wind, water, animals, etc. to reduce competition and inbreeding.
-- Stores food: The fleshy pulp of some fruits stores food for the seeds. The endosperm of some seeds also stores food.

### Classification of living organisms

- Five kingdom classification: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia
- Major groups and principles of classification in each kingdom:
- Monera: Prokaryotic unicellular organisms, classified based on cell structure, nutrition mode, etc. Example: Bacteria
- Protista: Eukaryotic unicellular organisms, classified based on mode of locomotion, nutrition, etc. Example: Amoeba, Plasmodium

    - Genus: The first word in the name refers to the genus, which is a group of closely related species. It is always capitalized.
    - Species: The second word refers to the species, which is a subset of the genus and represents organisms that can breed and produce fertile offspring. It is never capitalized.

    - Kingdom: Animalia
    - Phylum: Chordata
    - Class: Mammalia
    - Order: Primates
    - Family: Hominidae
    - Genus: Homo
    - Species: sapiens

### Mendelian Experiments

- Gregor Mendel, an Austrian monk, carried out hybridization experiments on garden peas and proposed the laws of inheritance in living organisms.
- He selected peas for his experiments because they had easily distinguishable traits with contrasting characters like tall/dwarf, round/wrinkled seeds, yellow/green seeds, etc.
- Mendel cross-bred thousands of pea plants and observed the inheritance of traits over generations. He proposed the Law of Segregation and Law of Independent Assortment.
- Law of Segregation: For each trait, organisms inherit two alleles from parents that segregate during gamete formation.
- Law of Independent Assortment: Inheritance of one trait is independent of inheritance of another trait. Alleles of one trait segregate independently of alleles of another trait during gamete formation.
- Mendel's laws form the foundation of genetics. They explain the transmission of traits from parents to offspring and the occurrence of dominant and recessive traits in offspring.

### Mitosis and Meiosis

- Mitosis: Cell division resulting in two identical daughter cells each having the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. Purpose is growth, asexual reproduction, and cell repair.
- Meiosis: Cell division resulting in four cells each with half the number of chromosomes as the parent cell. Purpose is production of gametes (eggs and sperm) for sexual reproduction.
- Differences between mitosis and meiosis:
  - Number of divisions: Mitosis has one round of division, meiosis has two rounds resulting in four cells.
  - Number of chromosomes: Daughter cells from mitosis have the same number of chromosomes as parent cell. Daughter cells from meiosis have half the number of chromosomes as parent cell.
  - Pairing of chromosomes: In meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair up and undergo crossover to produce genetic variation. No pairing in mitosis.
- Techniques to study mitosis and meiosis: Microscopy (light, electron), staining (e.g. aceto-orcein, Giemsa, hematoxylin), cell culture, biochemical assays.

### Techniques to study mitosis and meiosis
