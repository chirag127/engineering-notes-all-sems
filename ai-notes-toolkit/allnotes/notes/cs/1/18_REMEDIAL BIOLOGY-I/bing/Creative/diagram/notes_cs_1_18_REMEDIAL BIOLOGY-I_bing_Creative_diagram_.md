

# KCS

KCS stands for Knowledge-Centered Service, which is a methodology for creating and maintaining documentation as part of the service delivery process . KCS aims to:

- Improve service quality and efficiency by capturing and reusing knowledge from service interactions .
- Reduce costs and increase customer satisfaction by enabling self-service and reducing repeat incidents .
- Enhance organizational learning and innovation by fostering a culture of collaboration and feedback .

KCS is based on the following principles:

- Knowledge is a by-product of solving problems.
- Knowledge should be captured and structured in the context of the service interaction.
- Knowledge should be based on demand and usage, not on assumptions or predictions.
- Knowledge should be updated and improved over time, not static or outdated.
- Knowledge should be accessible and searchable by anyone who needs it, not hidden or siloed.
- Knowledge should be measured and evaluated by its value and impact, not by its volume or completeness.

KCS follows a four-phase adoption model:

- Phase 1: Planning and Design. This phase involves defining the vision, goals, scope, roles, and governance of the KCS initiative, as well as designing the knowledge base structure, workflow, and tools.
- Phase 2: Adoption. This phase involves training and coaching the service staff on the KCS practices and processes, as well as monitoring and rewarding their performance and behavior.
- Phase 3: Proficiency. This phase involves achieving a high level of quality and consistency in the knowledge base, as well as enhancing the search and self-service capabilities.
- Phase 4: Optimization. This phase involves leveraging the knowledge base for continuous improvement and innovation, as well as expanding the KCS scope and benefits to other areas of the organization.

### Structure of prokaryotic cells

- Prokaryotic cells are unicellular organisms that lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.
- Prokaryotic cells have a simple structure, consisting of the following components:
  - Plasma membrane: an outer covering that separates the cell's interior from its surrounding environment. It regulates the passage of substances into and out of the cell.
  - Cytoplasm: a gel-like substance that fills the cell and contains the genetic material and other cellular structures.
  - Nucleoid: an irregularly-shaped region that contains the circular, double-stranded DNA of the cell. It is not surrounded by a nuclear envelope.
  - Ribosomes: small structures that synthesize proteins using the genetic information from the DNA.
  - Plasmids: small, circular pieces of DNA that can replicate independently of the main chromosome. They often carry genes for antibiotic resistance or other traits that benefit the cell.
  - Flagella: long, whip-like appendages that enable the cell to move. Not all prokaryotic cells have flagella.
  - Pili: short, hair-like projections that help the cell attach to surfaces or exchange genetic material with other cells. Not all prokaryotic cells have pili.
  - Capsule: an additional outer layer that covers some prokaryotic cells. It protects the cell from being engulfed by other organisms or from drying out. It also helps the cell adhere to surfaces or form biofilms.
  - Cell wall: a rigid layer that surrounds the plasma membrane and gives shape and support to the cell. The cell wall of most bacteria is made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of linked sugars and polypeptides. The cell wall of archaea, another group of prokaryotes, is made of different substances, such as pseudopeptidoglycan or proteins.

- Prokaryotic cells are classified into two domains: bacteria and archaea. Bacteria are the most diverse and abundant group of prokaryotes, and can be found in almost every environment on Earth. Archaea are less common and often live in extreme conditions, such as high temperatures, salinity, or acidity.
- Prokaryotic cells are important for many biological processes, such as nutrient cycling, decomposition, symbiosis, and pathogenesis. They also have many applications in biotechnology, medicine, agriculture, and industry.



### Eukaryotic Cells

- Eukaryotic cells are one of the two types of cells that make up all living things, the other being prokaryotic cells.
- Eukaryotic cells have a **nucleus** that contains the genetic material (DNA) and is surrounded by a **nuclear membrane**  .
- Eukaryotic cells also have various **membrane-bound organelles** that perform specific functions, such as **mitochondria** (energy production), **endoplasmic reticulum** (protein synthesis and transport), **Golgi apparatus** (modification and sorting of proteins), **lysosomes** (digestion and recycling of cellular components), **peroxisomes** (detoxification and metabolism of fatty acids), **chloroplasts** (photosynthesis in plants and algae), and **vacuoles** (storage and regulation of water and solutes) .
- Eukaryotic cells are typically much larger than prokaryotic cells, having a volume of around 10,000 times greater.
- Eukaryotic cells have a **cytoskeleton** composed of **microtubules**, **microfilaments**, and **intermediate filaments** that provide structural support, shape, movement, and cell division .
- Eukaryotic cells are divided into four major groups based on their evolutionary history and characteristics: **protists** (single-celled or simple multicellular organisms that are not plants, animals, or fungi), **fungi** (organisms that decompose organic matter and absorb nutrients), **plants** (organisms that produce their own food by photosynthesis), and **animals** (multicellular organisms that ingest food and have specialized tissues and organs) .
- Eukaryotic cells are the basis of complex life forms and have a high degree of diversity and specialization .



### Plant Cells

Plant cells are the basic units of life in green plants, photosynthetic eukaryotes of the kingdom Plantae. They have some distinctive features that differentiate them from animal cells and other types of eukaryotic cells. Here are some of the main characteristics of plant cells:

- Plant cells have a **cell wall** surrounding the cell membrane. The cell wall is a rigid structure made of cellulose, hemicelluloses, and pectin. It provides support, protection, and shape to the plant cell. It also allows the exchange of substances between the cell and the environment through pores called plasmodesmata .
- Plant cells have **chloroplasts**, specialized organelles that contain the pigment chlorophyll and are the site of photosynthesis. Photosynthesis is the process by which plants convert light energy into chemical energy, producing organic molecules and oxygen. Chloroplasts have their own DNA and can divide independently of the cell nucleus .
- Plant cells have a large **central vacuole**, a water-filled space enclosed by a membrane called the tonoplast. The central vacuole occupies most of the volume of the plant cell and serves various functions, such as maintaining turgor pressure, storing nutrients and waste products, and regulating pH .
- Plant cells have other organelles that are common to most eukaryotic cells, such as the nucleus, the endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi apparatus, the mitochondria, the cytoskeleton, and the ribosomes. However, some of these organelles may have different shapes or functions in plant cells compared to animal cells. For example, plant mitochondria have cristae that are tubular rather than flattened, and plant ribosomes are slightly smaller than animal ribosomes.
- Plant cells communicate with each other through chemical signals and electrical impulses. Some of the molecules involved in plant cell signaling are hormones, cytokinins, auxins, ethylene, and jasmonates. These molecules can regulate plant growth, development, defense, and stress responses. Plant cells can also sense and respond to environmental stimuli, such as light, gravity, temperature, and water availability.

### Structure and function of cell membrane

- The cell membrane, also called the plasma membrane, is found in all cells and separates the interior of the cell from the outside environment  .
- The cell membrane consists of a lipid bilayer that is semipermeable, meaning it allows some substances to cross, but not others  .
- The lipid bilayer is composed of phospholipids, which have a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and a hydrophobic (water-fearing) tail. The phospholipids are arranged in two layers, with the heads facing the water and the tails facing each other.
- The cell membrane also contains cholesterol, which helps to maintain the fluidity and stability of the membrane.
- The cell membrane also contains proteins, which have various functions, such as  :
  - Structural proteins: provide support and shape to the cell
  - Transport proteins: form channels or carriers for the passage of materials across the membrane
  - Receptor proteins: bind to specific molecules (such as hormones, neurotransmitters, or signaling molecules) and trigger a response in the cell
  - Enzymatic proteins: catalyze chemical reactions on the membrane or inside the cell
  - Marker proteins: act as identification tags for the cell and help in cell recognition and communication
- The cell membrane also contains carbohydrate groups that are attached to some of the lipids and proteins, forming glycolipids and glycoproteins. These molecules serve as recognition sites for other cells and molecules, and play a role in cell adhesion and immunity.
- The cell membrane regulates the transport of materials entering and exiting the cell, and maintains the homeostasis of the cell . The cell membrane can use different methods of transport, such as :
  - Passive transport: does not require energy and moves substances along their concentration gradient (from high to low concentration)
    - Diffusion: the random movement of molecules from high to low concentration
    - Osmosis: the diffusion of water across a semipermeable membrane
    - Facilitated diffusion: the diffusion of molecules through transport proteins
  - Active transport: requires energy and moves substances against their concentration gradient (from low to high concentration)
    - Primary active transport: uses ATP (a form of energy) to directly pump molecules across the membrane
    - Secondary active transport: uses the energy stored in an electrochemical gradient to indirectly transport molecules across the membrane
    - Endocytosis: the process of taking in materials by forming vesicles from the membrane
    - Exocytosis: the process of releasing materials by fusing vesicles with the membrane
- The cell membrane also participates in cell signaling, which is the communication between cells using chemical or electrical signals . The cell membrane can receive, transmit, or amplify signals, and can also modulate the response of the cell to the signals. Cell signaling is essential for the coordination and regulation of cellular activities, such as growth, differentiation, metabolism, and apoptosis.

### Basics of cell signaling

- Cell signaling is the process of cellular communication within the body driven by cells releasing and receiving hormones and other signaling molecules.
- Cell signaling enables coordination within multicellular organisms, such as responding to external stimuli, regulating growth and development, and maintaining homeostasis.
- Cell signaling involves three stages: reception, transduction, and response  .
  - Reception: A cell detects a signaling molecule from the outside of the cell. The signaling molecule binds to a specific receptor protein on the cell surface or inside the cell  .
  - Transduction: When the signaling molecule binds the receptor, it changes the receptor protein in some way. This triggers a series of events or a signal relay cascade inside the cell, which may involve other molecules, enzymes, or ions  .
  - Response: Finally, the signal triggers a specific cellular response, such as a change in gene expression, enzyme activity, cell division, or cell death  .
- Cell signaling can be classified into different forms based on the distance and type of communication between the sending and receiving cells .
  - Paracrine signaling: The signaling molecule travels a short distance and affects nearby cells . For example, growth factors that stimulate cell proliferation.
  - Endocrine signaling: The signaling molecule travels a long distance and affects distant cells . For example, hormones that are released into the bloodstream and act on target organs.
  - Autocrine signaling: The signaling molecule acts on the same cell that produced it . For example, cytokines that regulate immune responses.
  - Juxtacrine signaling: The signaling molecule is attached to the cell membrane and affects adjacent cells that are in direct contact . For example, notch and delta proteins that regulate cell differentiation.
  - Intracrine signaling: The signaling molecule is produced and acts inside the same cell. For example, steroid hormones that bind to intracellular receptors and modulate gene expression.

### Structure and use of compound microscope

- A compound microscope is an instrument that magnifies objects otherwise too small to be seen, producing an image in which the object appears larger.
- A compound microscope has two or more convex lenses, one objective is used at a time, it produces 2-dimensional images, and its typical magnification is between 40x and 1000x.
- A compound microscope is mainly used for studying the structural details of cell, tissue, or sections of organs.
- A compound microscope has two main parts: non-optical parts and optical parts .

#### Non-optical parts

- Base: The base is also known as the foot which is either U or horseshoe-shaped. It supports the whole microscope and contains the light source.
- Pillar: The pillar is a vertical metal rod that connects the base to the arm.
- Arm: The arm is a curved metal part that holds the upper parts of the microscope. It is used to carry the microscope.
- Stage: The stage is a flat platform where the specimen is placed for observation. It has a hole in the center to allow light to pass through. It also has clips or a mechanical stage to hold the specimen in place .
- Coarse adjustment knob: The coarse adjustment knob is a large knob that moves the stage up and down to bring the specimen into focus. It is used for low power objectives .
- Fine adjustment knob: The fine adjustment knob is a smaller knob that moves the stage slightly to sharpen the focus. It is used for high power objectives .
- Condenser: The condenser is a lens system that collects and concentrates the light from the source and directs it to the specimen. It has a knob to adjust its height and a diaphragm to control the amount of light .

#### Optical parts

- Eyepiece or ocular lens: The eyepiece or ocular lens is the lens that is closest to the eye of the observer. It magnifies the image formed by the objective lens. It usually has a magnification of 10x .
- Body tube: The body tube is a hollow metal cylinder that holds the eyepiece and the objective lenses. It maintains the correct distance between them .
- Nosepiece or revolving turret: The nosepiece or revolving turret is a rotating part that holds the objective lenses. It allows the user to change the objective lens by rotating it .
- Objective lenses: The objective lenses are the lenses that are closest to the specimen. They magnify the specimen and form an image inside the microscope. They have different magnifications, such as 4x, 10x, 40x, and 100x .
- Light source or illuminator: The light source or illuminator is a lamp that provides light for the microscope. It can be either a mirror that reflects external light or an electric bulb that emits light .

### Basic chemical constituents of living body

- The living body is composed of various chemical elements and compounds that are essential for its structure and function.
- The most abundant element in the living body is **oxygen**, which accounts for about 65% of the body mass. Oxygen is mainly found in water, which is the primary solvent and medium for biochemical reactions in the body. Oxygen is also a component of organic molecules, such as carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids   .
- The second most abundant element in the living body is **carbon**, which accounts for about 18% of the body mass. Carbon is the basic unit for organic molecules, as it can form four covalent bonds with other atoms. Carbon is the backbone of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, which are the major macromolecules of life   .
- The third most abundant element in the living body is **hydrogen**, which accounts for about 10% of the body mass. Hydrogen is mainly found in water, as well as in organic molecules. Hydrogen forms polar covalent bonds with oxygen and nitrogen, which give rise to hydrogen bonds that are important for the stability and function of macromolecules   .
- The fourth most abundant element in the living body is **nitrogen**, which accounts for about 3% of the body mass. Nitrogen is mainly found in proteins and nucleic acids, as it is a component of amino acids and nucleotides, respectively. Nitrogen is also involved in the synthesis and metabolism of various biological compounds, such as hormones, neurotransmitters, and vitamins   .
- The fifth most abundant element in the living body is **calcium**, which accounts for about 1.5% of the body mass. Calcium is mainly found in bones and teeth, as it is a component of hydroxyapatite, a mineral that gives strength and rigidity to the skeletal system. Calcium is also involved in muscle contraction, nerve transmission, blood clotting, and enzyme activation  .
- The sixth most abundant element in the living body is **phosphorus**, which accounts for about 1% of the body mass. Phosphorus is mainly found in bones and teeth, as it is a component of hydroxyapatite, as well as in nucleic acids, as it is a component of the phosphate group that links the nucleotides. Phosphorus is also involved in energy transfer, as it is a component of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the universal energy currency of the cell  .
- The other five elements that make up about 0.85% of the body mass are **potassium, sulfur, sodium, chlorine, and magnesium**. These elements are mainly found in the extracellular and intracellular fluids of the body, where they maintain the osmotic pressure, pH, and electrical potential of the cells. These elements are also involved in various biochemical reactions, such as protein synthesis, enzyme activity, and nerve impulse transmission  .
- The remaining elements that make up about 0.15% of the body mass are **trace elements**, such as iron, iodine, copper, zinc, selenium, fluorine, and cobalt. These elements are required in very small amounts, but they are essential for the function of certain enzymes, hormones, and cofactors   .

### Tissues in animals and plants

- A tissue is a level of organization in the body of a multicellular organism. It is formed by the merging of cells having the same structure and function.
- Tissues are found in both animals and plants, but they have some differences and similarities.
- The four main types of animal tissues are:
  - Epithelial tissue: It is formed by the cells that line the external and internal surfaces of the organs. It provides protection, secretion, absorption, and sensation .
  - Connective tissue: It is formed by the cells that are embedded in an extracellular matrix. It provides support, binding, insulation, and transportation .
  - Muscle tissue: It is formed by the cells that have the ability to contract and relax. It provides movement, posture, and heat production .
  - Nervous tissue: It is formed by the cells that transmit and receive electrical impulses. It provides communication, coordination, and integration .
- The three main types of plant tissues are:
  - Epidermis: It is the outermost layer of the plant body. It provides protection, gas exchange, and water retention.
  - Ground tissue: It is the bulk of the plant body. It provides storage, photosynthesis, and support.
  - Vascular tissue: It is the tissue that carries water and minerals from the roots to the leaves, and organic substances from the leaves to the rest of the plant. It consists of xylem and phloem.
- Some similarities between animal and plant tissues are:
  - They are both composed of cells that have a common origin and function.

### Leaf

A leaf is a flattened, usually green, structure that is attached to the stem of a plant. The main function of a leaf is to produce food for the plant by photosynthesis, which is the process of converting light energy into chemical energy. 

#### Leaf structure

The internal structure of the leaf is protected by the leaf epidermis, which is a layer of cells that covers both sides of the leaf. The epidermis may have hairs, glands, or other structures that help the leaf adapt to its environment. The epidermis also has pores called stomata, which allow gas exchange between the leaf and the atmosphere. The stomata are surrounded by guard cells, which regulate the opening and closing of the stomata.

The leaf tissue between the upper and lower epidermis is called the mesophyll, which is divided into two layers: the palisade mesophyll and the spongy mesophyll. The palisade mesophyll is composed of elongated cells that are packed with chloroplasts, the organelles that contain chlorophyll and perform photosynthesis. The spongy mesophyll is composed of loosely arranged cells that have air spaces between them, which facilitate the diffusion of gases and water vapor. The mesophyll also contains veins, which are bundles of vascular tissue that transport water and nutrients to and from the leaf.

#### Leaf function

The main function of a leaf is to produce food for the plant by photosynthesis. Photosynthesis involves two main steps: the light reactions and the Calvin cycle. The light reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplasts, where light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll and converted into chemical energy in the form of ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma of the chloroplasts, where the chemical energy from the light reactions is used to fix carbon dioxide into organic molecules, such as glucose.

The leaf also performs other functions, such as transpiration, respiration, and defense. Transpiration is the loss of water vapor from the leaf through the stomata, which helps to cool the leaf and create a negative pressure that draws water up from the roots. Respiration is the opposite of photosynthesis, where organic molecules are broken down into carbon dioxide and water, releasing energy for the plant. Defense is the ability of the leaf to protect itself from herbivores, pathogens, or environmental stress, by producing secondary metabolites, such as alkaloids, terpenes, or phenolics, that have toxic, repellent, or antifungal properties.

#### Leaf adaptation

Leaves can vary in shape, size, color, and arrangement, depending on the plant species and the environmental conditions. Some of the common leaf adaptations are:

- Coniferous leaves: These are needle-like or scale-like leaves that are found in coniferous plants, such as pine, spruce, and fir. These leaves have sunken stomata and a smaller surface area, which reduce water loss and prevent freezing. They also have a thick cuticle, which is a waxy layer that covers the epidermis and prevents water loss. Coniferous leaves are evergreen, which means they do not shed their leaves in winter.
- Deciduous leaves: These are broad, flat leaves that are found in deciduous plants, such as maple, oak, and birch. These leaves have a large surface area, which maximizes light absorption and photosynthesis. They also have thin cuticles and many stomata, which allow gas exchange and transpiration. Deciduous leaves are seasonal, which means they change color and fall off in autumn, to reduce water loss and avoid damage from cold and snow.
- Succulent leaves: These are fleshy, thick leaves that are found in succulent plants, such as cactus, aloe, and jade. These leaves store water and nutrients in their tissues, which help them survive in dry and harsh environments. They also have few or no stomata, which minimize water loss and prevent overheating. Succulent leaves are often modified into spines, which protect the plant from herbivores and provide shade.

Direct observation can show how organisms have evolved in response to natural selection, genetic drift, or human intervention. For example, the peppered moth in England changed its color from light to dark, due to the pollution that made the trees darker. This increased its survival rate from predators. On the other hand, the antibiotic-resistant bacteria have evolved due to the overuse of antibiotics that killed the susceptible bacteria. This decreased their mortality rate from drugs .

These are some of the evidences for biological evolution that can help us understand how life has diversified and adapted over time.



### Mechanism of evolution

- Evolution is the process by which modern organisms have descended from ancient ancestors.
- Evolution is responsible for both the remarkable similarities and the amazing diversity of life.
- Evolution occurs when the frequencies of traits (and the genes that control them) in populations change over generations.
- There are five main mechanisms of evolution: natural selection, sexual selection, artificial selection, genetic drift, and gene flow.
- Natural selection is the process by which individuals with certain traits have higher survival and reproduction than others in a given environment .
- Sexual selection is the process by which individuals with certain traits have higher mating success than others in a given population.
- Artificial selection is the process by which humans intentionally breed individuals with certain traits for specific purposes.
- Genetic drift is the random change in allele frequencies due to chance events in small populations .
- Gene flow is the movement of alleles between populations due to migration, dispersal, or interbreeding .
- These mechanisms of evolution can act in isolation or in combination, and can result in microevolution (evolution within a population) or macroevolution (evolution above the species level)  .
- Evolution is supported by multiple lines of evidence, such as fossils, comparative anatomy, molecular biology, biogeography, and experimental evolution .



### Variation

Variation is the difference between individuals of the same species, caused by genetic and environmental factors . Variation can be classified into two types: continuous and discontinuous.

- Continuous variation is when the data come in a range, such as height, weight, or skin color. Continuous variation is influenced by both genes and the environment .
- Discontinuous variation is when the data come in groups, such as blood type, eye color, or sex. Discontinuous variation is determined by genes alone .

Variation is important for the survival and evolution of species, as it provides the raw material for natural selection . Natural selection is the process by which organisms with favorable traits are more likely to survive and reproduce than those with unfavorable traits . Natural selection can result in different types of evolution, such as:

- Directional selection, when one extreme of a trait is favored over the other, such as larger body size or darker fur color.
- Stabilizing selection, when the intermediate form of a trait is favored over the extremes, such as medium body size or medium fur color.
- Disruptive selection, when both extremes of a trait are favored over the intermediate, such as small or large body size or light or dark fur color.
- Sexual selection, when traits that increase the attractiveness or competitiveness of an organism are favored, such as bright colors, elaborate ornaments, or vocalizations.

Variation can arise from two main sources: mutation and recombination  .

- Mutation is a change in the DNA base sequence that results in the generation of one or more new alleles . Mutations can be caused by errors in DNA replication, environmental factors, or random events . Mutations can be beneficial, neutral, or harmful, depending on their effect on the organism's fitness .
- Recombination is the exchange of genetic material between homologous chromosomes during meiosis, the process of cell division that produces gametes (sperm and egg cells)  . Recombination increases the genetic diversity of the offspring by creating new combinations of alleles  .

Variation is one of the key factors that explain the origin and evolution of life on Earth. The fossil record, comparative anatomy, molecular biology, and biogeography provide evidence for biological evolution, the change in the genetic composition of populations over time . Some of the major events in the history of life include:

- The origin of life, which is hypothesized to have occurred about 3.8 billion years ago, when the first self-replicating molecules emerged from a primordial soup of organic compounds .
- The origin of cells, which is hypothesized to have occurred about 3.5 billion years ago, when the first prokaryotic cells (cells without a nucleus) evolved from membrane-bound vesicles .
- The origin of eukaryotic cells, which is hypothesized to have occurred about 2 billion years ago, when some prokaryotic cells engulfed other prokaryotic cells and formed a symbiotic relationship, giving rise to organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts .
- The origin of multicellular organisms, which is hypothesized to have occurred about 1.5 billion years ago, when some eukaryotic cells aggregated and specialized into different functions, forming tissues and organs .
- The origin of animals, which is hypothesized to have occurred about 600 million years ago, when some multicellular organisms developed nervous systems, muscles, and sensory organs, allowing them to move and respond to stimuli .
- The origin of plants, which is hypothesized to have occurred about 500 million years ago, when some multicellular organisms adapted to life on land and developed roots, stems, leaves, and reproductive structures .

### Mutation and Recombination

- Mutation is a change in the DNA sequence of an organism, which can be inherited by its offspring. Mutation can be caused by various factors, such as errors in DNA replication, exposure to radiation, chemicals, or viruses, or insertion of foreign DNA by transposable elements.
- Recombination is the exchange of genetic material between two DNA molecules, which can result in new combinations of alleles. Recombination can occur during meiosis, when homologous chromosomes pair up and cross over, or during DNA repair, when broken DNA ends are joined together.
- Both mutation and recombination result in variation, which is the main driving force of natural selection, which results in evolution. Random mutations can lead to new inheritable traits, which make an organism better adapted to its environment. Recombination can increase the genetic diversity of a population, which can enhance its ability to respond to changing environmental conditions.
- Mutation and recombination are especially important for the evolution of pathogens, such as bacteria, viruses, and parasites, which face constant challenges from host immune systems and drug therapies. Pathogens can evolve resistance to these defenses by acquiring new mutations or recombining with other strains. This can lead to epidemic outbreaks or chronic infections.
- Mutation and recombination can also affect the evolution of recombination itself, by influencing the costs and benefits of recombination for an organism. For example, recombination can be favored if it breaks negative associations among selected alleles, which interfere with adaptation, or if it creates novel combinations of alleles, which can confer new functions or advantages. However, recombination can also be disfavored if it disrupts favorable associations among selected alleles, which enhance adaptation, or if it exposes deleterious recessive alleles, which can reduce fitness.
