- Orthographic projection follows two main principles: the principle of projection and the principle of alignment. The principle of alignment states that the corresponding points on different views are aligned along a common line perpendicular to the plane of projection.
- Orthographic projection can be done using two methods: first-angle projection and third-angle projection. The front view is projected on the vertical plane, the top view is projected on the horizontal plane, and the side view is projected on the plane perpendicular to the horizontal and vertical planes. The views are then unfolded to form a two-dimensional drawing, with the front view on the right, the top view on the bottom, and the side view on the left.
- In third-angle projection, the object or system is placed in the third quadrant, behind the horizontal and vertical planes of projection.   - Use a single dimensioning system, either metric or imperial, and do not mix them in the same drawing.
  - Place dimensions outside the views, unless they are too large or interfere with other dimensions or views.
  - Align dimensions with the dimension lines and place them above the dimension lines, except for angular dimensions, which are placed in the arc.
  - Use horizontal and vertical dimension lines, and avoid inclined or curved dimension lines, unless necessary for circular or angular features.
  - Use leader lines to connect dimensions to the features, and make them straight and inclined at 30° or 60° to the horizontal or vertical.
  - Use arrowheads or dots to indicate the ends of the dimension lines and the points of the leader lines.
  - Use extension lines to extend the features beyond the outline of the object and leave a small gap between them and the outline.
  - Avoid crossing or overlapping dimension lines, extension lines, leader lines, or other lines in the drawing.
  - Use chain or continuous dimensioning for linear dimensions, and avoid dimensioning the same feature twice or using unnecessary dimensions.
  - Use aligned or parallel dimensioning for circular or angular dimensions, and place them in the most descriptive view.
  - Use auxiliary views or sections for dimensioning features that are not clearly visible or measurable in the principal views.
  - Use symbols, abbreviations, and notes to indicate special features or requirements, such as diameter, radius, chamfer, countersink, counterbore, thread, surface finish, tolerance, etc.

### Transformation of Projections

- A projection is a method of representing a three-dimensional (3D) object on a two-dimensional (2D) plane, such as a sheet of paper or a computer screen.
- A projection is produced by connecting the points where the lines of sight from an observer to the object pierce the projection plane. As a result, the 3D object is transformed into a 2D view.
- There are different types of projections, depending on the orientation of the projection plane and the direction of the lines of sight. Some common types are:
  - Parallel projection: The lines of sight are assumed to be parallel, and the projection plane is perpendicular to a principal axis of the object. This type of projection preserves the true shape and size of the object, but distorts the angles and distances. Parallel projection is often used for engineering drawings and drafting.
  - Perspective projection: The lines of sight converge at a single point, called the center of projection or the eye point. The projection plane is perpendicular to the line joining the center of projection and the center of the object. This type of projection mimics the way humans see the world, and creates a realistic impression of depth and distance. However, it also distorts the shape and size of the object.
  - Oblique projection: The lines of sight are parallel, but the projection plane is not perpendicular to any principal axis of the object. This type of projection creates a distorted view of the object, but allows some of its features to be seen more clearly. Oblique projection is often used for pictorial drawings and sketches.
- A transformation of projection is a change of the projection type, the projection plane, or the center of projection. This can be done by applying a matrix multiplication to the coordinates of the object, or by using geometric constructions. A transformation of projection can alter the appearance of the object, but not its intrinsic properties.
- Some examples of transformation of projection are:
  - Projective transformation: A transformation that preserves the collinearity and the cross-ratio of points, but not the parallelism or the angles. This type of transformation can be used to convert between different perspective projections, or between a perspective projection and a parallel projection.
  - Affine transformation: A transformation that preserves the parallelism and the ratios of distances, but not the angles or the lengths. This type of transformation can be used to convert between different parallel projections, or between a parallel projection and an oblique projection.
  - Orthographic transformation: A transformation that preserves the angles and the lengths, but not the ratios of distances. This type of transformation can be used to convert between different orthographic projections, which are a special case of parallel projection where the projection plane is perpendicular to one of the principal planes of the object.

### Geometry and topology of engineered components

- Geometry is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties, measurements, and relationships of points, lines, angles, surfaces, and solids in space.
- Topology is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties of shapes that are preserved under continuous deformations, such as stretching, twisting, bending, and shrinking, but not tearing or gluing.
- Geometry and topology are closely related, as many geometric concepts can be generalized and abstracted to topological ones, and many topological problems can be approached using geometric methods and tools.
- In engineering, geometry and topology are important for designing, modeling, analyzing, and optimizing the shape, structure, and function of components, systems, and materials.
- Some examples of applications of geometry and topology in engineering are:

  - Computer-aided design (CAD) is the use of computer software to create, modify, analyze, and optimize the geometry and topology of 2D or 3D models of physical objects or systems.
  - Finite element analysis (FEA) is a numerical method for solving partial differential equations that arise from physical phenomena, such as heat transfer, fluid flow, stress, and deformation, by discretizing the domain into a mesh of finite elements with specified geometry and topology.
  - Computational geometry is the study of algorithms and data structures for solving geometric problems, such as convex hulls, Voronoi diagrams, Delaunay triangulations, intersection detection, and mesh generation.
  - Computational topology is the study of algorithms and data structures for solving topological problems, such as homology, homotopy, persistent homology, Morse theory, and knot theory.
  - Geometric modeling is the process of creating and manipulating the geometry and topology of curves, surfaces, and solids, using mathematical representations, such as parametric, implicit, or polygonal models.
  - Geometric processing is the process of transforming, simplifying, smoothing, repairing, or optimizing the geometry and topology of geometric models, using techniques such as subdivision, decimation, remeshing, fairing, or parameterization.
  - Topological optimization is the process of finding the optimal shape and topology of a structure or material that satisfies certain constraints and objectives, such as minimizing weight, maximizing stiffness, or enhancing functionality.



### Creation of engineering models and their presentation in standard 2D blueprint form

- Engineering models are representations of physical objects or systems that capture their essential features and behavior. They can be used for analysis, design, simulation, testing, or communication purposes.
- Engineering models can be created using various methods, such as sketching, drafting, CAD (computer-aided design), or CAE (computer-aided engineering) software. The choice of method depends on the complexity, accuracy, and purpose of the model.
- Engineering models can be presented in different forms, such as 2D blueprints, 3D wireframes, 3D shaded solids, or meshed topologies. The choice of form depends on the level of detail, clarity, and standardization required for the presentation.
- 2D blueprints are drawings that show the geometry, dimensions, tolerances, and annotations of an engineering model in two dimensions. They are often used for manufacturing, assembly, inspection, or documentation purposes.
- 2D blueprints follow certain rules and conventions, such as ISO (International Organization for Standardization) or ANSI (American National Standards Institute) standards, for coordinate dimensioning and tolerancing, geometric dimensioning and tolerancing, projection methods, line types, symbols, and units.
- 2D blueprints can be created manually or automatically from 3D models using CAD software. The software can also generate associative models, which link the 2D blueprints to the 3D models, and allow for automatic updates, interference checking, and bill of materials generation.
