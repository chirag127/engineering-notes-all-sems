- Orthographic projection can be further divided into two types: first-angle projection and third-angle projection. First-angle projection is used in Europe and Asia, while third-angle projection is used in North America and Australia.
- In first-angle projection, the object is placed in the first quadrant, and the views are projected onto the planes as if they are folded inward. The front view is on the right, the top view is below, and the left view is on the left.
- In third-angle projection, the object is placed in the third quadrant, and the views are projected onto the planes as if they are folded outward. The front view is on the left, the top view is above, and the right view is on the right.
- The views in orthographic projection are aligned according to the projection lines, which are perpendicular to the planes and pass through the corresponding points on the object.
- The views in orthographic projection should show the true shape and size of the features of the object, and should avoid hidden lines, overlapping lines, and unnecessary details.

### Dimensioning

- Dimensioning is the process of adding measurements and other information to a drawing to specify the size, shape, location, and features of an object.
- Dimensioning is essential for engineering drawings, as it allows the fabrication, inspection, and assembly of the object according to the design specifications.
- Dimensioning follows certain rules and conventions to ensure clarity, accuracy, and consistency of the drawings.
- Some of the basic rules and conventions of dimensioning are:

  - Dimensions should be placed outside the outline of the object, unless there is not enough space or it would reduce the clarity of the drawing.
  - Dimensions should be aligned with the dimension lines, which are thin, continuous lines with arrowheads at both ends to indicate the extent of the dimension.
  - Dimensions should be placed above the dimension lines, except for angular dimensions, which are placed in the arc of the angle.
  - Dimensions should be given in millimeters (mm) or meters (m), unless otherwise specified.
  - Dimensions should be given to the nearest 0.1 mm for sizes up to 100 mm, and to the nearest 1 mm for sizes above 100 mm, unless otherwise specified.
  - Dimensions should not be repeated or duplicated on the same drawing or on different views of the same object.
  - Dimensions should not be derived from other dimensions, such as by adding or subtracting, unless they are clearly marked as reference dimensions, which are given in parentheses.
  - Dimensions should not cross or coincide with other dimension lines, extension lines, or outlines of the object.
  - Extension lines are thin, continuous lines that extend from the outline of the object to the dimension lines, with a small gap between them.
  - Extension lines should not extend beyond the dimension lines, unless they are used to dimension a feature that is hidden or not visible in the view.
  - Dimensions should be placed in the most descriptive view of the object, where the shape and size of the feature are clearly shown.
  - Dimensions should be arranged in a logical order, from general to specific, and from overall to detail.
  - Dimensions should be grouped or aligned whenever possible, to avoid clutter and confusion.
  - Dimensions should be placed parallel to the feature being dimensioned, or to the horizontal or vertical axis of the drawing, unless otherwise specified.
  - Dimensions should be placed on the same side of the object as the feature being dimensioned, unless it would reduce the clarity of the drawing.
  - Dimensions should be placed symmetrically about the centerline or axis of symmetry of the object, if applicable.
  - Dimensions should be placed with respect to the datum or reference plane of the object, if applicable.
  - Datum or reference plane is a plane or surface that is used as a basis for measuring or locating other features of the object.
  - Dimensions should be placed with respect to the functional or manufacturing requirements of the object, such as tolerances, fits, surface finish, etc., if applicable.
  - Tolerances are the allowable variations in the dimensions of the object, which are specified by adding upper and lower limits to the nominal dimension, or by using symbols or abbreviations.
  - Fits are the degree of tightness or looseness between two mating parts, such as a shaft and a hole, which are specified by using standard codes or symbols.
  - Surface finish is the quality or texture of the surface of the object, which is specified by using symbols or abbreviations.

### Classification of Solids

- A solid is an object that has three dimensions, i.e., length, breadth and height.
- Solids can be classified into two types: **crystalline solids** and **amorphous solids**.
- Crystalline solids are solids that have a regular and orderly arrangement of atoms, ions or molecules in a three-dimensional lattice. Crystalline solids have sharp melting points, definite shapes and angles, and can be cleaved along specific planes.
- Amorphous solids are solids that have a random and irregular arrangement of atoms, ions or molecules in a three-dimensional structure. Amorphous solids have no sharp melting points, no definite shapes and angles, and break into irregular pieces when cut.
- Crystalline solids can be further classified into four types based on the nature of the bonding between the constituent particles. These are: **ionic solids**, **metallic solids**, **covalent solids**, and **molecular solids**.
- Ionic solids are solids that consist of cations and anions held together by strong electrostatic forces. Ionic solids have high melting points, low electrical conductivity in solid state, and are hard and brittle. Examples of ionic solids are sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, and calcium carbonate.
- Metallic solids are solids that consist of metal atoms held together by metallic bonds. Metallic solids have high melting points, high electrical and thermal conductivity, and are malleable and ductile. Examples of metallic solids are iron, copper, and gold.
- Covalent solids are solids that consist of atoms held together by covalent bonds. Covalent solids have very high melting points, low or no electrical conductivity, and are hard and rigid. Examples of covalent solids are diamond, graphite, and silicon.
- Molecular solids are solids that consist of molecules held together by intermolecular forces. Molecular solids have low melting points, low or no electrical conductivity, and are soft and flexible. Examples of molecular solids are ice, sugar, and wax.

- The projections of solids can be used to study the shape, size, and position of the solids in different views . The projections of solids can also be used to draw the sections of the solids by cutting them with planes.

### Transformation of Projections

- A projection is a method of representing a three-dimensional (3D) object on a two-dimensional (2D) plane, such as a sheet of paper or a computer screen.
- A projection is obtained by drawing lines of sight from an observer's eye to the points on the object and marking where these lines intersect a plane of projection.
- A plane of projection is an imaginary flat surface that acts as a screen for the projected image of the object.
- A projection can be classified into two types: parallel and perspective.
- A parallel projection is a projection where the lines of sight are parallel to each other and perpendicular to the plane of projection. This type of projection preserves the relative proportions and angles of the object, but not the true distances or sizes.
- A perspective projection is a projection where the lines of sight converge at a single point, called the center of projection or the eye point. This type of projection mimics the way humans see objects, but distorts the relative proportions and angles of the object.
- A projection can also be classified by the orientation of the plane of projection relative to the object. The most common orientations are: top, front, right, left, bottom, and back. These are also called orthographic projections, because they are obtained by projecting the object along the orthogonal axes of a coordinate system.
- A projection can also be classified by the angle between the plane of projection and the lines of sight. The most common angles are: 0°, 30°, 45°, and 90°. These are also called axonometric projections, because they show the object along three axes. The special cases of axonometric projections are: isometric, dimetric, and trimetric projections.
- A transformation of projections is a process of changing the type, orientation, or angle of a projection, while preserving the essential features of the object. This can be done by applying mathematical operations, such as translation, rotation, scaling, or shearing, to the coordinates of the projected points.
- A transformation of projections can be useful for various purposes, such as: showing different views of the object, simplifying the drawing, enhancing the visibility, or creating artistic effects.

### Geometry and topology of engineered components

- Geometry is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties, measurements, and relationships of points, lines, angles, surfaces, and solids in space.
- Topology is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties of shapes that are preserved under continuous deformations, such as stretching, twisting, bending, and shrinking, but not tearing or gluing.
- Geometry and topology are closely related, as many geometric concepts can be generalized and abstracted to topological ones, and many topological problems can be approached by geometric methods.
- In engineering, geometry and topology are important for designing and analyzing the shape, size, structure, and function of various components, such as mechanical parts, electrical circuits, optical devices, and biological systems.
