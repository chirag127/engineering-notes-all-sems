

## LIST OF EXPERIMENTS

The following is a list of experiments that are commonly conducted in science and engineering fields. These experiments are designed to test hypotheses, validate theories, and provide insights into various phenomena.

1. **Simple Pendulum Experiment:** This experiment involves suspending a small mass from a string and measuring its oscillations. It is used to determine the relationship between the period of oscillation and the length of the string.

2. **Ohm's Law Experiment:** This experiment involves measuring the voltage and current in a circuit to determine the relationship between them. It is used to validate Ohm's law and to determine the resistance of a circuit.

3. **Boyle's Law Experiment:** This experiment involves measuring the volume and pressure of a gas at constant temperature. It is used to validate Boyle's law and to determine the pressure-volume relationship of a gas.

4. **Young's Double-Slit Experiment:** This experiment involves shining a light through two slits and observing the interference pattern that results. It is used to demonstrate the wave nature of light and to determine the wavelength of the light.

5. **Michelson-Morley Experiment:** This experiment involves measuring the speed of light in different directions to test the idea of the luminiferous ether. It is used to demonstrate that there is no ether and that the speed of light is constant.

6. **Millikan Oil Drop Experiment:** This experiment involves measuring the charge on an oil droplet to determine the charge of an electron. It is used to validate the atomic model and to determine the fundamental charge of an electron.

7. **Rutherford Scattering Experiment:** This experiment involves firing alpha particles at a thin gold foil and observing their scattering patterns. It is used to demonstrate the structure of the atom and to determine the size of its nucleus.

8. **Magnetic Field Mapping Experiment:** This experiment involves mapping the magnetic field around a magnet or current-carrying wire. It is used to visualize the magnetic field and to determine its strength and direction.

9. **Titration Experiment:** This experiment involves adding a titrant to a solution until a chemical reaction is complete. It is used to determine the concentration of the unknown solution and to validate chemical equations.

10. **Chromatography Experiment:** This experiment involves separating a mixture of compounds by their different affinities to a stationary or mobile phase. It is used to identify the components of a mixture and to quantify their amounts.

In conclusion, these experiments have played a significant role in advancing our understanding of the natural world and have contributed to the development of many technologies. It is essential for students of science and engineering to become familiar with these experiments and to understand their underlying concepts and principles.

# 1. Verification of Kirchhoff’s laws

Kirchhoff’s laws are fundamental principles in electrical circuit analysis. They are used to determine the behavior of electric circuits in a wide range of applications. There are two Kirchhoff’s laws: Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL). In this section, we will learn how to verify these laws experimentally.

## Verification of Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL)

Kirchhoff’s Current Law states that the total current entering a node is equal to the total current leaving that node. This law can be verified experimentally using the following steps:

1. Set up a simple circuit with a power supply, a resistor, and a few wires.
2. Connect an ammeter in series with the resistor to measure the current flowing through it.
3. Connect the wires to a breadboard and connect them to the power supply and resistor.
4. Add a few more wires to create a node where the current splits into two or more branches.
5. Connect the branches to the breadboard and measure the current flowing through each branch using the ammeter.
6. Add up the currents flowing through each branch and verify that the total current entering the node is equal to the total current leaving the node.

## Verification of Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL)

Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law states that the sum of the voltages in a closed loop is equal to zero. This law can be verified experimentally using the following steps:

1. Set up a simple circuit with a power supply, a few resistors, and a few wires.
2. Connect a voltmeter in parallel with each resistor to measure the voltage drop across it.
3. Connect the wires to a breadboard and connect them to the power supply and resistors.
4. Create a closed loop by connecting the resistors in series or parallel.
5. Measure the voltage drop across each resistor using the voltmeter and write down the values.
6. Add up the voltages across each resistor in the loop and verify that the sum is equal to zero.

In conclusion, Kirchhoff’s laws are fundamental principles in electrical circuit analysis that can be verified experimentally. By following the steps outlined above, you can verify Kirchhoff’s Current Law and Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law in a simple circuit. These laws are essential for analyzing complex circuits and understanding the behavior of electrical systems.

The study of resonance in an RLC series circuit is an important topic in the field of electronics. Here are some key points to help you understand this phenomenon:

1. An RLC series circuit consists of a resistor, an inductor, and a capacitor connected in series.
2. When an AC voltage is applied to the circuit, the voltage across each component will vary with time due to the varying current.
3. At certain frequencies, the voltage across the inductor and the capacitor will be equal in magnitude but opposite in phase. This results in the cancellation of the voltage across the capacitor and the inductor, leading to a minimum in the total circuit impedance.
4. This minimum impedance is called the resonant impedance, and the frequency at which it occurs is called the resonant frequency.
5. The resonant frequency can be calculated using the formula f = 1 / (2 * pi * sqrt(LC)), where f is the resonant frequency, L is the inductance of the inductor, and C is the capacitance of the capacitor.
6. At frequencies above and below the resonant frequency, the impedance of the circuit increases due to the increased voltage across the inductor and capacitor.
7. The resonance phenomenon is used in various applications, such as in radio and television circuits, filters, and oscillators.

In conclusion, understanding the phenomenon of resonance in an RLC series circuit and the calculation of resonant frequency is crucial in the field of electronics.

# 1. Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's Laws are fundamental laws in the field of electrical engineering and physics. They are named after the German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff, who first introduced them in 1845. These laws are used to analyze and understand the behavior of electrical circuits. There are two laws, namely Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL). Let's understand each of these laws in detail.

## Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Kirchhoff's Current Law is also known as the first law of Kirchhoff or the Kirchhoff's junction rule. This law states that the total current entering a junction or node in an electrical circuit is equal to the total current leaving that junction. In other words, the algebraic sum of the currents flowing towards a node (or junction) is equal to the algebraic sum of the currents flowing away from that node. The KCL is based on the principle of conservation of charge.

Some important points to remember about KCL are:

- The sum of currents entering a junction is equal to the sum of currents leaving that junction.
- The current is a scalar quantity, and its direction is determined by the choice of the reference direction.
- The KCL can be used to analyze both DC and AC circuits.

## Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law is also known as the second law of Kirchhoff or the Kirchhoff's loop rule. This law states that the algebraic sum of the voltage drops (or rises) around any closed loop in an electrical circuit is equal to zero. In other words, the sum of the voltage drops across all the elements in a closed loop is equal to the sum of the voltage rises across all the elements in the same loop. The KVL is based on the principle of conservation of energy.

Some important points to remember about KVL are:

- The sum of voltage drops (or rises) around a closed loop is equal to zero.
- The voltage is a scalar quantity, and its direction is determined by the choice of the reference direction.
- The KVL can be used to analyze both DC and AC circuits.

In conclusion, Kirchhoff's Laws are essential tools for analyzing and understanding the behavior of electrical circuits. By applying these laws, one can determine the current and voltage in any part of the circuit. These laws are widely used in the design and analysis of electrical circuits, and understanding them is crucial for any student or professional in the field of electrical engineering.

    f = 1 / (2π √(LC))

    Q = R √(C / L)

- The bandwidth of an RLC circuit is the range of frequencies over which the circuit is resonant, and is given by:

    BW = f / Q

    where f is the resonant frequency and Q is the quality factor.

Iron losses are losses that occur due to hysteresis and eddy currents in the motor core. These losses are proportional to the flux density and the square of the frequency of the alternating magnetic field.

##### CO 4 Calculate efficiency of a single phase transformer and DC machine. K4

Efficiency calculation is an important aspect when it comes to the evaluation of electrical machines. In this section, we will discuss the methods to calculate efficiency of a single phase transformer and DC machine. 

### Efficiency Calculation of Single Phase Transformer

The efficiency of a transformer is defined as the ratio of output power to the input power. It is given by the following formula:

Efficiency = Output power / Input power

Where, Output power = VI cosΦ2
Input power = VI cosΦ1

VI is the voltage and current at the input and output of the transformer respectively, cosΦ1 is the power factor at the primary side, and cosΦ2 is the power factor at the secondary side.

### Efficiency Calculation of DC Machine

The efficiency of a DC machine is defined as the ratio of output power to the input power. It is given by the following formula:

Efficiency = Output power / Input power

Where, Output power = VI
Input power = VI + losses

VI is the voltage and current at the output of the machine, and losses include copper losses, core losses, and mechanical losses.

### Copper Losses

Copper losses are the power losses that occur due to the resistance of the copper winding in the machine. Copper losses can be calculated using the formula:

Copper losses = I²R

Where, I is the current flowing through the winding and R is the resistance of the winding.

### Core Losses

Core losses are the power losses that occur due to hysteresis and eddy current losses in the core of the machine. Core losses can be calculated using the formula:

Core losses = Kf Bmax^1.6 + Ke f^2 t^2 Bmax^2

Where, Kf and Ke are constants, f is the frequency of the input power, t is the thickness of the core, and Bmax is the maximum flux density.

### Mechanical Losses

Mechanical losses are the power losses that occur due to friction and windage losses in the machine. Mechanical losses can be calculated using the formula:

Mechanical losses = Kw N^3

Where, Kw is the constant and N is the speed of the machine.

In conclusion, the efficiency of a single phase transformer and DC machine can be calculated using the formula of output power to input power. Copper losses, core losses, and mechanical losses are important factors that need to be considered while calculating the efficiency of a DC machine.

   - There are two types of tachometers: contact and non-contact.
   - Contact tachometers use physical contact with the rotating shaft to measure the speed, while non-contact tachometers use optical or magnetic sensors.
   - The speed of the motor can be measured in RPM (revolutions per minute) or RPS (revolutions per second).
   - The measured speed can be compared to the rated speed of the motor to determine if the motor is operating correctly.

2. Reversal of Direction Experiment:
   - The direction of rotation of a three phase induction motor can be reversed by swapping any two of the three motor leads.
   - This can be done manually or with a reversing starter.
   - A reversing starter is a device that automatically reverses the direction of rotation of a motor.
   - The reversing starter consists of two contactors and a mechanical interlock.
   - When one contactor is energized, the motor rotates in one direction, and when the other contactor is energized, the motor rotates in the opposite direction.

##### Identification of DC and AC Machines Based on their Construction

1. DC Machine Identification:
   - DC machines have a commutator and brushes.
   - The commutator is a rotating electrical switch that converts the AC current in the armature windings to DC current in the brushes.
   - The brushes are made of graphite or carbon and make contact with the commutator.
   - The armature windings are located on the rotor, while the field windings are located on the stator.
   - DC machines can be further classified as either a DC generator or a DC motor.

2. AC Machine Identification:
   - AC machines do not have a commutator or brushes.
   - AC machines can be further classified as either a synchronous machine or an induction machine.
   - Synchronous machines operate at a constant speed and are commonly used in power generation.
   - Induction machines are the most commonly used type of AC machine and are used in a variety of applications, including pumps, fans, and compressors.
   - Induction machines consist of a stator and a rotor.
   - The stator contains the primary winding, while the rotor contains the secondary winding.
   - Induction machines operate based on the principle of electromagnetic induction.
