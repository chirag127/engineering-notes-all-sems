##### 1. Verification of Kirchhoff’s laws

Kirchhoff’s laws are two laws that deal with the conservation of charge and energy in electrical circuits. These laws are known as Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL).

- **Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL)**: This law states that the algebraic sum of currents entering and leaving a node in an electrical circuit is zero. In other words, the total current entering a node is equal to the total current leaving the node.

- **Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL)**: This law states that the algebraic sum of the voltage drops around any closed loop in an electrical circuit is zero. In other words, the total voltage rise in a closed loop is equal to the total voltage drop in the same loop.

To verify Kirchhoff’s laws, an experiment can be performed using a simple electrical circuit consisting of a battery, resistors, and a multimeter. The multimeter can be used to measure the current and voltage at different points in the circuit. By comparing the measured values with the values predicted by Kirchhoff’s laws, the validity of the laws can be verified.

##### 2. Thevenin Theorem
Thevenin's Theorem is a technique used to analyze complex electrical circuits by simplifying them into an equivalent circuit composed of a single voltage source, a single resistor, and a load. The theorem states that any linear, bilateral circuit can be replaced by an equivalent circuit consisting of a voltage source and a series resistor.

The steps to apply Thevenin's Theorem are as follows:
1. Remove the load resistor from the original circuit.
2. Calculate the voltage across the open connection where the load resistor was removed. This voltage is known as the Thevenin voltage (Vth).
3. Calculate the equivalent resistance of the circuit as seen from the open connection where the load resistor was removed. This resistance is known as the Thevenin resistance (Rth).
4. Replace the original circuit with an equivalent circuit consisting of a voltage source with a voltage equal to the Thevenin voltage (Vth) in series with a resistor with a resistance equal to the Thevenin resistance (Rth).
5. Reconnect the load resistor to the equivalent circuit.

Thevenin's Theorem can be used to analyze both DC and AC circuits. It is a powerful tool for simplifying complex circuits and making them easier to analyze. It is also useful for analyzing circuits with multiple sources and for determining the maximum power transfer to a load.

Course outcomes (COs) are statements that describe the knowledge, skills, and abilities that students should possess and can demonstrate upon completion of a course. COs are typically written at the course level and are used to guide the development of the course and its assessments.

For example, a CO that requires students to “recall the steps of the scientific method” would be at the Remembering level of Bloom’s Taxonomy, while a CO that requires students to “design an experiment to test a hypothesis” would be at the Creating level.

- KCL (Kirchhoff's Current Law) states that the sum of the currents entering a node in a circuit is equal to the sum of the currents leaving that node.
- Network theorems, such as Thevenin's theorem and Norton's theorem, can be used to simplify complex circuits and make it easier to analyze them.
- To conduct experiments illustrating the application of KVL/KCL and network theorems to DC electrical circuits, one can set up a simple circuit with known resistances and voltage sources.

- One can also use network theorems to simplify the circuit and predict the behavior of the circuit, and then verify the predictions through experimentation.
- These experiments can help students understand the fundamental principles of circuit analysis and the practical application of network theorems.

##### CO 3 Perform experiment illustrating BH curve of magnetic materials. K3

The BH curve, also known as the hysteresis loop, is a graphical representation of the relationship between the magnetic field strength (H) and the magnetic flux density (B) of a magnetic material. The curve is used to characterize the magnetic properties of a material and can be used to predict its behavior in the presence of an external magnetic field.

To perform an experiment illustrating the BH curve of a magnetic material, the following steps can be followed:

1. Prepare a sample of the magnetic material to be tested. This can be in the form of a ring or a toroid.
2. Set up the test equipment, which typically includes a magnetizing coil, a flux meter, and a power supply.
3. Place the sample inside the magnetizing coil and connect the flux meter to measure the magnetic flux density (B) of the sample.
4. Gradually increase the current in the magnetizing coil to increase the magnetic field strength (H) applied to the sample.
5. Record the values of B and H as the current is increased.
6. Gradually decrease the current in the magnetizing coil to reduce the magnetic field strength (H) applied to the sample.
7. Record the values of B and H as the current is decreased.
8. Plot the recorded values of B and H on a graph to obtain the BH curve of the magnetic material.

The shape of the BH curve can provide valuable information about the magnetic properties of the material, such as its coercivity, remanence, and saturation magnetization. These properties can be used to predict the behavior of the material in the presence of an external magnetic field and can be useful in the design of magnetic devices and systems.

1. Measure the input voltage and current to the transformer.
2. Measure the output voltage and current from the transformer.
3. Calculate the input power by multiplying the input voltage by the input current.
4. Calculate the output power by multiplying the output voltage by the output current.
5. Divide the output power by the input power to obtain the efficiency of the transformer.

2. Measure the mechanical output power of the DC machine.

#### K1 – Remember, K2 – Understand, K3 – Apply, K4 – Analyze, K5 – Evaluate, K6 – Create

These are the six levels of Bloom's Taxonomy, a framework for categorizing educational goals and objectives. The taxonomy is hierarchical, meaning that each level builds upon the previous one.

1. **K1 – Remember**: This level involves recalling information from memory. Examples of tasks at this level include defining terms, listing facts, and reciting information.
2. **K2 – Understand**: This level involves comprehending the meaning of information. Examples of tasks at this level include explaining concepts, summarizing information, and interpreting data.
3. **K3 – Apply**: This level involves using information in a new situation. Examples of tasks at this level include solving problems, applying theories, and demonstrating techniques.
4. **K4 – Analyze**: This level involves breaking down information into parts and examining their relationships. Examples of tasks at this level include comparing and contrasting, categorizing, and identifying cause and effect.
5. **K5 – Evaluate**: This level involves making judgments based on criteria and standards. Examples of tasks at this level include evaluating the validity of arguments, assessing the quality of work, and making decisions.
6. **K6 – Create**: This level involves combining elements to form a new whole. Examples of tasks at this level include designing, constructing, and planning.

These levels can be used to design educational activities and assessments that promote higher-order thinking skills. By moving beyond simple recall and comprehension, students can develop deeper understanding and the ability to apply, analyze, evaluate, and create new knowledge.
