

## LIST OF EXPERIMENTS

- An experiment is a scientific procedure that is carried out to test a hypothesis, observe a phenomenon, or measure a variable.
- Experiments usually involve manipulating one or more independent variables and measuring their effects on one or more dependent variables.
- Experiments can be classified into different types based on their design, purpose, or outcome, such as:

  - Controlled experiments: These are experiments where all the variables except the independent variable are kept constant or controlled, and the effects of the independent variable on the dependent variable are observed.
  - Randomized experiments: These are experiments where the subjects or units are randomly assigned to different groups or treatments, and the outcomes are compared across the groups or treatments.
  - Natural experiments: These are experiments where the independent variable is not manipulated by the experimenter, but varies naturally due to some external factor, and the effects of the independent variable on the dependent variable are observed.
  - Quasi-experiments: These are experiments where the independent variable is not randomly assigned, but is determined by some pre-existing condition, and the effects of the independent variable on the dependent variable are observed.
  - Field experiments: These are experiments that are conducted in a natural setting, rather than in a laboratory or a controlled environment.
  - Laboratory experiments: These are experiments that are conducted in a controlled environment, such as a laboratory, where the variables can be manipulated and measured more precisely.
  - Simulation experiments: These are experiments that are conducted using a computer model or a mathematical equation, rather than using real subjects or units, to study the behavior of a system or a phenomenon.
  - Single-subject experiments: These are experiments that involve only one subject or unit, and the effects of different treatments or conditions are measured over time.
  - Factorial experiments: These are experiments that involve two or more independent variables, and the effects of each variable and their interactions on the dependent variable are measured.
  - Repeated measures experiments: These are experiments where the same subjects or units are exposed to different treatments or conditions over time, and the effects of the treatments or conditions on the dependent variable are measured.
  - Cross-sectional experiments: These are experiments where the subjects or units are measured at one point in time, and the differences or relationships between the variables are analyzed.
  - Longitudinal experiments: These are experiments where the subjects or units are measured at multiple points in time, and the changes or trends in the variables are analyzed.

# Verification of Kirchhoff’s laws

Kirchhoff’s laws are two rules that describe the conservation of electric current and electric potential in electrical circuits. They are named after Gustav Kirchhoff, a German physicist who formulated them in 1845. The laws are:

- Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL): The algebraic sum of all currents entering and exiting a node must equal zero. This means that the current that flows into a junction of wires is equal to the current that flows out of the junction. This law is based on the principle of charge conservation, which states that electric charge cannot be created or destroyed.

- Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL): The algebraic sum of all the voltages around any closed loop in a circuit must equal zero. This means that the sum of the voltage drops across the resistors, capacitors, inductors, and other components in a loop is equal to the sum of the voltage sources in the loop. This law is based on the principle of energy conservation, which states that energy cannot be created or destroyed.

To verify Kirchhoff’s laws experimentally, we need to set up a circuit with a known voltage source, such as a battery, and measure the current and voltage at different points using an ammeter and a voltmeter. We can then apply KCL to any node in the circuit and check if the sum of the currents is zero. Similarly, we can apply KVL to any loop in the circuit and check if the sum of the voltages is zero. If the measurements agree with the laws, then we have verified them. If not, then there may be some errors in the circuit or the instruments.

# Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's laws are a set of two laws that describe how current and voltage behave in electrical circuits. They are based on the principles of conservation of charge and energy, and they can be used to analyze complex circuits with multiple loops and branches.

## Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Kirchhoff's current law, also known as Kirchhoff's first law or Kirchhoff's junction rule, states that the sum of currents entering a node (or junction) in a circuit is equal to the sum of currents leaving that node. In other words, the algebraic sum of currents at any node is zero. This law implies that charge is conserved at any point in a circuit, and that current does not accumulate or disappear at any node.

Mathematically, Kirchhoff's current law can be written as:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n I_k = 0$$

# RLC series resonance

- RLC series resonance is a phenomenon that occurs in a circuit containing a resistor (R), an inductor (L), and a capacitor (C) connected in series.
- When the circuit is driven by an alternating voltage source with a frequency that matches the natural frequency of the circuit, the circuit exhibits maximum current and minimum impedance.
- The natural frequency of the circuit is given by the formula:

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

- At this frequency, the reactance of the inductor and the capacitor cancel each other, and the circuit behaves like a pure resistor with a resistance equal to R.
- The current in the circuit is in phase with the voltage source, and the voltage across each component is proportional to its impedance.
- The voltage across the resistor is equal to the source voltage, the voltage across the inductor leads the current by 90 degrees, and the voltage across the capacitor lags the current by 90 degrees.
- The phasor diagram of the circuit at resonance is shown below:

RLC series resonance phasor diagram

- The quality factor (Q) of the circuit is a measure of how sharp the resonance is, and is given by the formula:

$$Q = \frac{f_0}{\Delta f} = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$$

- The quality factor indicates how much the current in the circuit increases at resonance compared to the current at other frequencies. A higher Q means a narrower bandwidth and a more selective circuit.
- The bandwidth ($\Delta f$) of the circuit is the range of frequencies around the resonant frequency where the current is at least 70.7% of the maximum current. It is given by the formula:

$$\Delta f = \frac{f_0}{Q} = \frac{R}{2\pi L}$$

- The power factor (PF) of the circuit is the ratio of the real power (P) to the apparent power (S) in the circuit. It is given by the formula:

$$PF = \frac{P}{S} = \frac{V_R I}{V_S I} = \frac{R}{Z}$$

- The power factor indicates how efficiently the circuit converts the input voltage to useful power. A higher PF means less reactive power and less power loss in the circuit.
- At resonance, the power factor of the circuit is 1, meaning that the circuit is purely resistive and there is no reactive power. The real power is equal to the apparent power, and is given by the formula:

$$P = S = V_S I = I^2 R$$

# Determination of the efficiency of a dc motor by loss summation method (Swinburne's test)

- The efficiency of a dc motor is defined as the ratio of output (mechanical) power to input (electrical) power. 
- The efficiency of a dc motor can be measured by direct or indirect methods. 
- The direct method requires measuring the input power by a power analyzer and the output power by a speed and torque meter. 
- The indirect method, also known as the loss summation method or Swinburne's test, involves determining the losses of the dc motor without loading it. 
- The losses of a dc motor can be classified into constant losses and variable losses. 
- The constant losses are the losses that do not depend on the load, such as core loss, friction loss, and windage loss. 
- The variable losses are the losses that depend on the load, such as copper loss in the armature and the field windings. 
- The Swinburne's test is performed by running the dc motor at no load and rated speed, and measuring the no-load current and voltage. 
- The no-load input power is equal to the sum of the constant losses and the shunt field copper loss. 
- The armature copper loss can be calculated by multiplying the armature resistance and the square of the rated current. 
- The total losses can be obtained by adding the no-load input power and the armature copper loss. 
- The efficiency of the dc motor can be calculated by subtracting the total losses from the rated output power and dividing by the rated output power. 
- The Swinburne's test has the advantages of convenience, economy, and accuracy, as it does not require loading the motor or measuring the output power. 
- The Swinburne's test has the limitations of being applicable only to shunt and compound motors, and not accounting for the temperature rise and stray load losses that occur at full load.

- The efficiency of a single phase transformer is defined as the ratio of output power to input power, expressed as a percentage .
- The input and output power are measured in the same unit, either in kW or W.
- The efficiency of a single phase transformer is given by the formula :

  $$\eta = \frac{P_o}{P_i} \times 100 \%$$

  where $\eta$ is the efficiency, $P_o$ is the output power, and $P_i$ is the input power.

- The output power of a single phase transformer is equal to the product of the secondary voltage, the secondary current, and the power factor of the load :

  $$P_o = V_s I_s \cos \phi$$

  where $V_s$ is the secondary voltage, $I_s$ is the secondary current, and $\cos \phi$ is the power factor of the load.

- The input power of a single phase transformer is equal to the sum of the output power and the losses in the transformer :

  $$P_i = P_o + P_L$$

  where $P_L$ is the total losses in the transformer.

- The losses in a single phase transformer consist of two types: core losses and copper losses   .

- Core losses are caused by the alternating magnetic flux in the core, which induces eddy currents and hysteresis in the core material   .

  - Eddy currents are circular currents that flow in the core due to the changing magnetic flux. They produce heat and reduce the efficiency of the transformer   .
  - Hysteresis is the phenomenon of the magnetic domains in the core material aligning and realigning with the magnetic flux. This process also produces heat and reduces the efficiency of the transformer   .

- Core losses are constant and independent of the load. They can be reduced by using a laminated core, which reduces the eddy currents, and by using a high permeability and low hysteresis material, such as silicon steel, for the core   .

- Copper losses are caused by the resistance of the primary and secondary windings, which produce heat when current flows through them   .

  - Copper losses are proportional to the square of the current and depend on the load. They can be reduced by using thicker wires, which have lower resistance, for the windings   .

# K1 – Remember, K2 – Understand, K3 – Apply, K4 – Analyze, K5 – Evaluate, K6 – Create

- These are the six levels of cognitive learning according to Bloom's taxonomy, a framework for classifying educational objectives and outcomes.
- The levels are arranged in a hierarchy, from lower-order to higher-order thinking skills, and represent the degree of complexity and depth of learning.

  - **K1 – Remember**: This level involves recalling facts, terms, definitions, concepts, or procedures from memory. Examples of verbs that indicate this level are: list, name, define, identify, label, recognize, recall, etc.
