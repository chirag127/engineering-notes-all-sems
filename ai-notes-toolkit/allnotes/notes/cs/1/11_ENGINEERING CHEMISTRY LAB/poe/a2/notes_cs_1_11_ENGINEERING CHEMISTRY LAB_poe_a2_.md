1. pH
- pH is a measure of acidity or basicity of a liquid. It is measured on a scale of 0 to 14.

2. Surface Tension
- Surface tension refers to the tendency of a liquid to minimize its surface area. It arises due to cohesive forces between molecules in the liquid.
- It can be measured using a surface tensiometer which measures the force required to expand the surface of a liquid.
- Liquids with high surface tension like water form droplets while liquids with low surface tension spread out and wet surfaces.

3. Viscosity
- Viscosity refers to the resistance of a liquid to flow. It depends on the intermolecular forces between molecules of the liquid.
- It is measured using devices like viscometers which measure the time taken for a liquid to flow through a tube.
- Liquids with high viscosity like honey flow slowly while liquids with low viscosity like water flow fast.

### 1. Calibration of Analytical Equipment and apparatus.

1. Calibration is essential to ensure accuracy and precision of measurements. It is a process of determining and adjusting the accuracy of the measuring instrument by comparing its measurements with those of a standard over the appropriate range of measurements.
2. Calibration is done using certified reference materials (CRMs) whose physical properties are well characterized. The calibration establishes a relationship between the instrument response and the property being measured and is usually done by linear regression analysis.
3. For most analytical equipment like pH meters, spectrophotometers, balances, etc., calibration involves determining the values corresponding to two or more points across the range of measurements and fitting a linear regression line. This line is then used to determine unknown values.
4. For some instruments like gas and liquid chromatography equipment, computer-controlled calibration procedures may be used. The retention times and peak widths are recorded for standards and used to construct a calibration curve.
5. It is recommended to calibrate instruments before first use and then routinely as per the manufacturer's recommendations or internal laboratory protocols to account for drift. Calibration data should be properly documented for each instrument.
6. Uncalibrated or improperly calibrated instruments can give erroneous results and affect accuracy, precision, and reliability of measurements. Hence, calibration is a critical laboratory practice.

### 2. Determination of Hardness of water sample by EDTA method.

1. Take 100 ml of the given water sample in a conical flask.
2. Add 2-3 drops of Eriochrome Black T indicator and shake well. The water sample remains blue in colour.
3. Prepare EDTA solution of appropriate concentration, say 0.01 M or 0.05 M.
4. Using a burette, add EDTA solution to the conical flask containing water sample drop by drop while shaking continuously.
5. As EDTA is added, the colour of solution changes from blue to reddish-purple. Note down the initial burette reading at this point. This indicates the end point of reaction.
6. The amount of EDTA solution consumed gives the hardness of water sample. Note down the final burette reading.
7. Calculate the hardness of water sample using the formula,
Hardness (ppm) = (Volume of EDTA used × Molarity of EDTA × 50)

The EDTA method determines the total hardness of water which includes both temporary and permanent hardness. The hardness is expressed in terms of calcium carbonate (CaCO3) in parts per million (ppm).

### 3. Determination of Alkalinity of water sample.

1. Take 100 ml of the water sample in a conical flask.

Alkalinity (mg/L as CaCO3) = (Volume of HCl used (ml) x Concentration of HCl (N) x Equivalent weight of CaCO3 (50)) / Volume of water sample (L)

### 6. Determination of Viscosity of a given liquid by viscometer.

1. Take the viscometer and clean it thoroughly.
2. Fill the outer jacket of viscometer with the liquid of which viscosity is to be determined. The temperature of the liquid should be noted using a thermometer.
3. Allow the liquid in the outer jacket to attain a uniform temperature. This is ensured by stirring the liquid gently using a stirrer. This step is important as the viscosity of a liquid depends on its temperature.
4. Once the temperature becomes uniform, note down the time taken by the spherical ball to travel the calibrated distance inside the viscometer. This time can be noted using a stopwatch.
5. Determine the viscosity of the given liquid using the relation: Viscosity = K × Time Taken
Where, K is the Viscometer constant.
6. Repeat the experiment 2-3 times and take the average time to get more accurate results.
7. Note down the density of the liquid and determine the kinematic viscosity using the relation:
Kinematic Viscosity = (Viscosity)/Density

The viscosity and kinematic viscosity of a given liquid can be determined using the viscometer by following the above procedure. The results should be accurate if proper care is taken to maintain uniform temperature and the experiments are performed several times. The viscosity values depend on the factors like intermolecular forces between the particles, shape and size of the particles, temperature, etc.

1. Bleaching powder is calcium hypochlorite (Ca(OCl)2) and contains 35-40% available chlorine.

- Avoid exposure to iodine vapours. Perform the experiment in a well-ventilated place.

3. The type and amount of acid catalyst added controls the rate of reaction and final properties of resin.
4. The extent of reaction is monitored by measuring viscosity. When desired viscosity is reached, the reaction is stopped.
5. The resin is neutralized and diluted for storage and further use.

### 14. Determination of Cell Conductance of a solution.

1. Take two electrodes - preferably inert electrodes like platinum electrodes.
2. Immerse them in the given solution and connect them to a voltmeter and a constant cell.
3. Note down the initial reading of the voltmeter. Let this be initial potential (Ei).
4. Now pass a known current (I) through the solution and note the potential (E) across the electrodes.
5. Cell conductance (g) = Change in Voltage (E - Ei) / Applied current (I)

The conductance value gives an idea of how conductive the solution is. Higher the concentration of ions, higher will be the conductance. The determined conductance value can be used to determine the concentration of the solution using standard tables. The method gives reasonably accurate results for dilute solutions of strong electrolytes.

### 15. Determination of Rate constant of hydrolysis of esters.

1. Esters are hydrolyzed by acids and alkalis. The rate of hydrolysis can be determined by measuring the amount of ester hydrolyzed at different time intervals.
2. The rate of reaction is directly proportional to the concentration of ester. Therefore, the plot of log of concentration of ester vs time gives a straight line.
3. The slope of the straight line gives the rate constant of hydrolysis of ester.
4. The effect of temperature, pH and catalysts on the rate of hydrolysis can also be studied to get the activation energy and other constants.
5. The rate constant determined in this way helps to decide the suitability of an ester as a flavoring agent or polymer. Esters with high rate constants get hydrolyzed quickly and hence are not suitable for applications requiring stability.

- Series circuits have only one path for current to flow while parallel circuits have multiple paths.
- An important application is in batteries, where cells are often connected in series to increase the voltage or in parallel to increase the available current.

### 4. Determination of pH by titrimetric method.

1. Take a known volume of the solution whose pH is to be determined.
2. Add a few drops of pH indicator to it. Select the indicator depending upon the expected pH range of the solution.
3. Titrate the solution with a standard solution of acid/base.
4. Note the endpoint of the titration by observing the color change of the pH indicator.
5. Calculate the pH using the formula:
pH = pKw - log[conc. of H+/conc. of OH-]

Where pKw is the negative logarithm of the ionization constant of water.
Conc. of H+ can be determined from the volume and molarity of the acid/base used.
Conc. of OH- can be determined from the ionization equilibrium of water.

## Aim:
To determine the viscosity of a given liquid using a viscometer.

## Apparatus:
- Viscometer

- Given liquid (viscosity to be determined)

## Theory:
Viscosity is the resistance offered by a fluid to the flow of another fluid. It is a measure of the internal friction of a moving fluid.
The viscometer works on the principle of measuring the time taken by a fixed volume of liquid to flow through a narrow calibrated capillary tube under gravity. The viscosity of a liquid is directly proportional to the time taken and inversely proportional to the flow rate.

## Procedure:
1. Clean and dry the viscometer thoroughly.
2. Fill the upper bulb of the viscometer with the given liquid.
3. Note the time taken (in seconds) for the level of the liquid to fall from the upper mark to the lower mark on the capillary tube. This gives the flow time (t).
4. Calculate the viscosity (η) using the formula:

η = K × t

Where,
K - Viscometer constant
t - Flow time in seconds

5. Repeat the experiment 3 times and take the average flow time to calculate the viscosity.

## Precautions:
1. Ensure there are no air bubbles in the capillary tube.
2. Hold the viscometer vertically and do not disturb it during the flow of liquid.

# 12. Preparation of Urea formaldehyde (UF) resin.

2.  In the first step, urea and formaldehyde are made to react under alkaline conditions with a mole ratio of 1:2 at temperatures around 60-70°C to produce methylol ureas. The properties of the final resin depend upon the extent of condensation. A proper balance has to be maintained between the methylolation and condensation reactions to obtain desired properties in the resin.
4.  The common acid catalysts used are oxalic acid or sulfuric acid and the condensation is carried out at temperatures around 80-95°C. The ratio of formaldehyde to urea, the nature of the catalyst and the temperature are the major factors controlling the properties of the urea-formaldehyde resin.
5.  The performance of urea-formaldehyde resin depends upon factors like viscosity, pH, free formaldehyde content and the degree of polymerization. The resin is diluted before use to control the viscosity. The pH is adjusted as per the end use. The free formaldehyde must be very low as it is toxic and affects the stability of the resin. 2. DNA: In prokaryotic cells, DNA is circular and single-stranded. In eukaryotic cells, DNA is linear and double-stranded, organized into chromosomes.
3. Cell components: Prokaryotic cells have fewer cell components including ribosomes and plasma membrane. Eukaryotic cells have more cellular components including organelles like mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, etc.
4. Cell Size: Prokaryotic cells are usually smaller in size compared to eukaryotic cells which have more cellular components and an organized nucleus surrounded by a membrane.
5. Reproduction: Prokaryotic cells undergo binary fission, a type of asexual reproduction. Eukaryotic cells can undergo mitosis and meiosis for sexual reproduction.

The key differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells lie in the cellular organization and components. Prokaryotic cells are simpler in structure compared to the complex and compartmentalized structure of eukaryotic cells with organelles and a true nucleus.

### 3. To enable the students to understand about the measure of pH, surface tension and viscosity of a liquid.

1. pH: pH is a measure of how acidic/basic a liquid is. It is measured on a scale of 0 to 14 with 0 being highly acidic, 7 being neutral and 14 being highly basic. The pH can be measured using a pH meter or pH paper which changes color depending on the pH. Lower pH indicates higher H+ ion concentration and higher pH indicates higher OH- ion concentration.

2. Surface Tension: Surface tension is a measure of how difficult it is to stretch the surface of a liquid to increase its surface area. Liquids with high surface tension have a strong cohesive force between molecules. Surface tension can be measured using a du Nouy ring or a drop weight method. High surface tension liquids like water form droplets to minimize surface area. Low surface tension liquids spread out and wet surfaces easily.

3. Viscosity: Viscosity refers to the resistance of a liquid to flow. Liquids with high viscosity are thick and flow slowly while liquids with low viscosity are thin and flow fast. Viscosity can be measured using devices like viscometers which apply shear stress to the liquid and measure its flow rate. Highly viscous liquids like honey have stronger intermolecular forces between molecules resulting in slower flow. Low viscous liquids like alcohol have weaker intermolecular forces allowing faster flow.

The properties of pH, surface tension and viscosity are useful to understand the behavior and applications of different liquids. They can be used to determine the suitability of a liquid for a particular purpose.

### 3. Determination of Alkalinity of water sample.

### 4. Determination of pH by titrimetric method.

Normality of unknown solution = (Normality of standard solution) x (Volume of standard solution consumed/Volume of unknown solution taken)

pH = -log(1/Normality of acid or base)

- Structure of ester - Branched chain esters react faster than straight chain esters

### CO-5 Estimate the rate constant of reaction. K3

1. The rate constant (k) of a chemical reaction can be estimated from the slope of the line obtained by plotting concentration of reactant versus time on a graph. The units of rate constant are always M-1s-1 as the rate depends on concentration of the reactant.

2. For a first order reaction, the rate law can be written as, rate = k[A]
Where, [A] is the concentration of reactant. Integrating the rate law gives, k = 2.303/t log([A]0/[A])
Where, [A]0 is the initial concentration of reactant. A plot of log([A]0/[A]) versus time (t) gives a straight line with slope equal to -k/2.303. The value of rate constant (k) can be calculated from the slope.

3. For a second order reaction, the rate law can be written as, rate = k[A]2
Integrating the rate law gives, 1/k = 1/([A]0 - [A])
A plot of 1/[A] versus time gives a straight line with slope k. The rate constant (k) can be calculated from the slope.
