### 1. Study of various types of Active & Passive Components based on their ratings.

- Active components are those that can amplify or control the flow of electric current or voltage. They usually require an external source of energy to operate. Examples of active components are transistors, diodes, integrated circuits, etc.
- Passive components are those that do not amplify or control the electric current or voltage. They only store or dissipate energy. Examples of passive components are resistors, capacitors, inductors, etc.
- The ratings of components are the values of their electrical parameters that specify their performance and limitations. They are usually given by the manufacturers or standards. Some common ratings of components are:
  - Resistance: The opposition to the flow of electric current, measured in ohms (Ω).
  - Capacitance: The ability to store electric charge, measured in farads (F).
  - Inductance: The ability to generate an electromotive force (emf) due to a change in current, measured in henrys (H).
  - Voltage: The potential difference between two points, measured in volts (V).
  - Current: The rate of flow of electric charge, measured in amperes (A).
  - Power: The rate of doing work or transferring energy, measured in watts (W).
  - Frequency: The number of cycles of an alternating current or voltage per second, measured in hertz (Hz).
  - Impedance: The total opposition to the flow of alternating current, measured in ohms (Ω). It is a complex quantity that consists of resistance and reactance.
  - Reactance: The opposition to the flow of alternating current due to capacitance or inductance, measured in ohms (Ω). It is a complex quantity that consists of capacitive reactance and inductive reactance.
  - Gain: The ratio of output to input of a component, such as voltage gain, current gain, or power gain. It is a dimensionless quantity that can be expressed in decibels (dB).
  - Efficiency: The ratio of output power to input power of a component, expressed as a percentage (%).
  - Bandwidth: The range of frequencies over which a component can operate effectively, measured in hertz (Hz).
  - Noise: The unwanted or random fluctuations in the output of a component, measured in decibels (dB).
  - Temperature: The measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules of a component, measured in degrees Celsius (°C) or Kelvin (K).

### 4. Winding shop: Step down transformer winding of less than 5VA.

A step down transformer is a device that reduces the voltage from the primary winding to the secondary winding. It is used to convert high voltage AC to low voltage AC for various applications. The power rating of a transformer is measured in volt-ampere (VA), which is the product of the voltage and the current in the circuit. A transformer of less than 5VA is a small transformer that can be used for low power devices such as LED lights, sensors, or chargers.

The winding shop is the place where the transformer coils are made. The winding process involves the following steps:

- Selecting the appropriate wire gauge and length for the primary and secondary windings. The wire gauge determines the resistance and the current carrying capacity of the coil. The length determines the number of turns and the inductance of the coil. The wire should be insulated with enamel or varnish to prevent short circuits.
- Winding the primary coil on a cylindrical or toroidal core. The core can be made of iron, ferrite, or laminated steel. The core provides a path for the magnetic flux and reduces the leakage inductance and eddy current losses. The primary coil should be wound tightly and evenly on the core, leaving some space for the secondary coil.
- Winding the secondary coil on top of the primary coil. The secondary coil should have fewer turns than the primary coil, according to the desired voltage ratio. The secondary coil should be wound in the same direction as the primary coil, to maintain the polarity of the output voltage. The secondary coil should also be insulated from the primary coil and the core.
- Soldering the leads of the primary and secondary coils to the terminals or pins of the transformer. The leads should be stripped of the insulation and tinned with solder. The terminals or pins should be marked with the input and output voltage ratings and the polarity of the coils.
- Testing the transformer for the voltage ratio, the resistance, the continuity, and the insulation. The voltage ratio can be measured by applying a known AC voltage to the primary coil and measuring the output voltage from the secondary coil. The resistance can be measured by using an ohmmeter across the terminals of each coil. The continuity can be checked by using a continuity tester or a multimeter across the terminals of each coil. Disordering is a process of removing soldered components from a PCB by melting the solder and lifting the component off the board. Disordering is used to repair or replace faulty or damaged components on a PCB.

#### Soldering and disordering of resistor in PCB

A resistor is a passive electronic component that limits or regulates the flow of electric current in a circuit. Resistors are usually soldered on the PCB by their leads, which are thin metal wires that extend from the body of the resistor.

To solder a resistor on a PCB, follow these steps:

- Clean the PCB and the resistor leads with a flux or a solvent to remove any dirt, grease or oxidation.
- Heat up the soldering iron and tin the tip with a small amount of solder. Tin means to coat the tip with a thin layer of solder to improve heat transfer and prevent oxidation.
- Place the resistor on the PCB and align its leads with the corresponding holes or pads on the board. Bend the leads slightly to hold the resistor in place.
- Touch the tip of the soldering iron to one of the leads and the pad at the same time. Wait for a few seconds until the solder melts and flows into the joint. Remove the iron and let the solder cool and solidify. Repeat the same for the other lead and pad.
- Cut off the excess length of the leads with a wire cutter or a nipper. Be careful not to damage the solder joint or the PCB.
- Inspect the solder joint for any defects, such as cold solder, solder bridges, solder balls or dry joints. A good solder joint should be shiny, smooth and concave, with no gaps or cracks.

To disorder a resistor from a PCB, follow these steps:

- Heat up the soldering iron and tin the tip with a small amount of solder.
- Touch the tip of the soldering iron to one of the solder joints and wait for the solder to melt. Use a solder sucker or a desoldering braid to remove the molten solder from the joint. Repeat the same for the other solder joint.
- Gently pull the resistor off the board by its leads. Do not use excessive force or twist the resistor, as this may damage the PCB or the component.
- Clean the PCB and the resistor leads with a flux or a solvent to remove any residual solder or flux.

### 2. P-N Junction diode: Characteristics of PN Junction diode - Static and dynamic resistance measurement from graph.

A P-N junction diode is a semiconductor device that allows current to flow in one direction only. It is formed by joining a p-type and an n-type semiconductor material, creating a depletion region at the junction. The depletion region acts as a potential barrier that prevents the flow of electrons from the n-type to the p-type, and holes from the p-type to the n-type, unless an external voltage is applied.

The characteristics of a P-N junction diode are the relationship between the current and the voltage across the diode under different conditions. The characteristics can be divided into two types: static and dynamic.

- Static characteristics are the current-voltage (I-V) curves obtained when the diode is in steady state, i.e., when the voltage and current are constant. Static characteristics show the behavior of the diode under different biasing conditions: forward bias, reverse bias, and zero bias.

- Dynamic characteristics are the response of the diode to a changing voltage or current, i.e., when the diode is in transient state. Dynamic characteristics show the behavior of the diode under different frequencies, amplitudes, and waveforms of the input signal. Dynamic characteristics include parameters such as capacitance, inductance, switching speed, and frequency response.

The static and dynamic resistance of a P-N junction diode are the measures of how the diode opposes the flow of current under different conditions. The resistance of a diode is not constant, but varies with the applied voltage and current.

- Static resistance is the ratio of the applied voltage to the resulting current at a given point on the I-V curve. Static resistance can be calculated by using Ohm's law: R = V/I. Static resistance is different for forward and reverse biasing. For forward biasing, the static resistance is low, as the diode allows more current to flow. For reverse biasing, the static resistance is high, as the diode blocks most of the current.

- Dynamic resistance is the ratio of the small change in voltage to the corresponding small change in current around a given operating point on the I-V curve. Dynamic resistance can be calculated by using the slope of the tangent to the I-V curve at that point: r = dV/dI. Dynamic resistance is also different for forward and reverse biasing. For forward biasing, the dynamic resistance is low near the knee voltage, and increases as the voltage increases. For reverse biasing, the dynamic resistance is high near the breakdown voltage, and decreases as the voltage increases.

The static and dynamic resistance of a P-N junction diode can be measured from the graph of the I-V curve by using the following steps:

- To measure the static resistance, select a point on the I-V curve that corresponds to the desired biasing condition and voltage level. Read the values of the voltage and current at that point, and use Ohm's law to calculate the static resistance.

- To measure the dynamic resistance, select a point on the I-V curve that corresponds to the desired operating point and draw a tangent to the curve at that point. Read the values of the voltage and current at two nearby points on the tangent, and use the slope formula to calculate the dynamic resistance. Alternatively, use a ruler to measure the length and height of a small segment of the tangent, and use the ratio of the height to the length to calculate the dynamic resistance.

- A rectifier is a circuit that converts alternating current (AC) to direct current (DC). Rectifiers use diodes to allow only the positive or negative half of the AC waveform to pass through, while blocking the other half. Rectifiers are used in power supplies, detectors, modulators, and other applications that require DC voltage or current .
- A half-wave rectifier is a rectifier that uses only one diode to convert AC to DC. It allows only one half of the AC cycle to pass through, while blocking the other half. The output of a half-wave rectifier is a pulsating DC voltage that has the same frequency as the input AC voltage .
- A full-wave rectifier is a rectifier that uses two or four diodes to convert AC to DC. It allows both halves of the AC cycle to pass through, but in opposite directions. The output of a full-wave rectifier is a smoother DC voltage that has twice the frequency as the input AC voltage .
- Vrms is the root mean square value of the AC voltage. It is the effective value of the AC voltage that produces the same heating effect as a DC voltage of the same magnitude. Vrms can be calculated by taking the square root of the average of the squares of the instantaneous voltages over one cycle .
- Vdc is the average or mean value of the DC voltage. It is the value of the DC voltage that is equivalent to the area under the curve of the rectified output voltage over one cycle. Vdc can be calculated by taking the average of the instantaneous voltages over one cycle .
- Ripple factor is a measure of the variation or fluctuation of the DC output voltage of a rectifier. It is the ratio of the root mean square value of the AC component to the average value of the DC component of the output voltage. Ripple factor can be calculated by taking the square root of the ratio of the mean square value of the AC component to the square of the average value of the DC component of the output voltage .

### 5. Characteristic of BJT: BJT in CE configuration

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a type of semiconductor device that can amplify or switch electrical signals. A BJT has three terminals: the base (B), the collector (C), and the emitter (E). The base is the control terminal that determines the amount of current flowing from the collector to the emitter. The collector is the output terminal that collects the charge carriers from the base. The emitter is the input terminal that emits the charge carriers to the base.

A BJT can be configured in different ways depending on which terminal is connected to the common (ground) point. The most common configuration is the common-emitter (CE) configuration, where the emitter is connected to the common point. In this configuration, the base is the input and the collector is the output. The CE configuration is widely used for amplification and switching applications because it has high voltage gain, high current gain, and high power gain.

The characteristics of a BJT in CE configuration can be described by three curves: the input characteristic, the output characteristic, and the transfer characteristic.

- The input characteristic is the curve that shows the relation between the base current (IB) and the base-emitter voltage (VBE) for a given collector-emitter voltage (VCE). The input characteristic is similar to that of a forward-biased diode, as the base-emitter junction is forward-biased in the active region of operation. The input characteristic can be approximated by the equation:

  `IB = IS * (exp(VBE / VT) - 1)`

  where IS is the saturation current, and VT is the thermal voltage.

- The output characteristic is the curve that shows the relation between the collector current (IC) and the collector-emitter voltage (VCE) for a given base current (IB). The output characteristic has three regions: the cutoff region, the active region, and the saturation region. In the cutoff region, the base current is zero and the collector current is negligible. In the active region, the collector current is proportional to the base current and the collector-emitter voltage is almost constant. In the saturation region, the collector current reaches a maximum value and the collector-emitter voltage drops to a low value. The output characteristic can be approximated by the equation:

  `IC = beta * IB`

  where beta is the current gain of the transistor.

- The transfer characteristic is the curve that shows the relation between the collector current (IC) and the base current (IB) for a given collector-emitter voltage (VCE). The transfer characteristic is a straight line that passes through the origin and has a slope equal to the current gain of the transistor. The transfer characteristic can be derived from the output characteristic by eliminating the collector-emitter voltage. The transfer characteristic can be expressed by the equation:

  `IC = beta * IB`

  where beta is the current gain of the transistor.

### 6. To study Operational Amplifier as Adder and Subtractor

- An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a device that can perform various linear operations such as amplification, addition, subtraction, integration, differentiation, etc.
- An op-amp has two input terminals: the inverting input (-) and the non-inverting input (+), and one output terminal.
- An op-amp can be configured as an adder or a subtractor by using resistors and feedback loops.
- An adder is a circuit that can add two or more input voltages and produce a single output voltage that is proportional to the sum of the inputs.
- A subtractor is a circuit that can subtract one input voltage from another and produce a single output voltage that is proportional to the difference of the inputs.

#### Adder Circuit

- An adder circuit can be constructed by connecting two or more resistors to the inverting input of an op-amp and a feedback resistor to the output and the inverting input.
- The output voltage of the adder circuit is given by:

V_o = -R_f (\frac{V_1}{R_1} + \frac{V_2}{R_2} + ... + \frac{V_n}{R_n})

- The output voltage is negative because the op-amp is in the inverting mode.
- The output voltage is proportional to the sum of the input voltages, with a scaling factor of -R_f.
- The adder circuit can also be called a summing amplifier.

#### Subtractor Circuit

- A subtractor circuit can be constructed by connecting two resistors to the inverting input of an op-amp and two resistors to the non-inverting input of an op-amp, and a feedback resistor to the output and the inverting input.
- The output voltage of the subtractor circuit is given by:

V_o = R_f (\frac{V_2}{R_2} - \frac{V_1}{R_1}) (\frac{R_1 + R_f}{R_1 + R_2 + R_f})

- The output voltage is positive because the op-amp is in the non-inverting mode.
- The output voltage is proportional to the difference of the input voltages, with a scaling factor of R_f (R_1 + R_f) / (R_1 + R_2 + R_f).
- The subtractor circuit can also be called a difference amplifier.

### PN Junction Diode

A PN junction diode is a semiconductor device that consists of a p-type and an n-type region, separated by a depletion region. The p-type region has an excess of holes, while the n-type region has an excess of electrons. The depletion region is formed when some of the electrons from the n-type region diffuse into the p-type region and recombine with the holes, creating a region with no free charge carriers. The depletion region also creates a potential barrier, or a built-in voltage, that opposes the further diffusion of charge carriers.

A PN junction diode has two terminals: anode and cathode. The anode is connected to the p-type region, while the cathode is connected to the n-type region. The diode allows current to flow only when it is forward biased, that is, when the anode is at a higher potential than the cathode. When the diode is reverse biased, that is, when the anode is at a lower potential than the cathode, the diode blocks the current flow.

### Characteristics of PN Junction Diode
