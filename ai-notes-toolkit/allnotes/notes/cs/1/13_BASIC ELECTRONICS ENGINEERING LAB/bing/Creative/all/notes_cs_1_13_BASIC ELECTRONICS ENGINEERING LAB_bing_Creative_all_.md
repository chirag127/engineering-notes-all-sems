# 1. Study of various types of Active & Passive Components based on their ratings.

## Active Components
- Active components are electronic devices that can amplify, control, or generate electric signals.
- Active components require an external source of energy to operate, such as a battery or a power supply.
- Active components can increase the power of a signal, such as a transistor or an operational amplifier.
- Active components can also perform logic operations, such as a logic gate or a microcontroller.
- Some examples of active components are:

  - Transistors: These are semiconductor devices that can act as switches or amplifiers. They have three terminals: base, collector, and emitter. The base controls the current flow between the collector and the emitter. Transistors can be classified into bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) and field-effect transistors (FETs) based on their structure and operation.
  - Diodes: These are semiconductor devices that allow current to flow in one direction only. They have two terminals: anode and cathode. The anode is connected to the positive terminal of the power supply, and the cathode is connected to the negative terminal. Diodes can be classified into rectifier diodes, zener diodes, light-emitting diodes (LEDs), photodiodes, etc. based on their function and application.
  - Integrated Circuits (ICs): These are complex circuits that contain many active and passive components on a single chip. They have multiple pins that connect to the external circuit. ICs can be classified into analog ICs, digital ICs, mixed-signal ICs, etc. based on their signal processing and functionality.
  - Sensors: These are devices that can detect physical or chemical changes and convert them into electrical signals. They have two terminals: output and ground. The output varies according to the input stimulus. Sensors can be classified into temperature sensors, pressure sensors, light sensors, etc. based on their sensing element and application.

## Passive Components
- Passive components are electronic devices that do not require an external source of energy to operate. They can only store, dissipate, or transfer electric energy.
- Passive components cannot amplify, control, or generate electric signals. They can only modify the signal characteristics, such as amplitude, frequency, phase, etc.
- Passive components can also perform filtering, impedance matching, signal coupling, etc.
- Some examples of passive components are:

  - Resistors: These are devices that oppose the flow of current and produce a voltage drop across them. They have two terminals: positive and negative. The resistance value is measured in ohms (Ω) and depends on the material, length, cross-sectional area, and temperature of the resistor. Resistors can be classified into fixed resistors, variable resistors, thermistors, etc. based on their construction and behavior.
  - Capacitors: These are devices that store electric charge and produce a voltage across them. They have two terminals: positive and negative. The capacitance value is measured in farads (F) and depends on the area, distance, and dielectric material of the plates. Capacitors can be classified into fixed capacitors, variable capacitors, electrolytic capacitors, etc. based on their construction and behavior.
  - Inductors: These are devices that store magnetic energy and produce a voltage across them. They have two terminals: positive and negative. The inductance value is measured in henrys (H) and depends on the number of turns, core material, and shape of the coil. Inductors can be classified into fixed inductors, variable inductors, air-core inductors, etc. based on their construction and behavior.
  - Transformers: These are devices that transfer electric energy from one circuit to another by electromagnetic induction. They have two or more coils: primary and secondary. The primary coil is connected to the input voltage source, and the secondary coil is connected to the output load. The voltage ratio between the primary and secondary coils depends on the turns ratio of the coils. Transformers can be classified into step-up transformers, step-down transformers, isolation transformers, etc. based on their function and application.



# 2. Identification of various types of Printed Circuit Boards (PCB) and soldering Techniques

## Types of Printed Circuit Boards (PCB)

Printed circuit boards (PCBs) are thin boards made of an insulating material, with a metal coated surface, sometimes on both sides, that contain printed or etched circuits and components. PCBs are used to connect electronic devices and components in a compact and reliable way. There are various types of PCBs, depending on their design, functionality, and application. Some of the common types are:

- **Single-sided PCBs**: These are the simplest and most basic type of PCBs, where the components and circuits are only on one side of the board. They are easy to design and manufacture, but have limited functionality and density. They are usually used for simple and low-cost devices, such as calculators, radios, and toys.

- **Double-sided PCBs**: These are the most widely used type of PCBs, where the components and circuits are on both sides of the board. They have more flexibility and density than single-sided PCBs, as they can use through-hole or surface mount technology (SMT) to connect the components. They are used for more complex and advanced devices, such as computers, smartphones, and cameras.

- **Multilayer PCBs**: These are the most complex and sophisticated type of PCBs, where the components and circuits are on multiple layers of the board, usually more than two. They have the highest functionality and density, as they can use vias and interconnections to connect the layers. They are used for high-performance and high-speed devices, such as servers, satellites, and medical equipment.

- **Flexible PCBs**: These are a special type of PCBs, where the board is made of a flexible material, such as plastic or polyimide, that can bend and fold without breaking. They have the advantage of being lightweight, durable, and adaptable to different shapes and sizes. They are used for devices that require flexibility and mobility, such as wearable devices, keyboards, and displays.

- **Metal-core PCBs**: These are another special type of PCBs, where the board has a metal core, usually aluminum or copper, that acts as a heat sink and a ground plane. They have the benefit of being able to dissipate heat efficiently and reduce electromagnetic interference (EMI). They are used for devices that generate a lot of heat and require thermal management, such as LED lights, power converters, and automotive systems.

## Soldering Techniques

Soldering is the process of joining two or more metal parts by melting and flowing a filler metal, called solder, into the joint. Soldering is used to create electrical and mechanical connections between components and PCBs. There are various methods and techniques of soldering, depending on the type of PCB, the type of components, and the desired quality and reliability. Some of the common techniques are:

- **Soft soldering**: This is the most common and basic technique of soldering, where the solder has a low melting point, usually below 400°C, and is usually a tin-lead alloy. Soft soldering uses a soldering iron, solder, soldering wick, and flux to heat and melt the solder, which flows into the joint and solidifies. Soft soldering is easy to perform and provides a good electrical conductivity, but has a low mechanical strength and a poor resistance to high temperatures and corrosion. Soft soldering is used for simple and low-cost PCBs, such as single-sided and double-sided PCBs .

- **Hard soldering**: This is a more advanced and complex technique of soldering, where the solder has a high melting point, usually above 450°C, and is usually a silver or copper alloy. Hard soldering uses a torch, solder, and flux to heat and melt the solder, which flows into the joint and solidifies. Hard soldering provides a high mechanical strength and a good resistance to high temperatures and corrosion, but has a low electrical conductivity and is difficult to perform and control. Hard soldering is used for high-performance and high-reliability PCBs, such as multilayer and metal-core PCBs .

- **Wave soldering**: This is a large-scale and automated technique of soldering, where the PCB is moved over a wave of hot solder liquid, which solidifies and fixes the components. Wave soldering is fast and efficient, and can solder multiple joints at once, but requires a lot of equipment

## a. Artwork & printing of a simple PCB

- A PCB (printed circuit board) is a thin board that holds electronic components and connects them with copper traces.
- To make a PCB, one needs to design the circuit layout and create an artwork that shows the positions and sizes of the components and traces.
- The artwork can be created using a software tool such as Eagle, KiCad, or Fritzing, or by drawing it manually on a paper or a transparent film.
- The artwork should have the following features:
  - It should be clear and accurate, with no errors or overlaps.
  - It should have a 1:1 scale, meaning that the dimensions of the artwork match the dimensions of the actual PCB.
  - It should have a mirror image of the circuit layout, as the artwork will be transferred to the copper side of the PCB.
  - It should have alignment marks or holes to help align the artwork with the PCB.
  - It should have a legend or a label that indicates the name, date, and version of the artwork.
- To print the artwork, one can use a laser printer or a photocopier to print it on a special paper or film that can transfer the toner to the PCB.
- Alternatively, one can use a UV printer or a plotter to print the artwork directly on the PCB.

## b. Etching & drilling of PCB

- Etching is the process of removing the unwanted copper from the PCB, leaving only the traces and pads that form the circuit.
- To etch a PCB, one needs to prepare the following materials and equipment:
  - A PCB with a copper layer on one or both sides.
  - An etchant solution, such as ferric chloride or sodium persulfate, that can dissolve copper.
  - A plastic tray or a glass container that can hold the etchant solution and the PCB.
  - A pair of gloves, goggles, and apron to protect oneself from the etchant solution.
  - A sponge or a brush to apply the etchant solution to the PCB.
  - A water source and a sink to rinse the PCB after etching.
- The steps for etching a PCB are as follows:
  - Transfer the artwork to the PCB using one of the methods mentioned above, such as ironing, UV exposure, or direct printing.
  - Cut the PCB to the desired size and shape, leaving some margin around the artwork.
  - Drill the alignment holes on the PCB using a drill bit that matches the size of the holes on the artwork.
  - Align the artwork with the PCB using the alignment holes and tape the edges to secure the artwork to the PCB.
  - Place the PCB in the tray or container and pour enough etchant solution to cover the PCB.
  - Gently agitate the tray or container to ensure that the etchant solution reaches all the copper areas on the PCB.
  - Monitor the etching process and check the PCB periodically to see if the unwanted copper is removed.
  - When the etching is complete, remove the PCB from the tray or container and rinse it with water to stop the etching reaction and remove the etchant residue.
  - Peel off the artwork from the PCB and dry the PCB with a cloth or a paper towel.
- Drilling is the process of making holes on the PCB for mounting the components and connecting them with wires or headers.
- To drill a PCB, one needs to prepare the following materials and equipment:
  - A PCB with the etched circuit layout.
  - A drill or a drill press with a set of drill bits that match the sizes of the holes on the artwork.
  - A clamp or a vise to hold the PCB securely while drilling.
  - A piece of wood or a scrap PCB to place under the PCB while drilling to avoid damaging the drill bit or the work surface.
  - A pair of pliers or a tweezers to remove the burrs or the copper flakes from the drilled holes.
- The steps for drilling a PCB are as follows:
  - Mark the center of each hole on the PCB using a marker or a punch.
  - Select the appropriate drill bit for each hole and insert it into the drill or the drill press.
  - Clamp or vise the PCB to the wood or the scrap PCB and align the drill bit with the marked hole.
  - Drill the hole slowly and steadily, applying moderate pressure and avoiding excessive heat or vibration.
  - Repeat the process for each hole on the PCB, changing the drill bit as needed.
  - Remove the clamp or the vise and inspect the PCB for any defects or errors.

- A rectifier is a circuit that converts alternating current (AC) to direct current (DC). Rectifiers are used in power supplies, radio receivers, and other electronic devices that require DC voltage.
- A half-wave rectifier is a rectifier that uses only one diode and passes only one half-cycle of the AC input voltage to the output. The other half-cycle is blocked by the reverse-biased diode. The output voltage is pulsating DC, which has a lot of ripple (fluctuation).
- A full-wave rectifier is a rectifier that uses two or four diodes and passes both half-cycles of the AC input voltage to the output. The diodes are arranged in such a way that the polarity of the output voltage is always the same. The output voltage is also pulsating DC, but with less ripple than the half-wave rectifier.
- Vrms is the root mean square value of the AC input voltage. It is a measure of the effective voltage that produces the same power as the AC voltage. Vrms is equal to 0.707 times the peak voltage of the AC input.
- Vdc is the average value of the DC output voltage. It is a measure of the average power delivered by the rectifier. Vdc is equal to the area under the output voltage curve divided by the time period of the AC input.
- Ripple factor is the ratio of the root mean square value of the AC component of the output voltage to the DC component of the output voltage. It is a measure of the smoothness of the output voltage. Ripple factor is equal to the square root of the ratio of the mean square value of the output voltage to the square of the average value of the output voltage minus one.

- In the CE configuration, the input signal is applied between the base and the emitter, and the output signal is taken from the collector and the emitter.
- The CE configuration is an inverting amplifier, which means that the output signal is 180 degrees out of phase with the input signal.
- The CE configuration has high voltage gain, high current gain, and high power gain, which makes it suitable for amplification applications.
- The CE configuration has two types of characteristics: input characteristics and output characteristics.

- The input characteristics can be used to find the input resistance (Rin) of the CE configuration, which is the ratio of the change in input voltage to the change in input current.
- The output characteristics show the relation between the output current (IC) and the output voltage (VCE) for different values of the input current (IB).
- The output characteristics are divided into three regions: active region, saturation region, and cut-off region.
- The active region is where the output current is proportional to the input current, and the output voltage is high. This is the region where the CE configuration works as an amplifier.
- The saturation region is where the output current is maximum, and the output voltage is low. This is the region where the CE configuration works as a switch that is ON.

- The output characteristics can be used to find the output resistance (Rout) of the CE configuration, which is the ratio of the change in output voltage to the change in output current.
- The output characteristics can also be used to find the voltage gain (Av), current gain (Ai), and power gain (Ap) of the CE configuration, which are the ratios of the output voltage, current, and power to the input voltage, current, and power, respectively.



### 6. To study Operational Amplifier as Adder and Subtractor

- An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a high-gain, direct-coupled electronic circuit that can perform various mathematical operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, differentiation, and integration.
- An op-amp has two input terminals: the inverting (-) and the non-inverting (+), and one output terminal. The output voltage is proportional to the difference between the input voltages, multiplied by the open-loop gain of the op-amp.
- An op-amp can be used as an adder or a subtractor by connecting resistors to the input and feedback terminals. The resistors determine the input and output voltages of the op-amp circuit.
- An adder is a circuit that can add two or more input voltages and produce a single output voltage. An adder can be implemented by using a non-inverting op-amp with multiple input resistors and a single feedback resistor. The output voltage is given by:

  `Vout = (Rf/R1) * V1 + (Rf/R2) * V2 + ... + (Rf/Rn) * Vn`

  where Rf is the feedback resistor, R1, R2, ..., Rn are the input resistors, and V1, V2, ..., Vn are the input voltages.
