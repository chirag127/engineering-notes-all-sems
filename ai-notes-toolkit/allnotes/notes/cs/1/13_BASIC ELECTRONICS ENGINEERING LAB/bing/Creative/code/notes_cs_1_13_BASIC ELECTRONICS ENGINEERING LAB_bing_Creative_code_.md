### 1. Study of various types of Active & Passive Components based on their ratings.

- Active components are those that can generate or amplify electrical signals, such as transistors, diodes, LEDs, and integrated circuits. Passive components are those that can only store or dissipate electrical energy, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, and transformers.
- Active and passive components can be classified based on their ratings, which are the maximum values of voltage, current, power, or frequency that they can handle without damage or degradation. Ratings are usually specified by the manufacturer and depend on the material, design, and operating conditions of the component.
- Some examples of ratings for active and passive components are:

  - Transistors: Collector-emitter voltage (Vce), collector current (Ic), power dissipation (Pc), and frequency (f).
  - Diodes: Peak inverse voltage (PIV), forward current (If), power dissipation (Pd), and frequency (f).
  - LEDs: Forward voltage (Vf), forward current (If), power dissipation (Pd), and wavelength (λ).
  - Integrated circuits: Supply voltage (Vcc), input/output voltage (Vin/Vout), input/output current (Iin/Iout), power dissipation (Pd), and frequency (f).
  - Resistors: Resistance (R), power rating (P), and tolerance (T).
  - Capacitors: Capacitance (C), voltage rating (V), and tolerance (T).
  - Inductors: Inductance (L), current rating (I), and resistance (R).
  - Transformers: Primary/secondary voltage (Vp/Vs), primary/secondary current (Ip/Is), turns ratio (Np/Ns), and frequency (f).

- The ratings of active and passive components are important to consider when designing or analyzing electronic circuits, as they determine the performance, efficiency, and reliability of the components and the circuit as a whole. Exceeding the ratings of a component can cause it to overheat, burn out, or fail. Therefore, it is advisable to choose components with ratings that are higher than the expected values of voltage, current, power, or frequency in the circuit, and to provide adequate cooling and protection for the components.



### 2. Identification of various types of Printed Circuit Boards (PCB) and soldering Techniques.

- A printed circuit board (PCB) is a thin board made of fiberglass, composite epoxy, or other laminate material, on which electrical components are mounted and connected by copper traces.
- PCBs are used to create electronic circuits and devices, such as computers, radios, televisions, and smartphones.
- There are various types of PCBs, depending on their design, functionality, and complexity. Some of the common types are:

  - Single-sided PCB: A PCB with copper traces and components on one side only. It is the simplest and cheapest type of PCB, but it has limited wiring density and functionality.
  - Double-sided PCB: A PCB with copper traces and components on both sides. It allows more connections and components than a single-sided PCB, but it requires through-hole or surface mount technology to connect the two sides.
  - Multilayer PCB: A PCB with more than two layers of copper traces and components, separated by insulating layers. It allows high wiring density and functionality, but it is more expensive and complex to manufacture and design.
  - Rigid PCB: A PCB that is stiff and cannot be bent or folded. It is the most common type of PCB, used for most electronic devices and applications.
  - Flexible PCB: A PCB that is flexible and can be bent or folded. It is used for applications that require conformability, such as wearable devices, medical devices, and aerospace devices.
  - Rigid-flex PCB: A PCB that combines rigid and flexible sections. It is used for applications that require both stability and flexibility, such as cameras, laptops, and military devices.

- Soldering is the process of joining two or more metal parts by melting and flowing a filler metal (solder) into the joint. Soldering is used to create electrical and mechanical connections on PCBs and other electronic components.
- There are various types of soldering techniques, depending on the type of solder, the type of components, and the type of PCB. Some of the common types are:

  - Through-hole soldering: A soldering technique that involves inserting the leads of components through holes on the PCB and soldering them on the other side. It is used for single-sided and double-sided PCBs, and for components that require strong mechanical support.
  - Surface mount soldering: A soldering technique that involves placing the components directly on the surface of the PCB and soldering them with small amounts of solder. It is used for multilayer PCBs, and for components that are small, lightweight, and sensitive to heat.
  - Reflow soldering: A soldering technique that involves applying solder paste (a mixture of solder and flux) to the PCB and the components, and then heating the entire assembly in an oven or a hot air gun. The solder paste melts and flows into the joints, creating a strong and uniform connection. It is used for surface mount soldering, and for mass production of PCBs.
  - Wave soldering: A soldering technique that involves passing the PCB over a wave of molten solder in a conveyor belt. The solder adheres to the exposed metal parts, creating a fast and reliable connection. It is used for through-hole soldering, and for mass production of PCBs.

- A rectifier is a circuit that converts alternating current (AC) to direct current (DC). Rectifiers are used in power supplies, radio receivers, and other applications that require a steady DC voltage.
- A half-wave rectifier is a rectifier that uses only one diode and passes only one half of the AC cycle to the output. The other half is blocked by the diode. The output of a half-wave rectifier is a pulsating DC voltage with a frequency equal to the input AC frequency.
- A full-wave rectifier is a rectifier that uses two diodes and passes both halves of the AC cycle to the output. The diodes are connected in such a way that they reverse the polarity of the negative half-cycle and make it positive. The output of a full-wave rectifier is a pulsating DC voltage with a frequency equal to twice the input AC frequency.
- The advantages of a full-wave rectifier over a half-wave rectifier are: it has a higher output voltage, a higher output power, a smoother output waveform, and a lower ripple factor.
- The ripple factor is a measure of the amount of AC component in the output of a rectifier. It is defined as the ratio of the root mean square (RMS) value of the AC component to the DC component of the output voltage. The lower the ripple factor, the better the quality of the rectified output.
- The RMS value of a voltage is the equivalent DC voltage that would produce the same amount of heat in a resistor as the AC voltage. The RMS value of a sinusoidal voltage is equal to its peak value divided by the square root of two.
- The DC value of a voltage is the average value of the voltage over one cycle. The DC value of a sinusoidal voltage is zero, since the positive and negative halves cancel out. The DC value of a rectified voltage is equal to its peak value multiplied by a factor that depends on the type of rectifier.

  - It is similar to the forward characteristic of a diode, because the base-emitter junction is forward biased in CE configuration.

# PN Junction Diode: Characteristics of PN Junction diode-Static and dynamic resistance measurement from graph

- A PN junction diode is a semiconductor device that allows current to flow in one direction only. It is formed by joining a p-type semiconductor (which has excess holes) and an n-type semiconductor (which has excess electrons) together. The interface between the two regions is called the PN junction.
- When a PN junction diode is connected to a circuit, the voltage applied across it can be either forward or reverse. In forward bias, the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the p-region and the negative terminal to the n-region. This reduces the potential barrier at the junction and allows current to flow. In reverse bias, the polarity of the battery is reversed. This increases the potential barrier at the junction and prevents current from flowing.
- The current-voltage (I-V) characteristic of a PN junction diode is a graph that shows how the current through the diode varies with the applied voltage. The graph has two regions: the forward region and the reverse region. The forward region shows the exponential increase of current with voltage in forward bias. The reverse region shows the negligible current with voltage in reverse bias, until a certain voltage called the breakdown voltage is reached. At this point, the current increases rapidly and the diode is damaged.
- The static resistance of a PN junction diode is the ratio of the applied voltage to the current at a given point on the I-V characteristic. It is also called the DC resistance. The static resistance can be calculated by drawing a tangent to the curve at the given point and finding the slope of the tangent. The static resistance is the reciprocal of the slope. The static resistance of a diode varies with the applied voltage and the temperature. In general, the static resistance is low in forward bias and high in reverse bias.
- The dynamic resistance of a PN junction diode is the ratio of the small change in voltage to the corresponding small change in current at a given point on the I-V characteristic. It is also called the AC resistance or the differential resistance. The dynamic resistance can be calculated by drawing a chord between two nearby points on the curve and finding the slope of the chord. The dynamic resistance is the reciprocal of the slope. The dynamic resistance of a diode is also dependent on the applied voltage and the temperature. In general, the dynamic resistance is lower than the static resistance in forward bias and higher than the static resistance in reverse bias.
- The dynamic resistance of a diode is useful for analyzing the behavior of the diode in alternating current (AC) circuits, where the voltage and current vary periodically. The dynamic resistance determines the power dissipation and the efficiency of the diode in AC circuits.
