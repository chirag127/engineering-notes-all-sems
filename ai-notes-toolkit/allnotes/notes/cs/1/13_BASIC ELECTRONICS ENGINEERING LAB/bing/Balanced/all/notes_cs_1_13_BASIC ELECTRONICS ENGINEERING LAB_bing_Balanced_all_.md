### 1. Study of various types of Active & Passive Components based on their ratings.

- Active components are those that can amplify or control the flow of electric current in a circuit. They require an external source of energy to operate. Examples of active components are transistors, diodes, integrated circuits, etc.
- Passive components are those that do not require any external source of energy to operate. They can only store or dissipate energy in a circuit. Examples of passive components are resistors, capacitors, inductors, etc.
- The ratings of components are the values of their electrical parameters that specify their performance and limitations. They are usually given by the manufacturers or standards organizations. Some common ratings of components are:
  - Resistance: The opposition to the flow of current in a resistor. It is measured in ohms (Ω).
  - Capacitance: The ability to store electric charge in a capacitor. It is measured in farads (F).
  - Inductance: The ability to store magnetic energy in an inductor. It is measured in henries (H).
  - Voltage: The potential difference across a component. It is measured in volts (V).
  - Current: The rate of flow of electric charge in a component. It is measured in amperes (A).
  - Power: The rate of energy conversion or dissipation in a component. It is measured in watts (W).
  - Frequency: The number of cycles of alternating current or voltage in a component per second. It is measured in hertz (Hz).
  - Impedance: The total opposition to the flow of alternating current in a component. It is a complex quantity that consists of resistance and reactance. It is measured in ohms (Ω).
  - Reactance: The opposition to the flow of alternating current in a component due to capacitance or inductance. It is a complex quantity that depends on the frequency. It is measured in ohms (Ω).
  - Gain: The ratio of output to input voltage, current, or power in an active component. It is a dimensionless quantity that indicates the amplification or attenuation of a signal.
  - Bandwidth: The range of frequencies that an active component can amplify or process without significant loss or distortion. It is measured in hertz (Hz).
  - Efficiency: The ratio of output power to input power in an active component. It is a dimensionless quantity that indicates the percentage of input power that is converted to useful output power.



# 2. Identification of various types of Printed Circuit Boards (PCB) and soldering Techniques.

- A printed circuit board (PCB) is a thin board made of fiberglass, composite epoxy, or other laminate material, on which electrical components are mounted and connected by copper traces.
- PCBs are used to create electronic circuits, devices, and systems, such as computers, radios, televisions, and smartphones.
- There are different types of PCBs, depending on their design, functionality, and complexity. Some of the common types are:

  - Single-sided PCB: A PCB with only one layer of copper traces on one side of the board. The components are usually mounted on the same side as the traces, or through holes that connect to the other side. This is the simplest and cheapest type of PCB, but it has limited wiring density and routing options.
  - Double-sided PCB: A PCB with two layers of copper traces, one on each side of the board. The components can be mounted on either side, and the traces can be connected by through holes or vias. This type of PCB allows for more wiring density and routing flexibility than single-sided PCB, but it is more expensive and requires more careful design and fabrication.
  - Multilayer PCB: A PCB with more than two layers of copper traces, sandwiched between layers of insulating material. The traces can be connected by through holes or vias, and the components can be mounted on any layer. This type of PCB allows for the highest wiring density and routing complexity, as well as better electrical performance and noise reduction. However, it is also the most expensive and difficult to design and manufacture.
  - Rigid PCB: A PCB that is stiff and cannot be bent or folded. Most PCBs are rigid, as they provide better mechanical support and durability for the components and circuits. Rigid PCBs can be single-sided, double-sided, or multilayer, depending on the application and design requirements.
  - Flexible PCB: A PCB that is flexible and can be bent or folded to fit into different shapes and spaces. Flexible PCBs are made of thin and flexible materials, such as polyimide, and are often used for applications that require lightweight, compact, or conformable electronics, such as wearable devices, medical implants, or aerospace systems. Flexible PCBs can also be single-sided, double-sided, or multilayer, depending on the design and functionality.
  - Rigid-flex PCB: A PCB that combines rigid and flexible sections, connected by interconnects or connectors. Rigid-flex PCBs are used for applications that require both mechanical stability and flexibility, such as cameras, laptops, or military equipment. Rigid-flex PCBs can also have different numbers of layers and traces, depending on the design and functionality.

- Soldering is the process of joining two or more metal parts by melting and flowing a filler metal (solder) into the joint. Soldering is used to create electrical connections between components and traces on PCBs, as well as to repair or modify existing circuits.
- There are different types of soldering techniques, depending on the equipment, materials, and skills involved. Some of the common types are:

  - Iron soldering: A soldering technique that uses a heated metal tool (soldering iron) to melt and apply the solder to the joint. Iron soldering is the most basic and widely used type of soldering, as it is simple, cheap, and versatile. However, it also requires manual skill, precision, and care, as it can damage the components or the board if not done properly.
  - Wave soldering: A soldering technique that uses a conveyor belt to move the PCBs over a tank of molten solder, creating a wave that flows over the bottom side of the board and solders all the joints at once. Wave soldering is a fast and efficient type of soldering, as it can solder large numbers of PCBs in a short time. However, it also requires special equipment, setup, and maintenance, as well as careful design and alignment of the components and traces to avoid solder bridges or skips.
  - Reflow soldering: A soldering technique that uses a paste of solder and flux (a chemical agent that cleans and aids the soldering process) to coat the joints, and then heats the entire board in an oven or a hot air gun, causing the solder to melt and flow into the joints. Reflow soldering is a precise and reliable type of soldering, as it can solder complex and fine-pitch components and traces with minimal defects. However, it also requires special equipment, materials, and skills, as well as accurate temperature and time control to avoid overheating or underheating the board or the components.

a. Artwork & printing of a simple PCB

- The first step in making a PCB is to create the artwork, which is the design of the circuit layout on the board. You can use software tools such as Eagle, KiCad, or Fritzing to draw the schematic and the board layout. Alternatively, you can draw the artwork by hand on a graph paper or a transparent sheet.
- The artwork should include the following elements:
  - The outline of the board and the holes for the components and the mounting screws.
  - The traces, which are the thin lines that connect the components and form the circuit. The traces should be as short and straight as possible, and avoid crossing each other. You can use different colors or symbols to distinguish the traces for different voltage levels or signals.
  - The pads, which are the circular or rectangular areas where the components are soldered to the board. The pads should be large enough to accommodate the leads of the components and provide good electrical contact. You can use the same color or symbol as the corresponding trace for the pads.
  - The labels, which are the names or numbers of the components and the pins. The labels should be clear and readable, and oriented in the same direction as the board. You can use a different color or symbol from the traces and the pads for the labels.
- The next step is to print the artwork on a paper or a film. You can use a laser printer or an inkjet printer to print the artwork, but make sure the printer settings are adjusted to produce a high-contrast and high-resolution image. You can also use a photocopier to copy the artwork from a paper or a film to another paper or film. The printed artwork should be the same size as the actual board, and should be checked for any errors or defects.
- The printed artwork is then transferred to the copper-clad board, which is the base material for the PCB. The copper-clad board consists of a thin layer of copper on one or both sides of a non-conductive substrate, such as fiberglass or epoxy. The copper layer is the part that will form the traces and the pads on the PCB. The artwork is placed on the copper layer, and aligned with the edges and the holes of the board. The artwork is then fixed to the board using a tape or a clamp.

b. Etching & drilling of PCB

- The etching process is the removal of the unwanted copper from the board, leaving only the traces and the pads. The etching process can be done using a chemical solution or a mechanical method. The chemical solution is a mixture of water and an acid, such as ferric chloride or ammonium persulfate, that dissolves the copper. The mechanical method is a tool, such as a milling machine or a laser cutter, that cuts or burns the copper. The etching process should be done carefully and safely, following the instructions and precautions of the chosen method.
- The drilling process is the creation of the holes for the components and the mounting screws. The drilling process can be done using a drill press or a hand drill, with a suitable drill bit size and speed. The drilling process should be done accurately and neatly, following the alignment and the spacing of the artwork. The drilled holes should be cleaned and deburred, using a file or a sandpaper, to remove any burrs or residues.

## Soldering and disordering of resistor in PCB

- To solder a resistor in a PCB, follow these steps:
  - Clean the PCB and the resistor leads with a brush or a cloth to remove any dirt or oxidation.
  - Insert the resistor leads through the holes in the PCB and bend them slightly to hold the resistor in place.
  - Heat up the soldering iron and apply a small amount of solder to the tip.
  - Touch the tip of the soldering iron to the joint where the resistor lead and the copper trace meet, and feed more solder until the joint is covered with a shiny and smooth layer of solder.
  - Remove the soldering iron and let the joint cool down. Do not move the resistor until the solder is solidified.
  - Cut off the excess resistor leads with a wire cutter or a plier.
  - Repeat the same steps for the other resistor lead and any other resistors you want to solder in the PCB.
- To disorder a resistor from a PCB, follow these steps:
  - Heat up the soldering iron and apply a small amount of solder to the tip.
  - Touch the tip of the soldering iron to the joint where the resistor lead and the copper trace meet, and melt the solder.
  - Use a solder sucker or a desoldering braid to remove the molten solder from the joint. Be careful not to damage the PCB or the resistor.
  - Pull out the resistor lead from the hole in the PCB with a tweezer or a plier. Do not apply too much force or you may break the resistor or the PCB.
  - Repeat the same steps for the other resistor lead and any other resistors you want to disorder from the PCB.

  - Remove the soldering iron and let the joint cool down. Do not move the IC until the solder is solidified.
  - Repeat the same steps for the other IC pins and any other ICs you want to solder in the PCB.

  - Repeat the same steps for the other IC pins and any other ICs you want to disorder from the PCB.

- A P-N junction diode is a semiconductor device that consists of a p-type and an n-type region separated by a depletion region where charge is stored.
- A P-N junction diode has the ability to rectify electric current, that is, to allow current to flow in one direction only .
- A P-N junction diode has a potential barrier voltage across the junction, which opposes the flow of current when the diode is reverse-biased, and reduces when the diode is forward-biased.
- A P-N junction diode has various nonlinear current-voltage characteristics, which depend on the biasing condition and the temperature .
- A P-N junction diode can also transform light energy into electrical energy, as in a photodiode or a solar cell, or emit light when current flows through it, as in a light-emitting diode (LED) .

- The current-voltage (I-V) characteristics of a P-N junction diode can be obtained by plotting the current (I) versus the voltage (V) across the diode for different biasing conditions.
- The I-V characteristics of a P-N junction diode can be divided into two regions: the forward region and the reverse region.
- In the forward region, the diode is forward-biased, that is, the p-type region is connected to the positive terminal of the battery and the n-type region is connected to the negative terminal.
- In the forward region, the current increases exponentially with the voltage, as the potential barrier is reduced and more charge carriers can cross the junction.
- In the forward region, the diode has a small resistance, called the static or dc resistance, which is the ratio of the voltage to the current at a given point on the I-V curve.
- In the forward region, the diode also has a dynamic or ac resistance, which is the ratio of the change in voltage to the change in current around a given point on the I-V curve.
- The dynamic resistance is smaller than the static resistance, and it decreases as the current increases.
- The dynamic resistance is useful for analyzing the behavior of the diode in alternating current (AC) circuits.

- In the reverse region, the diode is reverse-biased, that is, the p-type region is connected to the negative terminal of the battery and the n-type region is connected to the positive terminal.
- In the reverse region, the current is very small and almost constant, as the potential barrier is increased and very few charge carriers can cross the junction.
- In the reverse region, the diode has a large resistance, which is the ratio of the voltage to the current at a given point on the I-V curve.
- In the reverse region, the diode also has a breakdown voltage, which is the minimum voltage required to cause a sudden increase in the current, as the potential barrier is overcome by the strong electric field.
- The breakdown voltage depends on the doping level and the temperature of the diode.
- The breakdown voltage is useful for designing voltage regulators and protection circuits.

- The static and dynamic resistance of a P-N junction diode can be measured from the I-V graph by using the following steps:
  - For the static resistance, choose a point on the I-V curve, either in the forward or the reverse region, and note down the corresponding voltage and current values.
  - Divide the voltage by the current to get the static resistance at that point.
  - For the dynamic resistance, choose a small interval around a point on the I-V curve, either in the forward or the reverse region, and note down the voltage and current values at the endpoints of the interval.
  - Subtract the voltage values and the current values to get the change in voltage and the change in current over the interval.
  - Divide the change in voltage by the change in current to get the dynamic resistance around that point.

Op-amp subtractor circuit

- Connect the logic gate to a power supply and a voltmeter or an LED indicator.
- Connect the input terminals of the logic gate to switches that can be turned on or off to represent the binary values 1 and 0.
- For each row of the truth table, set the input switches to the corresponding values and observe the output voltage or the LED status.
- Record the output signal for each input combination and compare it with the expected value from the truth table.
- If the output signal matches the truth table for all input combinations, the logic gate is verified.

### Reverse Characteristics

The reverse characteristics show the behavior of the diode when it is reverse biased, meaning that the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the n-type region and the negative terminal to the p-type region.
