

## Unit 1 - Quantum Mechanics

- Quantum mechanics is a subfield of physics that describes the behavior of particles — atoms, electrons, photons and almost everything in the molecular and submolecular realm.
- Quantum mechanics is a fundamental theory in physics that provides a description of the physical properties of nature at the scale of atoms and subatomic particles.
- Quantum mechanics attempts to describe and account for the properties of molecules and atoms and their constituents— electrons, protons, neutrons, and other more esoteric particles such as quarks and gluons.
- Quantum mechanics is a mathematical machine for predicting the behaviors of microscopic particles — or, at least, of the measuring instruments we use to explore those behaviors.
- Quantum mechanics is based on a set of postulates, principles, and equations that govern the quantum phenomena, such as the uncertainty principle, the superposition principle, the Schrödinger equation, the Dirac equation, the Pauli exclusion principle, the Heisenberg picture, the Feynman path integral, the Born rule, and the Copenhagen interpretation.
- Quantum mechanics has many applications in various fields, such as quantum chemistry, quantum field theory, quantum technology, and quantum information science.
- Quantum mechanics also reveals many aspects of reality that are counterintuitive, paradoxical, and mysterious, such as the wave-particle duality, the quantum entanglement, the quantum tunneling, the quantum Zeno effect, the quantum eraser, and the quantum measurement problem .



### Inadequacy of classical mechanics

- Classical mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of macroscopic objects under the influence of forces.
- Classical mechanics is based on Newton's laws of motion, which describe how forces affect the motion of objects.
- Classical mechanics can explain correctly the motion of celestial bodies, such as planets and stars, and microscopic terrestrial bodies, such as bullets and cars, that move with non-relativistic speeds (much less than the speed of light).
- However, classical mechanics fails to explain certain phenomena observed in nature, especially at the atomic and subatomic scales, where the objects move with relativistic speeds (close to the speed of light) or interact with electromagnetic radiation.
- Some of the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot explain are:

  - The stability of atoms: According to classical mechanics, an electron orbiting a nucleus should emit electromagnetic radiation and lose energy, eventually falling into the nucleus. However, atoms are stable and do not collapse.
  - The discrete spectra of atoms: According to classical mechanics, an electron orbiting a nucleus should emit electromagnetic radiation of any frequency, depending on its energy. However, atoms emit and absorb electromagnetic radiation of only certain discrete frequencies, corresponding to the transitions between discrete energy levels of the electrons.
  - The blackbody radiation: According to classical mechanics, a blackbody (an ideal object that absorbs and emits all frequencies of electromagnetic radiation) should emit electromagnetic radiation of any frequency, depending on its temperature. However, the observed spectrum of blackbody radiation does not match the classical prediction, especially at high frequencies, where the classical theory predicts an infinite energy output (the ultraviolet catastrophe).
  - The photoelectric effect: According to classical mechanics, a metal surface exposed to electromagnetic radiation should emit electrons with any energy, depending on the intensity of the radiation. However, the observed phenomenon shows that the energy of the emitted electrons depends only on the frequency of the radiation, and there is a minimum frequency (the threshold frequency) below which no electrons are emitted, regardless of the intensity of the radiation.
  - The Compton effect: According to classical mechanics, a photon (a particle of light) colliding with an electron should transfer some of its energy and momentum to the electron, without changing its frequency. However, the observed phenomenon shows that the frequency of the photon decreases after the collision, and the amount of decrease depends on the angle of scattering.

- These phenomena indicate that classical mechanics is inadequate to describe the nature of matter and radiation at the microscopic level, and that a new theory is needed to account for the observed phenomena. This new theory is quantum mechanics, which introduces the concepts of wave-particle duality, uncertainty principle, quantization of energy and momentum, and superposition of states. Quantum mechanics is able to explain all the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot, and also reduces to classical mechanics in the limit of large objects and low speeds.

### de-Broglie concept of matter waves

- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves is based on the idea that **all matter exhibits wave-like properties** when in motion, and particle-like properties when at rest  .
- This concept was proposed by Louis de Broglie in 1924, inspired by the dual nature of light as both a wave and a particle  .
- The de-Broglie wavelength of a matter wave is given by the formula:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength, $h$ is the Planck's constant, and $p$ is the momentum of the particle  .

- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves explains the **quantization of the angular momentum** of electrons in atoms, as postulated by Bohr's quantum theory .
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves also predicts the **phenomena of interference and diffraction** of matter waves, which have been experimentally verified by electron diffraction and neutron interference  .
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves is one of the foundations of **quantum mechanics**, which describes the behavior of matter and energy at the atomic and subatomic scales  .



### Davisson and Germer Experiment

- The Davisson and Germer experiment was a 1923-27 experiment by Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Western Electric (later Bell Labs), in which electrons, scattered by the surface of a crystal of nickel metal, displayed a diffraction pattern.
- The experiment confirmed the de Broglie hypothesis that matter has wave-like behavior and established the wave–particle duality hypothesis which was a fundamental step in quantum theory.
- The experiment was based on the assumption that waves reflected from two distinct atomic levels of a Ni crystal will have a fixed phase difference and will interfere either constructively or destructively, producing a diffraction pattern .
- The experiment consisted of a vacuum chamber with an electron gun that emitted a beam of electrons of known energy, a nickel target that could be rotated to change the angle of incidence, and a Faraday cup that measured the intensity of the scattered electrons.
- The experiment showed that the intensity of the scattered electrons varied with the angle of incidence and reached a maximum at a certain angle, which corresponded to the Bragg condition for constructive interference of X-rays.
- The experiment also showed that the wavelength of the electrons could be calculated from the de Broglie relation and matched the observed diffraction pattern.
- The experiment was the first direct evidence of the wave nature of electrons and validated the de Broglie equation for the first time.



### Phase velocity and group velocity

- Phase velocity is the velocity at which the phase of a wave propagates in a medium. It is given by the ratio of the angular frequency to the wave number: v_p = ω/k .
- Group velocity is the velocity at which the envelope or shape of a wave packet propagates in a medium. It is given by the derivative of the angular frequency with respect to the wave number: v_g = dω/dk .
- Phase velocity and group velocity are related by Rayleigh's formula: v_g = v_p + kv_p' , where v_p' is the derivative of the phase velocity with respect to the wave number.
- In quantum mechanics, phase velocity and group velocity have different interpretations for the associated particle. The phase velocity is related to the total energy of the particle by the de Broglie relation: v_p = E/ℏk . The group velocity is related to the kinetic energy of the particle by the relation: v_g = 2E/ℏk .
- For a free particle, the phase velocity and the group velocity are constant and equal to v_p = ℏk/2m and v_g = ℏk/m, respectively. Therefore, the group velocity is twice the phase velocity for a free particle .
- For a particle in a potential, the phase velocity and the group velocity depend on the shape of the potential and the wave number. The group velocity can be interpreted as the classical velocity of the particle, while the phase velocity has no physical meaning .



### Time-dependent and time-independent Schrodinger wave equations

- The Schrodinger wave equation is a partial differential equation that describes the quantum state of a physical system.
- The equation can be written in two forms: time-dependent and time-independent.
- The time-dependent Schrodinger equation (TDSE) applies to a system that evolves over time and is affected by the potential energy of the system. The TDSE has the following expression:

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t} = \hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r},t)
$$

where $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ is the wave function of the system, $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator, $i$ is the imaginary unit, and $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant.

- The time-independent Schrodinger equation (TISE) applies to a system that does not change over time and has a constant potential energy. The TISE can be derived from the TDSE by using the separation of variables method, assuming that the wave function can be written as a product of a spatial function and a temporal function :

$$
\psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \psi(\mathbf{r}) e^{-i\omega t}
$$

where $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the spatial part of the wave function, $\omega$ is the angular frequency, and $e^{-i\omega t}$ is the temporal part of the wave function.

- Plugging this ansatz in the TDSE, we get the TISE:

$$
\hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r}) = E \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

where $E$ is the energy eigenvalue of the system, and $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the energy eigenfunction of the system.

- The TISE is an eigenvalue problem that can be solved for the allowed energy levels and the corresponding wave functions of the system. The TISE is simpler to solve than the TDSE, but it only applies to stationary states, which are states that do not change over time. The TDSE can describe any state of the system, including superpositions of stationary states.



### Physical interpretation of wave function

- A wave function is a mathematical function that describes the quantum state of a system of one or more particles.
- A wave function can be represented by a complex-valued function of space and time, or by a vector in a Hilbert space.
- A wave function contains all the information about the system, such as its position, momentum, energy, spin, etc.
- A wave function can be normalized, meaning that its square modulus integrated over all space is equal to one. This ensures that the wave function can be interpreted as a probability amplitude.
- A probability amplitude is a complex number that gives the probability of finding the system in a certain state or region of space. The probability is obtained by taking the square modulus of the probability amplitude.
- A probability density is the probability per unit volume of finding the system in a certain region of space. The probability density is obtained by taking the square modulus of the wave function.
- A probability current is the rate of flow of probability density across a surface. The probability current is obtained by taking the product of the wave function and its complex conjugate, multiplied by a constant factor that depends on the mass and charge of the particle.
- The wave function obeys the Schrödinger equation, which is a partial differential equation that relates the wave function to the potential energy of the system. The Schrödinger equation can be derived from the principle of least action, or from the Hamiltonian formalism of classical mechanics.
- The wave function can be expressed as a linear combination of eigenfunctions, which are the solutions of the Schrödinger equation for a given potential energy. The eigenfunctions have definite values of energy, momentum, angular momentum, etc., and are orthogonal to each other. The coefficients of the linear combination are called the expansion coefficients, and they give the probability amplitude of finding the system in a certain eigenstate.
- The wave function can also be expressed as a Fourier transform of the momentum space wave function, which is the wave function in terms of the momentum variables. The momentum space wave function gives the probability amplitude of finding the system with a certain momentum, and it obeys the same Schrödinger equation as the position space wave function, but with the potential energy replaced by the kinetic energy.
- The wave function can be interpreted in different ways, such as the Copenhagen interpretation, the pilot wave theory, the many-worlds interpretation, etc. These interpretations differ in how they explain the measurement process, the collapse of the wave function, the role of the observer, the nature of reality, etc. However, they all agree on the predictions of the wave function for observable quantities, and they are all consistent with the principles of quantum mechanics.



### Particle in a one-dimensional box

- A particle in a one-dimensional box is a fundamental quantum mechanical approximation describing the translational motion of a single particle confined inside an infinitely deep well from which it cannot escape   .
- The walls of a one-dimensional box may be seen as regions of space with an infinitely large potential energy, so the particle cannot penetrate them.
- The potential energy of the particle inside the box is zero, and the total energy is equal to the kinetic energy.
- The Schrödinger equation for the particle in a one-dimensional box is given by:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=E\psi$$

where $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $m$ is the mass of the particle, $x$ is the position coordinate, $\psi$ is the wavefunction, and $E$ is the energy   .

- The boundary conditions for the wavefunction are:

$$\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$$

where $L$ is the length of the box   .

- The solution to the Schrödinger equation is:

$$\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

where $n$ is a positive integer called the quantum number   .

- The energy of the particle is quantized, meaning it can only take discrete values given by:

$$E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$$

where $n$ is the same quantum number as in the wavefunction   .

- The lowest energy state, called the ground state, corresponds to $n=1$, and the higher energy states, called the excited states, correspond to $n>1$   .
- The probability density of finding the particle at a given position is given by:

$$|\psi_n(x)|^2=\frac{2}{L}\sin^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

which shows that the particle has zero probability of being at the walls of the box, and has maximum probability of being at the center of the box for the ground state, and at the nodes of the wavefunction for the excited states   .



## Unit 2 - Electromagnetic Field Theory

- Electromagnetic field theory is the study of the interaction between electric and magnetic fields, which are produced by electric charges and currents.
- The basic principles of electromagnetic field theory are derived from the experimental observations of Coulomb, Ampere, Faraday, and Maxwell, and are summarized by Maxwell's equations, which relate the electric and magnetic fields to their sources and to each other.
- The electric field is a vector field that exerts a force on a unit positive charge at any point in space. The electric field can be calculated from the electric potential, which is a scalar function that represents the work done to move a unit charge from a reference point to a given point. The electric potential can be obtained by solving Poisson's equation or Laplace's equation, depending on the presence or absence of electric charges in the region of interest.
- The magnetic field is also a vector field that exerts a force on a moving charge or a current at any point in space. The magnetic field can be calculated from the vector potential, which is a vector function that satisfies the condition that the curl of the vector potential is equal to the magnetic field. The vector potential can be obtained by solving a modified version of Poisson's equation, where the source term is the electric current density.
- The electric and magnetic fields are not independent of each other, but are related by the phenomena of electromagnetic induction and electromagnetic radiation. Electromagnetic induction is the process by which a changing magnetic field induces an electric field, and vice versa. Electromagnetic radiation is the process by which an accelerating charge or a changing current emits electromagnetic waves, which are self-propagating oscillations of the electric and magnetic fields that travel at the speed of light in vacuum.
- The electric and magnetic properties of matter affect the behavior of the electromagnetic fields in different ways. The electric properties of matter are characterized by the electric polarization, which is the electric dipole moment per unit volume induced by an external electric field. The magnetic properties of matter are characterized by the magnetization, which is the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume induced by an external magnetic field. The polarization and magnetization are related to the electric and magnetic susceptibilities, which measure the degree of response of the material to the applied fields. The electric and magnetic fields in matter are modified by the presence of bound charges and currents, which are the result of the polarization and magnetization of the material.
- Electromagnetic field theory has many applications in physics, engineering, and technology, such as electric circuits, antennas, transmission lines, waveguides, optical fibers, lasers, radar, wireless communication, and medical imaging.




### Basic concept of Stoke’s theorem and Divergence theorem

- Stoke's theorem and Divergence theorem are two important theorems in vector calculus that relate different types of integrals over different types of regions.
- Stoke's theorem states that the line integral of a vector field around a closed curve is equal to the surface integral of the curl of the vector field over any surface bounded by the curve.
- Divergence theorem states that the surface integral of a vector field over a closed surface is equal to the volume integral of the divergence of the vector field over any volume enclosed by the surface.
- Both theorems can be seen as generalizations of the fundamental theorem of calculus, which states that the integral of a function over an interval is equal to the difference of the values of its antiderivative at the endpoints of the interval.
- The main difference between Stoke's theorem and Divergence theorem is that Stoke's theorem relates a two-dimensional surface integral to a one-dimensional line integral, while Divergence theorem relates a three-dimensional volume integral to a two-dimensional surface integral.
- Both theorems have many applications in physics and engineering, such as calculating the circulation of a fluid, the flux of an electric field, or the work done by a force.



### Basic laws of electricity and magnetism

Electricity and magnetism are two aspects of the same phenomenon: electromagnetism. Electric charges and magnetic fields interact with each other in various ways, and these interactions are governed by some basic laws that can be expressed in mathematical equations. Here are some of the important laws of electricity and magnetism:

- **Coulomb's law**: This law states that the force of attraction or repulsion between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force is along the line joining the charges and has the same magnitude for both charges. The mathematical expression of Coulomb's law is:

  $$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

  where $F$ is the force, $k$ is a constant, $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charges, and $r$ is the distance between them.

- **Gauss's law**: This law states that the net electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the total charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space. The electric flux is a measure of the number of electric field lines passing through the surface. The mathematical expression of Gauss's law is:

  $$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$

  where $\Phi_E$ is the electric flux, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, $d\vec{A}$ is a differential area element of the surface, $Q$ is the total charge enclosed, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.

- **Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction**: This law states that the induced electromotive force (emf) in a loop of wire is equal to the rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop. The magnetic flux is a measure of the number of magnetic field lines passing through the loop. The mathematical expression of Faraday's law is:

  $$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

  where $\mathcal{E}$ is the emf, $\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux, and $t$ is the time.

- **Ampere's law**: This law states that the line integral of the magnetic field around any closed loop is equal to the product of the permeability of free space and the net current passing through the loop. The mathematical expression of Ampere's law is:

  $$\oint_C \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I$$

  where $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field, $d\vec{l}$ is a differential length element of the loop, $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space, and $I$ is the net current.

- **Lorentz force law**: This law states that the force on a charged particle moving in an electric and magnetic field is equal to the charge times the sum of the electric field and the cross product of the velocity and the magnetic field. The mathematical expression of Lorentz force law is:

  $$\vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B})$$

  where $\vec{F}$ is the force, $q$ is the charge, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, $\vec{v}$ is the velocity, and $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field.

- **Maxwell's equations**: These are four equations that summarize the basic laws of electricity and magnetism and show how they are related. They are:

  $$\nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$$

  $$\nabla \cdot \vec{B} = 0$$

  $$\nabla \times \vec{E} = -\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t}$$

  $$\nabla \times \vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{J} + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t}$$

  where $\nabla$ is the del operator, $\rho$ is the charge density, $\vec{J}$ is the current density, and the other symbols have been defined before.

### Continuity equation for current density

- Current density is a vector quantity that measures the amount of electric current flowing per unit area of a cross-section of a conductor. It is denoted by J and has the SI unit of ampere per square meter (A/m^2^) .
- The formula for current density is given as, J = I / A, where I is the current flowing through the conductor in amperes and A is the cross-sectional area of the conductor in square meters .
- The continuity equation for current density is a mathematical expression that relates the change in charge density (ρ) in a region of space to the current density (J) flowing out of that region. It is based on the principle of conservation of charge, which states that the net charge in a closed system is constant .
- The differential form of the continuity equation is: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇ ⋅ J = 0, where t is the time, ρ is the charge density, and ∇ ⋅ J is the divergence of the current density .
- The integral form of the continuity equation is: d/dt ∫_V_ ρ d^3^r = - ∮_S_ J ⋅ n d^2^r, where V is an arbitrary stationary volume bounded by the closed surface S, n is the unit normal vector to the surface, and J ⋅ n is the dot product of the current density and the normal vector .
- The continuity equation implies that the net current flowing out of any volume is equal to the rate of decrease of charge inside that volume. It also implies that the current density is zero at any point where the charge density is constant .



### Displacement current

- Displacement current is a phenomenon in electromagnetism that is analogous to an ordinary electric current, but does not involve the flow of electric charges .
- Displacement current is defined as the rate of change of the electric displacement field D, which is related to the electric field E by the permittivity of the medium ε.
- Displacement current density Jd is given by the equation Jd = ∂D/∂t, where t is time.
- Displacement current was introduced by James Clerk Maxwell in his equations to explain the magnetic field produced by a changing electric field, such as in a capacitor .
- Displacement current plays a central role in the propagation of electromagnetic waves, such as light and radio waves, through empty space or dielectric materials .
- Displacement current allows the electric and magnetic fields to be coupled when the electric flux is time-varying, and thus enables the existence of electromagnetic waves.



### Maxwell equations in integral and differential form

- Maxwell equations are a set of four equations that describe the relationship between electric and magnetic fields, charges and currents.
- They are the foundation of electromagnetic theory and have many applications in physics and engineering.
- They can be written in two equivalent forms: integral and differential.
- The integral form relates the electric and magnetic fields to the charges and currents enclosed by a closed surface or a closed loop.
- The differential form relates the electric and magnetic fields to the charges and currents at a point in space using the differential operators divergence and curl.
- The integral and differential forms are connected by the mathematical theorems of Gauss and Stokes.
- The four equations are:

  - Gauss's law for electricity: The electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the net charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space.

    - Integral form: $$\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$
    - Differential form: $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$$
    - Where $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field, $d\mathbf{A}$ is the differential area element, $Q$ is the net charge, $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space, $\nabla$ is the divergence operator, and $\rho$ is the charge density.

  - Gauss's law for magnetism: The magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero, which implies that there are no magnetic monopoles.

    - Integral form: $$\oint_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0$$
    - Differential form: $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$$
    - Where $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field.

  - Faraday's law of induction: The electromotive force (EMF) around any closed loop is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop.

    - Integral form: $$\oint_C \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$
    - Differential form: $$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$$

### Poynting vector and Poynting theorem

- The Poynting vector is a vector quantity that represents the directional energy flux of an electromagnetic field. It is defined as the cross product of the electric field and the magnetic field vectors:

  $$\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec{E} \times \vec{B}$$

  where $\vec{S}$ is the Poynting vector, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field, and $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space.

- The Poynting vector has the units of watts per square meter (W/m$^2$) and indicates the amount of power per unit area carried by the electromagnetic field.

- The direction of the Poynting vector is perpendicular to both the electric and magnetic fields and points in the direction of energy propagation.

- The Poynting theorem is a conservation law that relates the change in electromagnetic energy density to the work done by the fields on the charges and the energy flux of the fields. It can be derived from Maxwell's equations and is given by:

  $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \vec{S} = -\vec{E} \cdot \vec{J}$$

  where $u$ is the electromagnetic energy density, $\vec{S}$ is the Poynting vector, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, and $\vec{J}$ is the current density.

- The Poynting theorem can be interpreted as follows:

  - The left-hand side of the equation represents the rate of change of electromagnetic energy in a given volume. The first term is the change in energy density, and the second term is the divergence of the Poynting vector, which measures the net energy flux out of the volume.

  - The right-hand side of the equation represents the rate of work done by the fields on the charges in the volume. The negative sign indicates that the fields lose energy as they do work on the charges.

  - The equation implies that the electromagnetic energy is conserved in a closed system, as the change in energy in a volume is equal to the energy flux across the boundary minus the work done by the fields.

- The Poynting theorem can be applied to various situations involving electromagnetic fields, such as electromagnetic waves, circuits, antennas, and radiation. It can also be generalized to include the effects of displacement current, polarization, and magnetization.



### Plane electromagnetic waves in vacuum and their transverse nature

- A plane electromagnetic wave is a wave that has a constant amplitude and a constant frequency in a plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
- A plane electromagnetic wave consists of an electric field (**E**) and a magnetic field (**B**) that oscillate in phase and are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation .
- The direction of propagation of a plane electromagnetic wave is given by the vector product of **E** and **B**, i.e., **k** = **E** x **B**.
- In vacuum, a plane electromagnetic wave travels at a constant speed of **c** = 3 x 10^8^ m/s, which is the speed of light.
- The transverse nature of electromagnetic waves means that the electric and magnetic fields fluctuate in a plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation  .
- The transverse nature of electromagnetic waves can be derived from Gauss's law for electric and magnetic fields, which state that the net electric and magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero .
- This implies that the electric and magnetic fields have no component along the direction of propagation, and hence they are transverse .
- The transverse nature of electromagnetic waves can also be verified by experiments, such as the polarization of light, which shows that the electric field of light can be oriented in different directions in the plane perpendicular to the propagation .
- The transverse nature of electromagnetic waves is a distinctive feature that distinguishes them from longitudinal waves, such as sound waves, which have a component along the direction of propagation .



### Relation between electric and magnetic fields of an electromagnetic wave

- An electromagnetic wave is a disturbance that propagates through space and time, carrying both electric and magnetic fields.
- The electric field and the magnetic field of an electromagnetic wave are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation  .
- The electric field and the magnetic field of an electromagnetic wave have the same magnitude and the same frequency .
- The electric field and the magnetic field of an electromagnetic wave obey the following equations, known as Maxwell's equations:

  - Gauss's law for electricity: The net electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the net electric charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space.
  - Gauss's law for magnetism: The net magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero, meaning that there are no magnetic monopoles.
  - Faraday's law of induction: The electromotive force (emf) induced in a closed loop is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop.
  - Ampere-Maxwell law: The magnetic field around a closed loop is equal to the sum of the current through the loop and the displacement current, which is proportional to the rate of change of the electric field.

- The electric field and the magnetic field of an electromagnetic wave are related by the following equations, derived from Maxwell's equations:

  - The curl of the electric field is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic field.
  - The curl of the magnetic field is equal to the sum of the current density and the rate of change of the electric field multiplied by the permeability of free space.

- The electric field and the magnetic field of an electromagnetic wave can be expressed in terms of their components along the x, y, and z axes, assuming a plane wave traveling in the positive z direction:

  - The electric field has components E_x and E_y, which are functions of z and t, and E_z = 0.
  - The magnetic field has components B_x and B_y, which are functions of z and t, and B_z = 0.
  - The components of the electric field and the magnetic field are related by the following equations:

    - E_x = -B_y * c
    - E_y = B_x * c
    - B_x = E_y / c
    - B_y = -E_x / c

  - where c is the speed of light in vacuum.

- The electric field and the magnetic field of an electromagnetic wave carry energy and momentum, which are transferred to the medium or the object that absorbs or reflects the wave .
- The electric field and the magnetic field of an electromagnetic wave are responsible for various phenomena, such as light, heat, radio waves, microwaves, X-rays, and gamma rays .



### Plane electromagnetic waves in conducting medium

- A plane electromagnetic wave is a wave that has constant amplitude and direction of electric and magnetic fields in a plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
- A conducting medium is a medium that has a finite conductivity (\uD835\uDF82), which means that it allows electric currents to flow through it when an electric field is applied.
- When a plane electromagnetic wave propagates in a conducting medium, it experiences attenuation and phase shift due to the presence of free charges and currents in the medium.
- The wave equation for a plane electromagnetic wave in a conducting medium is given by:

$$\nabla^2 \vec{E} - \mu \epsilon \frac{\partial^2 \vec{E}}{\partial t^2} - \mu \sigma \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} = 0$$

where \uD835\uDF41 is the permeability, \uD835\uDF3A is the permittivity, and \uD835\uDF82 is the conductivity of the medium.
- The general solution for the electric field of a plane electromagnetic wave in a conducting medium is given by:

$$\vec{E} = \vec{E}_0 e^{-\alpha x} e^{i(\omega t - \beta x)}$$

where \uD835\uDC4E0 is the amplitude, \uD835\uDFB0 is the angular frequency, \uD835\uDFB1 is the phase constant, and \uD835\uDFB5 is the attenuation constant of the wave.
- The attenuation constant \uD835\uDFB5 and the phase constant \uD835\uDFB1 are related to the intrinsic impedance \uD835\uDFB2 of the medium by:

$$\eta = \sqrt{\frac{j \omega \mu}{\sigma + j \omega \epsilon}} = \frac{\alpha + j \beta}{\omega \epsilon}$$

where j is the imaginary unit.
- The intrinsic impedance \uD835\uDFB2 is a measure of the ratio of the electric and magnetic fields in the wave. It is given by:

$$\eta = \frac{E}{H}$$

where \uD835\uDC4E and \uD835\uDC4D are the magnitudes of the electric and magnetic fields, respectively.
- The power density \uD835\uDC5C of the wave is given by the Poynting vector \uD835\uDC5B, which is the cross product of the electric and magnetic fields:

$$\vec{S} = \vec{E} \times \vec{H}$$

The power density decreases exponentially as the wave propagates in the conducting medium due to the dissipation of energy by the currents.



### Skin depth

- Skin depth is a measure of how deep an electromagnetic wave can penetrate into a conducting material before it is significantly attenuated.
- Skin depth depends on the frequency of the wave, the electrical conductivity of the material, and the magnetic permeability of the material.
- Skin depth is given by the formula:

$$\delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f \mu \sigma}}$$

where $\delta$ is the skin depth, $f$ is the frequency of the wave, $\mu$ is the magnetic permeability of the material, and $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity of the material.
- Skin depth can also be interpreted as the distance over which the magnitude of the electric or magnetic field is reduced by a factor of $1/e \approx 0.368$ or the power in the wave is reduced by a factor of $(1/e)^2 \approx 0.135$.
- Skin depth is important for applications such as eddy current testing, induction heating, and radio transmission.



## Unit 3 - Wave Optics

- Wave optics, also known as physical optics, is the branch of optics that studies the interference, diffraction, polarization, and other phenomena that depend on the wave nature of light.
- The basic principle of wave optics is the superposition of waves, which states that when two or more waves overlap in space, the resultant wave is the sum of the individual waves at each point.
- Interference is the phenomenon of the formation of bright and dark regions when two or more coherent waves overlap in space. Coherent waves are waves that have a constant phase difference and the same frequency and wavelength.
- The conditions for constructive interference (bright regions) and destructive interference (dark regions) depend on the path difference and the phase difference between the interfering waves.
- The interference pattern can be observed on a screen or a detector placed at some distance from the sources of the waves.
- Some examples of interference phenomena are Young's double slit experiment, thin film interference, and Michelson interferometer.
- Diffraction is the phenomenon of the bending of light around the edges of an obstacle or an aperture. The amount of diffraction depends on the size of the obstacle or the aperture and the wavelength of light.
- The diffraction pattern can be observed on a screen or a detector placed at some distance from the obstacle or the aperture.
- Some examples of diffraction phenomena are single slit diffraction, diffraction grating, and Fresnel and Fraunhofer diffraction.
- Polarization is the phenomenon of the restriction of the vibration of light to a particular plane. Unpolarized light consists of waves that vibrate in all possible planes perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Polarized light consists of waves that vibrate in only one plane.
- The polarization of light can be achieved by various methods, such as reflection, refraction, scattering, and transmission through polarizing materials.
- Some examples of polarization phenomena are Brewster's law, Malus's law, and optical activity.
- Other phenomena that depend on the wave nature of light are the Doppler effect, the Zeeman effect, and the photoelectric effect.

### Newton’s Rings and its applications

- Newton's rings are a phenomenon in which an interference pattern is created by the reflection of light between two surfaces, typically a spherical surface and an adjacent touching flat surface.
- The interference pattern consists of concentric bright and dark rings, with a dark spot at the center, when viewed with monochromatic light.
- The radius of the nth bright ring is given by the equation:

`r_n = sqrt(n * lambda * R)`

where `r_n` is the radius of the nth ring, `n` is the order of the ring, `lambda` is the wavelength of the light, and `R` is the radius of curvature of the spherical surface.

- The radius of the nth dark ring is given by the equation:

`r_n = sqrt((n - 1/2) * lambda * R)`

- Newton's rings have many practical applications in optics and engineering. Some of them are:

  - For testing the uniformity of a polished surface by studying the interference pattern the surface makes when placed in contact with a perfectly flat glass surface.
  - For measuring the thickness of thin films, such as oil, soap, or air, by observing the change in the interference pattern when the film is inserted between the two surfaces.
  - For controlling the thickness of paint that is used on posters. Too much paint would exceed total weight requirements, and too little paint would result in poor visibility.
  - For determining the refractive index of liquids or gases by immersing the spherical surface in them and measuring the shift in the interference pattern.
  - For measuring the shape of lenses and other curved surfaces by comparing the interference pattern they produce with that of a known standard surface.



### Introduction to diffraction

- Diffraction is the process by which a beam of light or other system of waves is spread out as a result of passing through a narrow aperture or across an edge, typically accompanied by interference between the wave forms produced  .
- Diffraction occurs when the size of the obstacle or the slit is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength of the incident wave.
- Diffraction is a consequence of the wave nature of light and other electromagnetic radiation, as well as of sound waves and particles that exhibit wave-like behavior, such as electrons and neutrons.
- Diffraction can produce various phenomena, such as fringes of parallel light and dark or colored bands, diffraction patterns, diffraction gratings, and diffraction-limited resolution.
- Diffraction can be classified into two types: Fresnel diffraction and Fraunhofer diffraction, depending on the distance between the source, the obstacle or slit, and the screen or detector.
- Fresnel diffraction occurs when the source and the screen are at a finite distance from the obstacle or slit, and the wavefronts are spherical or curved.
- Fraunhofer diffraction occurs when the source and the screen are at an infinite distance from the obstacle or slit, and the wavefronts are plane or parallel.
- The mathematical analysis of diffraction involves the use of the Huygens-Fresnel principle, the Kirchhoff integral theorem, and the Fourier transform.



### Fraunhofer diffraction at single slit and double slit

- Fraunhofer diffraction is a type of diffraction that occurs when plane waves are incident on a diffracting object, and the diffraction pattern is viewed at a sufficiently long distance from the object (in the far-field region), or at the focal plane of a converging lens.
- Fraunhofer diffraction can be used to analyze the shape and size of the diffracting object, such as a slit, a grating, or an aperture, by measuring the intensity and angular distribution of the diffracted light.
- Fraunhofer diffraction at a single slit is the simplest case of diffraction, where a parallel beam of monochromatic light of wavelength λ is incident on a slit of width d, which is of the order of λ .
- According to Huygens' principle, every point of the wavefront in the plane of the slit acts as a secondary source and sends secondary waves in all directions. These secondary waves interfere with each other and form a diffraction pattern on a screen placed at a large distance from the slit, or at the focal plane of a lens .
- The diffraction pattern consists of a central bright maximum, called the principal maximum, and a series of alternate bright and dark fringes, called the secondary maxima and minima, on either side of the central maximum .
- The intensity of the diffraction pattern at any angle θ from the center of the slit can be calculated by using the Fraunhofer diffraction equation for a single slit, which is given by :

  I(θ) = I0 (sin(πd sin θ / λ) / (πd sin θ / λ))^2

  where I0 is the intensity at the center of the slit, d is the slit width, λ is the wavelength of light, and θ is the angle from the center of the slit.
- The intensity of the diffraction pattern is maximum when the argument of the sine function is zero, which occurs when θ = 0 (the principal maximum), or when πd sin θ / λ = nπ, where n is any integer other than zero (the secondary maxima). The intensity is zero when the argument of the sine function is ±π/2, ±3π/2, ..., which occurs when πd sin θ / λ = (n + 1/2)π, where n is any integer (the secondary minima) .
- The angular width of the central maximum, or the angular distance between the first minima on either side of the central maximum, is given by :

  β = 2θ = 2 (λ / d)

  where β is the angular width, λ is the wavelength of light, and d is the slit width.

- According to Huygens' principle, each slit acts as a secondary source and sends secondary waves in all directions. These secondary waves interfere with each other and form a diffraction pattern on a screen placed at a large distance from the slits, or at the focal plane of a lens  .

### Absent spectra

- Absent spectra are the missing orders of principal maxima in the diffraction pattern of a plane grating.
- They occur when the minima due to diffraction coincide with the maxima due to interference for a certain order of spectrum.
- The condition for absent spectra is given by the equation: n = N sin θ, where n is the order of spectrum, N is the number of lines per unit length of the grating, and θ is the angle of diffraction.
- The order of the first absent spectrum is given by the least value of n that satisfies the condition, which is n = N.
- The order of the second absent spectrum is given by the next least value of n that satisfies the condition, which is n = 2N, and so on.
- The number of absent spectra is equal to the number of lines in the grating.
- Absent spectra reduce the resolving power of the grating, as they decrease the number of observable spectral lines.
- Absent spectra can be avoided by using a blazed grating, which has a triangular profile that reflects more light in a particular direction.

### Spectra with grating

### Dispersive Power

- Dispersive power is a measure of how much a material can separate different wavelengths of light by refraction.
- Dispersive power of a prism is defined as the ratio of angular dispersion to mean deviation produced by the prism  .
- Angular dispersion is the difference between the angles of deviation of two extreme wavelengths of light (usually violet and red) when they pass through the prism.
- Mean deviation is the angle of deviation of the mean ray (the ray having the mean wavelength) when it passes symmetrically through the prism.
- The formula for dispersive power is given as:

$$\omega = \frac{\theta}{\delta}$$

where $\omega$ is the dispersive power, $\theta$ is the angular dispersion, and $\delta$ is the mean deviation.

- Dispersive power depends on the material and shape of the prism, as well as the wavelengths of light used.
- Dispersive power is inversely proportional to the refractive index of the material, and directly proportional to the angle of the prism.
- Dispersive power also depends on the difference between the refractive indices of the two extreme wavelengths of light. The greater the difference, the higher the dispersive power.
- The formula for dispersive power in terms of refractive indices is given as :

$$\omega = \frac{\mu - 1}{\mu_v - \mu_r}$$

where $\mu$ is the refractive index of the mean ray, $\mu_v$ is the refractive index of the violet ray, and $\mu_r$ is the refractive index of the red ray.

- Dispersive power can be used to compare the ability of different materials or prisms to disperse light into its spectrum.
- Dispersive power can also be used to calculate the angular dispersion or the mean deviation of a prism, if the other quantities are known.

### Resolving Power

- Resolving power is the ability of an optical instrument to separate two adjacent points or objects from each other from a considerable distance.
- Resolving power depends on the wavelength of the light used and the aperture of the instrument.
- The smaller the wavelength and the larger the aperture, the higher the resolving power.
- Resolving power is measured by the minimum angular separation between two points or objects that can be resolved by the instrument.
- Resolving power is also known as the chromatic resolution when applied to a system of grating.
- There is no generalized formula for resolving power of an optical instrument. It depends from one instrument to another.
- Some common formulas for resolving power are:

  - For a telescope: RP = d/1.22λ, where d is the diameter of the objective lens and λ is the wavelength of light .
  - For a microscope: RP = 2nsinθ/λ, where n is the refractive index of the medium, θ is the half-angle of the cone of light entering the objective lens, and λ is the wavelength of light .
  - For a prism: RP = λ/dλ, where λ is the wavelength of the spectral line and dλ is the difference between the wavelengths of two adjacent spectral lines .
  - For a system of grating: RP = λ/Δλ, where λ is the wavelength of the spectral line and Δλ is the difference between the wavelengths of two adjacent spectral lines .

- Resolving power is important for applications such as astronomy, microscopy, spectroscopy, and photography.

### Fibre Optics: Principle and construction of optical fiber

- Fibre optics is the science of transmitting data, voice, and images by the passage of light through thin, transparent fibers.
- Optical fibers are flexible, transparent fibers made by drawing glass or plastic to a diameter slightly thicker than that of a human hair.
- Optical fibers work on the principle of total internal reflection of light, which means that no part of the light signal gets refracted or transmitted to the second medium, but instead, all of the signal is retained inside the fiber .
- The condition for total internal reflection is that the light must travel from an optically denser medium (such as glass) to an optically rarer medium (such as air) and the angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle for the boundary.
- The construction of an optical fiber consists of three main parts: the core, the cladding, and the protective layer .
  - The core is the innermost part of the fiber, where the light is transmitted. It is made of glass or plastic with a high refractive index (n1).
  - The cladding is the outer layer of the fiber, which surrounds the core. It is made of glass or plastic with a lower refractive index (n2) than the core. The cladding acts as a mirror that reflects the light back into the core, preventing it from escaping.
  - The protective layer is the outermost layer of the fiber, which covers the cladding. It is made of plastic or rubber and provides mechanical strength and protection from moisture, dust, and abrasion.
- The diameter of the core and the cladding vary depending on the type and application of the optical fiber. The typical values are 8 μm and 125 μm respectively for a single-mode fiber, and 50 μm and 125 μm respectively for a multi-mode fiber.
- A single-mode fiber allows only one mode of light to propagate through the core, which means that the light travels in a straight line with minimal dispersion and attenuation. A single-mode fiber is used for long-distance and high-bandwidth communication.
- A multi-mode fiber allows multiple modes of light to propagate through the core, which means that the light travels in different paths with different speeds and angles. A multi-mode fiber is used for short-distance and low-bandwidth communication.



### Acceptance angle

- The acceptance angle of an optical fiber is defined based on a purely geometrical consideration (ray optics): it is the maximum angle of a ray (against the fiber axis) hitting the fiber core which allows the incident light to be guided by the core .
- The acceptance angle depends on the refractive indices of the core, the cladding, and the surrounding medium (usually air). It can be calculated using Snell's law and the condition for total internal reflection .
- The acceptance angle is related to the numerical aperture (NA) of the fiber by the formula NA = n0 sin θa, where n0 is the refractive index of the surrounding medium and θa is the acceptance angle .
- The acceptance angle determines the cone of light that can be coupled into the fiber. The larger the acceptance angle, the more light can be launched into the fiber .
- The acceptance angle also affects the bandwidth and dispersion of the fiber, as different angles of incidence correspond to different propagation modes and velocities of light in the fiber .



### Numerical aperture

- Numerical aperture (NA) is a measure of the ability of an optical fiber to collect or confine the incident light ray inside it .
- It is among the most basic property of optical fiber and shows the efficiency with which light is collected inside the fiber in order to get propagated.
- Numerical aperture is defined as the sine of half of the angle of the fiber's light acceptance cone , i.e., NA = sin θa, where θa is called the acceptance angle.
- The acceptance angle is the maximum angle at which the light can enter the fiber and still be transmitted along it .
- The numerical aperture depends on the refractive indices of the core and the cladding of the fiber, as well as the wavelength of the light .
- The numerical aperture can be calculated by the formula NA = √(n1^2 - n2^2), where n1 is the refractive index of the core and n2 is the refractive index of the cladding.
- The numerical aperture is a dimensionless quantity and has no units.
- The numerical aperture determines the resolution and light-gathering ability of an optical fiber .
- A higher numerical aperture means a larger acceptance angle and a higher ability to collect light, but also a lower resolution and a higher attenuation due to modal dispersion .
- A lower numerical aperture means a smaller acceptance angle and a lower ability to collect light, but also a higher resolution and a lower attenuation due to modal dispersion .
- Numerical aperture is commonly used in microscopy and fiber optics applications.



### Acceptance cone

- The acceptance cone is the cone-shaped region of incident light that can enter an optical fiber and be propagated along its core.
- The acceptance cone is derived by rotating the acceptance angle, which is the maximum angle of incidence for which total internal reflection occurs at the core-cladding interface, about the fiber axis .
- The size of the acceptance cone depends on the numerical aperture (NA) of the fiber, which is a measure of its light-gathering ability and resolution.
- The numerical aperture is defined as the sine of the acceptance angle, multiplied by the refractive index of the medium in which the fiber is immersed .
- The numerical aperture is also related to the refractive indices of the core and the cladding of the fiber, by the formula NA = sqrt(n_core^2 - n_cladding^2), where n_core and n_cladding are the core and cladding refractive indices, respectively .
- The larger the acceptance cone, the larger the numerical aperture, which means that the fiber can accept and propagate more light.
- However, a larger acceptance cone also implies a larger divergence of the output beam, which may reduce the quality of the transmitted signal.
- Therefore, there is a trade-off between the acceptance cone and the performance of the fiber optic system.



### Step index and graded index fibers

- Step index fiber and graded index fiber are two types of multimode fibers that can transmit multiple modes of light simultaneously.
- Step index fiber has a core with constant refractive index and a sharp change at the core-cladding interface. The core diameter is typically larger than 50 microns.
- Graded index fiber has a core with varying refractive index that decreases gradually from the center to the cladding. The core diameter is typically between 50 and 62.5 microns.
- The main differences between step index and graded index fibers are:

  - Graded index fiber offers superior transmission quality, higher bandwidth, and longer distance than step index fiber.
  - Step index fiber suffers from higher modal dispersion, which limits its bandwidth and data rate. Modal dispersion occurs when different modes travel at different speeds and arrive at different times at the receiver.
  - Graded index fiber reduces modal dispersion by making the higher modes travel faster in the lower refractive index regions of the core. This compensates for the longer path length of the higher modes and makes them arrive at the same time as the lower modes.
  - Step index fiber is cheaper and easier to manufacture than graded index fiber.
  - Graded index fiber is preferred for modern communication applications that require high bandwidth and long distance.
  - Step index fiber is mainly used for short distance and low bandwidth applications, such as local area networks (LANs) and sensors.



### Fiber optic communication principle

- The principle of fiber optic communication is based on total internal reflection, which occurs when light travels from a denser medium to a less dense medium at an angle greater than the critical angle .
- Total internal reflection ensures that the light is confined within the core of the fiber, and does not leak out or get absorbed by the surrounding cladding.
- The core and the cladding have different refractive indices, which determine the speed and direction of light in the fiber.
- The core has a higher refractive index than the cladding, which means that light travels faster in the cladding than in the core.
- The difference in refractive indices also affects the numerical aperture of the fiber, which is a measure of its ability to collect and transmit light.
- The numerical aperture is given by the formula: NA = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2), where n1 is the refractive index of the core, and n2 is the refractive index of the cladding.
- The numerical aperture determines the acceptance angle of the fiber, which is the maximum angle at which light can enter the fiber and still be guided by total internal reflection.
- The acceptance angle is given by the formula: theta = sin^-1(NA), where theta is the acceptance angle, and NA is the numerical aperture.
- The acceptance angle also determines the mode of propagation of light in the fiber, which is the number and shape of the light waves that can travel in the fiber.
- There are two main types of modes: single mode and multimode.
- Single mode fibers have a small core diameter and a low numerical aperture, which means that they can only support one mode of light propagation.
- Single mode fibers have the advantage of low attenuation, high bandwidth, and low dispersion, which means that they can transmit signals over long distances with high quality and speed.
- Multimode fibers have a large core diameter and a high numerical aperture, which means that they can support multiple modes of light propagation.

- Low attenuation means that fiber optic communication can transmit signals over long distances without the need for amplification or repeaters.

### Attenuation

- Attenuation is the reduction in intensity of the light beam (or signal) with respect to distance travelled through a transmission medium .
- Attenuation is measured in decibels (dB) and is calculated by the formula: `Attenuation (dB) = 10 x Log10 (P in/P out) = 20xLog10 (V in/V out)` where P is power and V is voltage.
- Attenuation limits the distance in which the signal can travel through optical fiber and affects the performance and reliability of the fiber optic network.
- Attenuation can be caused by intrinsic and extrinsic factors .
  - Intrinsic factors are inherent to the fiber optic material and include:
    - Absorption: the conversion of optical power into heat due to the interaction of light with the molecules of the fiber material or impurities in the fiber .
    - Scattering: the deflection of light from its original direction due to the microscopic variations in the density and refractive index of the fiber material .
    - Dispersion: the spreading of light pulses over time due to the different velocities of different wavelengths or modes of light in the fiber .
  - Extrinsic factors are external to the fiber optic material and include:
    - Bending: the loss of light due to the curvature of the fiber beyond its minimum bend radius .
    - Splicing: the loss of light due to the imperfect alignment or connection of two fiber segments .
    - Connector: the loss of light due to the reflection, misalignment or contamination of the fiber end faces at the connector interface .
    - Environmental: the loss of light due to the changes in temperature, humidity, pressure or vibration that affect the fiber characteristics .



### Dispersion in Fiber Optics

- Dispersion is the spreading of light pulses when they travel through an optical fiber from one end to another .
- Dispersion causes distortion and limits the data rate of digital signals sent over fiber optic cable.
- Dispersion can be classified into three types: material dispersion, waveguide dispersion, and intermodal dispersion .
- Material dispersion is caused by a change in the refractive index of the fiber optic material with different wavelengths. The higher the index, the slower the light travels.
- Waveguide dispersion is due to the distribution of light between the core and the cladding of the fiber. The light in the core travels faster than the light in the cladding.
- Intermodal dispersion is due to the different modes or paths that light can take through the fiber. The modes that travel closer to the axis of the fiber have shorter paths than the modes that travel near the cladding.
- Dispersion can be reduced by using single-mode fibers, dispersion-shifted fibers, dispersion-compensating fibers, or dispersion-flattened fibers.
- Dispersion can also be compensated by using optical filters, optical amplifiers, or dispersion compensating modules.

### Laser: Absorption of radiation

### Spontaneous and stimulated emission of radiation

- Spontaneous emission is the process in which an atom or molecule in an excited state decays to a lower energy state and emits a photon of energy without the aid of external agency.
- Stimulated emission is the process in which an atom or molecule in an excited state is induced to emit a photon of energy by interacting with another photon of the same frequency.
- The properties of photons generated by spontaneous and stimulated emission are quite different:
  - Spontaneous emission takes place without interaction with other photons, and the direction and phase are random. The emitted radiation is incoherent and has a broad spectrum .
  - Stimulated emission takes place when the excited electron interacts with another photon. The emitted photon has the same frequency, phase, polarization, and direction of propagation as the incident photon. The emitted radiation is coherent and has a narrow spectrum  .
- Spontaneous emission and stimulated emission are the basis of laser operation. A laser is a device that produces a coherent and intense beam of light by amplifying the stimulated emission of photons from a lasing medium.



### Population inversion

- Population inversion is a state of a system, for example a laser gain medium, where a higher-lying energy level is more strongly populated than a lower-lying level.
- Population inversion is an important concept for lasers optics, as it is essential for light amplification by stimulated emission.
- Population inversion cannot occur under conditions of thermal equilibrium, where the level populations are described by a Boltzmann distribution.
- Population inversion can be achieved by various methods, such as optical pumping, electrical pumping, chemical pumping, or collisional pumping.
- Population inversion can be achieved in different types of lasers, such as gas lasers, solid-state lasers, fiber lasers, and semiconductor lasers   .
- Population inversion can be characterized by the inversion factor, which is the ratio of the population densities of the upper and lower levels involved in the laser transition.



### Einstein's Coefficients

- Einstein's coefficients are mathematical quantities that describe the probability of absorption or emission of a photon by an atom or molecule.
- There are three types of Einstein's coefficients: A, B, and C.
- The Einstein A coefficient is related to the rate of spontaneous emission of light, which is the process of an atom or molecule in an excited state emitting a photon and returning to a lower energy state without any external influence .
- The Einstein B coefficient is related to the absorption and stimulated emission of light, which are the processes of an atom or molecule in a lower energy state absorbing a photon and jumping to a higher energy state, or an atom or molecule in an excited state emitting a photon and returning to a lower energy state due to the presence of another photon with the same energy and direction .
- The Einstein C coefficient is related to the induced absorption and emission of light, which are the processes of an atom or molecule in a lower or higher energy state absorbing or emitting a photon due to the presence of an external electric or magnetic field.
- The Einstein coefficients are fixed probabilities per time associated with each atom, and do not depend on the state of the gas of which the atoms are a part.
- The Einstein coefficients can be derived from the quantum theory of radiation and matter, and they satisfy certain relations that ensure the thermodynamic equilibrium and the conservation of energy and photons .
- The Einstein coefficients are important for understanding the spectral lines and the radiative processes in astrophysics, as well as the principles of lasers and optical amplifiers  .



### Principles of laser action

- Laser is an acronym for **Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation**.
- Laser action is the process of producing a coherent, monochromatic, and intense beam of light by stimulating atoms or molecules to emit photons of the same frequency, phase, and direction.
- The basic principles of laser action are :
  - **Spontaneous emission**: An atom or molecule in an excited state can decay to a lower energy state by emitting a photon of energy equal to the energy difference between the two states. This process is random and isotropic, meaning that the photon can be emitted in any direction and with any phase.
  - **Stimulated emission**: An atom or molecule in an excited state can also decay to a lower energy state by emitting a photon of energy equal to the energy difference between the two states, but only if it is stimulated by another photon of the same frequency and phase. This process is coherent and directional, meaning that the emitted photon has the same frequency, phase, and direction as the stimulating photon. The two photons are said to be in phase with each other.
  - **Stimulated absorption**: An atom or molecule in a lower energy state can absorb a photon of energy equal to the energy difference between the two states and jump to a higher energy state. This process is the reverse of stimulated emission and reduces the intensity of the light beam.
  - **Non-radiative decay**: An atom or molecule in an excited state can also decay to a lower energy state by transferring its excess energy to other atoms or molecules through collisions or vibrations. This process is non-radiative and does not involve the emission or absorption of photons. It reduces the population of the excited state and increases the population of the lower state.
  - **Population inversion**: For laser action to occur, there must be more atoms or molecules in the excited state than in the lower state, so that the rate of stimulated emission is greater than the rate of stimulated absorption. This condition is called population inversion and can be achieved by pumping energy into the system through various methods, such as electrical discharge, optical pumping, chemical reaction, etc.
  - **Amplification of light**: When a light beam passes through a medium with population inversion, it undergoes multiple cycles of stimulated emission and absorption, resulting in an exponential increase in its intensity. This process is called amplification of light and depends on the length of the medium, the cross-section of the beam, and the degree of population inversion. The amplified light beam is then extracted from the medium by using mirrors or other optical devices.



### Solid state Laser (Ruby laser) and Gas Laser (He-Ne laser)

- A solid state laser is a laser that uses a solid material as its gain medium, rather than a liquid or a gas. 
- A ruby laser is a type of solid state laser that uses a synthetic ruby crystal as its gain medium. 
- A ruby laser produces pulses of coherent visible light at a wavelength of 694.3 nm, which is a deep red color. 
- A ruby laser is a three level laser, meaning that it has three energy levels: the ground state, the metastable state, and the upper state. 
- A ruby laser is pumped by a flash lamp, which excites the electrons in the ruby crystal from the ground state to the upper state. 
- The electrons in the upper state quickly decay to the metastable state, where they can stay for a long time. This creates a population inversion between the metastable state and the ground state. 
- When a photon of the right wavelength passes through the ruby crystal, it can stimulate an electron in the metastable state to drop to the ground state, emitting another photon of the same wavelength and phase. This is the process of stimulated emission. 
- The photons are reflected back and forth by the mirrors at the ends of the ruby rod, forming a resonant cavity. Some of the photons escape through the partially transparent mirror, forming the laser beam. 
- A gas laser is a laser that uses a gas as its gain medium, rather than a solid or a liquid. 
- A helium-neon laser (He-Ne laser) is a type of gas laser that uses a mixture of helium and neon gases as its gain medium. 
- A He-Ne laser produces continuous wave (CW) coherent light at a wavelength of 632.8 nm, which is a bright red color. 
- A He-Ne laser is a four level laser, meaning that it has four energy levels: the ground state, the lower state, the upper state, and the metastable state. 
- A He-Ne laser is pumped by an electric discharge, which excites the electrons in the helium atoms from the ground state to the metastable state. 
- The excited helium atoms collide with the neon atoms, transferring some of their energy to them. This raises the neon atoms from the lower state to the upper state. This creates a population inversion between the upper state and the lower state. 
- When a photon of the right wavelength passes through the gas mixture, it can stimulate a neon atom in the upper state to drop to the lower state, emitting another photon of the same wavelength and phase. This is the process of stimulated emission. 
- The photons are reflected back and forth by the mirrors at the ends of the gas tube, forming a resonant cavity. Some of the photons escape through the partially transparent mirror, forming the laser beam.

### Superconductors: Temperature dependence of resistivity in superconducting materials

- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance below a certain critical temperature (Tc)  .
- The resistivity of materials depends on the temperature. The equation ρt = ρ0 [1 + α (T – T0) shows the relation between the temperature and the resistivity for any material  .
- In the equation, ρ0 is the resistivity at a standard temperature, ρt is the resistivity at t 0 C, T0 is the reference temperature and α is the temperature coefficient of resistivity  .
- For superconductors, the resistivity drops abruptly to zero when the temperature is lowered below Tc  .
- The value of Tc depends on the type and composition of the superconducting material. For most metals, Tc is very low, below 12 K. For some ceramics, Tc can be as high as 135 K  .
- The phenomenon of superconductivity is explained by two theories: the BCS theory and the Ginzburg-Landau theory  .
- The BCS theory proposes that below Tc, the electrons in the material form pairs called Cooper pairs, which behave as bosons and condense into a quantum state with zero resistance  .
- The Ginzburg-Landau theory describes the superconducting state as a complex order parameter that varies in space and time. The theory predicts the existence of vortices, which are regions of normal conductivity within the superconducting material  .
- The superconducting materials are classified into two types: type I and type II  .
- Type I superconductors are pure metals or alloys that have a single critical magnetic field (Hc) above which the superconductivity is destroyed. They have a complete Meissner effect, which means they expel all the magnetic flux from their interior  .
- Type II superconductors are mostly ceramics or compounds that have two critical magnetic fields (Hc1 and Hc2) between which the superconductivity is partial. They have a mixed state, which means they allow some magnetic flux to penetrate their interior in the form of vortices  .



### Meissner effect

- The Meissner effect is the expulsion of a magnetic field from the interior of a material that is in the process of becoming a superconductor .
- A superconductor is a material that loses its resistance to the flow of electrical currents when cooled below a certain temperature, called the critical temperature .
- The Meissner effect was discovered by the German physicists W. Meissner and R. Ochsenfeld in 1933 .
- The Meissner effect is a property of all superconductors, and it is one of the defining characteristics of the superconducting state .
- The Meissner effect can be demonstrated by making a magnet levitate above a superconductor, as seen in the figure below:

Meissner effect demonstration

- The Meissner effect occurs because the superconductor develops a surface current that generates a magnetic field that exactly cancels the applied magnetic field inside the material .
- The Meissner effect is a consequence of the perfect diamagnetism of superconductors, which means that they have a magnetic susceptibility of -1 and a zero magnetic flux density .
- The Meissner effect can be explained by the BCS theory of superconductivity, which states that the superconducting state is formed by the pairing of electrons into Cooper pairs that have a lower energy than the normal state.
- The Meissner effect is different from the ordinary diamagnetic effect, which is a weak and linear response of a material to an applied magnetic field that does not expel the magnetic field completely.
- The Meissner effect is also different from the electromagnetic induction effect, which is a temporary phenomenon that occurs when a changing magnetic field induces a current in a conductor that opposes the change.



### Temperature dependence of critical field for superconductors

- The critical field (Hc) of a superconductor is the maximum external magnetic field that can be applied without destroying the superconducting state.
- The critical field depends on the temperature (T) of the superconductor, and it decreases as the temperature increases, reaching zero at the critical temperature (Tc).
- The temperature dependence of the critical field can be expressed by the empirical formula:

$$H_c(T) = H_c(0) \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{T}{T_c} \right)^2 \right]$$

where $H_c(0)$ is the critical field at absolute zero.

- This formula is valid for ordinary (type I) superconductors, which have a single critical field and a single critical temperature.
- For high-temperature (type II) superconductors, which have two critical fields ($H_{c1}$ and $H_{c2}$) and a mixed state between them, the temperature dependence of the critical fields is more complicated and depends on the material properties and the dimensionality of the superconductor.
- Some general features of the temperature dependence of the critical fields for type II superconductors are:

  - $H_{c1}(T)$ is the lower critical field, below which the superconductor expels the magnetic field completely (Meissner effect). It decreases with increasing temperature, reaching zero at $T_c$.
  - $H_{c2}(T)$ is the upper critical field, above which the superconductor becomes normal. It increases with decreasing temperature, reaching a maximum value at $T = 0$.
  - The mixed state occurs between $H_{c1}(T)$ and $H_{c2}(T)$, where the superconductor allows the magnetic field to penetrate in the form of quantized vortices, which carry a normal core and a superconducting current around them.
  - The temperature dependence of $H_{c2}(T)$ can be affected by localization effects, which reduce the coherence length of the superconducting wavefunction and increase the orbital depairing of the Cooper pairs. This can result in an anomalous negative curvature of $H_{c2}(T)$ at low temperatures, as observed in some high-temperature superconductors.



### Persistent Current in Superconductors

- Persistent current is the electric current that flows indefinitely in a closed loop of superconducting material without any external power source.
- Persistent current is possible in superconductors because they have zero resistance below a critical temperature and can maintain a constant magnetic flux inside the loop .
- Persistent current can be induced in a superconducting loop by placing it in a magnetic field above its critical temperature and then cooling it below its critical temperature. The magnetic field will induce a current in the loop according to Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. When the magnetic field is removed, the current will persist in the loop due to its self-inductance .
- Persistent current can also be induced in a superconducting loop by connecting it to a voltage source and then disconnecting the voltage source. The current will flow in the loop without any decay as long as the loop remains superconducting.
- Persistent current has many applications in the form of superconducting magnets, which can generate very strong and stable magnetic fields for various purposes, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particle accelerators, and maglev trains.
- Persistent current can also exhibit quantum effects in some mesoscopic devices, such as quantum rings and Josephson junctions, where the current can be quantized and tunable by external parameters .



### Type I and Type II Superconductors

- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and perfect diamagnetism below a certain critical temperature.
- There are two types of superconductors: type I and type II, which differ in their behavior in the presence of an external magnetic field.
- Type I superconductors have only one critical magnetic field, Hc, above which they lose their superconductivity and become normal conductors. They expel all the magnetic flux from their interior when they are in the superconducting state, a phenomenon known as the Meissner effect.
- Type I superconductors are usually pure metals, such as mercury, lead, tin, and aluminum. They have low critical temperatures, typically below 10 K, and low critical magnetic fields, typically below 0.1 T.
- Type II superconductors have two critical magnetic fields, Hc1 and Hc2, between which they enter a mixed state, where some magnetic flux can penetrate their interior in the form of quantized vortices. They retain their superconductivity up to Hc2, which is much higher than Hc1 and Hc of type I superconductors.
- Type II superconductors are usually metal alloys or complex oxide ceramics. They have high critical temperatures, up to 138 K, and high critical magnetic fields, up to several tesla. All high-temperature superconductors are type II superconductors. Some examples of type II superconductors are niobium, vanadium, technetium, boron-doped diamond, and silicon, and various cuprates, iron-based compounds, and magnesium diboride.
- Type II superconductors have more applications than type I superconductors, because they can operate at higher temperatures and magnetic fields, and have higher current densities. Some of the applications of type II superconductors are superconducting magnets, power transmission lines, magnetic levitation trains, and quantum computers.



### High temperature superconductors

- High temperature superconductors (HTS) are materials that behave as superconductors at temperatures above 77 K (−196.2 °C; −321.1 °F), the boiling point of liquid nitrogen.
- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and can expel magnetic fields when cooled below a critical temperature (Tc).
- HTS have higher Tc than conventional superconductors, which are mostly metallic alloys that require liquid helium to reach superconductivity at temperatures below 30 K (−243.2 °C; −405.7 °F).
- HTS have potential applications in power transmission, magnetic levitation, quantum computing, and other fields that require high efficiency and low loss of energy.
- The main class of HTS is copper oxides combined with other metals, especially the rare-earth barium copper oxides (REBCOs) such as yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO).
- The second class of HTS is the iron-based compounds, which have a layered structure similar to the copper oxides but with iron and arsenic or other elements.
- The mechanism of HTS is still not fully understood, but it involves the formation of Cooper pairs, which are pairs of electrons that overcome their mutual repulsion and bind together at low temperatures .
- Cooper pairs can move through the lattice of atoms without resistance, as they are in a quantum state that is unaffected by the thermal vibrations of the atoms.
- The Cooper pairs are also affected by the magnetic fields, which can break them apart and destroy superconductivity. This is known as the Meissner effect, and it limits the maximum magnetic field (Hc) that a superconductor can withstand.
- HTS have higher Hc than conventional superconductors, which means they can operate in stronger magnetic fields and generate higher currents.
- HTS also have a higher critical current density (Jc), which is the maximum current that can flow through a superconductor without resistance. Jc depends on the temperature, the magnetic field, and the microstructure of the material.
- HTS are usually fabricated as thin films, wires, or tapes, which have to be aligned and textured to achieve high Jc and avoid defects that can degrade the performance of the superconductor.
- HTS are still challenging to produce, test, and use, as they require special techniques and equipment to handle the high temperatures, pressures, and magnetic fields involved.
- HTS are also subject to controversy, as some claims of superconductivity at very high temperatures (above 200 K) have not been replicated or verified by other researchers.
- HTS are an active area of research and development, as scientists and engineers seek to discover new materials, improve the existing ones, and explore the possible applications of HTS in various domains.

### Basics concept of Quantum Dots

- Quantum dots are **semiconductor nanocrystals** that have **quantum mechanical behavior** due to their small size  .
- Quantum dots have **discrete and quantized energy levels** that depend on their size, shape, composition, and structure .
- Quantum dots can **emit light** of different colors when they are excited by an external source, such as UV light . The color of the light depends on the size of the quantum dot, with smaller dots emitting shorter wavelengths (blue) and larger dots emitting longer wavelengths (red).
- Quantum dots have **many applications** in fields such as nanotechnology, materials science, biotechnology, optoelectronics, and quantum computing  .
- Some examples of quantum dot applications are:
  - **Quantum dot displays**: Quantum dots can be used to enhance the color and brightness of LCD and LED screens, by converting the backlight into different colors.
  - **Quantum dot solar cells**: Quantum dots can be used to improve the efficiency and stability of solar cells, by absorbing a wider range of the solar spectrum and generating multiple electron-hole pairs per photon .
  - **Quantum dot biosensors**: Quantum dots can be used to label and detect specific molecules, cells, or pathogens, by emitting fluorescent signals that can be observed under a microscope .
  - **Quantum dot lasers**: Quantum dots can be used to create lasers that have low threshold currents, high modulation speeds, and temperature stability, by confining the carriers in discrete energy levels .
  - **Quantum dot qubits**: Quantum dots can be used to store and manipulate quantum information, by exploiting the spin or charge states of the electrons or holes trapped in them .



### Quantum wires and Quantum well

- Quantum wires and quantum wells are examples of **nanostructures** that have one or more dimensions on the order of **10 nm** or smaller.
- These structures have applications in **nanoelectronics, optoelectronics, information technology, and biotechnology**, due to the quantum mechanical confinement provided by the small spatial extent of the structures.
- Quantum wells are **two-dimensional (2D)** structures, where the motion of electrons is confined in one direction, while they are free to move in the other two directions.
- Quantum wires are **one-dimensional (1D)** structures, where the motion of electrons is confined in two directions, while they are free to move in only one direction.
- Quantum wells and quantum wires are typically made by **including a lower bandgap semiconductor material inside a higher bandgap matrix material**. This creates a potential well for the electrons, where they can only occupy discrete energy levels.
- The energy levels and the optical and transport properties of quantum wells and quantum wires depend on the **size, shape, material, and orientation** of the structures.
- Quantum wells and quantum wires can be studied theoretically and computationally using methods such as **effective mass approximation, tight-binding model, k.p theory, and density functional theory**.
- Quantum wells and quantum wires have been used to create devices such as **quantum cascade lasers, quantum well infrared photodetectors, quantum dot solar cells, and quantum wire transistors** .



### Fabrication of nano materials -TopDown approach (CVD) and Bottom-Up approach (Sol Gel)

- Nanomaterials are materials with at least one dimension in the range of 1 to 100 nanometers (nm).
- Nanomaterials have unique physical, chemical, optical, and electrical properties that depend on their size, shape, and structure.
- Nanomaterials can be fabricated by two general approaches: top-down and bottom-up.
- Top-down approach is where a process starts from a large piece and subsequently uses finer and finer tools for creating correspondingly smaller structures.
- Bottom-up approach is where smaller components of atomic or molecular dimensions self-assemble together, according to a natural physical principle or an externally applied driving force, to give rise to larger and more organized systems.
- Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) is a top-down technique that involves the deposition of a thin film of a material on a substrate by exposing it to a gas or vapor containing the desired elements.
- CVD can produce various types of nanomaterials, such as carbon nanotubes, graphene, nanowires, nanodots, etc.
- CVD can produce high-quality nanomaterials with good control over the size, shape, and orientation of the nanostructures.
- CVD requires high temperature, vacuum, and expensive equipment and catalysts.
- Sol-gel processing is a bottom-up technique that involves the formation of a colloidal suspension (sol) of nanoparticles and their subsequent gelation into a solid network (gel).
- Sol-gel processing can produce various types of nanomaterials, such as metal oxides, ceramics, composites, etc.
- Sol-gel processing can produce nanomaterials with high purity, homogeneity, and porosity.
- Sol-gel processing requires low temperature, ambient pressure, and simple equipment and precursors.
- Sol-gel processing may suffer from cracking, shrinkage, and agglomeration of the nanoparticles.
