

## Unit 1 - Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the behavior of matter and energy at the smallest scales of the physical world. Quantum mechanics is based on the following principles:

- The wave-particle duality: Matter and energy can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties, depending on the situation. For example, light can behave as a stream of photons (particles) or as an electromagnetic wave, and electrons can behave as particles or as waves.
- The uncertainty principle: It is impossible to measure both the position and the momentum of a quantum object (such as an electron) simultaneously with perfect accuracy. The more precisely one quantity is measured, the more uncertain the other becomes. This implies that quantum objects have a probabilistic nature and can exist in a superposition of states until an observation is made.
- The Schrödinger equation: This is the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics that describes how the wave function of a quantum system evolves over time. The wave function is a mathematical object that contains all the information about the state of the system. The Schrödinger equation can be solved for different types of systems, such as atoms, molecules, or solids, to obtain their energy levels, wave functions, and other properties.
- The Born rule: This is the rule that connects the wave function of a quantum system to the observable outcomes of measurements. The Born rule states that the probability of finding a quantum object in a given state is proportional to the square of the amplitude of the wave function corresponding to that state. For example, the probability of finding an electron in a certain orbital of an atom is proportional to the square of the wave function of that orbital.
- The principle of superposition: This is the principle that states that a quantum system can exist in a linear combination of two or more states until an observation is made. For example, an electron can be in a superposition of spin up and spin down states until a measurement of its spin is performed. When the measurement is made, the system collapses to one of the possible states, and the outcome is random.
- The principle of entanglement: This is the principle that states that two or more quantum systems can become correlated in such a way that their states cannot be described independently, even when they are separated by large distances. For example, two electrons can be entangled in a singlet state, such that their spins are always opposite to each other, regardless of how far apart they are. When one of the electrons is measured, the state of the other is instantly determined, even though no physical signal is exchanged between them. This phenomenon is also known as quantum nonlocality or quantum spooky action at a distance.



### Inadequacy of classical mechanics

- Classical mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of macroscopic objects under the influence of forces.
- Classical mechanics is based on Newton's laws of motion, which describe how forces affect the motion of objects.
- Classical mechanics can explain correctly the motion of celestial bodies, such as planets and stars, and microscopic terrestrial bodies, such as bullets and cars, that move with non-relativistic speeds (much less than the speed of light).
- However, classical mechanics fails to explain certain phenomena observed in nature, especially at the atomic and subatomic scales, where objects move with relativistic speeds (close to the speed of light) or interact with electromagnetic radiation (light).
- Some of the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot explain are:

  - The stability of atoms: According to classical mechanics, an electron orbiting a nucleus should emit electromagnetic radiation and lose energy, causing it to spiral into the nucleus and collapse the atom. However, atoms are stable and do not collapse.
  - The discrete spectra of atoms: According to classical mechanics, an electron orbiting a nucleus can have any energy and emit or absorb any frequency of electromagnetic radiation. However, experiments show that atoms can only have certain discrete energy levels and emit or absorb only certain discrete frequencies of electromagnetic radiation, forming a characteristic spectrum for each element.
  - The blackbody radiation: According to classical mechanics, a blackbody (an ideal object that absorbs and emits all frequencies of electromagnetic radiation) should emit radiation with an intensity proportional to the frequency to the fourth power. However, experiments show that the intensity of blackbody radiation peaks at a certain frequency and then decreases rapidly for higher frequencies, forming a characteristic curve for each temperature. This discrepancy is known as the ultraviolet catastrophe.
  - The photoelectric effect: According to classical mechanics, a metal surface exposed to electromagnetic radiation should emit electrons with an energy proportional to the intensity of the radiation. However, experiments show that the energy of the emitted electrons depends only on the frequency of the radiation, and that there is a minimum frequency (threshold frequency) below which no electrons are emitted, regardless of the intensity of the radiation.
  - The Compton effect: According to classical mechanics, a photon (a particle of light) colliding with an electron should transfer some of its energy and momentum to the electron, but not change its frequency. However, experiments show that the frequency of the photon decreases after the collision, and that the amount of decrease depends on the angle of scattering.

- These phenomena indicate that classical mechanics is inadequate to describe the behavior of matter and radiation at the atomic and subatomic scales, and that a new theory is needed to account for them. This new theory is quantum mechanics, which incorporates the wave-particle duality of matter and radiation, the uncertainty principle, and the probabilistic nature of physical phenomena.



### Planck’s theory of black body radiation (qualitative)

- A black body is a hypothetical body that completely absorbs all radiant energy falling upon it, reaches some equilibrium temperature, and then reemits that energy as quickly as it absorbs it.
- The spectrum of the radiation emitted by a black body depends only on its temperature, and not on its shape, size, or composition.
- The classical theory of electromagnetism, based on the laws of Maxwell and Kirchhoff, failed to explain the observed spectrum of black body radiation, especially at high frequencies (short wavelengths), where it predicted an infinite energy density, known as the ultraviolet catastrophe .
- In 1900, German physicist Max Planck heuristically derived a formula for the observed spectrum by assuming that a hypothetical electrically charged oscillator in a cavity that contained black-body radiation could only change its energy in a minimal increment, E, that was proportional to the frequency of its associated electromagnetic wave .
- This increment, E, is given by E = hν, where h is a constant, now known as Planck's constant, and ν is the frequency of the radiation .

### Compton effect

- The Compton effect is the phenomenon of **elastic scattering** of photons by free or loosely bound electrons, resulting in a **change in wavelength** of the photons .
- The Compton effect was discovered by **Arthur Compton** in 1923, who measured the scattering of X-rays by graphite and observed that the scattered X-rays had a longer wavelength than the incident X-rays .
- The Compton effect provided experimental evidence for the **particle nature** of electromagnetic radiation, as it could not be explained by the classical wave theory of light .
- The Compton effect can be explained by treating the photons as **particles** with energy and momentum, and applying the **conservation laws** of energy and momentum to the collision between the photon and the electron.

### de-Broglie concept of matter waves

- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves is based on the idea that **all matter exhibits wave-like properties** when it is in motion .
- This concept was proposed by Louis de Broglie in 1924 as a **generalization of the photon theory of light**, which states that light can behave as both a wave and a particle .
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves implies that **matter has a dual nature**, similar to light. When matter is at rest, it shows particle properties, such as mass and momentum. When matter is in motion, it shows wave properties, such as wavelength and frequency .
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves can be expressed by the following equation  :

    \[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} \]

    where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the matter wave, $h$ is the Planck's constant, and $p$ is the momentum of the matter particle.
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves can explain some of the **quantum phenomena** observed in the microscopic world, such as the **quantization of the electron's angular momentum** in the hydrogen atom, the **diffraction and interference of electrons** by crystals, and the **uncertainty principle** of Heisenberg  .



### Davisson and Germer Experiment

- The Davisson and Germer experiment was a 1923-27 experiment by Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Western Electric (later Bell Labs), in which electrons, scattered by the surface of a crystal of nickel metal, displayed a diffraction pattern.
- The experiment confirmed the de Broglie hypothesis that matter has wave-like behavior and established the wave–particle duality hypothesis which was a fundamental step in quantum theory.
- The experiment was based on the assumption that waves reflected from two distinct atomic levels of a Ni crystal will have a fixed phase difference and will interfere either constructively or destructively, producing a diffraction pattern .
- The experiment consisted of a vacuum chamber with an electron gun that emitted a beam of electrons of known energy and a nickel target that could be rotated to change the angle of incidence of the electrons.
- The scattered electrons were detected by a Faraday cup connected to an electrometer that measured the electric current of the electrons.
- The experiment was initially performed to measure the dependence of the scattered electron current on the angle of incidence, but the results were inconclusive due to the oxidation of the nickel surface.
- The experiment was repeated after heating the nickel target to remove the oxide layer, and the results showed a surprising peak in the scattered electron current at a certain angle, indicating a maximum constructive interference of the reflected waves.
- The wavelength of the electrons was calculated from the angle of the peak and the interatomic spacing of the nickel crystal, and the result was found to be in agreement with the de Broglie equation for the wave nature of matter.
- The experiment was later repeated with different electron energies and different target materials, and the results confirmed the validity of the de Broglie relation and the wave nature of electrons.
- The experiment was one of the first direct evidences of quantum mechanics and the wave–particle duality of matter.



### Phase velocity and group velocity

- Phase velocity and group velocity are two important and related concepts in wave mechanics. They arise in quantum mechanics in the time development of the state function for the continuous case, i.e. wave packets.
- The phase velocity of a wave is the rate at which the wave propagates in any medium. This is the velocity at which the phase of any one frequency component of the wave travels.
- The group velocity of a wave is the velocity with which the overall envelope shape of the wave's amplitudes—known as the modulation or envelope of the wave—propagates through space.
- Phase velocity and group velocity are related through Rayleigh's formula:

$$v_g = v_p + \lambda \frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$$

- If the derivative term is zero, group velocity equals phase velocity. In this case, there is no dispersion.
- In quantum mechanics, the phase velocity and the group velocity have different interpretations for the associated particle. The phase velocity is related to the total energy of the particle, while the group velocity is related to the kinetic energy of the particle.
- Using the de Broglie postulates, one can show that the group velocity of a free particle is twice the phase velocity:

$$\lambda = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{p}$$
$$v_p = \frac{\omega}{\lambda} = \frac{E}{2\pi\hbar} \cdot \frac{p}{2\pi\hbar} = \frac{pE}{(2\pi\hbar)^2}$$
$$v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{d}{dk} \left( \frac{p^2}{2m} \right) = \frac{p}{m} = 2v_p$$



### Time-dependent and time-independent Schrodinger wave equations

- The Schrodinger wave equation is a partial differential equation that describes the quantum state of a physical system.
- The equation can be written in two forms: the time-dependent form and the time-independent form.
- The time-dependent form depicts a system that evolves over time and is thus dependent on the physical state of that system. The time-dependent form of Schrodinger's wave equation has the following expression:

$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial \psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t} = \hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r},t)
$$

where $i$ is the imaginary unit, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck's constant, $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ is the wave function of the system, $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator, $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector, and $t$ is the time.

- The time-independent form depicts a system that does not change over time and is thus independent of the physical state of that system. The time-independent form of Schrodinger's wave equation can be derived from the time-dependent form by using the separation of variables technique. The time-independent form of Schrodinger's wave equation has the following expression:

$$
\hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r}) = E \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

where $E$ is the energy eigenvalue of the system, and $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the eigenfunction of the Hamiltonian operator.

- The time-independent form is also known as the stationary state equation, because the solutions to this equation are standing waves that do not change over time.
- The time-dependent form is more general than the time-independent form, because it can describe both stationary and non-stationary states of the system. However, the time-independent form is simpler and more useful for many applications, because it can be solved analytically for some simple systems, such as the particle in a box, the harmonic oscillator, and the hydrogen atom.



### Physical interpretation of wave function

- A wave function is a mathematical function that describes the quantum state of a particle or a system of particles.
- A wave function is usually denoted by the symbol \(\psi\) (psi) and depends on the spatial coordinates and time of the particle or system.
- A wave function has no physical interpretation by itself, as it is not a measurable quantity. It is a complex function, meaning that it can have both real and imaginary parts.
- However, the square of the absolute value (or modulus) of the wave function, \(|\psi|^2\), has a physical interpretation. It represents the probability density of finding the particle or system in a certain location at a given instant.
- The probability density is normalized, meaning that the integral of \(|\psi|^2\) over all space and time is equal to one. This ensures that the total probability of finding the particle or system somewhere and sometime is 100%.
- The wave function also contains information about the momentum, energy, angular momentum, spin, and other physical properties of the particle or system. These properties can be obtained by applying certain operators to the wave function and finding the expectation values or the eigenvalues of the operators.
- The wave function evolves in time according to the Schrödinger equation, which is a partial differential equation that relates the wave function to the potential energy of the particle or system. The Schrödinger equation is one of the fundamental equations of quantum mechanics and describes how the quantum state changes over time.
- The wave function is subject to various interpretations and philosophical debates, as it implies some counterintuitive and paradoxical phenomena, such as the uncertainty principle, the superposition principle, the collapse of the wave function, and the entanglement of quantum states. Different interpretations of the wave function have different implications for the nature of reality, causality, determinism, and free will.



### Particle in a one-dimensional box

- A particle in a one-dimensional box is a fundamental quantum mechanical approximation describing the translational motion of a single particle confined inside an infinitely deep well from which it cannot escape  .
- The walls of a one-dimensional box may be seen as regions of space with an infinitely large potential energy.
- The particle can only exist inside the box, where the potential energy is zero, and its wavefunction must vanish at the boundaries of the box .
- The Schrödinger equation for the particle in a one-dimensional box is given by:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=E\psi$$

where $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $m$ is the mass of the particle, $x$ is the position coordinate, $E$ is the energy of the particle, and $\psi$ is the wavefunction of the particle .

- The general solution of the Schrödinger equation is:

$$\psi(x)=A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx)$$

where $A$ and $B$ are constants, and $k$ is the wave number given by:

$$k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}$$

- Applying the boundary conditions that $\psi(0)=0$ and $\psi(L)=0$, where $L$ is the length of the box, we obtain:

$$B=0$$

and

$$kL=n\pi$$

where $n$ is a positive integer .

- Therefore, the wavefunction of the particle in a one-dimensional box is:

$$\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

where $n=1,2,3,...$ is the quantum number .

- The energy of the particle in a one-dimensional box is:

$$E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$$

where $n=1,2,3,...$ is the quantum number .

- The energy levels of the particle in a one-dimensional box are discrete and quantized, and depend on the quantum number $n$ and the size of the box $L$ .
- The particle in a one-dimensional box model can be used to explain some phenomena such as the emission spectra of hydrogen atoms, the electronic structure of conjugated molecules, and the band structure of solids .



## Unit 2 - Electromagnetic Field Theory

- Electromagnetic field theory is the study of the physical phenomena associated with electric and magnetic fields, and their interactions with matter and radiation.
- Electric fields are produced by electric charges, and magnetic fields are produced by electric currents and changing electric fields.
- Electric and magnetic fields can exert forces on charged particles, and can also induce electric currents and voltages in conductors and dielectrics.
- Electric and magnetic fields can also propagate through space as electromagnetic waves, which carry energy and momentum, and can interact with matter and other electromagnetic waves.
- The fundamental equations that describe the behavior of electric and magnetic fields are Maxwell's equations, which are a set of four partial differential equations that relate the electric and magnetic fields to their sources and to each other.
- Maxwell's equations can be written in different forms, such as integral form, differential form, or vector form, depending on the choice of coordinate system and notation.
- Maxwell's equations can also be derived from the principle of least action, which states that the action of a physical system is minimized when the system follows the equations of motion.
- Maxwell's equations can be solved for various boundary conditions and sources, using analytical or numerical methods, to obtain the electric and magnetic fields in different regions of space and time.
- Some of the important concepts and applications of electromagnetic field theory are:

  - Coulomb's law, which gives the force between two point charges in vacuum.
  - Gauss's law, which relates the electric flux through a closed surface to the net charge enclosed by the surface.
  - Electric potential, which is the work done per unit charge to move a charge from a reference point to a given point in an electric field.
  - Capacitance, which is the ratio of the charge stored on a conductor to the potential difference across it.
  - Electric dipole, which is a pair of equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance, and produces an electric field that decreases as the inverse cube of the distance from the dipole.
  - Electric polarization, which is the alignment of electric dipoles in a dielectric material when subjected to an external electric field.
  - Dielectric constant, which is the ratio of the electric permittivity of a material to the electric permittivity of vacuum, and measures the ability of a material to store electric energy.
  - Biot-Savart law, which gives the magnetic field due to a steady current element in vacuum.
  - Ampere's law, which relates the magnetic flux through a closed loop to the net current passing through the loop.
  - Magnetic force, which is the force exerted by a magnetic field on a moving charge or a current-carrying conductor.
  - Magnetic dipole, which is a loop of current or a magnet, and produces a magnetic field that decreases as the inverse cube of the distance from the dipole.
  - Magnetic moment, which is a measure of the strength and orientation of a magnetic dipole.
  - Magnetic induction, which is the phenomenon of inducing an electric current or voltage in a conductor or a loop by changing the magnetic flux through it.
  - Inductance, which is the ratio of the magnetic flux linked by a loop to the current flowing through it.
  - Magnetic permeability, which is the ratio of the magnetic induction to the magnetic field in a material, and measures the ability of a material to support magnetic fields.
  - Faraday's law, which states that the electromotive force induced in a loop is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop.
  - Lenz's law, which states that the direction of the induced current or voltage is such that it opposes the change that caused it.
  - Electromagnetic induction, which is the phenomenon of inducing an electric field in a region of space by changing the magnetic field in that region.
  - Displacement current, which is a term added to Ampere's law to account for the changing electric flux in a capacitor or a dielectric.
  - Maxwell's correction, which is the modification of Ampere's law to include the displacement current, and makes the law consistent with the conservation of charge and the symmetry of electric and magnetic fields.
  - Electromagnetic wave, which is a self-sustaining disturbance of electric and magnetic fields that propagates through vacuum or a medium with a finite speed.
  - Wave equation, which is a second-order partial differential equation that describes the propagation of electromagnetic waves in a homogeneous and isotropic medium.
  - Poynting vector, which is a vector that gives the direction and magnitude of the energy flux carried by an electromagnetic wave.
  - Polarization, which is the orientation of the electric field vector of an electromagnetic wave in a plane



### Basic concept of Stoke’s theorem and Divergence theorem

- Stoke's theorem and Divergence theorem are two important theorems in vector calculus that relate different types of integrals over different types of regions.
- Stoke's theorem states that the line integral of a vector field around a closed curve is equal to the surface integral of the curl of that vector field over any surface bounded by that curve.
- Divergence theorem states that the surface integral of a vector field over a closed surface is equal to the volume integral of the divergence of that vector field over any volume enclosed by that surface.
- Both theorems are generalizations of the fundamental theorem of calculus, which states that the integral of a function over an interval is equal to the difference of the values of its antiderivative at the endpoints of the interval.
- Both theorems can be used to simplify the calculation of integrals by transforming them into simpler forms, or to prove other results in mathematics and physics.
- Both theorems require certain conditions to be satisfied, such as the smoothness and orientation of the curves, surfaces and volumes involved, and the existence and continuity of the vector fields and their derivatives .



### Basic laws of electricity and magnetism

Electricity and magnetism are two aspects of the same phenomenon: electromagnetism. Electric charges and magnetic fields interact with each other in various ways, and these interactions are governed by some basic laws. Here are some of the important laws of electricity and magnetism:

- **Coulomb's law**: This law states that the electric force between two point charges is proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force is along the line joining the charges and has the same magnitude for both charges. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs and repulsive if they have the same sign. The mathematical expression of Coulomb's law is:

  $$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

  where $F$ is the force, $k$ is a constant, $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charges, and $r$ is the distance between them.

- **Gauss's law**: This law states that the electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the net charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space. The electric flux is a measure of the number of electric field lines passing through the surface. The mathematical expression of Gauss's law is:

  $$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$

  where $\Phi_E$ is the electric flux, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, $d\vec{A}$ is a differential area element of the surface, $Q$ is the net charge enclosed, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.

- **Biot-Savart law**: This law states that the magnetic field produced by a current-carrying element is proportional to the current and the length of the element, and inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the element. The direction of the magnetic field is given by the right-hand rule: if the thumb of the right hand points in the direction of the current, the fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field. The mathematical expression of the Biot-Savart law is:

  $$d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2}$$

  where $d\vec{B}$ is the differential magnetic field, $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space, $I$ is the current, $d\vec{l}$ is the differential length element of the current, $\hat{r}$ is the unit vector pointing from the element to the point of observation, and $r$ is the distance from the element.

- **Ampere's law**: This law states that the magnetic flux through any closed loop is equal to the product of the permeability of free space and the net current enclosed by the loop. The magnetic flux is a measure of the number of magnetic field lines passing through the loop. The mathematical expression of Ampere's law is:

  $$\oint_L \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I$$

  where $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field, $d\vec{l}$ is a differential length element of the loop, and $I$ is the net current enclosed.

- **Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction**: This law states that the electromotive force (emf) induced in a loop of wire is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop. The emf is the potential difference that drives the current in the loop. The direction of the induced current is given by Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change in the magnetic flux that causes it. The mathematical expression of Faraday's law is:

  $$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

  where $\mathcal{E}$ is the emf, and $\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux.

### Continuity equation for current density

- Current density is a measure of the amount of electric current flowing through a unit area of a conductor. It is denoted by the symbol J and has the unit of ampere per square meter (A/m^2^) .
- The formula for current density is given as, J = I / A, where I is the current flowing through the conductor in amperes and A is the cross-sectional area of the conductor in square meters .
- The continuity equation for current density is a mathematical expression that relates the change in charge density (ρ) to the divergence of current density (J) in a given region of space. It is derived from the conservation of electric charge principle, which states that the net charge in any closed system remains constant .
- The differential form of the continuity equation is: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇ ⋅ J = 0, where t is the time and ∇ is the del operator . This equation implies that the rate of decrease of charge density in a volume is equal to the net outward flux of current density through the surface enclosing the volume .
- The integral form of the continuity equation is: d/dt ∫_V_ ρ d^3^r = - ∮_S_ J ⋅ n d^2^r, where V is an arbitrary stationary volume limited by the closed surface S, and n is the unit normal vector to the surface . This equation implies that the rate of change of total charge in a volume is equal to the net current leaving the volume through the surface .



### Displacement current

- Displacement current is a phenomenon in electromagnetism that is analogous to an ordinary electric current, but occurs when the electric field changes with time .
- Displacement current is defined as the product of the electric permittivity of the medium and the rate of change of the electric displacement field.
- Displacement current density is given by the equation:

$$\vec{J}_d = \epsilon \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t}$$

where $\vec{J}_d$ is the displacement current density, $\epsilon$ is the electric permittivity, and $\vec{E}$ is the electric field.

- Displacement current was introduced by James Clerk Maxwell in his famous set of equations that describe the electromagnetic field .
- Maxwell added the displacement current term to Ampere's circuital law, which relates the magnetic field to the electric current, to make it consistent with the principle of charge conservation .
- The modified Ampere's law is:

$$\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 \left( I + \epsilon \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \right)$$

where $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field, $d\vec{l}$ is an infinitesimal element of a closed loop, $\mu_0$ is the magnetic permeability of free space, $I$ is the conduction current, $\Phi_E$ is the electric flux, and $d\Phi_E/dt$ is the displacement current  .

- Displacement current shows that a changing electric field can produce a magnetic field, and vice versa, which leads to the concept of electromagnetic waves  .
- Displacement current is important for the propagation of electromagnetic waves in free space and in dielectric materials, as well as for the operation of devices such as capacitors and antennas  .



### Maxwell equations in integral and differential form

Maxwell equations are a set of four equations that describe the relationship between electric and magnetic fields, charges and currents. They are the foundation of electromagnetic theory and have many applications in physics and engineering.

The integral form of Maxwell equations can be used to make statements about a region of charge or current, by integrating the fields over a closed surface or a closed loop. The differential form of Maxwell equations can be used to make statements about individual points in space, by using the differential operators div and curl.

The integral and differential forms of Maxwell equations are equivalent, and can be derived from each other using the Gauss's divergence theorem and the Stokes' theorem.

The integral form of Maxwell equations are:

### Maxwell equations in vacuum and in conducting medium

- Maxwell equations are a set of four partial differential equations that describe the relationship between electric and magnetic fields in space and time.
- They are derived from the conservation laws of electric charge and magnetic flux, as well as the empirical laws of Gauss, Faraday, and Ampere.
- Maxwell equations can be written in two forms: differential form and integral form. The differential form uses the operators of vector calculus, such as divergence, curl, and gradient, to express the equations in terms of local field quantities. The integral form uses the theorems of Gauss, Stokes, and Green to express the equations in terms of fluxes and circulations of fields over surfaces and loops.
- In a vacuum, where there is no charge or current density, Maxwell equations can be written as:

  - Gauss's law for electricity: $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0$$
  - Gauss's law for magnetism: $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$$
  - Faraday's law of induction: $$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$$
  - Ampere's law with Maxwell's correction: $$\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$$

  where $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field, $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field, $\mu_0$ is the magnetic permeability of vacuum, and $\epsilon_0$ is the electric permittivity of vacuum.

- In a conducting medium, where there is a charge density $\rho$ and a current density $\mathbf{J}$, Maxwell equations can be written as:

  - Gauss's law for electricity: $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$$
  - Gauss's law for magnetism: $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$$
  - Faraday's law of induction: $$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$$
  - Ampere's law with Maxwell's correction: $$\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \left( \mathbf{J} + \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} \right)$$

  where $\mathbf{J}$ is the current density, which is related to the electric field by Ohm's law: $$\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E}$$ where $\sigma$ is the conductivity of the medium.

- Maxwell equations can be used to derive the wave equation for electromagnetic waves, which shows that they propagate at the speed of light in vacuum, and at a lower speed in a medium depending on its permittivity and permeability.
- Maxwell equations can also be used to explain various phenomena such as reflection, refraction, polarization, interference, diffraction, and dispersion of electromagnetic waves.



### Poynting vector and Poynting theorem

- The Poynting vector, denoted by **S**, is a vector quantity that represents the directional energy flux (the energy transfer per unit area per unit time) of an electromagnetic field.
- The Poynting vector is defined as the cross product of the electric field vector **E** and the magnetic field vector **H** at any point in space, that is, **S = E x H** .
- The Poynting vector has the units of watts per square meter (W/m^2^) and the direction of **S** is the direction of energy propagation.
- The Poynting vector is used in conjunction with Poynting's theorem, which is the continuity equation expressing the conservation of electromagnetic energy.
- Poynting's theorem states that the net electromagnetic power flowing into a region of space may be either dissipated, or used to change the energy stored in electric and magnetic fields within that region.
- Mathematically, Poynting's theorem can be written as:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot S = - \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{J}$$

where **u** is the energy density of the electromagnetic field, **S** is the Poynting vector, and **J** is the current density.
- The left-hand side of the equation represents the rate of change of electromagnetic energy in a volume, while the right-hand side represents the rate of work done by the electromagnetic field on the charges.
- Poynting's theorem can be derived from Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force law, by taking the dot product of **E** with the Ampere-Maxwell equation and the dot product of **H** with the Faraday's law, and then subtracting the two results.
- Poynting's theorem can be used to calculate the power flow in electromagnetic fields, such as in antennas, transmission lines, waveguides, and optical fibers.
- Poynting's theorem can also be used to understand the momentum density and the radiation pressure of electromagnetic fields, by relating the Poynting vector to the electromagnetic stress-energy tensor.

### Skin depth

- Skin depth is a measure of how deep an electromagnetic wave can penetrate into a conducting material before it is significantly attenuated.
- Skin depth depends on the frequency of the wave, the electrical conductivity of the material, and the magnetic permeability of the material.
- Skin depth is given by the formula:

$$\delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f \mu \sigma}}$$

where $\delta$ is the skin depth, $f$ is the frequency of the wave, $\mu$ is the magnetic permeability of the material, and $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity of the material.
- Skin depth can also be defined as the distance over which the magnitude of the electric or magnetic field is reduced by a factor of $1/e \approx 0.368$. Since power is proportional to the square of the field magnitude, skin depth can also be interpreted as the distance at which the power in the wave is reduced by a factor of $(1/e)^2 \approx 0.135$.
- Skin depth is inversely proportional to the square root of the frequency, the conductivity, and the permeability of the material. This means that higher frequency waves, more conductive materials, and more permeable materials have smaller skin depths and are more quickly attenuated.
- Skin depth is an important concept in applications such as eddy current testing, induction heating, radio transmission, and electromagnetic shielding.



## Unit 3 - Wave Optics

- Wave optics, also called physical optics, is a branch of optics that studies the behavior of light when it exhibits wave characteristics, such as interference, diffraction, polarization, and other phenomena that cannot be explained by ray approximation of geometric optics  .
- Some of the important concepts and formulas in wave optics are:
  - Huygens' principle: Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets that spread out in all directions with the same speed as the wave. The new wavefront is the envelope of all the secondary wavelets .
  - Superposition principle: When two or more waves overlap in a medium, the resultant displacement at any point is the algebraic sum of the displacements due to each wave  .
  - Interference: The phenomenon of superposition of two or more coherent waves to produce a resultant wave of varying intensity  .
  - Young's double slit experiment: An experiment that demonstrates the interference of light waves from two coherent sources (slits) separated by a small distance. The interference pattern consists of alternate bright and dark fringes on a screen  .
  - Fringe width: The distance between two consecutive bright or dark fringes in the interference pattern. It is given by the formula: $$\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$ where $\beta$ is the fringe width, $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light, $D$ is the distance between the slits and the screen, and $d$ is the separation between the slits  .
  - Diffraction: The phenomenon of bending of light waves around the edges of an obstacle or an aperture  .
  - Single slit diffraction: The diffraction pattern produced by a single slit of width $a$ illuminated by a monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda$. The pattern consists of a central bright maximum and a series of secondary maxima and minima on either side. The angular position of the first minimum is given by the formula: $$\sin \theta = \frac{\lambda}{a}$$ where $\theta$ is the angle between the central maximum and the first minimum  .
  - Polarization: The phenomenon of restricting the vibration of light waves to a single plane  .
  - Malus' law: The law that states that the intensity of polarized light transmitted through a polarizer is proportional to the square of the cosine of the angle between the plane of polarization of the incident light and the transmission axis of the polarizer. The formula is: $$I = I_0 \cos^2 \theta$$ where $I$ is the transmitted intensity, $I_0$ is the incident intensity, and $\theta$ is the angle between the planes of polarization  .
  - Brewster's law: The law that states that the angle of incidence for which the reflected light is completely polarized is equal to the polarizing angle. The formula is: $$\tan \theta_p = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$ where $\theta_p$ is the polarizing angle, $n_1$ is the refractive index of the incident medium, and $n_2$ is the refractive index of the reflecting medium  .

### Interference in uniform and wedge shaped thin films

- Interference is the phenomenon of superposition of two or more waves of the same frequency and phase, resulting in a new wave pattern.
- Interference can occur in thin films of transparent materials, such as air, water, oil, soap, etc., when light is reflected and transmitted at the boundaries of the film.
- The interference pattern depends on the thickness of the film, the wavelength of light, and the refractive indices of the film and the surrounding media.
- A uniform thin film is one that has a constant thickness throughout. A wedge shaped thin film is one that has a varying thickness, such as a thin air gap between two glass plates.
- For a uniform thin film, the interference pattern consists of concentric bright and dark rings, called Newton's rings, when viewed from above. The radius of the rings depends on the thickness of the film and the angle of incidence of light.
- For a wedge shaped thin film, the interference pattern consists of straight bright and dark bands, parallel to the edge of the wedge, when viewed from above. The spacing of the bands depends on the angle of the wedge and the wavelength of light.
- The condition for constructive interference in a thin film is given by:

  $$2nt = (m + \frac{1}{2})\lambda$$

  where $n$ is the refractive index of the film, $t$ is the thickness of the film, $m$ is an integer, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light in the film.

- The condition for destructive interference in a thin film is given by:

  $$2nt = m\lambda$$

  where $n$, $t$, $m$, and $\lambda$ are the same as above.

- In some cases, a phase change of $\pi$ radians occurs when light is reflected from a medium with a higher refractive index than the incident medium. This phase change affects the interference condition and must be taken into account.
- The applications of thin film interference include anti-reflective coatings, optical filters, soap bubbles, oil slicks, butterfly wings, and many others.

### Introduction to diffraction

- Diffraction is the process by which a beam of light or other system of waves is spread out as a result of passing through a narrow aperture or across an edge, typically accompanied by interference between the wave forms produced  .
- Diffraction occurs when the size of the obstacle or the slit is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength of the incident wave.
- Diffraction is a manifestation of the wave nature of light and other electromagnetic radiation, as well as sound, X-rays, and gamma rays, and even very small moving particles such as atoms, neutrons, and electrons, which show wavelike properties .
- One consequence of diffraction is that sharp shadows are not produced by opaque objects. Instead, the edges of the shadows are fuzzy and fringed with bands of light and dark or colored regions .
- The degree of diffraction depends on the ratio of the wavelength to the size of the obstacle or the slit. The smaller the ratio, the less the diffraction. The larger the ratio, the more the diffraction.
- The pattern of diffraction can be analyzed using the principle of Huygens, which states that every point on a wavefront can be considered as a source of secondary wavelets that spread out in all directions with the same speed and frequency as the primary wave.
- The diffraction pattern is formed by the superposition of the secondary wavelets at the observation point. The regions where the wavelets are in phase (constructive interference) appear as bright spots, while the regions where the wavelets are out of phase (destructive interference) appear as dark spots.
- There are different types of diffraction, such as Fresnel diffraction and Fraunhofer diffraction, depending on the distance between the source, the obstacle or the slit, and the observation point. Fresnel diffraction occurs when the source and the observation point are at a finite distance from the obstacle or the slit, while Fraunhofer diffraction occurs when the source and the observation point are at an infinite distance from the obstacle or the slit.
- Diffraction is an important phenomenon in many fields of science and engineering, such as optics, acoustics, astronomy, crystallography, microscopy, spectroscopy, and quantum mechanics. Diffraction can be used to study the structure and properties of matter, to create images and holograms, to measure wavelengths and distances, and to manipulate light and other waves.

### Absent spectra

### Diffraction grating

- A diffraction grating is an optical component with a periodic structure that diffracts light into several beams travelling in different directions (i.e., different diffraction angles).
- A diffraction grating can be a glass plate or a mirror with a large number of equidistant parallel lines or grooves on its surface . It causes diffraction of transmitted or reflected light, ultraviolet radiation, or X-rays.

- A diffraction grating can be used to measure the wavelength of light, to analyze the spectral composition of light sources, to disperse light for spectroscopy, and to create optical effects in devices such as lasers and spectrometers.
- A diffraction grating can be classified into two types: transmission grating and reflection grating. A transmission grating has slits or openings that allow light to pass through, while a reflection grating has reflective surfaces that reflect light.

- A **grating spectrum** is the pattern of bright and dark lines or bands produced by a grating when it is illuminated by a monochromatic or polychromatic source of light.
- The **principle of grating** is based on the interference of light waves from the slits or grooves of the grating. The path difference between the waves from adjacent slits or grooves determines the constructive or destructive interference at a given angle of observation.
- The **grating equation** relates the wavelength of light, the grating spacing, the angle of incidence and the angle of diffraction for a grating. It is given by:

    `nλ = d(sin i + sin r)`

    where n is the order of diffraction, λ is the wavelength of light, d is the grating spacing, i is the angle of incidence and r is the angle of diffraction.

- The **grating spectrometer** is an instrument that uses a grating to measure the wavelengths or frequencies of light emitted or absorbed by a source. It consists of a collimator, a grating, a telescope and a scale.
- The **procedure of grating spectrometer** is as follows:

    - Adjust the collimator, the grating and the telescope to make them parallel and perpendicular to each other.
    - Illuminate the collimator slit with a monochromatic or polychromatic source of light and observe the grating spectrum through the telescope.
    - Measure the angles of diffraction for the different orders and wavelengths of the spectrum using the scale.
    - Apply the grating equation to calculate the wavelengths or frequencies of the spectrum.

- The **advantages of grating spectrometer** are:

    - It can produce a high-resolution spectrum with distinct color separations and sharp lines or bands.
    - It can disperse a wide range of wavelengths or frequencies with a large angular dispersion.
    - It can measure the wavelengths or frequencies of light with high accuracy and precision.

- The **disadvantages of grating spectrometer** are:

    - It requires a large amount of light to produce a bright spectrum.
    - It is affected by the quality and alignment of the grating and the optical components.
    - It is susceptible to stray light and background noise that may interfere with the spectrum.



### Dispersive Power

- Dispersive power is a measure of how much a material or a device can separate light of different wavelengths by refraction or diffraction.
- The higher the dispersive power, the more the material or device can split light into its constituent colors.
- One example of a device that has dispersive power is a prism. A prism can refract white light into a spectrum of colors, as shown in the diagram below.

- The dispersive power of a prism can be calculated using the formula given above, if the refractive indices and the angles of deviation are known. Alternatively, the dispersive power can be measured experimentally using a spectrometer or a spectroscope.
- The dispersive power of a prism is a dimensionless quantity, as it is a ratio of two angles. It has no units.
- The dispersive power of a prism is a positive quantity, as the angular dispersion is always greater than the mean deviation.
- The dispersive power of a prism is affected by the prism material, the prism angle, and the wavelength range of the incident light. Different materials have different dispersive powers, as they have different refractive indices for different wavelengths. The prism angle determines the amount of refraction that occurs at the prism surfaces. The wavelength range of the incident light determines the extent of dispersion that occurs inside the prism.
- The dispersive power of a prism is useful for studying the spectrum of light sources, such as stars, lamps, lasers, etc. It can also be used to measure the refractive indices of different materials for different wavelengths of light. However, the dispersive power of a prism also causes chromatic aberration, which is the distortion of images due to the different focal lengths of different colors of light. ### Resolving Power

- Resolving power is the ability of an optical instrument to separate two adjacent points or objects from each other from a considerable distance.
- Resolving power depends on the wavelength of the light used and the aperture of the instrument.
- The smaller the wavelength and the larger the aperture, the higher the resolving power.
- Resolving power is measured by the minimum angular separation between two points or objects that can be resolved by the instrument.
- Resolving power is the reciprocal of the angular separation: Resolving Power = 1/Δθ.
- Different optical instruments have different formulas for resolving power based on their design and function.
- For a telescope, the resolving power is given by: Resolving Power = d/1.22λ, where d is the diameter of the objective lens and λ is the wavelength of the light.
- For a microscope, the resolving power is given by: Resolving Power = 2nsinθ/λ, where n is the refractive index of the medium, θ is the half-angle of the cone of light entering the objective lens, and λ is the wavelength of the light.
- For a prism, the resolving power is given by: Resolving Power = λ/dλ, where λ is the wavelength of the spectral line and dλ is the difference between the wavelengths of two adjacent spectral lines.
- For a grating, the resolving power is given by: Resolving Power = Nm, where N is the number of lines per unit length of the grating and m is the order of the spectrum.
- Resolving power is important for applications such as astronomy, microscopy, spectroscopy, and photography .



### Rayleigh’s criterion of resolution

- Rayleigh's criterion of resolution is a measure of the ability of an optical system to distinguish two closely spaced point sources of light, such as stars or distant objects.
- According to Rayleigh's criterion, two point sources are just resolvable when the center of the diffraction pattern of one source coincides with the first minimum of the diffraction pattern of the other source. See Figure 1.
- Rayleigh's criterion can be applied to any aperture shape, but it is commonly used for circular apertures, such as lenses or telescopes. For circular apertures, the diffraction pattern is called an Airy disk, and the criterion for resolution is when the center of one Airy disk falls on the first minimum of the other Airy disk. - Rayleigh's criterion can be expressed mathematically as:

  - θ = 1.22 λ / D

- Rayleigh's criterion is based on the assumption that the human eye can distinguish two sources when the intensity at the midpoint between them falls to 73.5% of the maximum intensity. However, this is not always the case, and some sources may be resolved even when they are closer than the Rayleigh limit, or may not be resolved even when they are farther than the Rayleigh limit. Therefore, Rayleigh's criterion is only an approximate rule of thumb, and not a strict law of physics.

### Resolving power of grating

- Resolving power of grating is the ability of a grating to separate two spectral lines of very close wavelengths into distinct maxima.
- Resolving power of grating is measured by the ratio of the mean wavelength to the smallest difference in wavelengths that can be resolved, i.e. R.P. = λ/dλ .
- Resolving power of grating depends on the order of diffraction, the number of lines per unit length of the grating, and the width of the grating .
- Resolving power of grating can be derived by considering the condition for two wavelengths λ and λ + dλ to be just resolved in the same order of diffraction n .
- The condition is that the maxima of one wavelength should coincide with the minima of the other wavelength, i.e. n(λ + dλ) - nλ = d, where d is the grating element .
- Simplifying the above equation, we get dλ = λ/nN, where N is the number of lines illuminated by the incident light .
- Therefore, the resolving power of grating is R.P. = λ/dλ = nN .
- Resolving power of grating is maximum when the order of diffraction is maximum and the number of lines illuminated is maximum .
- Resolving power of grating is independent of the incident angle and the slit width .
- Resolving power of grating is used to measure the spectral resolution of a spectroscopic instrument, such as resolving the sodium doublet or the hydrogen and deuterium lines .



## Unit 4 - Fiber Optics & Laser

- Fiber optics are thin strands of glass or plastic that can transmit light signals over long distances with minimal loss or interference.
- Fiber optics have many applications in telecommunications, medicine, sensing, lighting, and imaging.
- The basic components of a fiber optic system are a transmitter, a fiber, and a receiver.
- The transmitter converts an electrical signal into a light signal, which is coupled into the fiber.
- The fiber guides the light signal along its length, with the help of total internal reflection and cladding.
- The receiver detects the light signal and converts it back into an electrical signal.
- The main advantages of fiber optics are high bandwidth, low attenuation, immunity to electromagnetic interference, security, and flexibility.
- The main disadvantages of fiber optics are high cost, fragility, difficulty of splicing, and need for specialized equipment and skills.
- The main types of fiber optics are single-mode, multimode, and plastic optical fiber (POF).
- Single-mode fibers have a small core diameter and can carry only one mode of light, which minimizes dispersion and allows for long-distance transmission.
- Multimode fibers have a larger core diameter and can carry multiple modes of light, which increases dispersion and limits the bandwidth and distance.
- POFs are made of plastic and have a very large core diameter, which makes them easy to handle and install, but also reduces the bandwidth and distance.
- The main parameters that characterize a fiber optic are numerical aperture (NA), acceptance angle, mode field diameter (MFD), cutoff wavelength, attenuation, dispersion, and bandwidth.
- NA is a measure of the light-gathering ability of a fiber, and it determines the maximum angle at which light can enter the fiber and still be guided.
- Acceptance angle is the maximum angle at which light can be launched into the fiber from the air and still be guided.
- MFD is the diameter of the region in the fiber core where most of the light power is confined, and it affects the coupling efficiency and splicing loss.
- Cutoff wavelength is the minimum wavelength at which a fiber can support only one mode of light, and it defines the boundary between single-mode and multimode operation.
- Attenuation is the loss of light power as it travels along the fiber, and it is caused by absorption, scattering, and bending.
- Dispersion is the spreading of light pulses as they travel along the fiber, and it is caused by modal dispersion, chromatic dispersion, and polarization mode dispersion.
- Bandwidth is the maximum data rate that can be transmitted over a fiber, and it is limited by attenuation and dispersion.
- The main sources of light for fiber optics are light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and lasers.
- LEDs are semiconductor devices that emit light when a current flows through them, and they have low cost, low power consumption, long lifetime, and wide spectral width.
- Lasers are devices that emit coherent and monochromatic light by stimulated emission of photons, and they have high power, high modulation speed, narrow spectral width, and high directionality.
- The main types of lasers are gas lasers, solid-state lasers, semiconductor lasers, and fiber lasers.
- Gas lasers use a gas mixture as the active medium, and they can produce high-power and continuous-wave output, but they are bulky, expensive, and inefficient.
- Solid-state lasers use a solid crystal or glass as the active medium, and they can produce high-power and pulsed output, but they require cooling and pumping systems.
- Semiconductor lasers use a p-n junction as the active medium, and they can produce low-power and modulated output, but they are sensitive to temperature and feedback.
- Fiber lasers use a doped fiber as the active medium, and they can produce high-power and tunable output, but they require optical isolators and couplers.



### Fibre Optics: Principle and construction of optical fiber

- Fibre optics is the science of transmitting data, voice, and images by the passage of light through thin, transparent fibers.
- Optical fibers are flexible, transparent fibers made by drawing glass or plastic to a diameter slightly thicker than that of a human hair.
- Optical fibers work on the principle of total internal reflection of light, which means that no part of the light signal gets refracted or transmitted to the second medium, but instead, all of the signal is retained inside the fiber .
- The basic structure of an optical fiber consists of three parts: the core, the cladding, and the protective layer .
  - The core is the innermost part of the fiber, where the light is transmitted. It has a higher refractive index than the cladding, which is the concentric layer of lower refractive index glass that surrounds the core. The difference in refractive index ensures that the light is totally internally reflected at the core-cladding interface .
  - The protective layer is the outermost part of the fiber, which provides mechanical strength and protection from environmental factors. It is usually made of plastic or polymer .
- There are two main types of optical fibers: single-mode and multi-mode.
  - Single-mode fibers have a very thin core, typically 8 to 10 micrometers in diameter, and can carry only one mode of light. They have lower attenuation and higher bandwidth than multi-mode fibers, and are used for long-distance and high-speed communication.
  - Multi-mode fibers have a thicker core, typically 50 to 100 micrometers in diameter, and can carry multiple modes of light. They have higher attenuation and lower bandwidth than single-mode fibers, and are used for short-distance and low-speed communication.
- Optical fibers have many advantages over conventional copper wires, such as:
  - Higher bandwidth and data transmission rate.
  - Lower attenuation and signal loss.
  - Immunity to electromagnetic interference and noise.
  - Smaller size and weight.
  - Higher security and reliability.
- Optical fibers have many applications in various fields, such as:
  - Telecommunications, such as telephone, internet, and cable TV.
  - Medicine, such as endoscopy, laser surgery, and biomedical sensing.
  - Industry, such as fiber-optic sensors, fiber lasers, and fiber-optic gyroscopes.
  - Military, such as secure communication, surveillance, and guidance systems.
  - Research, such as astronomy, spectroscopy, and quantum optics.

### Numerical aperture

- Numerical aperture (NA) is a measure of the ability of an optical fiber to collect or confine the incident light ray inside it .
- It is among the most basic property of optical fiber and shows the efficiency with which light is collected inside the fiber in order to get propagated.
- Numerical aperture is defined as the sine of half of the angle of the fiber's light acceptance cone .
- Mathematically, NA = sin θa, where θa is the acceptance angle, which is the maximum angle at which the incident light can enter the fiber and be transmitted along it .
- The numerical aperture depends on the refractive indices of the core and the cladding of the fiber, as well as the wavelength of the light .
- The higher the numerical aperture, the larger the acceptance angle and the more light can be coupled into the fiber .
- However, a higher numerical aperture also means a lower bandwidth and a higher modal dispersion, which can degrade the signal quality and limit the transmission distance .
- Therefore, there is a trade-off between the numerical aperture and the performance of the optical fiber .

### Step index and graded index fibers

- Step index and graded index fibers are two types of multimode fibers that are used for optical communication.
- Multimode fibers allow multiple modes or rays of light to propagate through the fiber core, which can cause modal dispersion and limit the bandwidth and distance of the transmission.
- Step index fiber has a core with a constant refractive index and a cladding with a lower refractive index. The core-cladding interface forms a sharp boundary or a step, hence the name step index fiber.
- Graded index fiber has a core with a varying refractive index that decreases gradually from the center to the edge of the core. The core-cladding interface forms a smooth curve, hence the name graded index fiber.
- The main differences between step index and graded index fibers are  :
  - Step index fiber can be either single mode or multimode, while graded index fiber is only multimode.
  - Step index fiber has higher modal dispersion than graded index fiber, because different modes travel at different angles and speeds in the core. Graded index fiber reduces modal dispersion by making the higher modes travel faster in the lower refractive index regions of the core.
  - Step index fiber has lower bandwidth and shorter distance than graded index fiber, because of the higher modal dispersion. Graded index fiber can achieve higher bandwidth and longer distance by minimizing modal dispersion.
  - Step index fiber is cheaper and easier to manufacture than graded index fiber, because of the simpler structure and materials. Graded index fiber requires more complex fabrication and doping techniques to create the varying refractive index profile.
  - Step index fiber is more suitable for low-speed and short-distance applications, such as local area networks (LANs) and sensors. Graded index fiber is more suitable for high-speed and long-distance applications, such as telecommunication and data transmission.
- Conclusion:
  - Step index fiber and graded index fiber are two types of multimode fibers that have different refractive index profiles, modal dispersion characteristics, bandwidth and distance capabilities, and cost and fabrication requirements.
  - Graded index fiber offers superior transmission quality, higher bandwidth, and longer distance than step index fiber, but it is more expensive and complex to produce.
  - In modern communication applications, graded index fiber is preferred over step index fiber, because of the higher demand for speed and distance. Step index fiber is still used for some low-speed and short-distance applications, where cost and simplicity are more important.



### Fiber optic communication principle

- Fiber optic communication is a method of transmitting information from one place to another by sending pulses of infrared light through an optical fiber.
- The light is a form of carrier wave that is modulated to carry information. The modulation can be done by varying the intensity, phase, frequency, or polarization of the light.
- The optical fiber is a thin, flexible, transparent strand of glass or plastic that acts as a waveguide for the light. It has a core, a cladding, and a protective coating.
- The core is the central region of the fiber where the light is confined and propagated. It has a higher refractive index than the cladding, which is the outer layer of the fiber that surrounds the core and reflects the light back into the core.
- The protective coating is a material that covers the cladding and protects the fiber from damage and environmental factors.
- The principle of fiber optic communication is based on total internal reflection, which is a phenomenon that occurs when light travels from a denser medium to a rarer medium and is reflected back at the interface if the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.
- The critical angle is the minimum angle of incidence for which total internal reflection occurs. It depends on the refractive indices of the two media.
- In an optical fiber, the core acts as the denser medium and the cladding acts as the rarer medium. The light rays that enter the core at an angle less than the acceptance angle, which is the maximum angle of incidence for which the light can be guided by the fiber, are totally internally reflected at the core-cladding interface and travel along the fiber.
- The acceptance angle depends on the numerical aperture of the fiber, which is a measure of its light-gathering ability. It is given by the formula NA = n0 sin θa, where n0 is the refractive index of the medium outside the fiber and θa is the acceptance angle.
- The light rays that enter the core at an angle greater than the acceptance angle are refracted out of the fiber and lost. This is called attenuation, which is the loss of optical power in the fiber due to various factors, such as absorption, scattering, bending, and dispersion.
- Absorption is the conversion of optical power into heat by the molecules in the fiber material. It depends on the wavelength of the light and the impurities in the fiber.
- Scattering is the deflection of light by the microscopic fluctuations in the density and refractive index of the fiber material. It also depends on the wavelength of the light and the temperature of the fiber.
- Bending is the deviation of light from its original path due to the curvature of the fiber. It can cause leakage of light from the core to the cladding or the surrounding medium. It depends on the radius of curvature and the mode of propagation of the light.
- Dispersion is the spreading of light pulses due to the different velocities of different wavelengths or modes of light in the fiber. It can cause distortion and interference of the signals. It depends on the type and length of the fiber and the bandwidth of the signals.
- The advantages of fiber optic communication over other forms of communication are:

  - Higher bandwidth and data rate, which means more information can be transmitted in a given time.
  - Lower attenuation and interference, which means less signal degradation and noise.
  - Smaller size and weight, which means less space and cost for installation and maintenance.
  - Higher security and reliability, which means less risk of hacking and damage.
  - Greater flexibility and compatibility, which means more applications and integration with other technologies.



### Attenuation

- Attenuation is the reduction in intensity of the light beam (or signal) with respect to distance travelled through a transmission medium .
- Attenuation is measured in decibels (dB) and is calculated by the formula: Attenuation (dB) = 10 x Log 10 (P in/P out) = 20xLog 10 (V in/V out) where P is the power and V is the voltage of the signal.
- Attenuation limits the distance in which the signal can travel through optical fiber and affects the performance and reliability of the fiber optic network.
- Attenuation can be classified into two types: intrinsic and extrinsic .
- Intrinsic attenuation is the inherent loss of light in the fiber due to the physical properties of the fiber material and the manufacturing process. It includes absorption, scattering and bending losses .
- Absorption is the conversion of light energy into heat due to the interaction of light with the atoms and molecules of the fiber material. It depends on the wavelength of the light and the impurities in the fiber .
- Scattering is the deflection of light from its original direction due to the microscopic variations in the density and refractive index of the fiber material. It also depends on the wavelength of the light and the temperature of the fiber .
- Bending is the loss of light due to the curvature of the fiber. It can be macrobending or microbending. Macrobending is the large-scale bending of the fiber due to improper installation or handling. Microbending is the small-scale bending of the fiber due to external stresses or defects in the fiber .
- Extrinsic attenuation is the loss of light due to the external factors that affect the fiber optic network. It includes splicing, connectors, couplers, splitters and other devices that join or divide the fiber .
- Splicing is the process of joining two fibers by melting or fusing them together. It can cause loss of light due to the mismatch in the core diameter, numerical aperture, mode field diameter or refractive index of the fibers .
- Connectors are the devices that connect the fiber to the source, receiver or other fibers. They can cause loss of light due to the misalignment, air gap, dirt, dust or damage of the connector surfaces .
- Couplers and splitters are the devices that divide the light from one fiber into two or more fibers or combine the light from two or more fibers into one fiber. They can cause loss of light due to the insertion loss, reflection loss, excess loss or crosstalk of the device .
- Attenuation can be minimized by choosing the appropriate fiber type, wavelength, source, receiver and network design. It can also be compensated by using amplifiers, repeaters or regenerators that boost or regenerate the signal along the fiber .



### Dispersion in Fiber Optics

- Dispersion is the spreading of light pulses when they travel through an optical fiber from one end to another.
- Dispersion causes distortion and limits the data rate of digital signals sent over fiber optic cable.
- Dispersion can be classified into three types: material dispersion, waveguide dispersion, and modal dispersion.
- Material dispersion is caused by a change in the refractive index of the fiber optic material with different wavelengths. The higher the index, the slower the light travels.
- Waveguide dispersion is due to the distribution of light between the core and the cladding of the fiber. The light in the core travels faster than the light in the cladding.
- Modal dispersion is due to the different paths that light can take through a multimode fiber. The light that travels along the axis of the fiber arrives sooner than the light that travels near the cladding.
- Dispersion can be reduced by using single-mode fibers, dispersion-shifted fibers, dispersion-compensating fibers, or dispersion-flattened fibers.

### Spontaneous and stimulated emission of radiation

- Spontaneous emission is the process in which an atom or molecule in an excited state decays to a lower energy state and emits a photon of energy without any external influence .
- Stimulated emission is the process in which an atom or molecule in an excited state is induced to emit a photon of energy by interacting with another photon of the same frequency and phase  .
- The properties of photons generated by spontaneous and stimulated emission are quite different  :
  - Spontaneous emission produces photons that are random in direction, phase, polarization, and frequency. The emitted radiation is incoherent and has a broad spectrum.
  - Stimulated emission produces photons that are identical to the incident photon in direction, phase, polarization, and frequency. The emitted radiation is coherent and has a narrow spectrum.
- Spontaneous emission is the dominant process in ordinary light sources, such as lamps and LEDs. Stimulated emission is the basis of laser operation, which requires a population inversion of atoms or molecules in the excited state.



### Population inversion

- Population inversion is a state of a system, for example a laser gain medium, where a higher-lying energy level is more strongly populated than a lower-lying level.
- Population inversion is an important concept for lasers optics, as it is essential for light amplification by stimulated emission .
- Light amplification by stimulated emission occurs when an incoming photon causes an excited atom to emit a photon of the same frequency and phase, resulting in a coherent beam of light.
- Population inversion cannot occur under conditions of thermal equilibrium, where the level populations are described by a Boltzmann distribution.
- Population inversion can be achieved by various methods, such as optical pumping, electrical pumping, chemical pumping, or nuclear pumping.
- Optical pumping is a method of creating population inversion by using a light source to excite atoms from a lower energy level to a higher one.
- Electrical pumping is a method of creating population inversion by using an electric current to excite atoms from a lower energy level to a higher one.
- Chemical pumping is a method of creating population inversion by using a chemical reaction to produce excited molecules or atoms.
- Nuclear pumping is a method of creating population inversion by using nuclear radiation to excite atoms from a lower energy level to a higher one.
- Population inversion can be achieved in different types of lasers, amplifiers, and superluminescent light sources, such as rare-earth-doped optical fibers, gas lasers, semiconductor lasers, dye lasers, etc  .
- Population inversion is a necessary condition for all lasers, regardless of their power or wavelength, because all lasers operate on the same basic principle of a chain reaction.
- Population inversion saturation is a phenomenon where the population inversion reaches a maximum value and cannot be increased further by increasing the pumping power.
- Population inversion saturation affects the performance of lasers, such as the transverse mode instability, the output power, the beam quality, etc.



### Einstein’s Coefficients

- Einstein’s coefficients are mathematical quantities that describe the probability of absorption or emission of a photon by an atom or molecule .
- There are three Einstein coefficients: A, B12 and B21 .
- The Einstein A coefficient is related to the rate of spontaneous emission of light, which is the process of an atom or molecule in an excited state emitting a photon and returning to a lower energy state without any external influence .
- The Einstein B12 coefficient is related to the rate of absorption of light, which is the process of an atom or molecule in a lower energy state absorbing a photon and jumping to a higher energy state .
- The Einstein B21 coefficient is related to the rate of stimulated emission of light, which is the process of an atom or molecule in an excited state emitting a photon and returning to a lower energy state when stimulated by another photon of the same frequency .
- The Einstein coefficients are fixed probabilities per time associated with each atom, and do not depend on the state of the gas of which the atoms are a part.
- The Einstein coefficients are related by the following equations  :

  - A21 = (8πhν3/c3)B21, where h is the Planck constant, ν is the frequency of the photon, and c is the speed of light.
  - B12 = B21, which implies that the probability of absorption and stimulated emission are equal for the same transition.
  - B12 = (g1/g2)B21, where g1 and g2 are the statistical weights of the lower and upper energy levels, respectively.

- The Einstein coefficients are important for understanding the processes of light-matter interaction, such as absorption, emission, and scattering of radiation. They are also useful for calculating the spectral line intensities and shapes of atoms and molecules.



### Principles of laser action

- Laser is an acronym for **Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation**.
- Laser action is the process of producing a coherent, monochromatic, and intense beam of light by stimulating the atoms or molecules of a medium to emit photons in a synchronized manner .
- The basic principles of laser action are  :
  - **Spontaneous emission**: This is the natural process of an excited atom or molecule returning to a lower energy level and emitting a photon of a random wavelength, phase, and direction.
  - **Stimulated emission**: This is the process of an excited atom or molecule being stimulated by an incoming photon of the same wavelength and phase as the transition between the energy levels, and emitting a second photon of the same wavelength, phase, and direction as the incoming photon. This results in the amplification of the photon flux.
  - **Stimulated absorption**: This is the process of an atom or molecule in a lower energy level absorbing an incoming photon of the same wavelength and phase as the transition between the energy levels, and jumping to a higher energy level. This results in the attenuation of the photon flux.
  - **Non-radiative decay**: This is the process of an excited atom or molecule losing energy by other means than emitting a photon, such as collisions, vibrations, or heat. This results in the reduction of the population of the excited state.
  - **Population inversion**: This is the condition in which the number of atoms or molecules in a higher energy level is greater than the number of atoms or molecules in a lower energy level. This is necessary for achieving net gain in the stimulated emission process, and hence laser action.
  - **Amplification of light**: This is the process of increasing the intensity of the light beam by passing it through a medium with population inversion. The medium is called the **gain medium** or the **active medium**, and the device that provides the amplification is called the **laser cavity** or the **optical resonator**. The laser cavity consists of two mirrors, one of which is partially transparent, that reflect the light back and forth through the gain medium, allowing multiple passes of stimulated emission. The partially transparent mirror allows some of the amplified light to escape as the **laser output**.
- The methods of pumping action are the techniques of creating population inversion in the gain medium by supplying energy to the atoms or molecules. The energy can be supplied in various forms, such as electrical current, optical radiation, chemical reaction, or nuclear reaction. Some examples of pumping methods are:
  - **Optical pumping**: This is the method of using a flash lamp or another laser to excite the atoms or molecules of the gain medium by absorbing photons of a suitable wavelength.
  - **Electrical pumping**: This is the method of using an electric current to excite the atoms or molecules of the gain medium by ionizing them or creating a plasma.
  - **Chemical pumping**: This is the method of using a chemical reaction to excite the atoms or molecules of the gain medium by releasing energy in the form of heat or photons.
  - **Nuclear pumping**: This is the method of using a nuclear reaction to excite the atoms or molecules of the gain medium by emitting particles or radiation.

### Superconductors: Temperature dependence of resistivity in superconducting materials

- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance below a certain critical temperature (Tc)  .
- The resistivity of materials depends on the temperature. The equation ρt = ρ0 [1 + α (T – T0) shows the relation between the temperature and the resistivity for any material  .
- In the equation, ρ0 is the resistivity at a standard temperature, ρt is the resistivity at t 0 C, T0 is the reference temperature and α is the temperature coefficient of resistivity  .
- For normal metals, the resistivity increases with increasing temperature, as the thermal vibrations of the atoms scatter the electrons more  .
- For superconductors, the resistivity drops to zero abruptly when the temperature is lowered below Tc, as the electrons form Cooper pairs that can move without resistance  .
- The value of Tc depends on the type and structure of the superconducting material. Most conventional superconductors have low Tc values, below 12 K, and are made of metals or alloys .
- Some unconventional superconductors have high Tc values, above 130 K, and are made of ceramics or organic compounds  .
- The mechanism of high-temperature superconductivity is not fully understood, but it is related to the strong interactions between the electrons and the lattice vibrations .



### Meissner effect

- The Meissner effect is the expulsion of a magnetic field from the interior of a material that is in the process of becoming a superconductor .
- A superconductor is a material that loses its resistance to the flow of electrical currents when cooled below a certain temperature, called the critical temperature .
- The Meissner effect was discovered by the German physicists W. Meissner and R. Ochsenfeld in 1933 .
- The Meissner effect is a property of all superconductors, and it distinguishes them from perfect conductors, which do not expel magnetic fields .
- The Meissner effect can be explained by the formation of supercurrents on the surface of the superconductor, which cancel out the applied magnetic field inside the material .
- The Meissner effect can be observed by placing a magnet above a superconductor that is cooled below its critical temperature. The magnet will levitate due to the repulsive force of the expelled magnetic field .
- The Meissner effect has applications in magnetic levitation, superconducting magnets, and quantum computing .



### Temperature dependence of critical field for superconductors

- The critical field is the maximum magnetic field that a superconductor can withstand without losing its superconductivity.
- The critical field depends on the temperature of the superconductor, and generally decreases as the temperature increases.
- The temperature dependence of the critical field can be described approximately by the empirical formula:

$$
B_c(T) = B_c(0) \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^2 \right]
$$

where $B_c(0)$ is the critical field at absolute zero temperature, and $T_c$ is the critical temperature of the superconductor.

- The critical field can also be divided into two components: the lower critical field $H_{c1}$ and the upper critical field $H_{c2}$.
- The lower critical field is the minimum magnetic field that can penetrate the superconductor and create vortices of supercurrents. Below this field, the superconductor behaves as a perfect diamagnet and expels all the magnetic flux (Meissner effect).
- The upper critical field is the maximum magnetic field that the superconductor can sustain without destroying the superconducting state. Above this field, the superconductor becomes a normal conductor.
- The lower and upper critical fields also depend on the temperature of the superconductor, and can be expressed by the following formulas:

$$
H_{c1}(T) = H_{c1}(0) \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^2 \right]
$$

$$
H_{c2}(T) = H_{c2}(0) \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right) \right]
$$

where $H_{c1}(0)$ and $H_{c2}(0)$ are the lower and upper critical fields at absolute zero temperature, respectively.

- The temperature dependence of the critical fields can vary for different types of superconductors, such as conventional (low-temperature) superconductors, high-temperature superconductors, and unconventional superconductors.
- For example, some high-temperature superconductors show an anomalous temperature dependence of the upper critical field, with a negative curvature at any temperature, which can be explained by the influence of localization effects in two-dimensional or quasi-two-dimensional systems .
- Some unconventional superconductors, such as heavy-fermion compounds, show a non-monotonic temperature dependence of the lower critical field, with a minimum at a certain temperature, which can be attributed to the presence of multiple superconducting bands or a spin-triplet component in the superconducting wavefunction.



### Persistent current in superconductors

- Persistent current is the electric current that flows indefinitely in a closed loop of superconducting material without any external power source.
- Persistent current is possible in superconductors because they have zero resistance and no energy dissipation .
- Persistent current can be induced in a superconducting ring by placing it in a magnetic field above its critical temperature, then cooling it below its critical temperature, and then removing the magnetic field .
- Persistent current can also be induced in a superconducting ring by applying an electric field to it and then disconnecting the electric field.
- Persistent current can create a magnetic field that opposes the change in the external magnetic field, according to Lenz's law.
- Persistent current can be used to make superconducting magnets, which have many applications in science and technology, such as MRI machines, particle accelerators, and maglev trains.



### Type I and Type II Superconductors

- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and perfect diamagnetism below a certain critical temperature.
- Superconductors can be classified into two types based on their response to an external magnetic field: type I and type II.
- Type I superconductors have only one critical magnetic field, Hc. When the applied magnetic field exceeds Hc, the superconductivity is destroyed and the material becomes normal.
- Type I superconductors are usually pure metals, such as mercury, lead, tin, aluminum, etc.
- Type I superconductors have a sharp transition from the superconducting state to the normal state, and they expel all the magnetic flux from their interior (Meissner effect).
- Type II superconductors have two critical magnetic fields, Hc1 and Hc2. When the applied magnetic field is between Hc1 and Hc2, the superconductivity is not completely destroyed, but the material allows some magnetic flux to penetrate into its interior (mixed state or vortex state).
- Type II superconductors are usually metal alloys or complex oxide ceramics, such as niobium, vanadium, technetium, yttrium barium copper oxide, etc.
- Type II superconductors have a gradual transition from the superconducting state to the normal state, and they can sustain much higher magnetic fields than type I superconductors.
- All high-temperature superconductors (those that have critical temperatures above 77 K) are type II superconductors.



### High temperature superconductors

- High temperature superconductors (HTS) are materials that behave as superconductors at temperatures above 77 K (−196.2 °C; −321.1 °F), the boiling point of liquid nitrogen.
- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and can expel magnetic fields when cooled below a critical temperature (Tc).
- HTS have higher Tc than conventional superconductors, which usually have Tc below 30 K (−243.2 °C; −405.7 °F).
- HTS have potential applications in power transmission, magnetic levitation, quantum computing, and other fields that require high efficiency and low loss of energy.
- The main class of HTS is copper oxides combined with other metals, especially the rare-earth barium copper oxides (REBCOs) such as yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO).
- The second class of HTS is the iron-based compounds, such as iron pnictides and iron chalcogenides.
- The mechanism of HTS is not fully understood, but it is believed to involve the formation of Cooper pairs, which are pairs of electrons that are bound together by a quantum phenomenon called superexchange .
- A recent experiment by Davis et al. (2021) claimed to have observed the superexchange process in a copper oxide superconductor using a scanning tunneling microscope.
- However, this claim has been met with skepticism and criticism by some experts, who argue that the experiment has flaws and does not provide conclusive evidence for the superexchange mechanism.
- The discovery of HTS remains one of the most important and challenging problems in condensed matter physics and materials science.

### Basics concept of Quantum Dots

- Quantum dots are **nanoscale particles** of semiconducting material that can be embedded in cells or organisms for various experimental purposes, such as labeling proteins.
- Quantum dots have **optical and electronic properties** that differ from those of larger particles as a result of **quantum mechanics** . They are a central topic in **nanotechnology** and **materials science**.
- Quantum dots can be **defined** by lithographically patterned gate electrodes, or by etching on two-dimensional electron gases in semiconductor heterostructures. They can also be **synthesized** by chemical methods, such as colloidal synthesis.
- Quantum dots show **quantum effects** because they are so little. This means that electrons inside the dot are **trapped** and can only occupy **defined energy levels**. The energy levels depend on the **size** and **shape** of the dot, as well as the **material** it is made of .
- Quantum dots can **emit** or **absorb** light of different wavelengths depending on their energy levels. ### Quantum wires and quantum wells

- Quantum wires and quantum wells are examples of **semiconductor nanostructures** that exhibit **quantum confinement** effects due to their reduced dimensions .
- Quantum confinement occurs when the size of a material is comparable to or smaller than the **de Broglie wavelength** of the charge carriers, such as electrons and holes.
- Quantum confinement leads to the formation of **discrete energy levels** and changes in the **electronic, optical and transport properties** of the material .
- Quantum wells are **two-dimensional** structures, where the charge carriers are confined in one direction, usually the growth direction, and free to move in the other two directions  .
- Quantum wells are typically formed by sandwiching a thin layer of a **lower bandgap** semiconductor between two layers of a **higher bandgap** semiconductor, creating a **potential well** for the charge carriers .
- Quantum wells can be used to make devices such as **lasers, modulators, detectors, solar cells** and **transistors** .
- Quantum wires are **one-dimensional** structures, where the charge carriers are confined in two directions and free to move in only one direction .
- Quantum wires can be fabricated by various methods, such as **etching, growth, deposition** and **self-assembly** .
- Quantum wires have higher **density of states** and lower **threshold current** than quantum wells, making them suitable for **high-speed and low-power** applications .
- Quantum wires can be used to make devices such as **nanosensors, nanoelectronics, nanophotonics** and **quantum computing** .

- Nanomedicine: Nanomaterials can be used to diagnose, treat, and prevent diseases, as well as to enhance the performance and delivery of drugs, vaccines, and implants. They can also be used to engineer tissues, organs, and systems, as well as to monitor and regulate biological functions.
- Nanotechnology: Nanomaterials can be used to create novel devices, systems, and structures, as well as to manipulate and control matter at the nanoscale. They can also be used to integrate and interface with other technologies, such as biotechnology, information technology, and cognitive science.
- Nanomaterials science: Nanomaterials can be used to study and understand the fundamental properties and phenomena of matter at the nanoscale, as well as to discover and design new materials with desired functions and features.
