

## Unit 1 - Quantum Mechanics

- Quantum mechanics is a subfield of physics that describes the behavior of particles — atoms, electrons, photons and almost everything in the molecular and submolecular realm.
- Quantum mechanics is a fundamental theory in physics that provides a description of the physical properties of nature at the scale of atoms and subatomic particles.
- Quantum mechanics attempts to describe and account for the properties of molecules and atoms and their constituents— electrons, protons, neutrons, and other more esoteric particles such as quarks and gluons.
- Quantum mechanics is, at least at first glance and at least in part, a mathematical machine for predicting the behaviors of microscopic particles — or, at least, of the measuring instruments we use to explore those behaviors.
- Quantum mechanics is based on a set of postulates, principles, and mathematical formalisms that allow us to calculate the probabilities of various outcomes of physical measurements.
- Quantum mechanics reveals that the physical world is not deterministic, but probabilistic, and that some physical quantities are complementary, meaning that they cannot be measured simultaneously with arbitrary precision.
- Quantum mechanics also shows that the nature of reality is not local, but nonlocal, meaning that distant events can influence each other instantaneously, without any physical connection or signal.
- Quantum mechanics has many applications in various fields of science and technology, such as quantum chemistry, quantum field theory, quantum technology, and quantum information science.
- Quantum mechanics also poses many conceptual and philosophical challenges, such as the interpretation of the wave function, the measurement problem, the uncertainty principle, the Schrödinger's cat paradox, and the quantum entanglement phenomenon.



### Inadequacy of classical mechanics

Classical mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of macroscopic objects under the influence of forces. It is based on Newton's laws of motion and the principle of conservation of energy and momentum. Classical mechanics can explain correctly the motion of celestial bodies, such as planets and stars, and microscopic terrestrial bodies, such as bullets and balls, that move with non-relativistic speeds (much less than the speed of light).

However, classical mechanics fails to account for certain phenomena observed in nature, especially at the atomic and subatomic scales. Some of the limitations of classical mechanics are:

- It cannot explain the stability of atoms. According to classical mechanics, an electron orbiting a nucleus should emit electromagnetic radiation and lose energy, eventually falling into the nucleus. This would make atoms unstable and collapse. However, atoms are observed to be stable and emit radiation only when they undergo transitions between discrete energy levels.
- It cannot explain the origin of the discrete spectra of atoms. According to classical mechanics, an atom should emit a continuous spectrum of radiation, as the electron can have any energy value. However, atoms are observed to emit or absorb radiation only at certain specific frequencies, corresponding to the difference between the energy levels of the electron. This implies that the electron can have only certain discrete energy values, which are called quantum numbers.
- It cannot explain the observed spectra of black body radiation. A black body is an idealized object that absorbs and emits all frequencies of electromagnetic radiation. According to classical mechanics, the energy distribution of the emitted radiation should depend only on the temperature of the black body, and increase continuously with the frequency. However, the experimental results show that the energy distribution has a peak at a certain frequency, and decreases rapidly at higher frequencies. This is known as the ultraviolet catastrophe, as classical mechanics predicts an infinite amount of energy at the ultraviolet region of the spectrum.
- It cannot explain the variation of the specific heat of metals and gases. The specific heat is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a unit mass of a substance by one degree. According to classical mechanics, the specific heat of a metal should be constant, as it depends only on the number of atoms and their degrees of freedom. However, the experimental results show that the specific heat of a metal decreases as the temperature approaches zero. Similarly, the specific heat of a gas should depend only on the number of molecules and their degrees of freedom, but the experimental results show that the specific heat of a gas varies with the temperature and the pressure.
- It cannot explain the photoelectric effect. The photoelectric effect is the phenomenon of emission of electrons from a metal surface when it is exposed to light. According to classical mechanics, the energy of the emitted electrons should depend on the intensity of the light, and the emission should occur for any frequency of the light. However, the experimental results show that the energy of the emitted electrons depends on the frequency of the light, and the emission occurs only when the frequency of the light exceeds a certain threshold value, called the work function of the metal.

These phenomena indicate that classical mechanics is inadequate to describe the behavior of matter and radiation at the microscopic level, and that a new theory is needed to explain them. This new theory is called quantum mechanics, which is based on the assumption that matter and radiation have both particle and wave properties, and that energy and momentum are quantized, meaning that they can have only discrete values. Quantum mechanics can explain all the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot, and also reduces to classical mechanics in the limit of large objects and low energies. Quantum mechanics is the foundation of modern physics, and has many applications in various fields, such as chemistry, biology, nanotechnology, and information technology.

### Compton effect

- The Compton effect is the phenomenon of **elastic scattering** of photons by electrons, resulting in a **change in the wavelength** of the photons .
- The Compton effect was discovered by **Arthur Compton** in 1923, who measured the scattering of X-rays by graphite and observed that the scattered X-rays had a longer wavelength than the incident ones .
- The Compton effect can be explained by the **particle nature** of light, where photons collide with electrons and transfer some of their energy and momentum to them .
- The Compton effect can be described by the following formula, known as the **Compton formula** :

    $$\lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_e c}(1 - \cos \theta)$$

    where $\lambda$ and $\lambda'$ are the wavelengths of the incident and scattered photons, respectively, $h$ is the Planck constant, $m_e$ is the electron mass, $c$ is the speed of light, and $\theta$ is the angle of scattering.

- The Compton effect shows that light has both **wave and particle** properties, and supports the **quantum theory** of electromagnetic radiation .
- The Compton effect has applications in **gamma spectroscopy**, where it produces a characteristic feature called the **Compton edge**, and in **astrophysics**, where it explains the origin of some cosmic rays and the **cosmic microwave background** radiation.



### de-Broglie concept of matter waves

- According to the de-Broglie concept of matter waves, the matter has **dual nature**. It means when the matter is moving it shows the **wave properties** (like interference, diffraction etc.) and when it is in the state of rest then it shows **particle properties**.
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves was inspired by the **photon theory** of light, which established that an electromagnetic wave can behave like a particle of light (photon) when interacting with matter .
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves was also supported by the **quantum theory** of the atom, which postulated that electrons orbit the nucleus as **standing waves** (stationary waves). Standing waves are formed as a result of interference between two waves of equal frequency and amplitude travelling in opposite directions .
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves can be expressed by the following equation, which relates the **wavelength** of matter to its **momentum** :

\[\lambda = \frac{h}{p}\]

where \(\lambda\) is the wavelength, \(h\) is the Planck's constant, and \(p\) is the momentum.

- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves has been experimentally verified by various phenomena, such as the **electron diffraction** by crystals, the **electron interference** in the double-slit experiment, and the **electron microscopy**.



### Davisson and Germer Experiment

- The Davisson and Germer experiment was a 1923-27 experiment by Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Western Electric (later Bell Labs), in which electrons, scattered by the surface of a crystal of nickel metal, displayed a diffraction pattern.
- The experiment confirmed the de Broglie hypothesis that matter has wave-like behavior and established the wave–particle duality hypothesis which was a fundamental step in quantum theory.
- The experiment was based on the assumption that waves reflected from two distinct atomic levels of a Ni crystal will have a fixed phase difference and will interfere either constructively or destructively, producing a diffraction pattern .
- The experiment consisted of a vacuum chamber with an electron gun that emitted a beam of electrons of known energy, a nickel target that could be heated to remove oxide layers, and a Faraday cup that could measure the current of electrons scattered at different angles.
- The experiment was initially designed to measure the dependence of the scattered electron current on the angle of incidence, but due to an accidental heating of the nickel target, the crystal structure of the target changed and the experimenters observed a peak in the current at a certain angle.
- The experimenters realized that the peak corresponded to the Bragg condition for constructive interference of X-rays, and that the wavelength of the electrons could be calculated from the de Broglie relation.
- The experimenters found that the wavelength of the electrons was about 1.65 angstroms, which matched the predicted value from the de Broglie equation for the given energy of the electrons.
- The experiment was the first direct evidence of the wave nature of electrons and validated the de Broglie equation for the first time .

### Phase velocity and group velocity

- Phase velocity is the rate at which the phase of a wave propagates in any medium. It is given by the ratio of the angular frequency to the wave number: `v_p = ω/k`.
- Group velocity is the velocity with which the overall envelope shape of the wave's amplitudes propagates through space. It is given by the derivative of the angular frequency with respect to the wave number: `v_g = dω/dk`.
- Phase velocity and group velocity are related by Rayleigh's formula: `v_p v_g = c^2`, where `c` is the speed of light in vacuum.
- If the wave is non-dispersive, meaning that the angular frequency is proportional to the wave number, then the phase velocity and the group velocity are equal and constant.
- If the wave is dispersive, meaning that the angular frequency depends on the wave number in a non-linear way, then the phase velocity and the group velocity are different and vary with the wave number.
- In quantum mechanics, a wave packet is a superposition of plane waves with different wave numbers and frequencies. The wave packet represents the state of a particle with a certain position and momentum uncertainty.
- The phase velocity of a wave packet is the velocity of the individual plane waves that make up the packet. It is related to the energy of the particle by the de Broglie relation: `E = ℏω = ℏv_p k`.
- The group velocity of a wave packet is the velocity of the envelope of the packet. It is related to the momentum of the particle by the de Broglie relation: `p = ℏk = ℏv_g dω/dk`.
- For a free particle, the angular frequency is proportional to the square of the wave number: `ω = ℏk^2/2m`, where `m` is the mass of the particle. Therefore, the phase velocity is `v_p = ℏk/2m` and the group velocity is `v_g = ℏk/m`.
- For a free particle, the group velocity is twice the phase velocity: `v_g = 2v_p`. This means that the envelope of the wave packet moves faster than the individual plane waves. The group velocity is also equal to the classical velocity of the particle: `v_g = p/m`.



### Time-dependent and time-independent Schrodinger wave equations

- The Schrodinger wave equation is a partial differential equation that describes the quantum state of a physical system.
- The equation can be written in two forms: the time-dependent form and the time-independent form.
- The time-dependent form depicts a system that evolves over time and is thus dependent on the physical state of that system. The time-dependent form of Schrodinger's wave equation has the following expression:

$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial \psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t} = \hat{H}\psi(\mathbf{r},t)
$$

- where $i$ is the imaginary unit, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ is the wave function of the system, and $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator, which represents the total energy of the system.
- The time-dependent form can be solved for any initial condition and any potential function, but it is often difficult to do so analytically.
- The time-independent form assumes that the system is in a stationary state, meaning that the wave function does not change with time, except for a phase factor. The time-independent form of Schrodinger's wave equation has the following expression:

$$
\hat{H}\psi(\mathbf{r}) = E\psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

- where $E$ is the energy eigenvalue of the stationary state, and $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the corresponding eigenfunction of the Hamiltonian operator.
- The time-independent form is an eigenvalue problem, which can be solved for discrete or continuous values of $E$ and $\psi(\mathbf{r})$, depending on the nature of the potential function.
- The time-independent form can be derived from the time-dependent form by using the separation of variables technique, which assumes that the wave function can be written as a product of a space-only function and a time-only function:

$$
\psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \psi(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\omega t}
$$

- where $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the wave. Plugging this ansatz in the time-dependent Schrodinger equation, we get the time-independent Schrodinger equation:

$$
\hat{H}\psi(\mathbf{r}) = \hbar\omega\psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

- where we identify $E = \hbar\omega$ as the energy eigenvalue of the stationary state.
- The time-independent form is simpler to solve than the time-dependent form, and it gives us the possible energy levels and wave functions of the system. However, it does not tell us how the system transitions from one state to another, or how it responds to external perturbations. For that, we need to use the time-dependent form.



### Physical interpretation of wave function

- A wave function is a mathematical function that describes the quantum state of a particle or a system of particles.
- The wave function, at a particular time, contains all the information that anybody at that time can have about the particle .
- But the wave function itself has no physical interpretation. It is not measurable . It is complex and can be expressed as \Psi(x,y,z,t) = a + ib, where a and b are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit.
- However, the square of the absolute value of the wave function has a physical interpretation. It gives the probability density of finding the particle in a certain location at a given instant  . It can be written as |\Psi(x,y,z,t)|^2 = a^2 + b^2.
- The probability density is normalized, meaning that the integral of |\Psi(x,y,z,t)|^2 over all space is equal to 1. This ensures that the particle must be somewhere in the universe.
- The wave function also satisfies the Schrodinger equation, which is a partial differential equation that governs the evolution of the quantum state over time.
- The wave function can be used to determine probabilities of various outcomes of measurements, such as position, momentum, energy, spin, etc .
- The wave function can also be used to calculate expectation values of physical quantities, which are the average values that would be obtained from repeated measurements .
- The wave function is not unique, meaning that there can be different wave functions that describe the same quantum state. These wave functions are related by a phase factor, which is a complex number of unit magnitude.
- The wave function is not observable, meaning that it cannot be directly measured or verified by experiments. It is only a theoretical construct that helps us understand and predict the behavior of quantum systems.

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\psi$$

$$\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$$

$$k = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}$$

$$B = 0$$

and

$$kL = n\pi$$

where $n$ is a positive integer.

where $n = 1, 2, 3, ...$

### Basic concept of Stoke’s theorem and Divergence theorem

- Stoke's theorem and Divergence theorem are two important theorems in vector calculus that relate different types of integrals over different types of regions.
- Stoke's theorem (also known as Kelvin-Stoke's theorem) states that the line integral of a vector field around a closed curve is equal to the surface integral of the curl of the vector field over any surface bounded by the curve.
- Mathematically, Stoke's theorem can be expressed as:

$$\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

- Where $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field, $\partial S$ is the boundary curve of the surface $S$, and $d\mathbf{r}$ and $d\mathbf{S}$ are the differential elements of the curve and the surface respectively.
- Stoke's theorem can be seen as a generalization of the fundamental theorem of calculus and Green's theorem to three dimensions.
- Stoke's theorem can be used to simplify the calculation of line integrals when the curl of the vector field is easier to evaluate than the vector field itself.
- Stoke's theorem can also be used to prove some important results in physics, such as the conservation of angular momentum, the Maxwell-Faraday equation, and the Helmholtz decomposition theorem.

- Divergence theorem (also known as Gauss's theorem or Ostrogradsky's theorem) states that the surface integral of a vector field over a closed surface is equal to the volume integral of the divergence of the vector field over the region enclosed by the surface.
- Mathematically, Divergence theorem can be expressed as:

$$\oiint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) dV$$

### Basic laws of electricity and magnetism

Electricity and magnetism are two aspects of the same phenomenon: electromagnetism. Electric charges and magnetic fields interact with each other in various ways, and these interactions are described by four basic laws:

- **Coulomb's law**: This law states that the electric force between two point charges is proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs and repulsive if they have the same sign. The mathematical expression of Coulomb's law is:

$$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

where $F$ is the force, $k$ is a constant, $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charges, and $r$ is the distance.

- **Gauss's law**: This law states that the electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the net charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space. The electric flux is a measure of the number of electric field lines passing through the surface. The mathematical expression of Gauss's law is:

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$

where $\Phi_E$ is the electric flux, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, $d\vec{A}$ is a differential area element, $Q$ is the net charge, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.

- **Ampere's law**: This law states that the magnetic flux through any closed loop is equal to the product of the permeability of free space and the net current passing through the loop. The magnetic flux is a measure of the number of magnetic field lines passing through the loop. The mathematical expression of Ampere's law is:

$$\Phi_B = \oint_L \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I$$

where $\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux, $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field, $d\vec{l}$ is a differential length element, $I$ is the net current, and $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space.

- **Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction**: This law states that the electromotive force (emf) induced in a loop of wire is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop. The emf is the potential difference that drives the current in the loop. The mathematical expression of Faraday's law is:

$$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

where $\mathcal{E}$ is the emf, and $t$ is the time.

These four laws can be combined into a set of differential equations known as **Maxwell's equations**, which describe the behavior of electric and magnetic fields in any situation. Maxwell's equations also predict the existence of **electromagnetic waves**, which are oscillating electric and magnetic fields that propagate through space at the speed of light. Examples of electromagnetic waves are light, radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays.



### Continuity equation for current density

- Current density is a measure of the amount of electric current flowing through a unit area of a conductor. It is denoted by the symbol J and has the unit of ampere per square meter (A/m^2^) .
- The formula for current density is given as, J = I / A, where I is the current flowing through the conductor in amperes and A is the cross-sectional area of the conductor in square meters .
- The continuity equation for current density is a mathematical expression that relates the change in charge density (ρ) to the divergence of current density (J) in a given region of space. It is derived from the conservation of electric charge principle, which states that the net charge in any closed system remains constant .
- The differential form of the continuity equation is: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇ ⋅ J = 0, where t is the time and ∇ is the del operator . This equation implies that the rate of change of charge density at any point is equal to the negative of the net current density flowing out of that point.
- The integral form of the continuity equation is: d/dt ∫V ρ d^3^r = - ∮S J ⋅ n d^2^r, where V is an arbitrary stationary volume bounded by the closed surface S, and n is the unit normal vector to the surface . This equation implies that the rate of change of total charge in any volume is equal to the negative of the net current flowing out of the surface enclosing that volume.



### Displacement current

- Displacement current is a quantity appearing in Maxwell's equations that is defined in terms of the rate of change of the electric displacement field (D) .
- Displacement current density has the same units as electric current density, and it is a source of the magnetic field just as actual current is .
- Displacement current can be explained by the phenomenon observed in a capacitor. When a capacitor starts charging, there is no conduction of charge between the plates, but there is a changing electric field that induces a magnetic field .
- Displacement current is given by the formula:

`I_d = ε_0 dφ_E/dt`

where `I_d` is the displacement current, `ε_0` is the permittivity of free space, `φ_E` is the electric flux, and `t` is the time .

`∮ B ⋅ dl = μ_0 (I_c + I_d)`

where `B` is the magnetic field, `dl` is the differential length element, `μ_0` is the permeability of free space, and `I_c` is the conduction current .

- Displacement current is important for the consistency of Maxwell's equations and the existence of electromagnetic waves. Without displacement current, there would be no magnetic field associated with a changing electric field, and vice versa .

### Newton’s Rings and its applications

Newton's rings are a phenomenon in which an interference pattern is created by the reflection of light between two surfaces, typically a spherical surface and an adjacent touching flat surface. It is named after Isaac Newton, who investigated the effect in 1666.

The interference pattern consists of a series of concentric bright and dark rings, with a dark spot at the center. The radius of the nth bright ring is given by the formula:

r_n = \sqrt{n \lambda R}

where r_n is the radius of the nth bright ring, n is the order of the ring, \lambda is the wavelength of the light, and R is the radius of curvature of the spherical surface.

The interference pattern is formed due to the difference in the optical path length of the light rays reflected from the two surfaces. The optical path length depends on the thickness of the air film between the surfaces and the refractive index of the air. The thickness of the air film varies with the distance from the center, and hence the optical path difference also varies. When the optical path difference is an integral multiple of the wavelength, constructive interference occurs and a bright ring is seen. When the optical path difference is an odd multiple of half the wavelength, destructive interference occurs and a dark ring is seen.

Newton's rings have many practical applications in optics and engineering. Some of them are  :

- For testing the uniformity of a polished surface by studying the interference pattern the surface makes when placed in contact with a perfectly flat glass surface. Any deviation from a perfect circle in the rings indicates a defect or irregularity in the surface.
- For measuring the thickness of thin films, such as oil, soap, or paint, by observing the interference pattern they produce when illuminated by a monochromatic light source. The thickness of the film can be calculated from the radius of the rings and the wavelength of the light.
- For determining the shape of lenses and other curved surfaces by comparing the interference pattern they produce with a known standard. The radius of curvature of the surface can be obtained from the radius of the rings and the wavelength of the light.
- For measuring the refractive index of transparent materials by observing the change in the interference pattern when the material is inserted between the two surfaces. The refractive index of the material can be calculated from the change in the radius of the rings and the wavelength of the light.



### Introduction to diffraction

- Diffraction is the process by which a beam of light or other system of waves is spread out as a result of passing through a narrow aperture or across an edge, typically accompanied by interference between the wave forms produced  .
- Diffraction occurs when the size of the obstacle or the slit is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength of the incident wave.
- Diffraction is a manifestation of the wave nature of light and other electromagnetic radiation, as well as sound, X-rays, and gamma rays, and even very small moving particles such as atoms, neutrons, and electrons, which show wavelike properties .
- One consequence of diffraction is that sharp shadows are not produced by opaque objects. Instead, the edges of the shadows are fuzzy and fringed with bands of light and dark or colored regions .
- The degree of diffraction depends on the ratio of the wavelength to the size of the obstacle or the slit. The smaller the ratio, the less diffraction occurs. The larger the ratio, the more diffraction occurs.
- The pattern of diffraction can be analyzed using the principle of Huygens, which states that every point on a wavefront can be considered as a source of secondary wavelets that spread out in all directions. The superposition of these secondary wavelets determines the shape of the new wavefront.
- There are different types of diffraction, such as Fresnel diffraction and Fraunhofer diffraction, depending on the distance between the source, the obstacle or the slit, and the screen where the diffraction pattern is observed. Fresnel diffraction occurs when the source and the screen are at finite distances from the obstacle or the slit, while Fraunhofer diffraction occurs when the source and the screen are at infinite distances from the obstacle or the slit.
- Diffraction is an important phenomenon in many fields of science and engineering, such as optics, acoustics, astronomy, crystallography, microscopy, spectroscopy, and quantum mechanics. Diffraction can be used to study the structure and properties of matter, to create optical devices and instruments, and to manipulate and control light and other waves .



### Fraunhofer diffraction at single slit and double slit

- Fraunhofer diffraction is a type of diffraction that occurs when plane waves are incident on a diffracting object, and the diffraction pattern is viewed at a sufficiently long distance from the object (in the far-field region), or at the focal plane of a converging lens.
- Fraunhofer diffraction can be used to analyze the shape and size of the diffracting object, such as a slit, a grating, or an aperture.
- Fraunhofer diffraction at a single slit is the simplest case of diffraction, where a parallel beam of monochromatic light of wavelength `λ` is incident on a slit of width `a` that is of the order of the wavelength of light.
- According to Huygens' principle, every point of the wavefront in the plane of the slit acts as a secondary source and sends secondary waves in all directions.
- The secondary waves interfere with each other and form a diffraction pattern on a screen placed at a large distance from the slit, or at the focal plane of a lens.
- The diffraction pattern consists of a central bright maximum, called the principal maximum, and a series of alternate bright and dark fringes on either side, called the secondary maxima and minima, respectively.
- The intensity of the diffraction pattern at an angle `θ` from the central maximum is given by the formula:

    `I(θ) = I0 (sin(πa sinθ/λ) / (πa sinθ/λ))^2`

    where `I0` is the intensity at the central maximum, and `a` is the slit width.

- The minima of the diffraction pattern occur when the argument of the sine function is equal to an integer multiple of `π`, i.e.,:

    `a sinθ = nλ`

    where `n` is an integer.

- The angular width of the central maximum is given by the difference between the angles of the first minima on either side of the central maximum, i.e.,:

    `β = 2θ1 = 2 sin^-1 (λ/a)`

### Fibre Optics: Principle and construction of optical fiber

- Fibre optics is the science of transmitting data, voice, and images by the passage of light through thin, transparent fibers.
- Optical fibers are flexible, transparent fibers made by drawing glass or plastic to a diameter slightly thicker than that of a human hair.
- Optical fibers work on the principle of total internal reflection of light, which means that no part of the light signal gets refracted or transmitted to the second medium, but instead, all of the signal is retained inside the fiber .
- The condition for total internal reflection is that the light must travel from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium, and the angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle for the boundary.
- The construction of an optical fiber consists of three main parts :
  - The core, which is the innermost layer of the fiber, where the light is transmitted. The core has a high refractive index and a small diameter (typically 8 μm for single-mode fibers and 50-62.5 μm for multimode fibers).
  - The cladding, which is the outer layer of the fiber, surrounding the core. The cladding has a lower refractive index than the core and a larger diameter (typically 125 μm). The cladding acts as a boundary for the light and prevents it from escaping the core.
  - The protective coating, which is a layer of plastic or other material that covers the cladding and protects the fiber from damage and moisture. The coating has a diameter of about 250 μm and can be removed by stripping tools when the fiber is spliced or connected to other devices.
- The types of optical fibers are mainly classified based on the number of modes or paths that the light can take inside the core :
  - Single-mode fibers, which have a small core diameter and allow only one mode of light to propagate. These fibers have low attenuation and high bandwidth, and are used for long-distance communication and high-speed data transmission.
  - Multimode fibers, which have a larger core diameter and allow multiple modes of light to propagate. These fibers have higher attenuation and lower bandwidth, and are used for short-distance communication and low-speed data transmission.
  - Other types of fibers include plastic optical fibers, which have a plastic core and cladding, and are cheaper and easier to handle than glass fibers, but have lower performance and durability; and specialty fibers, which have special properties or functions, such as polarization-maintaining fibers, dispersion-shifted fibers, and photonic crystal fibers.
- The uses of optical fibers are mainly in the field of fiber-optic communications, where they enable the transmission of large amounts of data over long distances and at high speeds, with low loss and interference .
  - Optical fibers are used to link computers within local area networks, to connect telephone lines and cable television networks, to provide internet access and broadband services, and to support wireless communication and mobile networks.
  - Optical fibers are also used for other applications, such as sensing, imaging, lighting, and medicine. ### Step index and graded index fibers

- Step index and graded index fibers are two types of multimode fibers that are used for optical communication.
- Multimode fibers allow multiple modes or paths of light to propagate through the fiber core, which can cause modal dispersion and limit the bandwidth and distance of the transmission.
- Step index fiber has a core with a constant refractive index and a cladding with a lower refractive index. The refractive index changes abruptly at the core-cladding interface, forming a step-like profile.
- Graded index fiber has a core with a varying refractive index that decreases gradually from the center to the edge of the core. The refractive index changes smoothly at the core-cladding interface, forming a parabolic profile.
- The main differences between step index and graded index fibers are:

  - Step index fiber has higher modal dispersion than graded index fiber, because different modes travel at different speeds and distances in the fiber .
  - Graded index fiber has higher bandwidth and longer distance than step index fiber, because the varying refractive index reduces the modal dispersion and equalizes the propagation time of different modes .
  - Step index fiber is cheaper and easier to manufacture than graded index fiber, because it requires only two materials with different refractive indices.
  - Graded index fiber is preferred for modern communication applications that require high data rates and long distances, such as local area networks (LANs) and fiber to the home (FTTH) .
  - Step index fiber can be either single mode or multimode, depending on the core diameter and the wavelength of the light. Single mode step index fiber has a very small core that allows only one mode to propagate, which eliminates modal dispersion and increases bandwidth and distance.
  - Graded index fiber is always multimode, because it has a larger core that allows multiple modes to propagate. However, it can achieve near-single mode performance by using a small number of modes and a high refractive index difference.

### Dispersion in Fiber Optics

- Dispersion is the spreading of light pulses when they travel through an optical fiber from one end to another .
- Dispersion causes distortion and limits the data rate of digital signals sent over fiber optic cable.
- Dispersion can be classified into three types: material dispersion, waveguide dispersion, and modal dispersion .
- Material dispersion is caused by a change in the refractive index of the fiber optic material with different wavelengths. The higher the index, the slower the light travels.
- Waveguide dispersion is due to the distribution of light between the core and the cladding of the fiber. The light in the core travels faster than the light in the cladding.
- Modal dispersion is due to the different paths that light can take through a multimode fiber. The light that travels along the axis of the fiber arrives faster than the light that travels near the cladding.
- Dispersion can be reduced by using single-mode fibers, dispersion-shifted fibers, dispersion-compensating fibers, or dispersion-flattened fibers.
- Dispersion can also be compensated by using optical filters, optical amplifiers, or optical equalizers.

### Principles of laser action

- Laser is an acronym for **Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation**.

  - **Stimulated absorption**: This is the process of an atom or molecule in a lower energy level to be excited by an incoming photon of the same frequency to a higher energy level and absorb the photon. This results in the attenuation of the incoming photon by removing it from the beam.

### Quantum wires and quantum well

- Quantum wires and quantum well are examples of **quantum confinement** in semiconductor nanostructures, where the motion of charge carriers (electrons and holes) is restricted in one or more dimensions.
- Quantum confinement leads to discrete energy levels and changes in the electronic, optical and transport properties of the nanostructures.

#### Quantum well

- A quantum well is a thin layer of a lower bandgap semiconductor material sandwiched between two layers of a higher bandgap material, forming a heterostructure.
- The charge carriers are confined in the lower bandgap layer (the well) by the potential barriers created by the higher bandgap layers (the barriers).
- The energy levels of the charge carriers in the well are quantized, forming subbands, due to the boundary conditions imposed by the barriers.
- The subband spacing depends on the well width, the effective mass of the charge carriers, and the band offset between the well and the barrier materials.

### Fabrication of nano materials by top-down and bottom-up approaches

- Nanomaterials are materials with at least one dimension in the range of 1 to 100 nanometers (nm).
- Nanomaterials have unique physical, chemical, optical, and electrical properties that differ from their bulk counterparts.
- Nanomaterials can be fabricated by two main approaches: top-down and bottom-up.
- Top-down approach is where a process starts from a large piece and subsequently uses finer and finer tools for creating correspondingly smaller structures.
- Bottom-up approach is where smaller components of atomic or molecular dimensions self-assemble together, according to a natural physical principle or an externally applied driving force, to give rise to larger and more organized systems.

#### Top-down approach: Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD)

- CVD is a process where a gas or vapor containing the desired elements is introduced into a reaction chamber, where it decomposes or reacts on a heated substrate, forming a thin film or a nanostructure.
- CVD can be used to fabricate various nanomaterials, such as carbon nanotubes, graphene, nanowires, nanodots, etc.
- CVD can be classified into different types, such as thermal CVD, plasma-enhanced CVD, laser-assisted CVD, etc., depending on the source of energy or activation for the gas-phase reactions.
- CVD can produce high-quality nanomaterials with good control over the size, shape, orientation, and crystallinity of the nanostructures.
- CVD requires high temperatures, vacuum conditions, and expensive equipment and precursors.

#### Bottom-up approach: Sol-Gel

- Sol-gel is a process where a solution of metal or metalloid precursors (such as alkoxides, nitrates, acetates, etc.) is converted into a gel, which is then dried and heat-treated to form a solid material with nanostructures.
- Sol-gel can be used to fabricate various nanomaterials, such as metal oxides, ceramics, glasses, composites, etc.
- Sol-gel can be modified by adding different additives, such as surfactants, polymers, organic molecules, etc., to control the size, shape, porosity, and functionality of the nanostructures.
- Sol-gel can produce nanomaterials with low cost, low temperature, and ambient pressure conditions.
- Sol-gel may suffer from drawbacks such as poor reproducibility, contamination, and cracking of the gel.
