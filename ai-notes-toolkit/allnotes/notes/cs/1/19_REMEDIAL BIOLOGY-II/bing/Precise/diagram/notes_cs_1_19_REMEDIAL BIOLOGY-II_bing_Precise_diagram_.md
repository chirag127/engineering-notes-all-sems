### Types of Microorganisms

Microorganisms, also known as microbes, are tiny living organisms that are too small to be seen with the naked eye. They are found in almost every habitat on Earth, from soil to water to air, and even inside other living organisms. There are several different types of microorganisms, including:

1. **Bacteria:** These are single-celled organisms that lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are found in almost every environment on Earth and play important roles in many ecological processes, including decomposition and nutrient cycling.

5. **Algae:** These are photosynthetic organisms that range in size from single-celled to multicellular. They are found in many different aquatic and terrestrial environments and play important roles in primary production and oxygen production.

6. **Viruses:** These are tiny particles that consist of genetic material (either DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat. They are not considered to be living organisms because they cannot reproduce on their own. Instead, they must infect a host cell and use its machinery to replicate. Viruses can cause a wide range of diseases in plants, animals, and humans.

Microorganisms play important roles in many different industries, including the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals. For example, bacteria and fungi are used to produce antibiotics, enzymes, and other important compounds. ### Proteobacteria

Proteobacteria is a phylum of gram-negative bacteria discovered by Carl Woese in the 1980s based on nucleotide sequence homology. It is also known as Pseudomonadota, although the renaming of phyla in 2021 remains controversial among microbiologists. Proteobacteria are further classified into the classes alpha-, beta-, gamma-, delta- and epsilonproteobacteria, each class having separate orders, families, genera, and species.

The first class of Proteobacteria is the Alphaproteobacteria. The unifying characteristic of this class is that they are oligotrophs, organisms capable of living in low-nutrient environments such as deep oceanic sediments, glacial ice, or deep undersurface soil.

The phylum Proteobacteria includes a wide variety of pathogenic genera, such as Escherichia, Salmonella, Vibrio, Yersinia, Legionella, and many others. Others are free-living (non-parasitic) and include many of the bacteria responsible for nitrogen fixation.

Proteobacteria is the second largest phylum of hydrogenogenic CO oxidizers, which are composed of mesophilic and neutrophilic bacteria. The members of this phylum are found in diverse classes (alpha-, beta-, gamma-, and epsilonproteobacteria).

### Protozoa

Protozoa are unicellular eukaryotic microorganisms that belong to the kingdom Protista. They are found in a wide range of habitats, including soil, water, and inside other organisms. Protozoa are classified based on their mode of locomotion, which includes flagella, cilia, and pseudopodia.

Some important characteristics of protozoa include:
- They are unicellular organisms.
- They have a eukaryotic cell structure, which means they have a well-defined nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.
- They can reproduce both sexually and asexually.
- They can exist as free-living organisms or as parasites.
- They can move using flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia.

Protozoa play important roles in their ecosystems. They are a crucial part of the food chain, serving as prey for larger organisms and consuming smaller ones. Some protozoa are also important decomposers, breaking down dead organic matter and releasing nutrients back into the environment.

In addition, some protozoa can cause diseases in humans and other animals. For example, Plasmodium species cause malaria, while Entamoeba histolytica causes amoebic dysentery.

Overall, protozoa are a diverse group of microorganisms that play important roles in their ecosystems and can have significant impacts on human health.

### Viral Structures

- The basic structure of all viruses includes nucleic acid, either DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) or RNA (Ribonucleic Acid) called a genome which is enveloped by a protein coat called a capsid .
- Many viruses have a lipoprotein bilayer that encloses the capsid, called an envelope .
- The nucleic acid of a virus is found within its inner core that contains the genetic information for the synthesis of proteins and replication .
- Viruses are observed in a wide range of shapes and sizes, but these shapes are constant in a particular viral family .

- Viruses are very diverse .
- Viruses are much smaller than bacteria and consist of a single- or double-stranded nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein shell called a capsid; some viruses also have an outer envelope composed of lipids and proteins .
- The two main classes of viruses are RNA viruses and DNA viruses .




### Viral Multiplication

Viral multiplication refers to the process by which viruses replicate and produce new virus particles. This process is essential for the survival and spread of viruses. The process of viral multiplication can be divided into several stages:

1. **Attachment:** The virus attaches to the host cell by binding to specific receptors on the cell surface.
2. **Penetration:** The virus enters the host cell, either by fusion with the cell membrane or by being taken up by the cell through endocytosis.
3. **Uncoating:** The viral genome is released from the protective protein coat, or capsid, and becomes available for replication.
4. **Replication:** The viral genome is replicated using the host cell's machinery. This may involve the synthesis of new viral proteins and the replication of the viral genome.
5. **Assembly:** New virus particles are assembled from the newly synthesized viral components.
6. **Release:** The new virus particles are released from the host cell, either by budding from the cell membrane or by causing the cell to lyse (break open).

The specific details of viral multiplication can vary depending on the type of virus and the host cell. Some viruses, such as retroviruses, integrate their genome into the host cell's DNA and can remain dormant for long periods of time before being activated and replicating. Other viruses, such as lytic phages, replicate rapidly and cause the host cell to lyse, releasing new virus particles.

Viral multiplication plays a crucial role in the spread of viral infections and the development of viral diseases. Understanding the process of viral multiplication can help in the development of antiviral drugs and vaccines to prevent and treat viral infections.

# Unit 3 - Catabolic & Anabolic Reactions: Enzymes, Energy Production and Carbohydrate Metabolism. Lipid & Protein Catabolism, Energy Production Mechanism, Metabolic Diversity & Pathways of Energy Use. Integration of Metabolism

## Catabolic and Anabolic Reactions
- Catabolic reactions are those that break down complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy in the process.
- Anabolic reactions, on the other hand, are those that build complex molecules from simpler ones, requiring energy input.
- These two types of reactions are complementary and together constitute metabolism.

## Enzymes
- Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions in the body.
- They work by lowering the activation energy required for a reaction to occur, making it easier for the reaction to proceed.
- Enzymes are specific to the reactions they catalyze and are regulated by various mechanisms to ensure proper functioning of the cell.

## Energy Production and Carbohydrate Metabolism
- Carbohydrates are broken down into glucose, which is then used to produce ATP (adenosine triphosphate) through a process called cellular respiration.
- ATP is the primary energy currency of the cell and is used to power various cellular processes.
- Cellular respiration can occur through two pathways: aerobic respiration, which requires oxygen, and anaerobic respiration, which does not.

## Lipid and Protein Catabolism
- Lipids and proteins can also be broken down to produce ATP.
- Lipids are broken down into fatty acids and glycerol, which can then enter the citric acid cycle to produce ATP.
- Proteins are broken down into amino acids, which can be converted into intermediates of the citric acid cycle or used to synthesize glucose through a process called gluconeogenesis.

## Energy Production Mechanism
- The primary mechanism of energy production in the cell is the electron transport chain, which is located in the inner membrane of the mitochondria.
- During this process, electrons are transferred from molecules of NADH and FADH2 (produced during earlier stages of cellular respiration) to oxygen, releasing energy that is used to produce ATP.

## Metabolic Diversity and Pathways of Energy Use
- Different organisms have different metabolic pathways and can use different sources of energy.
- Some organisms, such as plants, can produce their own food through photosynthesis, while others, such as animals, must consume other organisms to obtain energy.
- The energy obtained from food can be used for various purposes, such as growth, reproduction, and movement.

## Integration of Metabolism
- Metabolism is a complex and highly regulated process that involves the integration of multiple pathways.
- Hormones, such as insulin and glucagon, play a key role in regulating metabolism by controlling the uptake and release of glucose from the liver.
- The body also has mechanisms to store excess energy in the form of glycogen or fat, which can be mobilized when energy is needed.



### Catabolic & Anabolic Reactions

Catabolic and anabolic reactions are the two types of biochemical reactions that make up the metabolism. 

- **Anabolic reactions** involve the building of larger, complex molecules from smaller, simpler ones, and require an input of energy.
- **Catabolic reactions** are the opposite of anabolic reactions, and break the chemical bonds in larger, more complex molecules. This process releases energy while breaking larger molecules down into their smaller components.

Energy is transferred within the cell and the organism by ATP; anabolic reactions consume it, and catabolic reactions generate it. Every cellular chemical reaction is mediated by a specific enzyme. The process that breaks down a substance is usually not the reverse of the process that makes it, using a different enzyme.

Anabolic reactions are associated with growth, while catabolic reactions are associated with the release of energy and energy production. At any moment, both reactions are occurring. Collectively, all of these reactions are called your metabolism.



### Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions in living organisms. They are proteins that are folded into complex shapes that allow smaller molecules to fit into them. The place where these substrate molecules fit is called the active site.

- Enzymes are specific to their substrates, meaning that each enzyme will only catalyze a specific reaction.
- Enzymes work by lowering the activation energy of a reaction, making it easier for the reaction to occur.
- Enzymes can be affected by changes in pH and temperature, which can alter the shape of the enzyme and its active site, reducing its effectiveness.
- Enzymes can be regulated by inhibitors, which bind to the enzyme and prevent it from functioning, or by activators, which increase the enzyme's activity.

Enzymes play a crucial role in metabolism, including catabolic and anabolic reactions. Catabolic reactions break down larger molecules into smaller ones, releasing energy in the process. Anabolic reactions, on the other hand, build larger molecules from smaller ones, using energy.

In carbohydrate metabolism, enzymes are involved in the breakdown of carbohydrates into simple sugars, which can then be used for energy production. Overall, enzymes play a crucial role in the integration of metabolism, allowing for the efficient use of energy and the coordination of metabolic processes.



### Energy Production

Energy production in living organisms occurs through metabolic pathways. These pathways can be broadly divided into two categories: anabolic and catabolic.

- **Anabolic pathways** synthesize molecules and require energy. These pathways are used to build up large molecules from smaller ones, a process known as biosynthesis. Anabolic reactions are endergonic, meaning they require an input of energy to progress and are not spontaneous.

- **Catabolic pathways** break down molecules and produce energy. These pathways are used to break down large molecules into smaller ones, a process known as degradation. The reactions governing the breakdown of food to obtain energy are called catabolic reactions.

Because almost all metabolic reactions take place non-spontaneously, proteins called enzymes help facilitate those chemical reactions. Typically, anabolic and catabolic reactions are coupled, with catabolism providing the activation energy for anabolism.

Carbohydrate, protein, and lipid metabolic pathways are interconnected. Anabolic reactions use the energy produced by catabolic reactions to synthesize larger molecules from smaller ones, such as when the body forms proteins by stringing together amino acids.

#### Lipid Catabolism

#### Protein Catabolism

Protein catabolism is the process by which proteins are broken down into their constituent amino acids. This process occurs in the cytoplasm of cells and is catalyzed by enzymes called proteases.

Once the proteins have been broken down, the amino acids can be further metabolized to produce energy. Amino acids can be converted into various intermediates of the citric acid cycle, such as alpha-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate, which can then be used to produce ATP.

In summary, lipid and protein catabolism are important processes that allow living organisms to extract energy from the food they consume. ### Cellular Respiration

### Glycolysis

Glycolysis is the first step in the breakdown of glucose to extract energy for cellular metabolism. It is a series of ten chemical reactions that occur in the cytoplasm of the cell. The process can be divided into two phases: the energy investment phase and the energy payoff phase.

1. In the energy investment phase, two ATP molecules are used to phosphorylate glucose, forming fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.
2. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate is then split into two molecules of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P).
3. In the energy payoff phase, two molecules of NADH and four molecules of ATP are produced for each molecule of glucose that enters glycolysis.
4. The net gain of ATP from glycolysis is two molecules per molecule of glucose.
5. The end product of glycolysis is pyruvate, which can be further processed in the mitochondria through the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain to produce more ATP.

Glycolysis is an important process in the metabolism of carbohydrates and other compounds. It is the first step in the breakdown of glucose, which is the primary source of energy for cells.
