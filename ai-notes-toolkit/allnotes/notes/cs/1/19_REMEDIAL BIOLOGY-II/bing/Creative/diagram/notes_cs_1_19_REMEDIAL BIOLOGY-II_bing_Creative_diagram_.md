### Brief history of microbiology

- Microbiology is the scientific study of microorganisms, which are small or minute living things that are usually invisible to the naked eye.
- Microbiology has its origins in the 16th century, when some people observed the existence of tiny creatures in various substances, such as vinegar and milk, using simple magnifying lenses.
- However, the development of the microscope in the 17th century was a major breakthrough for microbiology, as it allowed the observation of microorganisms in greater detail and variety.
- Robert Hooke (1635-1703) was an English scientist who used a compound microscope to observe thin slices of cork and other plant materials. He coined the term "cell" to describe the basic units of life that he saw.
- Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1632-1723) was a Dutch draper and amateur scientist who made his own simple microscopes and used them to examine various samples of water, soil, blood, and saliva. He was the first to document the existence of bacteria, protozoa, fungi, algae, and other microorganisms, which he called "animalcules" or "little animals" .
- In the 18th and 19th centuries, microbiology progressed further with the contributions of many scientists who investigated the roles of microorganisms in fermentation, disease, immunity, and sanitation. Some of the notable figures in this period include Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch, Joseph Lister, Edward Jenner, Alexander Fleming, and Paul Ehrlich.
- In the 20th and 21st centuries, microbiology expanded into various branches and applications, such as virology, bacteriology, mycology, parasitology, immunology, biotechnology, molecular biology, and environmental microbiology. Microbiologists have used advanced techniques and tools, such as electron microscopy, DNA sequencing, genetic engineering, and bioinformatics, to explore the diversity, structure, function, and evolution of microorganisms and their interactions with other living things and the environment.



### Types of microorganisms

Microorganisms are microscopic organisms that exist as unicellular, multicellular, or cell clusters. They can be beneficial or harmful to life, and they can be divided into six major types :

- **Bacteria**: These are prokaryotic cells, meaning they lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They have a cell wall made of peptidoglycan, and they can be classified by their shape, Gram stain, metabolism, and genetic characteristics. Bacteria are found in almost every habitat on Earth, and they play important roles in nutrient cycling, decomposition, symbiosis, and pathogenesis. Some examples of bacteria are Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae.
- **Archaea**: These are also prokaryotic cells, but they differ from bacteria in their cell wall composition, membrane structure, and ribosomal RNA sequences. They are often adapted to extreme environments, such as high temperatures, salinity, or acidity. They also have diverse metabolic pathways, such as methanogenesis, halophilism, and thermophilism. Some examples of archaea are Methanobacterium, Halobacterium, and Thermococcus.
- **Protozoa**: These are eukaryotic cells, meaning they have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are usually unicellular and motile, and they can be classified by their mode of locomotion, such as flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia. They are mostly heterotrophic, meaning they feed on other organisms or organic matter, but some can also perform photosynthesis. They can be free-living or parasitic, and they can cause diseases such as malaria, amoebiasis, and giardiasis. Some examples of protozoa are Plasmodium, Entamoeba, and Giardia.
- **Algae**: These are also eukaryotic cells, but they are mostly photosynthetic, meaning they use light energy to produce organic molecules. They have chloroplasts that contain chlorophyll and other pigments, and they can be unicellular or multicellular. They are found in aquatic and moist environments, and they form the base of many food chains. They can also produce oxygen, biofuels, and other useful substances. Some examples of algae are Chlamydomonas, Spirogyra, and Ulva.
- **Fungi**: These are also eukaryotic cells, but they are mostly saprophytic, meaning they feed on dead or decaying organic matter. They have a cell wall made of chitin, and they can be unicellular or multicellular. They reproduce by spores, and they can form symbiotic or pathogenic relationships with other organisms. Some examples of fungi are Saccharomyces, Penicillium, and Candida.
- **Viruses**: These are not cells, but acellular particles that consist of a nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat. They are obligate intracellular parasites, meaning they can only replicate inside a host cell. They can infect all types of cells, and they can cause diseases such as influenza, AIDS, and COVID-19. Some examples of viruses are Influenza, HIV, and SARS-CoV-2.



### Basic idea of domain bacteria

- Domain bacteria is one of the three domains of life, along with domain archaea and domain eukarya.
- Domain bacteria includes the prokaryotes people encounter on an everyday basis . Prokaryotes are organisms that lack a membrane-bound nucleus and other organelles.
- Domain bacteria is also possibly the largest domain, containing possibly millions of unknown and unrecorded specimens. These small, single-celled organisms live almost everywhere, and are the size of most eukaryotic organelles.
- Domain bacteria can be classified into five main phyla: proteobacteria, cyanobacteria, firmicutes, actinobacteria, and spirochaetes. Each phylum has distinctive characteristics and functions.
- Most bacterial species are heterotrophic; that is, they acquire their food from organic matter . The largest number of bacteria are saprobic, meaning that they feed on dead or decaying organic matter . A few bacterial species are parasitic; these bacteria live within host organisms and cause disease .
- Some bacterial species are autotrophic; that is, they produce their own food from inorganic sources. For example, cyanobacteria are capable of photosynthesis, using light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into organic molecules . Cyanobacteria are also known as blue-green algae because of their color and appearance .
- Domain bacteria plays an important role in the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals, such as antibiotics, enzymes, vitamins, hormones, and biofuels. Bacteria can also be used for bioremediation, the process of using living organisms to clean up environmental pollutants.

- Proteobacteria have different **morphologies**, such as rods, cocci, spirilla, and stalked bacteria.
- Proteobacteria are found in various **habitats**, such as soil, water, plants, animals, and humans.

- Proteobacteria are involved in the **production** of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals, such as antibiotics, bioplastics, biofuels, and biosensors.

### Gram-positive bacteria

- Gram-positive bacteria are bacteria that have a **thick cell wall** composed of peptidoglycan and teichoic acids  .
- Gram-positive bacteria can be distinguished from gram-negative bacteria by the **Gram stain test**, which involves a chemical dye that stains the cell wall purple or blue  .
- Gram-positive bacteria include many **important pathogens** such as Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus, Clostridium, Corynebacterium, Listeria, and Mycobacterium .
- Gram-positive bacteria can cause various **infections** such as pneumonia, meningitis, anthrax, tetanus, diphtheria, tuberculosis, and leprosy .
- Gram-positive bacteria can also have **beneficial roles** such as producing antibiotics, probiotics, enzymes, and fermented foods.
- Gram-positive bacteria can undergo **bacterial transformation**, which is the uptake and expression of foreign DNA from the environment.
- Gram-positive bacteria can be treated with **antibiotics** that target their cell wall synthesis, such as penicillin, vancomycin, and daptomycin . However, some gram-positive bacteria have developed **resistance** to these drugs, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) .



### Lichens

- Lichens are **composite organisms** that consist of a **symbiotic association** of algae (usually green) or cyanobacteria and fungi (mostly ascomycetes and basidiomycetes).
- The dominant partner is the **fungus**, which gives the lichen the majority of its characteristics, from its **thallus shape** to its **fruiting bodies**.
- The alga or cyanobacterium provides **photosynthesis** and **nitrogen fixation** for the fungus, while the fungus provides **protection**, **water**, and **minerals** for the alga or cyanobacterium.
- Lichens are classified according to the **morphology** of their thallus, which can be **crustose** (crusty), **foliose** (leafy), **fruticose** (shrubby), or **squamulose** (scaly).
- Lichens are found **worldwide** and occur in a variety of **environmental conditions**. They are **pioneer species**, among the first living things to grow on bare rock or areas denuded of life by a disaster.
- Lichens have various **roles** in the ecosystem, such as **bioindicators**, **decomposers**, **nitrogen fixers**, **soil formers**, and **food sources** for animals.
- Lichens also have various **uses** for humans, such as **dyes**, **medicines**, **perfumes**, **litmus paper**, and **lichenometry**.



### Algae

- Algae are a large and diverse group of photosynthetic eukaryotic organisms that are typically aquatic and include seaweeds and many single-celled forms .
- Algae are classified into several groups based on their pigments, cell wall composition, flagella, storage products, and other features. Some of the major groups are:
  - Green algae (Chlorophyta): They have chlorophyll a and b, store starch, and have cellulose in their cell walls. They are the most closely related to land plants and can be unicellular, colonial, or multicellular. Examples: Spirogyra, Ulva, Volvox.
  - Brown algae (Phaeophyta): They have chlorophyll a and c, fucoxanthin, store laminarin, and have alginic acid in their cell walls. They are mostly marine and multicellular, forming large seaweeds and kelps. Examples: Fucus, Sargassum, Macrocystis.
  - Red algae (Rhodophyta): They have chlorophyll a and d, phycobilins, store floridean starch, and have agar and carrageenan in their cell walls. They are mostly marine and multicellular, forming red or purple seaweeds. Examples: Porphyra, Corallina, Gracilaria.
  - Diatoms (Bacillariophyta): They have chlorophyll a and c, fucoxanthin, store oil or chrysolaminarin, and have silica in their cell walls.   - Dinoflagellates (Dinophyta): They have chlorophyll a and c, peridinin, store starch, and have cellulose plates in their cell walls. They are unicellular and have two flagella, one in a groove around the cell and one trailing behind. ### Protozoa

### Helminthes

- Helminthes are **parasitic worms** that infect various hosts, such as humans, animals, and plants .
- Helminthes can be classified into two major groups: **flatworms** (platyhelminths) and **roundworms** (nematodes) .
- Flatworms have a **flattened** body shape and include **flukes** (trematodes) and **tapeworms** (cestodes) .
- Flukes are leaf-shaped worms that attach to the internal organs of their hosts, such as the liver, lungs, or blood vessels.
- Tapeworms are ribbon-like worms that consist of a head (scolex) and a series of segments (proglottids) that contain reproductive organs.
- Roundworms have a **cylindrical** body shape and include **intestinal worms** (ascaris, hookworms, pinworms, etc.), **filarial worms** (wuchereria, onchocerca, etc.), and **tissue worms** (trichinella, dracunculus, etc.) .

- Tissue worms invade the muscles, brain, or other organs of their hosts and cause inflammation, pain, or neurological disorders.
- Helminthes have complex life cycles that involve one or more intermediate hosts and a definitive host.

- Helminthes can be treated by anthelmintic drugs that target specific metabolic pathways or structures of the worms, such as microtubules, fumarate reductase, or acetylcholinesterase.

### Viral Structures

- Viruses are non-living infectious particles that can only replicate inside living cells.
- Viruses have a simple structure that consists of three main components:
  - **Nucleic acid**: The genetic material of the virus, either DNA or RNA, that contains the information for protein synthesis and replication. The nucleic acid can be single-stranded or double-stranded, linear or circular, segmented or non-segmented  .
  - **Capsid**: The protein coat that surrounds and protects the nucleic acid. The capsid is composed of subunits called capsomeres, which can have different shapes and arrangements. The capsid determines the shape and size of the virus  .
  - **Envelope**: Some viruses have an additional layer outside the capsid, made of lipids and proteins derived from the host cell membrane. The envelope helps the virus to attach to and enter the host cell, and also to evade the host immune system. The envelope may have spikes or glycoproteins on its surface that are specific for the virus  .
- Viruses can be classified into different types based on their nucleic acid, capsid, and envelope characteristics. Some examples of viral types are   :
  - **Helical viruses**: These viruses have a capsid that forms a hollow cylinder or helix around the nucleic acid. The capsid can be rigid or flexible. Some helical viruses have an envelope, such as influenza virus and rabies virus, while others do not, such as tobacco mosaic virus and Ebola virus.
  - **Icosahedral viruses**: These viruses have a capsid that forms a regular polyhedron with 20 triangular faces and 12 vertices. The capsid can accommodate a large amount of nucleic acid. Some icosahedral viruses have an envelope, such as herpes virus and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), while others do not, such as poliovirus and adenovirus.
  - **Complex viruses**: These viruses have a capsid that is neither helical nor icosahedral, but has a more complex structure. The capsid may have additional features, such as tails, fibers, or spikes. Some complex viruses have an envelope, such as poxvirus and coronavirus, while others do not, such as bacteriophage and mimivirus.



### Viral Multiplication

- Viral multiplication is the process of forming new viruses by using the cellular machinery of the host cells after infection.
- Viruses have different host ranges and tissue tropisms, which determine the types of cells and organisms they can infect and multiply in.
- Viral multiplication can be classified into two types based on the life cycle of the virus: lytic cycle and lysogenic cycle.
- Lytic cycle is the type of viral multiplication in which the virus infects the host cell, replicates its genome and proteins, assembles new virions, and lyses the host cell to release the progeny viruses.
- Lysogenic cycle is the type of viral multiplication in which the virus integrates its genome into the host cell's genome, forming a prophage, and remains dormant until a stimulus triggers its activation and entry into the lytic cycle.
- The general stages of viral multiplication are: attachment, penetration, uncoating, biosynthesis, assembly, and release .
- Attachment is the stage in which the virus recognizes and binds to specific receptors on the surface of the host cell .
- Penetration is the stage in which the virus enters the host cell by endocytosis or fusion .
- Uncoating is the stage in which the viral genome is released from the capsid inside the host cell .
- Biosynthesis is the stage in which the viral genome and proteins are replicated and synthesized by using the host cell's enzymes and ribosomes .
- Assembly is the stage in which the viral genome and proteins are assembled into new virions .
- Release is the stage in which the new virions exit the host cell by lysis or budding .
- Viruses are very diverse in their structure, genome, and replication strategies. Some viruses have an envelope derived from the host cell membrane, some have segmented genomes, some have reverse transcriptase, and some have different mechanisms of penetration and release.

### Energy production mechanism

- Energy is the capacity to do work or cause change in a system.
- Biological organisms are open systems that exchange energy and matter with their surroundings.
- Energy comes to living systems through electrons occupying high energy states, either from food (respiration) or from light (photosynthesis).
- Energy is stored in the chemical bonds of organic molecules, such as carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids, which are used as fuel and building material by the cells.
- Energy is released from organic molecules by breaking down their bonds through a series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions, called metabolic pathways.
- Metabolic pathways can be classified into two types: catabolic and anabolic.
- Catabolic pathways break down complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy and reducing power (electrons) in the process. Examples of catabolic pathways are glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.
- Anabolic pathways synthesize complex molecules from simpler ones, consuming energy and reducing power in the process. Examples of anabolic pathways are gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and fatty acid synthesis.
- The energy released from catabolic pathways is used to drive anabolic pathways, as well as other cellular processes, such as transport, movement, and signaling.
- The energy currency of the cell is adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which is composed of a nitrogenous base (adenine), a sugar (ribose), and three phosphate groups.
- ATP is synthesized from adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate (Pi) by adding a phosphate group, a process that requires energy input.
- ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP and Pi by removing a phosphate group, a process that releases energy output.
- The energy released from ATP hydrolysis is used to power various cellular activities, such as muscle contraction, protein synthesis, and membrane transport.
- The energy balance of the cell is maintained by coupling endergonic reactions (those that require energy) with exergonic reactions (those that release energy), often through the intermediacy of ATP.
- The efficiency of energy conversion in biological systems is limited by the second law of thermodynamics, which states that entropy (disorder) of the universe always increases.
- The energy flow in biological systems is unidirectional, from high to low energy states, and ultimately dissipated as heat to the environment.

  - Catabolic pathways break down complex molecules into simpler ones and release energy. Examples are glycolysis, Krebs cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.
  - Anabolic pathways build up complex molecules from simpler ones and consume energy. Examples are photosynthesis, gluconeogenesis, and protein synthesis.
- Metabolic pathways are regulated by enzymes, which catalyze the chemical reactions and control the rate and direction of the pathway.
- Metabolic pathways are also influenced by the availability of substrates, products, coenzymes, and environmental factors such as temperature and pH.

### Kreb cycle

- The Kreb cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is a series of chemical reactions that occur in the mitochondria of aerobic organisms  .
- The Kreb cycle is part of the cellular respiration process, which converts glucose and other organic molecules into energy in the form of ATP (adenosine triphosphate)  .
- The Kreb cycle is preceded by glycolysis, which breaks down glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, and by pyruvate oxidation, which converts pyruvate into acetyl-CoA, a two-carbon molecule that enters the Kreb cycle  .
- The Kreb cycle consists of eight steps, each catalyzed by a specific enzyme  :
  - Step 1: Acetyl-CoA combines with oxaloacetate, a four-carbon molecule, to form citrate, a six-carbon molecule. Coenzyme A is released and recycled  .
  - Step 2: Citrate is isomerized to isocitrate, a six-carbon molecule, by the removal and addition of a water molecule  .
  - Step 3: Isocitrate is oxidized to alpha-ketoglutarate, a five-carbon molecule, by the removal of a carbon dioxide molecule and the transfer of two electrons to NAD+, forming NADH  .

### Electron Transport System and Oxidative Phosphorylation

- Electron transport system (ETS) is a series of proteins and electron carriers located in the inner mitochondrial membrane that transfers electrons donated by the reduced molecules NADH and FADH2 through a series of electron acceptors, to oxygen.
- Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is a process involving a flow of electrons through the ETS, which creates an electrochemical gradient of protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- The electrochemical gradient of protons drives the synthesis of ATP by a membrane-bound enzyme called ATP synthase, which uses the energy of proton movement to catalyze the reaction ADP + Pi -> ATP.
- This process of coupling electron transport and ATP synthesis by a proton gradient is called chemiosmosis.
- Together, the ETS and chemiosmosis make up oxidative phosphorylation, which is the final stage of cellular respiration and produces most of the ATP generated from glucose oxidation.
- The ETS consists of four main complexes (I, II, III, and IV) and two mobile electron carriers (ubiquinone and cytochrome c) that shuttle electrons between the complexes.
- Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) receives electrons from NADH and transfers them to ubiquinone, while pumping four protons across the membrane.
- Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) receives electrons from FADH2 and transfers them to ubiquinone, without pumping protons.
- Complex III (cytochrome bc1) receives electrons from ubiquinone and transfers them to cytochrome c, while pumping four protons across the membrane.
- Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) receives electrons from cytochrome c and transfers them to oxygen, the final electron acceptor, while pumping two protons across the membrane.
- The oxygen molecule is reduced to water by accepting four electrons and four protons.
- The ETS establishes a proton gradient with a higher concentration of protons in the intermembrane space than in the matrix.
- The proton gradient creates a potential difference across the membrane, which is the driving force for ATP synthesis.
- ATP synthase is a complex enzyme that spans the inner mitochondrial membrane and has two main subunits: F0 and F1.
- F0 is a proton channel that allows protons to flow back into the matrix, following their electrochemical gradient.
- F1 is an ATPase that uses the energy of proton flow to rotate a central stalk and catalyze the formation of ATP from ADP and Pi.
- The rotation of the central stalk causes conformational changes in the F1 subunit that enable the binding and release of the substrates and products.
- Each rotation of the central stalk produces three molecules of ATP.
- The ETS and OXPHOS are regulated by the availability of substrates (NADH, FADH2, ADP, Pi, and O2) and the demand for ATP.
- The rate of ETS and OXPHOS increases when the cellular energy charge is low, and decreases when the cellular energy charge is high.
- The ETS and OXPHOS are also influenced by various inhibitors and uncouplers that interfere with the electron transport, proton gradient, or ATP synthesis.
- Some examples of inhibitors are rotenone (blocks complex I), antimycin A (blocks complex III), cyanide and carbon monoxide (block complex IV), and oligomycin (blocks ATP synthase).
- Some examples of uncouplers are 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) and thermogenin (UCP1), which allow protons to leak across the membrane, dissipating the proton gradient and reducing ATP synthesis.
- Uncouplers can generate heat by converting the energy of the proton gradient into thermal energy.
- This is the mechanism of non-shivering thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue, which contains a high amount of mitochondria and thermogenin.
