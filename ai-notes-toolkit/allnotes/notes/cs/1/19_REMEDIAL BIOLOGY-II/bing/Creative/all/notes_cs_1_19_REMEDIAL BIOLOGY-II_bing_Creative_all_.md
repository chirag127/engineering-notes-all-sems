KCS stands for Knowledge-Centered Service, a methodology that aims to improve service delivery and knowledge management in service organizations. Some of the main features and benefits of KCS are:

- KCS is based on the principle of "capture, structure, reuse, and improve" knowledge as part of the service process.
- KCS enables service agents to create and update knowledge articles in real time, based on the issues and solutions they encounter during service interactions.
- KCS empowers service agents to search and reuse existing knowledge articles to solve problems faster and more consistently, reducing duplication and rework.
- KCS fosters a culture of collaboration and feedback among service agents, who can rate, comment, and flag knowledge articles for quality and relevance.
- KCS leverages analytics and metrics to measure and improve the performance and value of the knowledge base and the service organization.
- KCS aligns with the business goals and strategies of the service organization, such as increasing customer satisfaction, reducing costs, and enhancing innovation.

# Brief history of microbiology

- Microbiology is the scientific study of microorganisms, which are usually too small to be seen with the naked eye.
- Microbiology has its origins in the 16th century, when some people observed the existence of invisible living things that caused diseases or fermentation.
- The development of the microscope in the 17th century enabled the direct observation of microorganisms by scientists such as Robert Hooke and Anton van Leeuwenhoek.
- Robert Hooke (1635-1703) was an English scientist who coined the term "cell" after observing cork and plant tissues under a microscope. He also observed strands of fungi and microfossils.
- Anton van Leeuwenhoek (1632-1723) was a Dutch draper and amateur microscopist who made his own lenses and microscopes. He was the first to observe and describe bacteria, protozoa, algae, and other microorganisms, which he called "animalcules" or "little animals" .
- In the 18th and 19th centuries, microbiology became a more established and diverse field, with contributions from many scientists such as Ferdinand Cohn, Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch, Joseph Lister, and others.
- Ferdinand Cohn (1828-1898) was a German botanist and microbiologist who founded the discipline of bacteriology. He classified bacteria into four groups based on their shape and mode of reproduction. He also discovered bacterial spores and endospores.
- Louis Pasteur (1822-1895) was a French chemist and microbiologist who demonstrated the role of microorganisms in fermentation, spoilage, and disease. He also developed the germ theory of disease, the process of pasteurization, and several vaccines against anthrax, rabies, and other diseases.
- Robert Koch (1843-1910) was a German physician and microbiologist who established the criteria for proving the causal relationship between a microbe and a disease, known as Koch's postulates. He also identified the causative agents of anthrax, tuberculosis, cholera, and other diseases.
- Joseph Lister (1827-1912) was a British surgeon and microbiologist who applied the germ theory of disease to surgery and introduced the use of antiseptics to prevent wound infections. He also developed methods for sterilizing surgical instruments and dressings.
- In the 20th and 21st centuries, microbiology has expanded to include many subdisciplines and applications, such as virology, immunology, molecular biology, biotechnology, genomics, metagenomics, microbiome, and others. Microbiology has also contributed to the fields of medicine, agriculture, industry, and environmental science.



# Types of microorganisms

Microorganisms are microscopic organisms that exist as unicellular, multicellular, or cell clusters. They can be beneficial or harmful to life, and they can be divided into six major types :

- **Bacteria**: These are prokaryotic cells, meaning they lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They have a cell wall made of peptidoglycan, and they can be classified by their shape, Gram stain, oxygen requirement, and metabolic diversity. Some examples of bacteria are Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae.
- **Archaea**: These are also prokaryotic cells, but they differ from bacteria in their cell wall composition, membrane lipids, and ribosomal RNA sequences. They are often found in extreme environments, such as hot springs, salt lakes, and deep-sea vents. Some examples of archaea are Methanobacterium, Halobacterium, and Thermococcus.
- **Protozoa**: These are eukaryotic cells, meaning they have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are usually motile and heterotrophic, meaning they obtain their nutrients by ingesting other organisms or organic matter. They can be classified by their mode of locomotion, such as flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia. Some examples of protozoa are Paramecium, Amoeba, and Plasmodium.
- **Algae**: These are also eukaryotic cells, but they are photosynthetic, meaning they use light energy to synthesize organic molecules. They have a cell wall made of cellulose, and they can be unicellular or multicellular. They can be classified by their pigments, such as chlorophyll, carotenoids, or phycobilins. Some examples of algae are Chlamydomonas, Spirogyra, and Ulva.
- **Fungi**: These are also eukaryotic cells, but they are saprophytic, meaning they decompose organic matter. They have a cell wall made of chitin, and they can be unicellular or multicellular. They can be classified by their mode of reproduction, such as sexual or asexual spores. Some examples of fungi are Saccharomyces, Penicillium, and Aspergillus.
- **Viruses**: These are acellular entities, meaning they are not composed of cells. They consist of a nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein coat, and sometimes a lipid envelope. They are obligate intracellular parasites, meaning they can only replicate inside a host cell. They can be classified by their shape, size, genome type, and host range. Some examples of viruses are Influenza, Herpes, and HIV.



# Basic idea of domain bacteria

- Domain bacteria is one of the three domains of life, along with domain archaea and domain eukarya.
- Domain bacteria includes the prokaryotes people encounter on an everyday basis. Prokaryotes are organisms that lack a membrane-bound nucleus and other organelles.
- Most bacterial species are heterotrophic; that is, they acquire their food from organic matter. The largest number of bacteria are saprobic, meaning that they feed on dead or decaying organic matter. A few bacterial species are parasitic; these bacteria live within host organisms and cause disease.
- Domain bacteria can be classified into five main phyla: proteobacteria, cyanobacteria, firmicutes, actinobacteria, and spirochaetes.
- Proteobacteria are the largest and most diverse group of bacteria. They include many common pathogens, such as E. coli, Salmonella, and H. pylori, as well as some beneficial bacteria, such as Rhizobium, which fix nitrogen in the soil.
- Cyanobacteria are also known as blue-green algae. They are capable of photosynthesis and produce oxygen. They are important for the evolution of life on Earth, as they are believed to be the first organisms to perform oxygenic photosynthesis.
- Firmicutes are mostly gram-positive bacteria, meaning that they have a thick cell wall that retains a purple stain in a laboratory test. They include some of the most common bacteria in the human body, such as Lactobacillus, which helps digest food, and Staphylococcus, which can cause infections.
- Actinobacteria are also mostly gram-positive bacteria, but they have a high G+C content in their DNA, meaning that they have more guanine and cytosine nucleotides than adenine and thymine. They include some of the most important soil bacteria, such as Streptomyces, which produce antibiotics, and Mycobacterium, which cause tuberculosis and leprosy.
- Spirochaetes are bacteria that have a spiral shape and a flexible cell wall. They can move by rotating their flagella, which are located inside the cell wall. They include some of the most notorious pathogens, such as Treponema, which causes syphilis, and Borrelia, which causes Lyme disease.



# Proteobacteria

- Proteobacteria is a phylum of gram-negative bacteria discovered by Carl Woese in the 1980s based on nucleotide sequence homology .
- Proteobacteria are further classified into the classes alpha-, beta-, gamma-, delta- and epsilonproteobacteria, each class having separate orders, families, genera, and species .
- Proteobacteria include a wide variety of pathogenic and free-living bacteria, such as Escherichia, Salmonella, Vibrio, Yersinia, Legionella, and many others .
- Proteobacteria also include many of the bacteria responsible for nitrogen fixation, a process that converts atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, which can be used by plants and other organisms .
- Proteobacteria are characterized by their diverse metabolic pathways, such as aerobic and anaerobic respiration, fermentation, photosynthesis, and chemolithotrophy.
- Proteobacteria are also capable of living in low-nutrient environments such as deep oceanic sediments, glacial ice, or deep undersurface soil.
- Proteobacteria are important for the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals, such as antibiotics, bioplastics, biofuels, and bioremediation agents.



# Nonproteobacteria Gram-Negative Bacteria

- Nonproteobacteria are a diverse group of bacteria that do not belong to the phylum Proteobacteria, which contains the majority of gram-negative bacteria.
- Nonproteobacteria can be either gram-negative or gram-positive, depending on the presence or absence of an outer membrane in their cell wall.
- Nonproteobacteria can have various metabolic modes, such as photoautotrophy, chemoheterotrophy, or chemoautotrophy.
- Some examples of nonproteobacteria are:

  - **Chlamydia**: These are obligate intracellular parasites that infect animals and humans, causing diseases such as trachoma, chlamydia, and lymphogranuloma venereum. They have a unique developmental cycle that involves two forms: elementary bodies and reticulate bodies. They lack peptidoglycan in their cell wall and depend on their host for ATP and other nutrients.
  - **Spirochetes**: These are motile, spiral-shaped bacteria with a long, narrow body. They have a unique structure called the axial filament, which is a bundle of flagella that runs between the inner and outer membranes of the cell wall. They can move by rotating their body like a corkscrew. They are difficult or impossible to culture in the laboratory. Some spirochetes are pathogens, such as Treponema pallidum (causes syphilis), Borrelia burgdorferi (causes Lyme disease), and Leptospira (causes leptospirosis) .
  - **CFB group**: This is a phylum of bacteria that includes the genera Cytophaga, Fusobacterium, and Bacteroides. They share some similarities in their DNA sequences, but they are phylogenetically diverse. They are mostly anaerobic and inhabit various environments, such as soil, water, and the human gut. Cytophaga are rod-shaped bacteria that can degrade cellulose and other complex carbohydrates. Fusobacterium are spindle-shaped bacteria that can cause infections in the mouth, throat, and lungs. Bacteroides are the most abundant bacteria in the human colon and play a role in digestion and immunity .
  - **Planctomycetes**: These are aquatic bacteria that have some unusual features, such as a lack of peptidoglycan in their cell wall, a compartmentalized cytoplasm, and the ability to produce membrane-bound vesicles. Some planctomycetes can perform anammox, which is a process of anaerobic ammonia oxidation that produces nitrogen gas. This process is important for the global nitrogen cycle.
  - **Phototrophic bacteria**: These are bacteria that can use light as an energy source to synthesize organic compounds. They can be either oxygenic or anoxygenic, depending on whether they produce oxygen or not during photosynthesis. Oxygenic phototrophic bacteria include cyanobacteria, which are the most abundant and diverse group of photosynthetic prokaryotes. They have chlorophyll a and phycobilins as pigments and can fix nitrogen in specialized cells called heterocysts. Anoxygenic phototrophic bacteria include purple bacteria, green bacteria, and heliobacteria, which have different types of pigments and electron donors for photosynthesis .



# Gram-positive bacteria

- Gram-positive bacteria are bacteria that have a thick cell wall composed of peptidoglycan and teichoic acids .
- Gram-positive bacteria can be distinguished from gram-negative bacteria by the Gram stain test, which involves a chemical dye that stains the cell wall purple or blue  .
- Gram-positive bacteria include many important pathogens, such as Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus, Clostridium, Corynebacterium, Listeria, and Mycobacterium .
- Gram-positive bacteria can cause various infections, such as skin infections, pneumonia, meningitis, anthrax, tetanus, diphtheria, tuberculosis, and leprosy .
- Gram-positive bacteria can also produce useful substances, such as antibiotics, enzymes, vitamins, and probiotics.
- Gram-positive bacteria can undergo bacterial transformation, which is the uptake and incorporation of DNA from the environment.
- Gram-positive bacteria are classified into two major groups based on their cell wall structure: Firmicutes and Actinobacteria.
- Firmicutes are mostly rod-shaped or spherical bacteria that can form endospores, which are resistant structures that allow them to survive harsh conditions.
- Actinobacteria are mostly filamentous or branched bacteria that can produce secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics and pigments.



# Lichens

- Lichens are **composite organisms** that consist of a **symbiotic association** of algae (usually green) or cyanobacteria and fungi (mostly ascomycetes and basidiomycetes).
- The dominant partner is the **fungus**, which gives the lichen the majority of its characteristics, from its **thallus shape** to its **fruiting bodies**. The alga can be either a green alga or a blue-green alga, otherwise known as **cyanobacteria**.
- Lichens are found **worldwide** and occur in a variety of **environmental conditions**. They are **pioneer species**, among the first living things to grow on bare rock or areas denuded of life by a disaster.
- Lichens have different **growth forms**, such as crustose (crusty), foliose (leafy), fruticose (shrubby), or squamulose (scaly). They can also have different **reproductive structures**, such as soredia, isidia, apothecia, or pycnidia.
- Lichens have various **roles** in the ecosystem, such as **nitrogen fixation**, **soil formation**, **biomonitoring**, **bioindication**, **lichenometry**, and **food and medicine** for humans and animals.



# Algae

- Algae are a large and diverse group of photosynthetic eukaryotic organisms that are typically aquatic and include seaweeds and many single-celled forms .
- Algae are classified into different groups based on their pigments, cell wall composition, flagella, storage products, and other features.
- Some of the major groups of algae are:
  - Green algae: They have chlorophyll a and b, cellulose in their cell walls, and store starch. They are closely related to plants and can be unicellular or multicellular. Examples: Spirogyra, Chlamydomonas, Ulva.
  - Brown algae: They have chlorophyll a and c, fucoxanthin (a brown pigment), alginic acid in their cell walls, and store laminarin. They are mostly marine and multicellular. Examples: Fucus, Sargassum, kelps.
  - Red algae: They have chlorophyll a and d, phycobilins (red pigments), agar and carrageenan in their cell walls, and store floridean starch. They are mostly marine and multicellular. Examples: Porphyra, Polysiphonia, coralline algae.
  - Diatoms: They have chlorophyll a and c, fucoxanthin, silica in their cell walls, and store oil or chrysolaminarin. They are unicellular and have two overlapping shells. They are important producers in aquatic ecosystems. Examples: Cyclotella, Navicula, Pinnularia.
  - Dinoflagellates: They have chlorophyll a and c, peridinin (a yellow pigment), cellulose plates in their cell walls, and store starch or oil. They are unicellular and have two flagella. Some of them are bioluminescent or cause red tides. Examples: Ceratium, Gonyaulax, Noctiluca.
  - Euglenoids: They have chlorophyll a and b, paramylon in their cell walls, and store paramylon. They are unicellular and have one or two flagella. Some of them are heterotrophic or mixotrophic. Examples: Euglena, Phacus, Trachelomonas.
- Algae play an important role in the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals, such as:
  - Agar: A polysaccharide extracted from red algae that is used as a solidifying agent in microbiology, biotechnology, and food industry.
  - Carrageenan: A polysaccharide extracted from red algae that is used as a thickening, stabilizing, and gelling agent in food, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals.
  - Alginic acid: A polysaccharide extracted from brown algae that is used as an emulsifier, stabilizer, and binder in food, textiles, and pharmaceuticals.
  - Iodine: A chemical element obtained from brown algae that is used as an antiseptic, disinfectant, and thyroid hormone supplement.
  - Beta-carotene: A pigment extracted from green algae that is used as a food colorant, antioxidant, and vitamin A precursor.
  - Spirulina: A blue-green alga that is used as a dietary supplement, protein source, and immune booster.



# Protozoa

- Protozoa are **unicellular, microscopic, and primitive types of animals**  that belong to the kingdom Protista .
- Protozoa are **heterotrophic**, meaning they use organic carbon as a source of energy. They also have a **true nucleus** and other membrane-bound organelles.
- Protozoa can be classified into four major groups based on their mode of locomotion   :
  - **Amoeboid protozoa**: They move by pseudopodia (false feet) that extend and retract from the cell. Examples are Amoeba, Entamoeba, and Naegleria.
  - **Flagellated protozoa**: They move by one or more whip-like structures called flagella. Examples are Euglena, Trypanosoma, and Giardia.
  - **Ciliated protozoa**: They move by numerous hair-like structures called cilia. Examples are Paramecium, Stentor, and Balantidium.
  - **Sporozoan protozoa**: They do not have any locomotory organelles and are mostly parasitic. Examples are Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, and Cryptosporidium.
- Protozoa are found in various habitats, such as freshwater, marine, soil, and even inside other organisms as symbionts or parasites  .
- Protozoa play important roles in the ecosystem, such as decomposing organic matter, recycling nutrients, and serving as food sources for other organisms  .
- Protozoa can also cause diseases in humans and animals, such as malaria, sleeping sickness, dysentery, and leishmaniasis   .



# Helminthes

- Helminthes are **worm-like parasites** that can infect humans and other animals .
- They are **invertebrates** characterized by elongated, flat or round bodies .
- They are classified into three major groups based on their general external shape and the host organ they inhabit:
  - **Flukes (trematodes)**: flat, leaf-shaped worms that live in the liver, lungs, blood vessels, or intestines of their hosts .
  - **Tapeworms (cestodes)**: flat, ribbon-like worms that live in the intestines of their hosts and have a scolex (head) with hooks and suckers for attachment .
  - **Roundworms (nematodes)**: cylindrical, thread-like worms that live in the intestines, blood, lymphatic system, or tissues of their hosts and have a complete digestive system .
- Helminthes have complex life cycles that involve one or more intermediate hosts and a definitive host .
- Helminthes can cause various diseases in humans, such as **schistosomiasis**, **taeniasis**, **ascariasis**, **filariasis**, and **trichinosis**  .
- Helminthes can be diagnosed by microscopic examination of stool, urine, blood, or tissue samples, or by serological tests .
- Helminthes can be treated by anthelmintic drugs that target specific biochemical pathways or structures of the worms .
- Helminthes can be prevented by improving sanitation, hygiene, and vector control, and by avoiding raw or undercooked meat or fish that may contain larvae .

# Viral Structures

- Viruses are noncellular genetic elements that use a living cell for their replication and have an extracellular state.
- Viruses are ultramicroscopic particles containing nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein shell called a capsid .
- Some viruses have an outer envelope composed of lipids and proteins.
- Viruses are very diverse and can be classified by their shape, size, genome type, host range, and mode of replication.
- The basic structure of all viruses includes three main components:
  - Nucleic acid: The nucleic acid of a virus is found within its inner core that contains the genetic information for the synthesis of proteins and replication. The nucleic acid can be either DNA or RNA, single-stranded or double-stranded, linear or circular, segmented or non-segmented.
  - Capsid: The capsid is a protein layer or covering that forms a shell enclosing the genetic material of the virus. The capsid is composed of subunits called capsomeres, which can be identical or different. The capsid protects the nucleic acid from degradation and determines the shape of the virus.
  - Envelope: Many viruses have a lipoprotein bilayer that encloses the capsid. The envelope is derived from the host cell membrane and contains viral proteins called spikes or peplomers, which are involved in attachment and entry of the virus into the host cell . The envelope makes the virus more susceptible to environmental factors such as heat and detergents.
- The shape of the virus is determined by the arrangement of the capsomeres in the capsid. There are three main types of viral shapes:
  - Helical: The capsomeres are arranged in a helix around the nucleic acid, forming a rod-shaped or filamentous structure . Examples of helical viruses are tobacco mosaic virus, rabies virus, and influenza virus.
  - Icosahedral: The capsomeres are arranged in a symmetrical pattern of 20 triangular faces, forming a spherical structure . Examples of icosahedral viruses are poliovirus, adenovirus, and herpesvirus.
  - Complex: The capsomeres are arranged in a combination of helical and icosahedral structures, or have additional components such as tails, fibers, or envelopes . Examples of complex viruses are bacteriophages, poxviruses, and coronavirus.
- The size of the virus is measured by its diameter or length, and can range from 10 to 400 nanometers. The size of the virus is influenced by the type and amount of nucleic acid and the structure of the capsid and envelope. Generally, RNA viruses are smaller than DNA viruses, and enveloped viruses are larger than non-enveloped viruses.



# Viral Multiplication

Viral multiplication is the process of producing new viruses using the host cell's machinery and resources. Viruses are infectious particles that consist of a nucleic acid genome (DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein shell called a capsid. Some viruses also have a lipid envelope surrounding the capsid. Viruses can only replicate inside living cells, and they have a specific host range and tissue tropism, meaning they can only infect certain types of cells and organs.

The general steps of viral multiplication are:

- **Attachment**: The virus binds to specific receptors on the surface of the host cell, using proteins or glycoproteins on its capsid or envelope. This determines the specificity and susceptibility of the virus to the host cell.
- **Penetration**: The virus enters the host cell by different mechanisms, depending on the type of virus. Some viruses fuse their envelope with the host cell membrane and release their nucleocapsid into the cytoplasm. Some viruses are endocytosed by the host cell and escape from the endosome. Some viruses inject their genome into the host cell, leaving the capsid outside.
- **Uncoating**: The virus releases its genome from the capsid, making it available for transcription and replication. This may occur in the cytoplasm or in the nucleus, depending on the type of virus and its genome.
- **Biosynthesis**: The virus uses the host cell's enzymes, ribosomes, and other components to synthesize its own proteins and nucleic acids. The viral genome is transcribed into mRNA, which is translated into viral proteins. The viral genome is also replicated, using either the host cell's DNA or RNA polymerases, or the virus's own polymerases.
- **Assembly**: The newly synthesized viral components are assembled into new virions, either in the cytoplasm or in the nucleus, depending on the type of virus. The assembly process may involve the formation of a capsid around the genome, the acquisition of an envelope from the host cell membrane, and the incorporation of viral proteins into the capsid or envelope.
- **Release**: The new virions exit the host cell by different mechanisms, depending on the type of virus. Some viruses cause the host cell to lyse, breaking the cell membrane and releasing the virions. Some viruses bud from the host cell membrane, taking a portion of the membrane as their envelope. Some viruses are exocytosed by the host cell, using vesicles to transport the virions to the cell surface.

The viral multiplication cycle can be classified into two types, based on the fate of the host cell and the viral genome:

- **Lytic cycle**: The virus replicates and produces new virions that kill the host cell upon release. The viral genome does not integrate into the host cell's genome, and the infection is usually acute and self-limiting. Examples of viruses that undergo the lytic cycle are influenza virus, poliovirus, and rhinovirus.
- **Lysogenic cycle**: The virus integrates its genome into the host cell's genome, forming a provirus that remains latent and does not produce new virions. The provirus can be activated by certain stimuli, such as stress, UV light, or chemicals, and enter the lytic cycle. The infection is usually chronic and persistent, and can lead to oncogenesis or transformation of the host cell. Examples of viruses that undergo the lysogenic cycle are herpesvirus, HIV, and hepatitis B virus.



# Role of microorganisms in the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals

- Microorganisms are used in industry to produce a variety of organic compounds including acids, growth stimulants, and enzymes.
- Microorganisms can be genetically modified or engineered to aid in large-scale production of desired products.
- Microorganisms can also use carbon dioxide and hydrogen as substrates to produce industrial chemicals and fuels.
- Some examples of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals produced by microorganisms are:

  - Amino acids: Microbes make only the L-isomer of amino acids, which are essential for protein synthesis and human nutrition. Some amino acids, such as lysine and glutamic acid, are used as food additives and flavor enhancers .
  - Citric acid: Produced by the mold Aspergillus niger, citric acid is widely used as a preservative, flavoring agent, and acidulant in food and beverage industries. It is also used in pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and detergents .
  - Enzymes: Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions. Microbes produce various enzymes, such as amylases, proteases, lipases, and cellulases, that are used in industries such as starch, textile, leather, paper, and biofuel .
  - Antibiotics: Antibiotics are substances that inhibit the growth or kill other microorganisms. They are used to treat bacterial infections and prevent diseases. Most antibiotics are produced by fungi or bacteria, such as penicillin by Penicillium, streptomycin by Streptomyces, and tetracycline by Streptomyces aureofaciens .
  - Vaccines: Vaccines are preparations that stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies against specific pathogens. They are used to prevent or reduce the severity of infectious diseases. Some vaccines are made from attenuated or killed microorganisms, such as polio vaccine from poliovirus, or from recombinant DNA technology, such as hepatitis B vaccine from yeast cells .
  - Vitamins: Vitamins are organic compounds that are essential for normal metabolism and growth. Some vitamins, such as vitamin B12 and riboflavin, are produced by microorganisms and used as supplements or fortifiers in food and animal feed .



# Unit 2 - Functional Anatomy of Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells: Size, shape, and arrangement of bacterial cells. Structure and function of cells

- Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are the two main types of cells that make up all living organisms.
- Prokaryotic cells are simpler and smaller than eukaryotic cells. They lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They have a single circular chromosome and plasmids that carry extra genetic information. They also have ribosomes, a cell wall, a cell membrane, and sometimes a capsule, flagella, pili, or fimbriae.
- Eukaryotic cells are more complex and larger than prokaryotic cells. They have a nucleus that contains linear chromosomes and a nucleolus that produces ribosomes. They also have various membrane-bound organelles, such as mitochondria, chloroplasts, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, peroxisomes, vacuoles, and vesicles. They have a cell membrane, a cytoskeleton, and sometimes a cell wall, cilia, or flagella.
- Bacterial cells are prokaryotic cells that can have different sizes, shapes, and arrangements. The size of bacterial cells ranges from 0.2 to 10 micrometers in diameter. The shape of bacterial cells can be classified into three main categories: cocci (spherical), bacilli (rod-shaped), and spirilla (spiral-shaped). The arrangement of bacterial cells can be determined by their pattern of division and attachment. Some common arrangements are: diplococci (pairs of cocci), streptococci (chains of cocci), staphylococci (clusters of cocci), diplobacilli (pairs of bacilli), streptobacilli (chains of bacilli), and spirochetes (flexible spirilla).
- The structure and function of cells are related to their adaptation to different environments and functions. For example, prokaryotic cells have a cell wall that protects them from osmotic pressure and provides shape and rigidity. Some prokaryotic cells have a capsule that helps them adhere to surfaces and evade the immune system. Some prokaryotic cells have flagella that enable them to move and sense their surroundings. Some prokaryotic cells have pili or fimbriae that facilitate attachment and genetic exchange. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus that separates the genetic material from the cytoplasm and regulates gene expression. Eukaryotic cells have mitochondria that produce energy through cellular respiration. Eukaryotic cells have chloroplasts that perform photosynthesis and produce organic molecules. Eukaryotic cells have endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus that synthesize and modify proteins and lipids. # Energy production mechanism

- Energy production is the process of converting energy from one form to another in living systems.
- Energy comes to living systems through electrons occupying high energy states, either from food (respiratory chains) or from light (photosynthesis).
- Energy is exchanged between living systems and their surroundings as they use energy from the sun to perform photosynthesis or consume energy-storing molecules and release energy to the environment by doing work and releasing heat.
- Energy is subject to physical laws, such as the first law of thermodynamics, which states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed or transferred, and the second law of thermodynamics, which states that the entropy (disorder) of the universe tends to increase.
- Energy is stored in the chemical bonds of organic molecules, such as carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids, which are used as fuel and building material by living systems.
- Energy can be released from organic molecules by breaking down their bonds through a series of chemical reactions called metabolic pathways, which can be either catabolic (breaking down molecules and releasing energy) or anabolic (building up molecules and consuming energy).
- The primary energy-releasing pathways of metabolism involve the breakdown and synthesis of carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids. The most common pathway for breaking down carbohydrates is glycolysis, which converts glucose into pyruvate and produces a net gain of two molecules of ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the main energy currency of the cell .
- The pyruvate produced by glycolysis can be further oxidized in the presence of oxygen by the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle), which generates more ATP and high-energy electron carriers, such as NADH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) and FADH2 (flavin adenine dinucleotide).

The electron transport chain uses the energy from the electrons to pump protons across the membrane, creating a proton gradient that drives the synthesis of ATP by the enzyme ATP synthase.
- The final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain is oxygen, which combines with protons to form water. This process is called oxidative phosphorylation, and it produces the majority of ATP in aerobic (oxygen-requiring) organisms.
- In the absence of oxygen, pyruvate can be reduced to lactate or ethanol by the process of fermentation, which regenerates NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) from NADH, allowing glycolysis to continue. Fermentation produces much less ATP than oxidative phosphorylation, and it results in the accumulation of waste products, such as lactic acid or alcohol .

Lipids can be hydrolyzed into glycerol and fatty acids, which can enter glycolysis and the citric acid cycle, respectively. Amino acids can be deaminated (removal of the amino group) and converted into intermediates of glycolysis or the citric acid cycle, depending on their chemical structure.
- The synthesis of organic molecules from simpler precursors is called biosynthesis or anabolism, and it requires energy input from ATP or other high-energy molecules. Biosynthesis allows living systems to grow, repair, and maintain their structures, as well as to store energy for future use.
- The integration of metabolism is the coordination and regulation of the various metabolic pathways to meet the changing needs and conditions of the organism. - The metabolic diversity of living systems reflects the adaptation to different environmental and ecological niches. # Metabolic Diversity and Pathways of Energy Use

- Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions that occur in a living organism to maintain its life functions, such as growth, reproduction, and response to stimuli.
- Metabolic diversity mainly refers to the different metabolic strategies that organisms have evolved to obtain energy from various sources, such as light, organic molecules, or inorganic molecules.
- Energy is the capacity to do work, and it can be stored in different forms, such as chemical, mechanical, electrical, or thermal energy.
- Energy metabolism is the general process by which living cells acquire and use the energy needed to stay alive, to grow, and to reproduce.
- Energy metabolism involves two types of reactions: catabolic and anabolic reactions.
  - Catabolic reactions are those that break down complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy in the process. For example, the breakdown of glucose into carbon dioxide and water is a catabolic reaction that releases chemical energy that can be used to make ATP, the universal energy currency of cells.
  - Anabolic reactions are those that build up complex molecules from simpler ones, consuming energy in the process. For example, the synthesis of proteins from amino acids is an anabolic reaction that requires energy from ATP.
- The energy released or consumed in a chemical reaction can be measured by its change in free energy, which is the portion of energy that is available to do work. The change in free energy is denoted by ΔG, and it can be calculated by the following equation: ΔG = ΔH - TΔS, where ΔH is the change in enthalpy (heat content), T is the absolute temperature, and ΔS is the change in entropy (disorder).
  - A reaction is spontaneous (or exergonic) if it has a negative ΔG, meaning that it releases free energy. A reaction is non-spontaneous (or endergonic) if it has a positive ΔG, meaning that it requires free energy. A reaction is at equilibrium if it has a zero ΔG, meaning that it does not change the free energy of the system.
- Cells use different metabolic pathways, or types of chemical reactions, to produce the right kind of energy to fuel different activities. The metabolic pathways can be broadly divided into three categories based on how quickly they need energy and how much of it they need:
  - The phosphagen system (or ATP-PC system) is the fastest and simplest way to produce energy, but it can only provide a small amount of energy for a short duration (about 10 seconds). It involves the use of stored ATP and creatine phosphate (CP) in the muscle cells to regenerate ATP from ADP. This system is used for high-intensity and short-duration activities, such as sprinting or lifting weights.
  - Glycolysis is the next fastest way to produce energy, but it can only provide a moderate amount of energy for a moderate duration (about 2 minutes). # Glycolysis

- Glycolysis is a metabolic pathway that converts glucose, a six-carbon sugar, into two molecules of pyruvate, a three-carbon compound.
- Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm of the cell and does not require oxygen. It is the first stage of cellular respiration, which produces energy in the form of ATP and NADH from glucose.
- Glycolysis consists of ten enzyme-catalyzed reactions that can be divided into two phases: the preparatory phase and the payoff phase.
- In the preparatory phase, two molecules of ATP are used to phosphorylate glucose and split it into two molecules of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P).
- In the payoff phase, each G3P molecule is oxidized and phosphorylated, producing two molecules of NADH and four molecules of ATP. The final product is two molecules of pyruvate.
- Glycolysis is an ancient metabolic pathway that is found in most living organisms. It can function in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions, depending on the availability of oxygen and the type of organism.
- In aerobic conditions, pyruvate can enter the mitochondria and undergo further oxidation in the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain, producing more ATP and CO2.
- In anaerobic conditions, pyruvate can be reduced to lactate or ethanol, depending on the organism, to regenerate NAD+ for glycolysis to continue. This process is called fermentation.



# Formation of Acetyl Co-A

- Acetyl Co-A (acetyl coenzyme A) is a molecule that participates in many biochemical reactions in protein, carbohydrate and lipid metabolism.
- Its main function is to deliver the acetyl group to the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) to be oxidized for energy production.
- Acetyl Co-A formation occurs inside or outside the cell mitochondria. As a metabolite (a substance necessary for metabolism), acetyl Co-A must be freely available.
- It can be produced via the catabolism (breakdown) of carbohydrates (glucose) and lipids (fatty acids).
- Acetyl Co-A formation most commonly occurs during glucose catabolism. After carbohydrates have been broken down by digestive enzymes, the first stage of cellular glucose metabolism or glycolysis can begin. Glycolysis is the breaking down of glucose molecules.
- Glycolysis produces two molecules of pyruvic acid (pyruvate) per molecule of glucose. Pyruvate then enters the mitochondria, where it is converted into acetyl Co-A by a complex enzyme called pyruvate dehydrogenase.
- The overall formation reaction of acetyl Co-A from pyruvate may be represented as:

  pyruvic acid + CoA + NAD+ → acetyl Co-A + NADH + H+ + CO2

- This reaction may be called the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvic acid to acetyl Co-A. It involves the removal of a carboxyl group (COOH) from pyruvate, releasing carbon dioxide, and the addition of a coenzyme A (CoA) to the remaining two-carbon fragment, forming acetyl Co-A.
- The reaction also transfers two electrons and a proton (H+) from pyruvate to the coenzyme NAD+, forming NADH. NADH is a high-energy electron carrier that can donate its electrons to the electron transport chain for ATP synthesis.
- Acetyl Co-A can also be formed from fatty acids through a process called beta-oxidation. Beta-oxidation occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and involves the sequential removal of two-carbon units from the fatty acid chain. Each two-carbon unit is then attached to CoA, forming acetyl Co-A.

# Kreb cycle

The Kreb cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is a series of chemical reactions that occur in the mitochondria of aerobic organisms. It is the second stage of cellular respiration, following glycolysis and preceding the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation. The Kreb cycle produces energy by oxidizing acetyl-CoA, which is derived from the breakdown of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. The Kreb cycle also provides precursors for the synthesis of certain amino acids and other biomolecules, and generates reducing agents (NADH and FADH2) that donate electrons to the electron transport chain.

The main steps of the Kreb cycle are:

- Acetyl-CoA (2 carbon atoms) combines with oxaloacetate (4 carbon atoms) to form citrate (6 carbon atoms) in a condensation reaction catalyzed by citrate synthase. This is the first and rate-limiting step of the cycle.
- Citrate is converted to isocitrate (6 carbon atoms) by a reversible isomerization reaction catalyzed by aconitase. This involves the removal and addition of a water molecule.
- Isocitrate is oxidized to alpha-ketoglutarate (5 carbon atoms) by a dehydrogenation reaction catalyzed by isocitrate dehydrogenase. This is the first oxidative decarboxylation reaction of the cycle, which releases a molecule of carbon dioxide and reduces a molecule of NAD+ to NADH.
- Alpha-ketoglutarate is further oxidized to succinyl-CoA (4 carbon atoms) by another oxidative decarboxylation reaction catalyzed by alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase. This also releases a molecule of carbon dioxide and reduces a molecule of NAD+ to NADH.
- Succinyl-CoA is converted to succinate (4 carbon atoms) by a substrate-level phosphorylation reaction catalyzed by succinyl-CoA synthetase. This involves the transfer of a phosphate group from succinyl-CoA to GDP, forming GTP, which can then be converted to ATP by nucleoside diphosphate kinase. This is the only step of the cycle that directly produces ATP (or GTP).
- Succinate is oxidized to fumarate (4 carbon atoms) by a dehydrogenation reaction catalyzed by succinate dehydrogenase. This is the only step of the cycle that occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where succinate dehydrogenase is part of complex II of the electron transport chain. This reaction reduces a molecule of FAD to FADH2, which can then donate electrons to the electron transport chain.
- Fumarate is converted to malate (4 carbon atoms) by a hydration reaction catalyzed by fumarase. This involves the addition of a water molecule across the double bond of fumarate.
- Malate is oxidized to oxaloacetate (4 carbon atoms) by a dehydrogenation reaction catalyzed by malate dehydrogenase. This reduces a molecule of NAD+ to NADH, which can then donate electrons to the electron transport chain.

The Kreb cycle completes one turn for each molecule of acetyl-CoA that enters it. Since each molecule of glucose produces two molecules of acetyl-CoA in the previous stage of glycolysis and pyruvate oxidation, the Kreb cycle turns twice for each molecule of glucose. The net products of the Kreb cycle per molecule of glucose are:

- 4 molecules of carbon dioxide (released as waste)
- 6 molecules of NADH (carrying high-energy electrons)
- 2 molecules of FADH2 (carrying high-energy electrons)
- 2 molecules of ATP (or GTP) (providing immediate energy)

The Kreb cycle is regulated by several factors, such as the availability of substrates (acetyl-CoA, NAD+, FAD, ADP, etc.), the feedback inhibition by products (NADH, ATP, etc.), and the allosteric modulation by other metabolites (citrate, calcium, etc.). The Kreb cycle is also influenced by hormonal signals, such as insulin and glucagon, that affect the metabolism of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. The Kreb cycle is essential for the energy production and biosynthesis of aerobic organisms, and its dysfunction can lead to various diseases, such as diabetes, cancer, and neurodegeneration.
