

# KCS

KCS stands for Knowledge-Centered Service, a methodology that aims to improve service delivery and knowledge management in service organizations. Some of the main features and benefits of KCS are :

- KCS is based on the principle of "capture, structure, reuse, and improve" knowledge as part of the service process.
- KCS enables service agents to create and update knowledge articles in real time, based on the issues and solutions they encounter during service interactions.
- KCS empowers service agents to search and access the existing knowledge base before escalating or creating new tickets, reducing duplication and increasing efficiency.
- KCS fosters a culture of collaboration and feedback among service agents, who can rate, comment, and flag knowledge articles for quality and relevance.
- KCS improves customer satisfaction and loyalty by providing consistent, accurate, and timely solutions to their problems.
- KCS reduces costs and increases productivity by minimizing rework, increasing first-contact resolution, and freeing up service agents for more complex and value-added tasks.

KCS is not to be confused with KCS, the abbreviation for Kansas City Southern, a railroad company that operates in the United States and Mexico .

- **Brief history of microbiology**
  - Microbiology is the scientific study of microorganisms, which are small or minute living things that are usually invisible to the naked eye.
  - Microbiology began with the invention of the microscope in the 16th century. Some of the pioneers of microbiology are:
    - Robert Hooke (1635-1703), an English scientist who observed fungi and plant cells under a microscope and coined the term "cell" .
    - Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1632-1723), a Dutch draper and amateur microscopist who discovered bacteria, protozoa, and other microorganisms in various samples of water, saliva, and dental plaque  .
    - Ferdinand Cohn (1828-1898), a German botanist and microbiologist who classified bacteria into four groups based on their shape and founded the field of bacteriology.
  - Microbiology also developed with the advancement of various techniques and theories, such as:
    - Staining, which is the use of dyes to enhance the contrast and visibility of microorganisms under a microscope.
    - Culture, which is the growth of microorganisms in artificial media under controlled conditions.
    - Koch's postulates, which are a set of criteria to establish the causal relationship between a microbe and a disease.
    - Pasteurization, which is the heating of liquids to kill harmful microorganisms and prevent spoilage.
    - Immunology, which is the study of the immune system and its response to pathogens.
    - Germ theory, which is the idea that many diseases are caused by microorganisms.
    - Antibiotics, which are substances that can kill or inhibit the growth of bacteria.
    - Biotechnology, which is the use of microorganisms or their products for various applications in medicine, agriculture, industry, and environment.

- **Types of microorganisms**
  - Microorganisms are diverse and can be classified into different groups based on their structure, function, and phylogeny. Some of the major types of microorganisms are:
    - Bacteria, which are prokaryotic cells that lack a nucleus and have a cell wall made of peptidoglycan. They can be found in almost every habitat and can perform various metabolic processes, such as photosynthesis, respiration, fermentation, and nitrogen fixation.
    - Archaea, which are prokaryotic cells that lack a nucleus and have a cell wall made of different substances than bacteria. They are often adapted to extreme environments, such as high temperature, salinity, acidity, or alkalinity. They can also perform various metabolic processes, such as methanogenesis, sulfate reduction, and halorespiration.
    - Eukarya, which are eukaryotic cells that have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They include four kingdoms of microorganisms: protists, fungi, plants, and animals.
      - Protists, which are mostly unicellular eukaryotes that can be autotrophic or heterotrophic. They include algae, protozoa, slime molds, and water molds.
      - Fungi, which are multicellular or unicellular eukaryotes that are heterotrophic and decompose organic matter. They include molds, yeasts, mushrooms, and lichens.
      - Plants, which are multicellular eukaryotes that are autotrophic and use photosynthesis to make organic molecules. They include algae, mosses, ferns, and flowering plants.
      - Animals, which are multicellular eukaryotes that are heterotrophic and ingest organic matter. They include sponges, worms, insects, and vertebrates.
    - Viruses, which are acellular entities that consist of a nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat. They are obligate intracellular parasites that can infect all types of cells and cause various diseases.



### Brief history of microbiology

- Microbiology is the scientific study of microorganisms, which are usually too small to be seen with the naked eye.
- Microbiology has its origins in the 16th century, when some people observed the existence of tiny creatures in various substances, such as vinegar and milk.
- The invention of the microscope in the 17th century enabled the systematic observation and documentation of microorganisms.
- Robert Hooke (1635-1703) was an English scientist who used a microscope to examine cork and other plant materials. He coined the term "cell" to describe the basic unit of life.
- Anton van Leeuwenhoek (1632-1723) was a Dutch draper and amateur microscopist who made his own lenses and microscopes. He was the first to observe and describe bacteria, protozoa, fungi, and other microorganisms, which he called "animalcules".
- In the 18th and 19th centuries, microbiology developed as a branch of biology and medicine, with contributions from many scientists, such as Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch, Joseph Lister, and Alexander Fleming.
- They discovered the roles of microorganisms in fermentation, disease, immunity, and antibiotics, and developed methods to isolate, culture, and identify them.
- In the 20th and 21st centuries, microbiology expanded to include the study of viruses, molecular biology, genetics, biotechnology, and ecology of microorganisms.
- Microbiology has applications in various fields, such as agriculture, industry, health, and environment.



### Types of microorganisms

- Microorganisms or microbes are microscopic organisms that exist as unicellular, multicellular, or cell clusters .
- Microorganisms are widespread in nature and are beneficial to life, but some can cause serious harm.
- Microorganisms can be divided into six major types: bacteria, archaea, fungi, protozoa, algae, and viruses .
- Each type has a characteristic cellular composition, morphology, mean of locomotion, and reproduction.

#### Bacteria
- Bacteria are prokaryotic cells, meaning that they do not have a nucleus or other membrane-bound organelles .
- Bacteria are the most abundant and diverse group of microorganisms, found in almost every environment on Earth .
- Bacteria can be classified by their shape, cell wall structure, metabolic activity, genetic makeup, and response to oxygen .
- Bacteria can cause diseases such as tuberculosis, cholera, syphilis, and anthrax, but they can also be beneficial for processes such as fermentation, decomposition, nitrogen fixation, and bioremediation .

#### Archaea
- Archaea are also prokaryotic cells, but they differ from bacteria in their genetic, biochemical, and structural features .
- Archaea are often found in extreme environments, such as hot springs, salt lakes, deep sea vents, and acidic soils, where they are called extremophiles .
- Archaea are not known to cause any human diseases, but they play important roles in the global carbon and nitrogen cycles .

#### Fungi
- Fungi are eukaryotic cells, meaning that they have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles .
- Fungi are heterotrophic, meaning that they obtain their energy from organic molecules .
- Fungi can be unicellular, such as yeasts, or multicellular, such as molds and mushrooms .
- Fungi can cause diseases such as ringworm, athlete's foot, candidiasis, and histoplasmosis, but they can also be beneficial for decomposing organic matter, producing antibiotics, and fermenting foods and beverages .

#### Protozoa
- Protozoa are eukaryotic cells that are usually unicellular and motile .
- Protozoa can be classified by their mode of locomotion, such as flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia .
- Protozoa can be free-living or parasitic, meaning that they can live independently or depend on a host organism for survival .
- Protozoa can cause diseases such as malaria, amoebic dysentery, giardiasis, and sleeping sickness, but they can also be beneficial for grazing on bacteria and algae, recycling nutrients, and serving as food sources for other organisms .

#### Algae
- Algae are eukaryotic cells that are usually photosynthetic, meaning that they can convert light energy into chemical energy .
- Algae can be unicellular, such as diatoms and dinoflagellates, or multicellular, such as seaweeds and kelps .
- Algae can be found in aquatic and moist environments, where they form the base of many food chains .
- Algae can cause problems such as algal blooms, red tides, and water pollution, but they can also be beneficial for producing oxygen, biofuels, and food supplements .

#### Viruses
- Viruses are acellular, meaning that they are not composed of cells .
- Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites, meaning that they can only replicate inside a living host cell .
- Viruses consist of a nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat, and sometimes a lipid envelope .
- Viruses can infect all types



### Basic idea of domain bacteria

- Domain bacteria is one of the three domains of life, along with domain archaea and domain eukarya.
- Domain bacteria includes the prokaryotes people encounter on an everyday basis. Prokaryotes are organisms that lack a membrane-bound nucleus and other organelles.
- Most bacterial species are heterotrophic; that is, they acquire their food from organic matter. The largest number of bacteria are saprobic, meaning that they feed on dead or decaying organic matter. A few bacterial species are parasitic; these bacteria live within host organisms and cause disease.
- Bacteria can also be classified by their shape, cell wall structure, metabolic pathways, and response to oxygen and other environmental factors.
- The domain bacteria is divided into several phyla, such as proteobacteria, cyanobacteria, firmicutes, actinobacteria, and spirochaetes. Each phylum contains different groups of bacteria with distinct characteristics and functions.
- Bacteria play important roles in the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals, such as antibiotics, enzymes, vitamins, hormones, and biofuels. Bacteria also participate in various biogeochemical cycles, such as the nitrogen cycle, the carbon cycle, and the sulfur cycle.
- Bacteria are essential for the health and survival of many living organisms, including humans. Bacteria help in digestion, immunity, and synthesis of vitamins and other nutrients. Bacteria also form symbiotic relationships with other organisms, such as lichens, algae, protozoa, and helminthes.



### Proteobacteria

- Proteobacteria is a phylum of gram-negative bacteria discovered by Carl Woese in the 1980s based on nucleotide sequence homology .
- Proteobacteria are further classified into the classes alpha-, beta-, gamma-, delta- and epsilonproteobacteria, each class having separate orders, families, genera, and species.
- Proteobacteria include a wide variety of pathogenic and free-living bacteria, such as Escherichia, Salmonella, Vibrio, Yersinia, Legionella, and many others.
- Proteobacteria are also involved in nitrogen fixation, carbon monoxide oxidation, and sulfur cycling .
- Proteobacteria have diverse shapes, metabolisms, and habitats, ranging from soil and water to animal and plant hosts .

Some examples of proteobacteria are:

- Alphaproteobacteria: oligotrophs, organisms capable of living in low-nutrient environments such as deep oceanic sediments, glacial ice, or deep undersurface soil. Some examples are Rhizobium (nitrogen-fixing symbionts of legumes), Rickettsia (intracellular parasites of animals), and Caulobacter (stalked bacteria).
- Betaproteobacteria: mostly aerobic or facultative anaerobic bacteria that can use various organic compounds as electron donors and acceptors. Some examples are Neisseria (human pathogens causing gonorrhea and meningitis), Nitrosomonas (nitrifying bacteria), and Burkholderia (plant and animal pathogens).
- Gammaproteobacteria: the largest and most diverse class of proteobacteria, comprising many medically and environmentally important bacteria. Some examples are Escherichia (intestinal bacteria and pathogens), Pseudomonas (opportunistic pathogens and bioremediators), and Vibrio (marine bacteria and pathogens).
- Deltaproteobacteria: mostly anaerobic bacteria that are involved in sulfur and iron cycling. Some examples are Desulfovibrio (sulfate-reducing bacteria), Bdellovibrio (predatory bacteria), and Myxococcus (gliding bacteria that form fruiting bodies).
- Epsilonproteobacteria: mostly microaerophilic or anaerobic bacteria that inhabit extreme environments such as hot springs, hydrothermal vents, and animal guts. Some examples are Helicobacter (human pathogens causing ulcers and gastritis), Campylobacter (animal pathogens causing diarrhea), and Wolinella (hydrogen-oxidizing bacteria).



### Nonproteobacteria Gram-Negative Bacteria

- Nonproteobacteria are a diverse group of bacteria that do not belong to the phylum Proteobacteria, which contains the majority of gram-negative bacteria.
- Nonproteobacteria can be either gram-negative or gram-positive, depending on the presence or absence of an outer membrane in their cell wall.
- Nonproteobacteria can have various metabolic modes, such as photoautotrophy, chemoheterotrophy, or chemoautotrophy.
- Some examples of nonproteobacteria are:

  - **Chlamydia**: Obligate intracellular parasites that infect animals and humans, causing diseases such as trachoma, chlamydia, and lymphogranuloma venereum. They have a unique developmental cycle that involves two forms: elementary bodies and reticulate bodies.
  - **Spirochetes**: Motile, spiral-shaped bacteria that have a flexible cell wall and axial filaments for locomotion. They can be free-living or pathogenic, causing diseases such as syphilis, Lyme disease, and leptospirosis. They are difficult or impossible to culture in the laboratory.
  - **CFB group**: A phylum of bacteria that includes the genera Cytophaga, Fusobacterium, and Bacteroides. They share some similarities in their DNA sequences, but are phylogenetically diverse. They are mostly anaerobic and inhabit various environments, such as soil, water, and the human gut. They can degrade complex organic molecules, such as cellulose, chitin, and polysaccharides.
  - **Planctomycetes**: A phylum of bacteria that have unusual features, such as a lack of peptidoglycan in their cell wall, a compartmentalized cytoplasm, and the ability to bud. They are aquatic and can be found in freshwater, marine, and brackish habitats. They can perform various metabolic processes, such as nitrification, denitrification, and anammox.
  - **Phototrophic bacteria**: A group of bacteria that can use light as an energy source for biosynthesis. They can be either oxygenic or anoxygenic, depending on whether they produce oxygen or not during photosynthesis. They can also have different types of pigments, such as chlorophylls, bacteriochlorophylls, or carotenoids. Some examples of phototrophic bacteria are cyanobacteria, purple bacteria, green bacteria, and heliobacteria.



### Gram-positive bacteria

- Gram-positive bacteria are bacteria that have a **thick cell wall** composed of peptidoglycan and teichoic acids  .
- Gram-positive bacteria can be distinguished from gram-negative bacteria by the **Gram stain test**, which involves a chemical dye that stains the cell wall purple or blue  .
- Gram-positive bacteria include many **important pathogens** such as Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus, Clostridium, Corynebacterium, Listeria, and Mycobacterium .
- Gram-positive bacteria can cause various **infections** such as pneumonia, meningitis, anthrax, tetanus, diphtheria, tuberculosis, and leprosy .
- Gram-positive bacteria can also have **beneficial roles** such as producing antibiotics, probiotics, enzymes, and fermented foods.
- Gram-positive bacteria can undergo **bacterial transformation**, which is the uptake and expression of foreign DNA from the environment.
- Gram-positive bacteria are usually **susceptible** to antibiotics that target the cell wall, such as penicillin, vancomycin, and bacitracin . However, some gram-positive bacteria have developed **resistance** to these drugs, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) .



### Lichens

- Lichens are **composite organisms** that consist of a **symbiotic association** of algae (usually green) or cyanobacteria and fungi (mostly ascomycetes and basidiomycetes).
- The dominant partner is the **fungus**, which gives the lichen the majority of its characteristics, from its **thallus shape** to its **fruiting bodies**. The alga or cyanobacterium provides **photosynthesis** and **nitrogen fixation** for the fungus.
- Lichens are found **worldwide** and occur in a variety of **environmental conditions**. They are **pioneer species**, among the first living things to grow on bare rock or areas denuded of life by a disaster.
- Lichens have different **growth forms**, such as crustose, foliose, fruticose, squamulose, and leprose. They also have different **reproduction methods**, such as vegetative propagation, soredia, isidia, and sexual spores.
- Lichens have various **roles** in the ecosystem, such as **bioindicators** of air quality, **decomposers** of organic matter, **soil stabilizers** and **erosion preventers**, and **food sources** and **habitats** for other organisms.
- Lichens also have various **applications** in human activities, such as **dyes**, **perfumes**, **medicines**, **litmus paper**, and **lichenometry**.



### Algae
- Algae are a large and diverse group of photosynthetic eukaryotic organisms that are typically aquatic and include seaweeds and many single-celled forms  .
- Algae are not plants, but they share some characteristics with them, such as having chlorophyll, cell walls, and the ability to produce oxygen .
- Algae belong to the kingdom Protista, which is a polyphyletic group of organisms that do not fit into any other kingdom.
- Algae have many types of life cycles, ranging from simple asexual reproduction to complex alternation of generations.
- Algae vary in size from microscopic to macroscopic, and they can be found in almost every habitat on Earth, from freshwater to marine, from soil to snow, from hot springs to polar regions.
- Algae are important for the biosphere, as they produce a large amount of oxygen, serve as food and habitat for many aquatic animals, and are sources of biofuels, bioplastics, pharmaceuticals, and other useful products .



### Protozoa

- Protozoa are **microscopic unicellular eukaryotes** that have a relatively complex internal structure and carry out complex metabolic activities    .
- Some protozoa have structures for **propulsion or other types of movement**, such as flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia    .
- Protozoa are either **free-living or parasitic**, that feed on organic matter such as other microorganisms or organic tissues and debris   .
- Protozoa are **heterotrophic**, using organic carbon as a source of energy.
- Protozoa have **one or more nuclei** and may have specialized organelles, such as contractile vacuoles, food vacuoles, or plastids  .
- Protozoa are **nonfilamentous**, in contrast to fungi and some algae.
- Protozoa are currently classified into **six phyla** on the basis of light and electron microscopic morphology :
  - **Sarcomastigophora**, which includes flagellates and amoebae.
  - **Apicomplexa**, which includes intracellular parasites such as Plasmodium and Toxoplasma.
  - **Ciliophora**, which includes ciliates such as Paramecium and Stentor.
  - **Microspora**, which includes spore-forming parasites such as Encephalitozoon and Nosema.
  - **Ascetosporea**, which includes parasites of marine invertebrates such as Myxozoa and Orthonectida.
  - **Myxozoa**, which includes parasites of fish and annelids such as Myxobolus and Ceratomyxa.
- Protozoa are **diverse and ecologically important** groups of organisms, playing roles in nutrient cycling, decomposition, symbiosis, and pathogenesis   .
- Protozoa can reproduce by **asexual or sexual methods**, depending on the species and environmental conditions  .
- Protozoa can exhibit different forms of **life cycles**, such as haploid, diploid, or alternation of generations  .
- Protozoa can be identified by their **morphological, biochemical, molecular, and immunological characteristics**, as well as by their **host range, transmission mode, and clinical manifestations**   .



### Helminthes
- Helminthes are **parasitic worms** that infect various hosts, such as humans, animals, and plants .
- Helminthes can be classified into two major groups: **flatworms** (platyhelminths) and **roundworms** (nematodes) .
- Flatworms have a **flat** and **leaf-like** body shape, and include **flukes** and **tapeworms** .
- Flukes are **trematodes** that have a **sucker** at one or both ends of their body, and infect the **blood**, **liver**, **lungs**, or **intestines** of their hosts.
- Tapeworms are **cestodes** that have a **scolex** (head) with **hooks** and **suckers**, and a **strobila** (body) composed of **proglottids** (segments) that contain **eggs**. Tapeworms infect the **intestines** of their hosts and can cause **malnutrition**, **abdominal pain**, and **cysticercosis**.
- Roundworms have a **cylindrical** and **thread-like** body shape, and include **ascarids**, **hookworms**, **pinworms**, **filarias**, and **trichinellas** .
- Ascarids are **large** and **whitish** worms that infect the **intestines** of their hosts and can cause **intestinal obstruction**, **malabsorption**, and **pneumonia**.
- Hookworms are **small** and **reddish-brown** worms that have **hook-like** mouthparts that attach to the **intestinal wall** of their hosts and suck **blood**. Hookworms can cause **anemia**, **diarrhea**, and **malnutrition**.
- Pinworms are **tiny** and **white** worms that infect the **colon** and **rectum** of their hosts and cause **itching** and **irritation** around the **anus**.
- Filarias are **thin** and **hair-like** worms that infect the **lymphatic system** or **subcutaneous tissues** of their hosts and cause **lymphedema**, **elephantiasis**, and **river blindness**.
- Trichinellas are **microscopic** and **coiled** worms that infect the **muscles** of their hosts and cause **fever**, **muscle pain**, and **inflammation**.
- Helminthes have different **life cycles** depending on their species, but they generally involve one or more **intermediate hosts** and a **definitive host**.
- Helminthes can be detected by **microscopic examination** of **stool**, **blood**, **urine**, or **tissue** samples, or by **immunological tests**.
- Helminthes can be treated by **antihelminthic drugs** that target their **metabolism**, **nervous system**, or **reproduction**.
- Helminthes can be prevented by **improving sanitation**, **wearing protective clothing**, **cooking food properly**, and **avoiding contact** with infected hosts.



### Viral Structures

- Viruses are noncellular genetic elements that use a living cell for their replication and have an extracellular state.
- Viruses are ultramicroscopic particles containing nucleic acid surrounded by protein, and in some cases, other macromolecular components such as a membranelike envelope.
- Viruses are observed in a wide range of shapes and sizes, but these shapes are constant in a particular viral family.
- The basic structure of all viruses includes nucleic acid, either DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) or RNA (Ribonucleic Acid) called a genome which is enveloped by a protein coat called a capsid .
- Many viruses have a lipoprotein bilayer that encloses the capsid, called an envelope .
- The nucleic acid of a virus contains the genetic information for the synthesis of proteins and replication.
- The capsid is composed of subunits called capsomeres, which may have different shapes and arrangements depending on the type of virus.
- The envelope may have spikes or glycoproteins that help the virus attach to the host cell or evade the host immune system .
- The two main classes of viruses are RNA viruses and DNA viruses, based on the type of nucleic acid they contain.
- RNA viruses have a single-stranded or double-stranded RNA genome, and may be positive-sense (meaning their RNA can be directly translated into proteins) or negative-sense (meaning their RNA has to be converted into a complementary positive-sense RNA before translation).
- DNA viruses have a single-stranded or double-stranded DNA genome, and may be linear or circular, and may have single or multiple segments.
- Some examples of viral shapes are icosahedral (having 20 triangular faces), helical (having a cylindrical shape), complex (having a combination of icosahedral and helical structures), and spherical (having a roughly round shape) .
- Some examples of viruses with different structures are:

### Viral Multiplication

- Viral multiplication is the process of forming new viruses by using the cellular machinery of the host cells after infection.
- Viruses have different host ranges and tissue tropisms, which determine the types of cells and organisms they can infect and multiply in.
- Viruses have different life cycles, which can be classified into two types: lytic cycle and lysogenic cycle.
- In the lytic cycle, the virus attaches to the host cell, injects its genetic material, replicates its genome and proteins, assembles new virions, and lyses the host cell to release the progeny viruses.
- In the lysogenic cycle, the virus integrates its genetic material into the host cell's genome, forming a prophage, which can remain dormant for a long time or be induced to enter the lytic cycle.
- The viral life cycle can be studied using a one-step multiplication curve, which shows the phases of inoculation, eclipse, and burst.
- The inoculation phase is when the virus attaches to the host cell and enters it.
- The eclipse phase is when the viral genome and proteins are synthesized.
- The burst phase is when the new virions are assembled and released from the host cell.
- The burst size is the number of virions produced per infected cell.
- Viruses are composed of a nucleic acid genome (DNA or RNA) and a protein coat called a capsid.
- Some viruses also have a lipid envelope derived from the host cell membrane.
- Viruses are very diverse in their shapes, sizes, and structures.



### Role of microorganisms in the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals

- Microorganisms are used in industry to produce a variety of organic compounds including acids, growth stimulants, and enzymes.
- Microorganisms can be genetically modified or engineered to aid in large-scale production of desired products.
- Some examples of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals produced by microorganisms are:

  - Amino acids: Microbes make only the L-isomer of amino acids, which are essential for protein synthesis and human nutrition. Some amino acids, such as lysine and glutamic acid, are produced by fermentation of sugars or molasses using bacteria such as Corynebacterium and Brevibacterium .
  - Citric acid: Citric acid is widely used as a food additive, preservative, and flavoring agent. It is produced by the mold Aspergillus niger, which converts glucose or sucrose into citric acid in a submerged aerobic fermentation process .
  - Enzymes: Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions. They are used in various industries such as food, detergent, textile, leather, paper, and biofuel. Some examples of enzymes produced by microorganisms are amylases, proteases, lipases, cellulases, and xylanases. These enzymes are obtained from fungi, bacteria, and actinomycetes, such as Aspergillus, Bacillus, Streptomyces, and Trichoderma .
  - Antibiotics: Antibiotics are substances that inhibit the growth or kill other microorganisms. They are used to treat bacterial infections and prevent diseases. Most antibiotics are derived from natural sources, such as fungi and bacteria. Some examples of antibiotics produced by microorganisms are penicillin, streptomycin, tetracycline, erythromycin, and cephalosporin .
  - Vitamins: Vitamins are organic compounds that are essential for normal metabolism and growth. They are obtained from food or dietary supplements. Some vitamins, such as B12, B2, and K, are produced by microorganisms, such as Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Propionibacterium .
  - Biofuels: Biofuels are renewable sources of energy that are derived from biological materials, such as plants, algae, and microorganisms. Some examples of biofuels produced by microorganisms are ethanol, biodiesel, biogas, and biohydrogen. These biofuels are obtained by fermentation, transesterification, anaerobic digestion, and photosynthesis, using microorganisms such as Saccharomyces, Escherichia, Methanobacterium, and Chlamydomonas .

- In the industrial process, microorganisms act like chemical factories. To be effective, they should liberate a large amount of a single product that can be efficiently isolated and purified. The microorganisms should be easy to maintain and cultivate, and should have genetic stability with infrequent mutations.
- The production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals by microorganisms involves several steps, such as strain selection, media optimization, fermentation, downstream processing, and quality control .
- The advantages of using microorganisms for industrial production are:

  - They are cheap and abundant sources of raw materials.
  - They have high specificity and selectivity for the desired products.
  - They can operate under mild conditions of temperature and pressure.
  - They can produce complex molecules that are difficult to synthesize chemically.
  - They can be genetically modified or engineered to enhance their productivity and diversity .

- The challenges of using microorganisms for industrial production are:

  - They are susceptible to contamination and infection by other microorganisms.
  - They may produce unwanted by-products or toxins that affect the quality and safety of the products.
  - They may lose their productivity or stability due to genetic mutations or environmental changes.
  - They may face ethical and social issues regarding the use of genetically modified or engineered organisms .



## Unit 2 - Functional Anatomy of Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells: Size, shape, and arrangement of bacterial cells. Structure and function of cells

- Prokaryotic cells are cells that lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are usually smaller and simpler than eukaryotic cells, which have a nucleus and other organelles.
- Eukaryotic cells are cells that have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are usually larger and more complex than prokaryotic cells, which lack a nucleus and other organelles.
- Bacterial cells are prokaryotic cells that belong to the domain Bacteria. They have a variety of shapes, sizes, and arrangements, depending on their species and environmental conditions.
- The most common shapes of bacterial cells are:
  - Coccus: spherical or oval
  - Bacillus: rod-shaped or cylindrical
  - Spirillum: spiral or curved
  - Vibrio: comma-shaped
  - Coccobacillus: short and plump
  - Spirochete: flexible and helical
- The most common arrangements of bacterial cells are:
  - Single: individual cells
  - Diplo: pairs of cells
  - Strepto: chains of cells
  - Staphylo: clusters of cells
  - Tetrads: groups of four cells
  - Sarcinae: groups of eight cells
  - Palisades: side-by-side rows of cells
- The structure and function of bacterial cells are determined by their cell envelope, which consists of three layers: the cell wall, the cell membrane, and the capsule.
  - The cell wall is a rigid layer that surrounds the cell membrane and provides shape, support, and protection to the cell. It also prevents the cell from bursting due to osmotic pressure. The cell wall is composed of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugar and amino acids, and may also contain other substances, such as lipopolysaccharide, teichoic acid, or mycolic acid, depending on the type of bacteria. Bacteria can be classified as gram-positive or gram-negative based on the structure and staining properties of their cell wall.
  - The cell membrane is a thin, flexible layer that encloses the cytoplasm and regulates the passage of materials in and out of the cell. It is composed of phospholipids and proteins, and may also contain hopanoids, which are sterol-like molecules that provide stability and fluidity to the membrane. The cell membrane is involved in many functions, such as respiration, photosynthesis, secretion, transport, and signal transduction.
  - The capsule is a sticky layer that surrounds the cell wall and the cell membrane and provides protection, adhesion, and virulence to the cell. It is composed of polysaccharides, proteins, or both, and may vary in thickness and composition, depending on the type of bacteria. The capsule helps the cell to evade the immune system, attach to surfaces, and form biofilms.



### Functional Anatomy of Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

### Carbohydrate Metabolism

- The most common and important disaccharides in living organisms are sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose), and maltose (glucose + glucose).
- The most common and important polysaccharides in living organisms are starch (a storage form of glucose in plants), glycogen (a storage form of glucose in animals), and cellulose (a structural component of plant cell walls).

### Glycolysis

- Glycolysis is a metabolic pathway that converts glucose, a six-carbon sugar, into two molecules of pyruvate, a three-carbon compound.
- Glycolysis does not require oxygen and can occur in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. In anaerobic conditions, pyruvate is further converted into lactic acid or ethanol.
- Glycolysis is an ancient metabolic pathway that is found in most living organisms. It is the first stage of cellular respiration, which produces energy in the form of ATP and NADH.
- Glycolysis consists of ten enzyme-catalyzed reactions that can be divided into two phases: the preparatory phase and the payoff phase.
- In the preparatory phase, two molecules of ATP are used to phosphorylate glucose and split it into two molecules of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P).
- In the payoff phase, each G3P molecule is oxidized and phosphorylated, producing two molecules of NADH and four molecules of ATP. The final product is two molecules of pyruvate.
- The net gain of glycolysis is two molecules of ATP and two molecules of NADH per molecule of glucose.

These reactions regenerate NAD+, which is essential for glycolysis to continue.

### Kreb cycle

- The Kreb cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is a series of chemical reactions that occur in the mitochondria of aerobic organisms  .
- The Kreb cycle is the second stage of cellular respiration, following glycolysis and preceding the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation  .
- The Kreb cycle produces energy by oxidizing acetyl-CoA, which is derived from the breakdown of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins   .
- The Kreb cycle consists of eight steps, each catalyzed by a specific enzyme  :
  - Step 1: Acetyl-CoA combines with oxaloacetate to form citrate, releasing coenzyme A.
  - Step 2: Citrate is converted to isocitrate by an isomerase enzyme.
  - Step 3: Isocitrate is oxidized to alpha-ketoglutarate, releasing a molecule of carbon dioxide and a molecule of NADH.
  - Step 4: Alpha-ketoglutarate is oxidized to succinyl-CoA, releasing another molecule of carbon dioxide and NADH.
  - Step 5: Succinyl-CoA is converted to succinate, releasing a molecule of GTP (or ATP in some organisms).
  - Step 6: Succinate is oxidized to fumarate, reducing a molecule of FAD to FADH2.
  - Step 7: Fumarate is hydrated to malate by an enzyme called fumarase.
  - Step 8: Malate is oxidized to oxaloacetate, regenerating NADH and completing the cycle.
- The Kreb cycle produces two molecules of carbon dioxide, three molecules of NADH, one molecule of FADH2, and one molecule of GTP (or ATP) per acetyl-CoA molecule that enters the cycle   .
- The Kreb cycle also provides intermediates for the synthesis of certain amino acids, such as aspartate, glutamate, and alanine .
- The Kreb cycle is regulated by the availability of substrates (such as acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate) and the feedback inhibition of enzymes (such as citrate synthase and isocitrate dehydrogenase) by the products of the cycle (such as ATP, NADH, and citrate)   .
- The Kreb cycle is one of the oldest metabolic pathways, as it is found in almost all living organisms that use oxygen for respiration . It is thought to have evolved from an ancient anaerobic pathway that used sulfur instead of oxygen as the final electron acceptor.



### Electron Transport System and Oxidative Phosphorylation

- Electron Transport System (ETS) is a series of proteins and electron carriers located in the inner mitochondrial membrane that transfers electrons donated by the reduced molecules NADH and FADH2 through a series of electron acceptors, to oxygen .
- Oxidative Phosphorylation (OP) is a process involving a flow of electrons through the ETS, which creates an electrochemical gradient of protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane .
- The electrochemical gradient of protons is used to drive the synthesis of ATP from ADP and Pi by a membrane-bound enzyme called ATP synthase, in a process called chemiosmosis .
- Together, the ETS and chemiosmosis make up OP, which is the final stage of cellular respiration and produces most of the ATP generated from the oxidation of glucose .
- The ETS consists of four major protein complexes (I, II, III, and IV) and two mobile electron carriers (ubiquinone and cytochrome c) that shuttle electrons between the complexes .
- Complex I receives electrons from NADH and transfers them to ubiquinone, while pumping four protons across the membrane .
- Complex II receives electrons from FADH2 and transfers them to ubiquinone, without pumping protons .
- Ubiquinone delivers electrons to complex III, which transfers them to cytochrome c, while pumping four protons across the membrane .
- Cytochrome c delivers electrons to complex IV, which transfers them to oxygen, the final electron acceptor, while pumping two protons across the membrane .
- Oxygen combines with protons to form water as a byproduct of OP .
- The ETS creates a proton gradient that is higher in the intermembrane space than in the matrix of the mitochondria .
- The proton gradient represents a potential energy that can be harnessed by ATP synthase to make ATP .
- ATP synthase is a complex enzyme that has two parts: F0 and F1 .
- F0 is embedded in the membrane and acts as a proton channel that allows protons to flow back into the matrix .
- F1 is attached to F0 and protrudes into the matrix, where it catalyzes the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP .
- The flow of protons through F0 causes F1 to rotate, which changes its conformation and enables the binding and release of ADP, Pi, and ATP .
- OP can produce up to 34 ATP molecules per glucose molecule, depending on the efficiency of the ETS and the transport of NADH and FADH2 into the mitochondria .
- OP is regulated by the availability of oxygen, NADH, FADH2, ADP, and Pi, as well as by the feedback inhibition of ATP on some enzymes of the ETS and glycolysis .
- OP is linked to the previous stages of cellular respiration, namely glycolysis, formation of acetyl-CoA, and Krebs cycle, which provide the substrates and cofactors for OP .
- OP is the main source of ATP for most aerobic organisms, and it is essential for the maintenance of cellular functions and homeostasis .



### ATP and Cellular Respiration

- ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the energy-carrying molecule used in cells because it can release energy very quickly.
- ATP is composed of a nitrogenous base (adenine), a sugar (ribose), and three phosphate groups. The bonds between the phosphate groups are high-energy bonds that can be broken to release energy.
- Cellular respiration refers to the breakdown of glucose and other respiratory substrates to make ATP. It involves three main steps: glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation  .
- Glycolysis is the first step of cellular respiration that occurs in the cytoplasm. It splits one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, producing a net of 2 ATP and 2 NADH (a coenzyme that carries electrons) per glucose molecule .
- Pyruvate oxidation is the second step of cellular respiration that occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. It converts each pyruvate molecule into acetyl-CoA (a two-carbon molecule attached to coenzyme A), releasing one molecule of CO2 and one molecule of NADH per pyruvate molecule .
- The citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle) is the third step of cellular respiration that occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. It completes the oxidation of glucose by breaking down acetyl-CoA into CO2 and H2O, producing 2 ATP, 6 NADH, and 2 FADH2 (another coenzyme that carries electrons) per glucose molecule .
- Oxidative phosphorylation is the final step of cellular respiration that occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It uses the electrons carried by NADH and FADH2 to create a proton gradient across the membrane, which drives the synthesis of ATP by ATP synthase. This process produces about 26-28 ATP per glucose molecule .
- Cellular respiration is regulated by various factors, such as the availability of oxygen, glucose, and other substrates, the demand for ATP, and the feedback inhibition of enzymes .
