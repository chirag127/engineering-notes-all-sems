### Brief history of microbiology

1. Discovery of microorganisms
 - Antonie van Leeuwenhoek first observed microorganisms in 1676 using his single-lens microscope. He observed bacteria, protozoa, spermatozoa, etc.
 - Robert Hooke observed the cellular structure of cork in 1665. The observation of microorganisms by Leeuwenhoek established the cell as the basic structural and functional unit of life.

2. Germ theory of disease
 - In the mid-1800s, Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch provided evidence for the germ theory of disease. They showed that specific microorganisms caused specific diseases.
 - Pasteur showed that microorganisms contaminated wine and beer and proposed pasteurization to eliminate them. Koch proposed Koch's postulates to prove that a microbe caused a disease.

3. Discovery of microbial physiology and metabolism
 - In the late 1800s and early 1900s, microbial physiology and metabolism were studied. The nitrification process, microbial degradation of organic matter, and anaerobic fermentation were understood.
 - The processes of photosynthesis, nitrogen fixation, and microbial production of foods and beverages were understood in this period.

4. Application of microbiology
 - The applications of microbiology, such as production of antibiotics, vaccines, and enzymes and microbial biotechnology, developed in the mid-1900s.
 - Recombinant DNA technology was developed in the 1970s, enabling the large-scale production of proteins and genetic engineering of microorganisms.

### Lichens

- Lichens are symbiotic associations between fungi and algae. The fungal component is known as mycobiont and the algal component is known as photobiont.
- The mycobiont provides shelter and absorbs water and minerals from the environment. The photobiont carries out photosynthesis and provides food to the mycobiont. Thus, lichens exhibit symbiosis.
- Lichens have properties of both fungi and algae. They have chlorophyll for photosynthesis from algae and also reproductive structures of fungi.
- Lichens grow in diverse habitats including rocks, trees, soil, deserts, etc. They are well adapted to harsh conditions. They are sensitive to air pollution and are used as bioindicators of air quality.
- Some common examples of lichens are Usnea, Ramalina, Evernia, Cladonia, etc. They are slow growing and long living. Lichens are not plants but symbiotic associations of fungi and algae.

### Protozoa

- Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic microorganisms.
- They are heterotrophic organisms that exhibit a variety of forms and levels of organization.
- Based on their mode of locomotion, protozoa are classified into:

1. Flagellates: Having one or more flagella, e.g. Trypanosoma, Leishmania.
2. Ciliates: Having cilia covering their body, e.g. Paramecium, Vorticella.
3. Amoebae: Having pseudopodia for locomotion, e.g. Amoeba, Entamoeba.

- According to nutritional mode, protozoa are classified into:

1. Holozoic: Ingest and digest solid food, e.g. Amoeba.
2. Holophytic: Perform photosynthesis, e.g. Euglena.
3. Saprozoic: Feed on dead and decaying matter, e.g. Amoeba proteus.
4. Parasitic: Depend on a host for nutrition, e.g. Plasmodium, Trypanosoma.

- Some protozoa act as pathogens and cause diseases in humans and animals, such as:

1. Malaria: Caused by Plasmodium species transmitted through mosquito bite.
2. Amoebiasis: Caused by Entamoeba histolytica transmitted through contaminated food/water.
3. Sleeping sickness: Caused by Trypanosoma brucei transmitted through tsetse fly bite.

- Role of protozoa: Help in decomposition of organic matter and maintaining ecological balance. Some species serve as food for other organisms.

### Viral Structures

- Viruses are acellular organisms i.e. they lack cellular organization. They are the smallest infectious agents that can replicate only inside the living host cells.
- Based on the shape, viruses are classified into helical, icosahedral, complex and enveloped viruses.
- Helical viruses: Rod shaped or filamentous e.g. Tobacco mosaic virus. The capsid is made up of protein subunits arranged in a helical symmetry.
- Icosahedral viruses: Spherical in shape e.g. Poliovirus, Tomato bushy stunt virus. The capsid is made up of protein subunits arranged in an icosahedral symmetry with 20 faces.
- Complex viruses: They have a complex structure e.g. Poxviruses, Herpes viruses. They have an outer lipid envelope, a capsid and a core containing nucleic acid.
- Enveloped viruses: They have an outer lipid membrane derived from the host cell membrane e.g. HIV, Influenza virus. The lipid membrane helps in the entry of virus into the host cell.

### Viral Multiplication

- Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites and hence they can replicate only inside the host cell.
- The process of replication of viruses inside the host cell is known as viral multiplication or viral replication.
- The steps involved in viral multiplication are:

1. Adsorption: The virus attaches to the surface of the host cell through adsorption. The viral protein interacts with the receptor molecules on the host cell surface.

2. Penetration: The viral nucleic acid is introduced into the host cell. This step is carried out in different ways for different viruses. Some viruses penetrate the cell by fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell membrane. Some viruses gain entry into the host cell through receptor-mediated endocytosis.

3. Uncoating: The protein coat of some viruses is removed, exposing the viral nucleic acid. This step is not shown by all viruses.

4. Biosynthesis: New viral components are synthesized using the machinery of the host cell. The viral nucleic acid directs the synthesis of viral proteins and nucleic acids.

5. Assembly: The newly synthesized viral components are assembled into complete viruses.

6. Release: The progeny viruses are released from the host cell. The viruses are released by budding or by lysis of the host cell.

- The rate of viral multiplication varies for different viruses. Some viruses take hours to multiply whereas some take days to multiply.
- Viral multiplication results in the production of a large number of progeny viruses which spread the infection to new host cells. This enables the spread of the viral disease to other hosts.

### Enzymes

- Enzymes are biological molecules (proteins) that act as catalysts and help in accelerating chemical reactions.
- They have an active site where substrate binding takes place.
- The shape of the active site is complementary to the substrate molecule.

### Glycolysis

- Glycolysis is the first step of cellular respiration that occurs in the cytoplasm.
- It is the breakdown of glucose into two pyruvate molecules, producing a small amount of ATP and NADH.
- The process does not require oxygen and is thus anaerobic. It consists of two phases:

1. Preparatory phase: 2 ATP molecules are consumed to convert glucose into glucose-6-phosphate.
2. Payoff phase: 4 ATP, 2 NADH and 2 pyruvate molecules are produced. Net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH.

- The products of glycolysis, pyruvate and NADH, then enter the mitochondria for the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain.
- Glycolysis allows cells to produce energy even under low oxygen conditions. It is a near-universal pathway found in both eukaryotes and prokaryotes.
- The rate of glycolysis is regulated by the energy requirements of the cell and availability of glucose. Key enzymes are regulated to control the rate of the pathway.

### Kreb cycle

- Kreb cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.
- It is also known as citric acid cycle or tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.
- It is the second stage of cellular respiration that oxidizes the two carbon acetyl CoA into carbon dioxide with the release of energy (NADH and FADH2).

### Electron Transport System and Oxidative Phosphorylation

- Electron Transport System (ETS) is the last stage of cellular respiration that takes place in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- It consists of five complexes (I to V) that transfer electrons and pump protons across the membrane to establish a proton gradient.
- Complex I accepts electrons from NADH and passes them to ubiquinone. Complex III receives electrons from ubiquinol and passes them to cytochrome c. Complex IV uses electrons from cytochrome c to reduce oxygen to water.
- The flow of electrons through the complexes powers the pumping of protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient.
- The proton gradient drives the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate by Complex V, which is also known as ATP synthase. This process of using the proton gradient to produce ATP is known as chemiosmosis.
- The net result of ETS and chemiosmosis is the production of about 34 ATP molecules from the oxidation of one molecule of NADH and the reduction of one molecule of oxygen to water. This makes oxidative phosphorylation the most efficient process of ATP production in cellular respiration.
- Factors affecting the rate of respiration including oxygen concentration, availability of nutrients like glucose and ADP, and temperature also affect the ETS and oxidative phosphorylation. When the demand for ATP is high due to increased cellular activities, the rate of electron flow and proton pumping increases to produce more ATP via oxidative phosphorylation.
