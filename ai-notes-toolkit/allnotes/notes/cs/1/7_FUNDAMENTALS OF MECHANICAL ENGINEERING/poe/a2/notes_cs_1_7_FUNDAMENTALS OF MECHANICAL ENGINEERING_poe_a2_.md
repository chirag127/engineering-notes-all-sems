## Unit 1 - Introduction to Mechanics

1. Mechanics: Mechanics is the study of forces and their effects on the motion of objects. It is one of the fundamental branches of physics.
2. Newton's Laws of Motion: Issac Newton proposed three laws that describe how forces affect the motion of objects:

- An object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.
- The acceleration a of a body as produced by a net force F is directly proportional to the magnitude of the net force, in the same direction as the net force, and inversely proportional to the mass m of the body, i.e., F = ma.
- For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

3. Inertia: Inertia is the resistance of an object to a change in its state of motion. An object will keep doing what it's doing unless a force acts on it. More massive objects have greater inertia.

### Force, Moment and Couple

- **Force:** Force is a push or pull that tends to accelerate an object. It is a vector quantity that has both magnitude and direction.
- **Moment:** Moment is the tendency of a force to rotate an object about a point or axis. It is calculated as force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force.
- **Couple:** A couple is a pair of equal and opposite parallel forces that do not share a line of action. A couple produces rotation without translation. The magnitude of the couple is the product of one of the forces and the perpendicular distance between them.

Couples provide a means of creating rotation in an object without creating translational motion. They are useful for analyzing situations where pure rotation occurs without translation.

The concepts of force, moment and couple are fundamental to the analysis of mechanisms and structures in mechanical engineering. They provide a means to quantitatively understand how systems react to applied loads.

### Principle of Transmissibility

- The internal forces between two particles of a body are equal action-reaction force pairs and their line of action passes through the particles.
- If the line of action of an internal force remains unchanged, the force is said to be transmitted across the body. This is known as the principle of transmissibility of force.
- The internal forces are transmitted from particle to particle within the body along their lines of action. The transmitted forces across any section of the body must be in equilibrium.
- The principle of transmissibility holds good only if the shape and size of the body do not change. If the shape or size of the body changes due to applied loads, the lines of action of internal forces also change. In such situations, the principle of transmissibility does not hold good.

### Varignon's theorem

Varignon's theorem states that the moment of a force about any point is equal to the sum of the moments of its components about the same point.

* Prove Varignon's theorem given a force **F** acting at a distance **d** from a point O and resolved into two rectangular components **F1** and **F2** acting at distances **d1** and **d2** from O respectively.
* Moment of **F** about O = **Fd**
* Moments of **F1** and **F2** about O are **F1d1** and **F2d2** respectively.
* By theorem, **Fd = F1d1 + F2d2**
* Hence proved.

Varignon's theorem is useful in analyzing the moment of a force and finding the net moment of concurrent forces about a point. It simplifies the task of finding the net moment to finding the sum of moments of individual components.

### Resultant of force system- concurrent and non-concurrent coplanar forces

- Concurrent coplanar forces: When multiple forces acting on a body intersect at a common point, they are called concurrent coplanar forces. The resultant of such a system can be determined by taking the vector sum of individual forces. As the forces act through a common point, the magnitude and direction of the resultant force can be determined without considering the moment of forces about any point.
- Non-concurrent coplanar forces: When forces acting on a body lie in a same plane but their lines of action do not meet at a common point, they are called non-concurrent coplanar forces. The resultant force of such a system can be determined by:

1. Resolving each force into components along two perpendicular axes lying in the plane
2. Taking the algebraic sum of the resolved components of forces along each axis to determine the magnitude and direction of the resultant force
3. Constructing the resultant force using the determined magnitude and direction

Additionally, the point of application of the resultant force can be determined by considering the moment balance of forces about any point.

The above points describe the procedure to determine the resultant of force system consisting of concurrent and non-concurrent coplanar forces. The concepts are important to understand the dynamics of particles and rigid bodies.

### Types of supports (Hinge, Roller) and loads (Point, UDL, UVL)

- **Hinge support:** A hinge support allows rotation about the hinge axis but prevents translation in the direction perpendicular to the hinge axis.
- **Roller support:** A roller support allows translation in one direction parallel to the surface of the roller but prevents translation in the perpendicular direction. It also allows rotation about an axis parallel to the roller axis.
- **Point load:** A point load is a load acting at a single point. It produces a linearly varying shear force and bending moment.
- **Uniformly distributed load (UDL):** A UDL is a load that is distributed uniformly over a length. It produces a linearly varying shear force and a parabolic bending moment.
- **Uniformly varying load (UVL):** A UVL is a load that varies uniformly along the length. It produces a non-linearly varying shear force and bending moment.

### Free Body Diagram

- A free body diagram is a diagram that depicts the forces acting on a body in isolation from its surroundings.
- It is a vector diagram that shows all the forces acting on an object, the magnitude of the forces, and the direction of the forces.
- To draw a free body diagram:

1. Identify the object and draw a simple representation of it.
2. Identify all the forces acting on the object and determine the direction of each force.
3. Represent each force with an arrow. The length of the arrow shaft indicates the magnitude of the force.
4. Label each force arrow with a description of the force.
5. Draw the forces to scale if quantitative information is given about the forces.

- Free body diagrams are useful to analyze forces and accelerations, determine equilibrium, and solve force problems.
- They help break down a complex problem into simpler parts and are a key tool for solving mechanics problems.

### Equilibrium Equations and Support Reactions

- Equilibrium equations: The sum of forces and sum of moments acting on a body must be zero for equilibrium.
- Sum of forces in x-direction = 0
- Sum of forces in y-direction = 0
- Sum of moments about any point = 0
- Support reactions: The reactions at the supports/constraints of a structure can be determined using equilibrium equations.
- Number of unknown support reactions <= number of equilibrium equations
- If more number of unknowns, the system is statically indeterminate and additional equations are required to solve for reactions.
- For 2D structures with pin, roller and fixed supports, the reactions can be determined by applying equilibrium equations.
- For 3D frames, the 3D equilibrium equations can be applied to determine the support reactions.

### Normal and shear Stress

- Normal stress occurs when an external load acts perpendicular to the cross-sectional area of a body. It causes the body to elongate or compress along the length.
- Shear stress occurs when an external load acts parallel to the cross-sectional area of a body. It causes the layers of the body to slide over each other.
- Normal stress is calculated by dividing the applied load by the cross-sectional area: `σ = F/A`
- Shear stress is calculated by dividing the applied shear force by the cross-sectional area: `τ = F/A`
- Failure occurs when the maximum stress values exceed the corresponding strength values (yield strength or ultimate strength) of the material.
- Both normal and shear stresses are vector quantities that act on planes (area) within the body. The planes can be at any orientation. The stresses are calculated for the planes that are most stressed.

### Strain

- Strain is the deformation of a body due to an applied force. It is expressed as the change in dimension (length, area or volume) per unit original dimension.
- The three types of strain are:
 - Normal strain: Change in length per unit original length along the line of action of the force.
 - Shear strain: Change in shape of a body without any change in volume. It is caused by forces acting tangent to the surface of the body.
 - Volumetric strain: Change in volume per unit original volume. It is equal to the sum of normal strains in three mutual perpendicular directions.
- Strain is a dimensionless quantity and is usually expressed in fractions or percentages.
- The elastic limit of a material is the maximum strain that it can sustain without any permanent deformation. Beyond the elastic limit, the strain is called plastic strain.
- Hooke's law is applicable only for strains within the elastic limit. It states that strain is directly proportional to the applied stress.
- Strain energy is the work done in deforming a body. It is equal to the area under the stress-strain curve.

### Hookes' law

- Hooke's law states that the extension produced in an elastic body is directly proportional to the load applied, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded.
- The law can be expressed mathematically as:
F = kx
Where,
F = Force applied
x = Extension produced
k = Constant of proportionality (Spring constant)
- The spring constant depends on the properties and geometry of the material.
- Hooke's law is valid for most solid materials as long as the elastic limit is not exceeded.
- The law breaks down when the elastic limit is exceeded and the material suffers permanent deformation.
- Applications of Hooke's law include:
-- Spring
-- Stress-strain analysis of materials
-- Force sensors
-- Weighing machines

### Poisson’s ratio for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction to Mechanics in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF MECHANICAL ENGINEERING

- Poisson’s ratio (ν) is the ratio of lateral strain to axial strain for an elastic material under uniaxial stress.
- It is a dimensionless quantity and has no units.
- For most materials, the value of Poisson's ratio ranges from 0 to 0.5.
- Materials with Poisson's ratio equal to 0 are incompressible while materials with Poisson's ratio equal to 0.5 have maximum compressibility.
- Poisson's ratio can be either positive or negative. For most materials, it is positive indicating lateral expansion upon stretching and lateral contraction upon compression.
- The sign of Poisson's ratio determines whether a material will bulge or neck under uniaxial loading.
- The value of Poisson's ratio affects the stiffness and strength of a material and is important in determining many properties of materials and structures.

### Elastic Constants

- Young's Modulus (E): It is a measure of stiffness of a material. It relates stress and strain in a material. A high value of Young's modulus means the material is stiff.
- Shear Modulus (G): It is a measure of a material's resistance to shear strain. It relates shear stress and shear strain. A high value indicates the material is resistant to sliding/shearing.
- Bulk Modulus (K): It is a measure of a material's resistance to uniform compression/expansion. It relates hydrostatic pressure and volumetric strain. A high value means the material is incompressible.

### Relationship between Elastic Constants

- For isotropic materials, the following relation exists between elastic constants:
E = 2G(1 + ν)
where ν is Poisson's ratio which relates lateral and axial strains.
- For most engineering materials, the bulk modulus is approximated as:
K = E/3(1 - 2ν)
- The elastic constants depend on the material, its microstructure and temperature. At very high stresses/strains, the elastic constants no longer remain constant and the material exhibits plastic/permanent deformation.

### Stress-Strain Diagram for Ductile and Brittle Materials

- Ductile materials: Metals such as steel and aluminum exhibit ductile behavior. Their stress-strain diagrams are as follows:
- As stress is applied, strain increases linearly upto the proportional limit. This is the elastic region.
- Beyond proportional limit, plastic deformation occurs and strain increases rapidly with increasing stress. This is the plastic region.
- Eventually, the material breaks at the ultimate tensile stress. The corresponding strain is called ultimate tensile strain.
- The area under the stress-strain curve represents the work done and energy absorbed by the material upto fracture. This indicates the toughness of the material.

- Brittle materials: Materials such as cast iron and ceramics exhibit brittle behavior. Their stress-strain diagrams are as follows:
- The elastic region is very small. The material fractures as soon as the yield point is reached.
- There is no plastic region or well-defined ultimate tensile stress. The fracture occurs at a low strain.
- The area under the stress-strain curve is very small, indicating low toughness. Brittle materials absorb very little energy before fracture.

The key differences between ductile and brittle materials from their stress-strain diagrams are:
- Existence of a plastic region beyond proportional limit for ductile materials. No such region for brittle materials.
- Gradual failure with significant plastic deformation for ductile materials. Sudden failure with negligible plastic deformation for brittle materials.
- Large work done and energy absorption (toughness) for ductile materials. Negligible work done and energy absorption (toughness) for brittle materials.

### Factor of Safety

- Factor of safety (FOS) is a safety margin or a margin of error designed into engineering components, structures, or systems.
- It is the ratio of the ultimate strength of a material to the actual applied stress or load.
- A higher factor of safety indicates a more conservative design, with a lower probability of failure under normal operating conditions.
- Factor of safety is determined based on the variability of the materials and loads, the precision with which those values can be determined, and the consequences of structural failure.
- For example, a bridge may have a factor of safety of 4, meaning it can withstand loads four times greater than the maximum expected loads before structural failure.
- The factor of safety is a important design consideration to ensure safety and reliability of mechanical components and structures. A higher factor of safety leads to a more robust but costlier design. The appropriate factor of safety depends on the precision of load and material property estimates and the consequences of failure.

## Unit 2 - Introduction to IC Engines and Electric Vehicles

1. Internal Combustion Engines
- An internal combustion engine (ICE) is an engine in which the combustion of fuel and an oxidizer (typically air) occurs in a confined space called a combustion chamber.
- The combustion produces hot gases which expand and apply pressure to surfaces of the engine, producing motion.
- The most common fuels for internal combustion engines are gasoline (petrol) and diesel fuel.
- The main components of an ICE are: piston, cylinder, valves, connecting rod, crankshaft, camshaft, timing belt/chain, fuel injector/carburetor, spark plug.

2. Working of an Internal Combustion Engine
- The key steps in the working of an ICE are: intake, compression, ignition, power, exhaust.
- In the intake step, air-fuel mixture is sucked into the cylinder through the inlet valve.
- In the compression step, the piston moves up and compresses the air-fuel mixture.
- In the ignition step, the spark plug produces a spark that ignites the compressed air-fuel mixture.
- In the power step, the combustion of the air-fuel mixture drives the piston down, which turns the crankshaft.
- In the exhaust step, the byproducts of combustion are emitted out of the cylinder through the exhaust valve.

3. Differences between Petrol and Diesel Engines
- In petrol engines, the air-fuel mixture is ignited by a spark plug while in diesel engines, the air is compressed to high pressure which ignites the fuel injected into it.
- Diesel engines are more fuel efficient but produce more emissions than petrol engines.
- Diesel engines have higher compression ratios than petrol engines.
- Diesel engines produce more torque than petrol engines which makes them suitable for heavy-duty vehicles.

### IC Engine

- An internal combustion engine (ICE) is a heat engine in which the combustion of a fuel occurs with an oxidizer (usually air) in a combustion chamber.
- The reciprocating type IC engine uses pistons that move in cylinders containing the combustion gases.
- The four-stroke cycle includes the intake, compression, power, and exhaust strokes. The piston moves downwards in the intake stroke, allowing the air-fuel mixture to enter the cylinder. The piston then moves up and compresses the mixture in the compression stroke. The compressed mixture is ignited, driving the piston downwards in the power stroke. The byproducts of combustion are exhausted in the exhaust stroke.
- The two-stroke cycle has charging and power strokes, with overlap that allows some of the intake charge to be exhausted and replaced by a fresh charge. Two-stroke engines are simpler but less efficient than four-stroke engines.
- Common fuels for IC engines include gasoline, diesel, and propane. The air-fuel mixture is ignited in gasoline engines using a spark from a spark plug. In diesel engines, the high compression of the air-fuel mixture causes it to ignite.
- IC engines have high power density but produce air pollution. They are widely used in vehicles and equipment but are being increasingly replaced by electric vehicles to reduce emissions.

### Methods of Refrigeration

1. Vapor-Compression Cycle
- It is the most commonly used refrigeration cycle.
- It uses a circulating liquid refrigerant as the medium.
- The refrigerant vaporizes and boils in the evaporator at low pressure, absorbing heat.
- The vapor is then compressed and condensed into liquid in the condenser, releasing heat.
- The condensed liquid refrigerant is throttled to low pressure, evaporating again in the evaporator and repeating the cycle.

2. Absorption Cycle
- It uses a liquid absorbent solution instead of a mechanical compressor to pump heat.
- The absorbent soaks up the refrigerant vapor, releasing it later when heated.
- The evaporator and condenser function similarly as in vapor-compression cycle.
- It is often used in residential air conditioning with waste heat or solar heat as the energy source.

3. Steam Jet Refrigeration
- It uses steam as the working fluid which is expanded in a nozzle to produce cooling.
- The expanded and cooled steam is condensed, releasing the latent heat of vaporization.
- The condensate is pumped back and evaporated again, repeating the cycle.
- It has low COP but is simple, reliable and uses steam as the working medium.

### Newtonian Fluid

- A Newtonian fluid is a fluid in which the shear stress is directly proportional to the shear rate.

    - Shear-thinning fluid: Viscosity decreases with increase in shear rate. Example: Blood and solutions of polymers like polyacrylamide.

### Pascal's Law and Continuity Equation

Pascal's Law states that pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions. This means that any change in pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished to every portion of the fluid and to the walls of the containing vessel.

Continuity equation is a statement of conservation of mass for an incompressible fluid. It states that the rate of flow into a system must equal the rate of flow out of the system. Mathematically,

$$
\dfrac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}t} + \nabla \cdot \vec{v} = 0
$$

Where $V$ is volume, $t$ is time and $\vec{v}$ is velocity.

The continuity equation can also be written in terms of mass flow rate:

$$
\dfrac{\text{d}{\rho V}}{\text{d}t} + \nabla \cdot(\rho \vec{v}) = 0
$$

Where $\rho$ is density. The continuity equation relates the velocity and density of a moving fluid and ensures that mass is conserved. It is useful in deriving the Navier-Stokes equations which describe fluid flow.

The points are:
1. Pascal's Law: Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions.
2. Continuity equation: Rate of flow in = Rate of flow out. Ensures conservation of mass.
3. Can be written in terms of volume or mass flow rate. Relates velocity and density.
4. Useful in deriving Navier-Stokes equations for fluid flow.

### Mass Flow Rate (Venturi Meter and Orifice Meter)

* Venturi Meter: It is a device used to measure fluid flow rate. It consists of a constricted section (throat) between two cylindrical sections (converging and diverging sections). As fluid flows through the converging section, its velocity increases and pressure decreases. In the throat section, fluid has maximum velocity and minimum pressure. In the diverging section, velocity decreases and pressure increases. The difference between pressures at two sections is measured which is proportional to the square of the fluid velocity. By knowing the fluid density and pressure difference, mass flow rate can be calculated.
* Orifice Meter: It is a device with a plate with a hole in between the pipe. As fluid flows through the orifice, its velocity increases and pressure decreases. The pressure difference between upstream and downstream of the orifice gives the measurement of fluid flow rate. The flow rate can be calculated if density and pressure difference are known. The flow rate is directly proportional to the square root of the pressure difference.
* Applications: Venturi meters and orifice meters are commonly used in fluid flow measurement in pipes. They are inexpensive and do not disturb the flow much. They are used in flow measurement of liquids and gases in industries.
