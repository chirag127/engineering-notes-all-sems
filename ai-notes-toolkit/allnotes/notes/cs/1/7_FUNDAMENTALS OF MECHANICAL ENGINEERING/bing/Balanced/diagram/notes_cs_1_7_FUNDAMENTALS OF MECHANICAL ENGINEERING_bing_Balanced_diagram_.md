## Unit 1 - Introduction to Mechanics

Mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion and interaction of objects under the influence of forces.

Some of the main topics in mechanics are:

- Kinematics: the study of how objects move without considering the causes of their motion.
- Dynamics: the study of how forces affect the motion of objects.
- Statics: the study of how objects remain at rest or in equilibrium under the action of forces.
- Work, energy and power: the study of how forces do work on objects and how energy is transferred and conserved.
- Momentum and collisions: the study of how the quantity of motion of objects is conserved and changed during interactions.
- Rotational motion: the study of how objects rotate around an axis and how angular quantities are related to linear quantities.
- Gravitation: the study of how objects attract each other due to their masses and how gravity affects the motion of objects in space.
- Fluid mechanics: the study of how fluids (liquids and gases) flow and exert pressure on objects.

Some of the main concepts and principles in mechanics are:

- Scalar and vector quantities: scalar quantities have only magnitude, while vector quantities have both magnitude and direction.
- Displacement, velocity and acceleration: displacement is the change in position of an object, velocity is the rate of change of displacement, and acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.
- Newton's laws of motion: the first law states that an object at rest or in uniform motion will remain so unless acted upon by a net force, the second law states that the net force on an object is equal to its mass times its acceleration, and the third law states that for every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force.
- Free-body diagrams: diagrams that show all the forces acting on an object and their directions.
- Friction: a force that opposes the relative motion of two surfaces in contact.
- Hooke's law: the law that states that the force exerted by a spring is proportional to its extension or compression.
- Conservation of energy: the principle that states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant.
- Conservation of momentum: the principle that states that the total momentum of an isolated system remains constant.
- Impulse: the product of force and time, equal to the change in momentum of an object.
- Elastic and inelastic collisions: elastic collisions are collisions in which kinetic energy is conserved, while inelastic collisions are collisions in which kinetic energy is not conserved.
- Torque: the product of force and perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation, equal to the change in angular momentum of an object.
- Angular displacement, velocity and acceleration: angular displacement is the angle through which an object rotates, angular velocity is the rate of change of angular displacement, and angular acceleration is the rate of change of angular velocity.
- Moment of inertia: the measure of an object's resistance to rotational motion, depending on its mass and shape.
- Newton's law of universal gravitation: the law that states that the gravitational force between two objects is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.
- Buoyancy: the upward force exerted by a fluid on an object immersed in it, equal to the weight of the displaced fluid.
- Bernoulli's principle: the principle that states that the pressure of a fluid decreases as its speed increases.

### Principle of Transmissibility

- The principle of transmissibility states that the point of application of a force can be moved anywhere along its line of action without changing the external reaction forces on a rigid body  .
- The line of action of a force is the infinite straight line that passes through the point of application of the force and is parallel to the direction of the force.
- The principle of transmissibility is based on the assumption that the rigid body is in equilibrium under the action of the forces and that the internal stresses and strains are negligible.
- The principle of transmissibility can be used to simplify the analysis of concurrent force systems, where all the forces intersect at a common point.
- The principle of transmissibility can also be used to replace a force by an equivalent force-couple system, where the force is moved to a different point along its line of action and a couple is added to balance the moment caused by the displacement of the force.
- The principle of transmissibility has some limitations, such as:
  - It cannot be applied to non-rigid bodies, where the internal stresses and strains are significant and affect the equilibrium and deformation of the body.

## Supports

- Supports are devices that connect a structural member to the ground or to another part of the structure and provide resistance to forces and moments.
- Supports can be classified into four types: simple, roller, hinge, and fixed   .
- Simple support: A simple support allows rotation about any axis and translation (horizontal and vertical movement) in any direction. It does not provide any reaction force or moment. It is generally not used in structures.
- Roller support: A roller support allows rotation about any axis and translation in any direction parallel to the surface on which it rests. It provides a reaction force perpendicular to the surface, but no moment. It is commonly used for beams and bridges.
- Hinge support: A hinge support allows rotation about any axis, but prevents translation in any direction. It provides two reaction forces, one horizontal and one vertical, but no moment. It is commonly used for frames and trusses .
- Fixed support: A fixed support prevents rotation about any axis and translation in any direction. It provides two reaction forces, one horizontal and one vertical, and a reaction moment. It is commonly used for columns and walls .

## Loads

- Loads are forces or moments that act on a structural member and cause deformation, stress, or displacement.
- Loads can be classified into three types: point, uniformly distributed (UDL), and uniformly varying (UVL) loads.
- Point load: A point load is a load that acts at a single point on a structural member. It can be either concentrated or distributed. A concentrated load is a load that acts over a very small area, such as a weight or a force. A distributed load is a load that acts over a finite area, such as a pressure or a moment.
- UDL load: A UDL load is a load that acts uniformly over a length of a structural member. It can be either constant or varying. A constant UDL load is a load that has the same magnitude and direction throughout the length, such as a self-weight or a wind load. A varying UDL load is a load that changes in magnitude and/or direction along the length, such as a snow load or a temperature load.
- UVL load: A UVL load is a load that acts uniformly over a length of a structural member, but varies linearly from zero at one end to a maximum value at the other end. ### Strain

Strain is a measure of the amount of deformation that an object undergoes due to an applied force. Strain is a dimensionless quantity that is calculated by dividing the change in length or shape of an object by its original length or shape. Strain can be positive or negative, depending on whether the object is stretched or compressed.

There are three main types of strain:

- **Longitudinal strain**: This is the strain that occurs when an object is subjected to a normal force (perpendicular to its surface) that causes a change in its length. Longitudinal strain is defined as the ratio of the change in length to the original length of the object. For example, if a metal rod of length 10 cm is stretched by 0.1 cm, its longitudinal strain is 0.1/10 = 0.01.

- **Volumetric strain**: This is the strain that occurs when an object is subjected to a force that causes a change in its volume. Volumetric strain is defined as the ratio of the change in volume to the original volume of the object. For example, if a balloon of volume 1000 cm^3 is inflated by 100 cm^3, its volumetric strain is 100/1000 = 0.1.

- **Shearing strain**: This is the strain that occurs when an object is subjected to a tangential force (parallel to its surface) that causes a change in its shape. Shearing strain is defined as the ratio of the change in angle to the original angle of the object. For example, if a square of side 10 cm is distorted by a force that makes one of its angles 91 degrees, its shearing strain is (91-90)/90 = 0.011.

The formula for strain is:

Strain = (Change in dimension) / (Original dimension)

The symbol for strain is the Greek letter epsilon (ε).

The unit for strain is none, as it is a dimensionless quantity. However, sometimes strain is expressed as a percentage, by multiplying the strain value by 100. For example, a strain of 0.01 can be written as 1%.



### Hookes’ law

- Hookes’ law is a law of elasticity that relates the force applied to a spring or other elastic object to the amount of deformation or displacement it undergoes.
- Hookes’ law can be expressed mathematically as F = kx, where F is the force, k is the spring constant or stiffness, and x is the displacement or extension of the spring from its equilibrium position.
- Hookes’ law is valid only for small deformations, where the force and displacement are proportional. For larger deformations, the relationship becomes nonlinear and more complex.
- Hookes’ law can be used to model the behavior of many elastic materials, such as metals, rubber, and biological tissues, as well as mechanical systems, such as springs, pendulums, and oscillators.
- Hookes’ law was discovered by the English scientist Robert Hooke in 1660, who observed that the extension of a spring is proportional to the tension applied to it. He stated his law as "ut tensio, sic vis", which means "as the extension, so the force" in Latin.

### Stress-Strain Diagram for Ductile and Brittle Materials

- A stress-strain diagram is a graphical representation of the relationship between the applied stress and the resulting strain in a material.
- Stress is the force per unit area acting on the material, and strain is the fractional deformation or change in length of the material due to the applied stress.
- Different materials have different stress-strain behaviors, which reflect their mechanical properties such as strength, stiffness, ductility, and brittleness.
- Ductile materials are those that can undergo large plastic deformation (permanent change in shape) before breaking, while brittle materials are those that fracture with little or no plastic deformation.
- The stress-strain diagram for a ductile material typically has the following regions:

  - Elastic region: The initial linear portion of the curve, where the material behaves elastically, meaning that it returns to its original shape when the stress is removed. The slope of this region is called the modulus of elasticity or Young's modulus, which measures the stiffness of the material.
  - Yield point: The point at which the material starts to deform plastically, meaning that it does not return to its original shape when the stress is removed. The stress at this point is called the yield strength, which measures the resistance of the material to plastic deformation.
  - Plastic region: The curved portion of the curve, where the material undergoes plastic deformation. The stress increases with increasing strain, but at a decreasing rate. This is due to the phenomenon of strain hardening, where the material becomes stronger as it deforms plastically.
  - Necking region: The final portion of the curve, where the material starts to narrow or neck down due to the localized reduction in cross-sectional area. The stress decreases with increasing strain, as the material becomes weaker and more unstable. This leads to the ultimate failure of the material. The maximum stress in this region is called the ultimate strength, which measures the maximum load that the material can withstand before breaking.
  - Fracture point: The point at which the material breaks or fractures. The strain at this point is called the fracture strain, which measures the ductility of the material.

- The stress-strain diagram for a brittle material typically has the following regions:

  - Elastic region: The initial linear portion of the curve, where the material behaves elastically, similar to the ductile material. The slope of this region is also called the modulus of elasticity or Young's modulus, which measures the stiffness of the material.
  - Fracture point: The point at which the material breaks or fractures, without undergoing any plastic deformation. The stress at this point is called the fracture strength, which measures the brittleness of the material. ## Unit 2 - Introduction to IC Engines and Electric Vehicles

- An **IC engine** or **internal combustion engine** is a type of engine that converts the chemical energy of a fuel into mechanical energy by burning it inside a combustion chamber.
- An **electric vehicle** or **EV** is a type of vehicle that uses an electric motor and a battery instead of an IC engine and a fuel tank to propel itself.
- The main differences between IC engines and electric vehicles are:

  - IC engines use fossil fuels such as gasoline or diesel, which emit harmful gases and contribute to global warming and air pollution. Electric vehicles use electricity, which can be generated from renewable sources such as solar or wind, and have zero tailpipe emissions.
  - IC engines have lower efficiency and higher maintenance costs than electric motors, which have fewer moving parts and can recover energy through regenerative braking. Electric vehicles also have lower operating costs due to cheaper electricity and less frequent servicing.
  - IC engines have higher power and torque output than electric motors, which have limited range and performance depending on the battery capacity and state of charge. Electric vehicles also face challenges such as long charging time, limited charging infrastructure, and battery degradation.

- There are different types of electric vehicles, such as:

  - **Battery electric vehicles (BEV)**, which run solely on electricity stored in a battery pack and need to be plugged in to recharge.
  - **Hybrid electric vehicles (HEV)**, which use both an electric motor and an IC engine. The IC engine generates the required power to drive the motor or charge the battery, and can also directly drive the wheels. ### Working of a Two Stroke Engine

### Scavenging Process

- There are three types of scavenging used in two-stroke engines:
  - Uniflow scavenging: The fresh charge enters from one end of the cylinder and the exhaust gas exits from the other end. This is the most efficient type of scavenging as it prevents the mixing of fresh and exhaust gases.

  - Loop or reverse scavenging: The fresh charge enters from the bottom of the cylinder and the exhaust gas exits from the top. This type of scavenging creates a loop of fresh charge that pushes the exhaust gas out of the cylinder.   - The refrigerant leaves the condenser as a high-pressure, moderate-temperature liquid and enters the expansion device, where it undergoes a sudden drop in pressure and temperature, partially vaporizing and expanding.
  - The refrigerant leaves the expansion device as a low-pressure, low-temperature mixture of liquid and vapor and enters the evaporator, where it absorbs heat from the space or substance to be cooled and changes its phase from liquid to vapor, completing the cycle.

Mass flow rate is the amount of mass of a fluid passing through a cross-sectional area per unit time. It is usually denoted by m-dot and has the units of kg/s.

The mass flow rate can then be calculated by using the continuity equation and the fluid density.

The fluid velocity increases in the converging section, reaches a maximum at the throat, and decreases in the diverging section. m-dot = A1 * sqrt(2 * rho * (P1 - P2) / (1 - (A2 / A1)^2))

where A1 and A2 are the cross-sectional areas of the inlet and the throat, rho is the fluid density, and P1 and P2 are the pressures at the inlet and the throat.

m-dot = Cd * A2 * sqrt(2 * rho * (P1 - P2))

### Evolution of measurement and mechatronics

- Mechatronics is the integration of mechanical, electrical, and computer engineering disciplines to design and control complex systems, such as robots, vehicles, machines, etc. Mechatronics systems combine sensors, actuators, controllers, and software to achieve desired functionality and performance.
- The evolution of measurement and mechatronics can be traced back to the following historical stages   :

  - Industrial Revolution (18th-19th centuries): The development of steam engines, railways, factories, and machines led to the need for accurate and reliable measurement of physical quantities, such as speed, force, power, etc. Measurement instruments such as chronometers, dynamometers, gauges, etc. were invented and improved to meet the industrial demands.
  - Semiconductor Revolution (20th century): The invention of transistors, integrated circuits, microprocessors, and microcontrollers enabled the miniaturization and digitization of electronic components and systems. This allowed for the development of more sophisticated and versatile measurement instruments, such as oscilloscopes, multimeters, logic analyzers, etc. that could process and display digital signals and data.
  - Information Revolution (21st century): The advancement of information and communication technologies, such as the internet, wireless networks, cloud computing, artificial intelligence, etc. enabled the interconnection and integration of measurement instruments and mechatronics systems. This allowed for the creation of smart and autonomous systems, such as drones, self-driving cars, robots, etc. that could communicate, sense, actuate, and control themselves and their environment.

- The evolution of measurement and mechatronics is driven by the following factors   :

  - Innovation: The invention and improvement of new technologies, methods, and concepts that enhance the functionality and performance of measurement instruments and mechatronics systems.
  - Application: The identification and exploitation of new domains and problems that require the use of measurement instruments and mechatronics systems.
  - Integration: The combination and coordination of different disciplines, components, and systems that enable the realization of measurement instruments and mechatronics systems.
  - Optimization: The refinement and adjustment of the design and operation of measurement instruments and mechatronics systems to achieve the best possible outcomes and efficiency.
