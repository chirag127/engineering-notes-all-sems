# Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the process of combining two or more atomic nuclei into a single, heavier nucleus, releasing a large amount of energy in the process. Nuclear fusion is the source of energy for the Sun and other stars.

Some of the main points to know about nuclear fusion are:

- Nuclear fusion requires high temperatures and pressures to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between positively charged nuclei. The conditions for fusion are achieved in the cores of stars, where the temperature is about 15 million degrees Celsius and the pressure is about 250 billion atmospheres.
- The most common fusion reaction in stars is the proton-proton chain, which converts four hydrogen nuclei (protons) into one helium nucleus (alpha particle), releasing two positrons, two neutrinos, and gamma rays. This reaction converts about 0.7% of the mass of the hydrogen into energy, according to Einstein's famous equation E = mc^2.
- Another fusion reaction that occurs in stars is the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen (CNO) cycle, which uses carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen as catalysts to fuse hydrogen into helium. This reaction is more efficient than the proton-proton chain, but requires higher temperatures and densities to operate. The CNO cycle dominates in stars that are more massive than the Sun.
- The fusion reaction that is most feasible for human-made reactors is the deuterium-tritium (D-T) reaction, which fuses a deuterium nucleus (one proton and one neutron) with a tritium nucleus (one proton and two neutrons) into a helium nucleus and a neutron, releasing a lot of energy. Deuterium and tritium are isotopes of hydrogen that have more neutrons than the normal hydrogen. Deuterium is abundant in seawater, while tritium can be produced from lithium.
- The main challenge of achieving nuclear fusion on Earth is to create and sustain a plasma, a state of matter where the nuclei and electrons are separated and can move freely. A plasma can be heated and compressed by various methods, such as magnetic confinement, inertial confinement, or laser fusion. The plasma must reach a high enough temperature, density, and confinement time to achieve a net positive energy output, which is known as the Lawson criterion or the ignition condition.
- The main advantages of nuclear fusion as a source of energy are that it is virtually inexhaustible, it does not produce greenhouse gases or long-lived radioactive waste, and it is safer than nuclear fission, as there is no risk of a meltdown or a runaway chain reaction. The main disadvantages are that it is very difficult and expensive to achieve, it requires a large and complex infrastructure, and it still produces some radioactive byproducts, such as the neutrons from the D-T reaction, which can damage the reactor materials and activate them.

### Force moment and couple

- A force is a push or pull that acts on a body and causes it to accelerate, deform, or rotate.
- A moment is the tendency of a force to cause rotation about a point or an axis. It is also called torque or moment of force. It is a vector quantity that depends on the magnitude and direction of the force, and the perpendicular distance from the point or axis to the line of action of the force.
- A couple is a system of two equal and opposite parallel forces that act on a body. A couple has no resultant force, but it has a resultant moment. The moment of a couple is the same about any point or axis, and it is equal to the product of one of the forces and the perpendicular distance between them. The perpendicular distance is also called the arm of the couple.
- A force can be resolved into a force and a couple at any other point. This means that the original force can be replaced by an equivalent force at a different point and a couple that has the same moment as the original force. This process does not change the net effect of the force on the body.
- Force moment and couple are important concepts in mechanics, as they are used to analyze the static and dynamic equilibrium of rigid bodies, structures, machines, and mechanisms. They are also used to calculate the work done by forces and the power transmitted by shafts.



### Principle of Transmissibility

- The principle of transmissibility states that the point of application of a force can be moved anywhere along its line of action without changing the external reaction forces on a rigid body  .
- The line of action of a force is the infinite straight line that passes through the point of application of the force and is parallel to the direction of the force.
- The principle of transmissibility is based on the assumption that the body is rigid, meaning that it does not deform under the action of forces.
- The principle of transmissibility allows us to simplify the analysis of concurrent force systems by replacing forces with equivalent forces that have the same magnitude and direction, but different points of application along the same line of action.
- The principle of transmissibility does not apply to non-concurrent force systems, where the forces do not have parallel lines of action, or to deformable bodies, where the forces cause changes in the shape or size of the body.
- The principle of transmissibility has some limitations, such as:
  - It does not account for the internal stresses and strains that may be induced by the forces in the body.
  - It does not account for the rotational effects that may be caused by the forces in the body.
  - It does not account for the frictional effects that may be present at the point of contact between the force and the body.



### Varignon's theorem

- Varignon's theorem is a theorem of French mathematician Pierre Varignon (1654–1722), published in 1687 in his book Projet d'une nouvelle mécanique.
- The theorem states that the torque of a resultant of two concurrent forces about any point is equal to the algebraic sum of the torques of its components about the same point .
- Mathematically, the theorem can be expressed as:

$$\vec{M}_O = \vec{M}_{O_1} + \vec{M}_{O_2}$$

where $\vec{M}_O$ is the moment of the resultant force $\vec{R}$ about point O, $\vec{M}_{O_1}$ is the moment of force $\vec{F}_1$ about point O, and $\vec{M}_{O_2}$ is the moment of force $\vec{F}_2$ about point O.

- The theorem can be extended to any number of concurrent forces, as follows:

$$\vec{M}_O = \sum_{i=1}^n \vec{M}_{O_i}$$

where $\vec{M}_O$ is the moment of the resultant force $\vec{R}$ about point O, and $\vec{M}_{O_i}$ is the moment of force $\vec{F}_i$ about point O.

- The theorem can be proved by using the definition of moment as the cross product of position vector and force vector, and the properties of cross product and vector addition.

- Varignon's theorem is extremely useful in calculating the moments of complex force systems, such as the centroid and centre of mass of rigid bodies, and the centre of pressure of hydrostatic forces on submerged objects.

### Equilibrium Equations and Support Reactions

- Equilibrium equations are the conditions that must be satisfied for a structure or a body to be in a state of static equilibrium, meaning that there is no net force or moment acting on it.
- The equilibrium equations can be derived from Newton's laws of motion, and they are:

  - Sum of forces in x-direction = 0
  - Sum of forces in y-direction = 0
  - Sum of moments about any point = 0

- Support reactions are the forces and moments that are exerted by the supports on the structure or the body to keep it in equilibrium.
- The types of support reactions depend on the types of supports, such as:

  - A pin or a hinge support can provide two reaction forces in x and y directions, but no moment.
  - A roller support can provide one reaction force perpendicular to the surface, but no moment.
  - A fixed or a built-in support can provide two reaction forces in x and y directions, and one reaction moment.

- To determine the support reactions, the following steps are usually followed:

  - Draw a free body diagram of the structure or the body, showing all the applied loads and the support reactions as unknowns.
  - Apply the equilibrium equations to the free body diagram, and solve for the unknowns.
  - If the number of unknowns is equal to the number of equilibrium equations, the structure or the body is determinate, meaning that the support reactions can be found by statics alone.
  - If the number of unknowns is greater than the number of equilibrium equations, the structure or the body is indeterminate, meaning that the support reactions cannot be found by statics alone, and additional methods such as compatibility equations or deformation analysis are needed.

### Strain

### Hookes’ law

- Hookes’ law is a law of elasticity that relates the force applied to a spring or other elastic object to the amount of deformation or displacement it undergoes.
- Hookes’ law can be expressed mathematically as F = kx, where F is the force, k is the spring constant or stiffness, and x is the displacement or extension of the spring from its equilibrium position.
- Hookes’ law is valid only for small deformations, where the force and displacement are proportional. For larger deformations, the relationship becomes nonlinear and more complex.
- Hookes’ law was discovered by the English scientist Robert Hooke in 1660, who observed that the extension of a spring is proportional to the tension applied to it.
- Hookes’ law can be used to describe the elastic behavior of many materials and structures, such as springs, rubber bands, wires, beams, columns, etc. Hookes’ law can also be applied to other types of deformation, such as torsion, bending, and shear.

### Elastic Constants and Their Relationship

- Elastic constants are the ratios of the applied stresses to the strains produced in an elastic body. They represent the elastic behaviour of objects and facilitate the understanding of the deformation and restoration of an elastic body under external forces .
- There are four main elastic constants: Young's modulus (E), bulk modulus (K), shear modulus (G) and Poisson's ratio (μ). Each of them describes a different aspect of the elastic response of a material  .
- Young's modulus (E) is the ratio of the longitudinal stress to the longitudinal strain within the elastic limit. It measures the stiffness or resistance to deformation of a material under uniaxial tension or compression .
- Bulk modulus (K) is the ratio of the hydrostatic stress to the volumetric strain within the elastic limit. It measures the resistance to uniform compression or expansion of a material under isotropic pressure .
- Shear modulus (G) is the ratio of the shear stress to the shear strain within the elastic limit. It measures the resistance to shear deformation or distortion of a material under tangential forces .
- Poisson's ratio (μ) is the ratio of the lateral strain to the longitudinal strain within the elastic limit. It measures the tendency of a material to contract or expand laterally when stretched or compressed longitudinally .
- The relationship between these elastic constants depends on the type of material and the state of stress. For an isotropic material, which has the same properties in all directions, the following relations hold  :

  - μ = (3K-2G)/(6K+2G)
  - K = 2G(1+μ)/(3(1-2μ))
  - G = E/(2(1+μ))

- For an anisotropic material, which has different properties in different directions, the elastic constants are not scalar values but tensors that relate the stress and strain components in different directions. ### Stress-Strain Diagram for Ductile and Brittle Materials

- A stress-strain diagram is a graphical representation of the relationship between the applied stress and the resulting strain in a material.
- The stress-strain diagram can be used to determine the mechanical properties of a material, such as the modulus of elasticity, the yield strength, the ultimate strength, and the fracture point.
- The shape of the stress-strain diagram depends on the type of material, whether it is ductile or brittle.
- A ductile material is one that can undergo large plastic deformation before fracture, such as metals, polymers, and some ceramics.
- A brittle material is one that fractures with little or no plastic deformation, such as glass, concrete, and some ceramics.
- The stress-strain diagram for a ductile material typically has the following features:

  - A linear elastic region, where the stress is proportional to the strain, and the material obeys Hooke's law. The slope of this region is the modulus of elasticity, or Young's modulus, of the material.
  - A nonlinear elastic region, where the stress is still reversible, but not proportional to the strain. The material exhibits anelastic behavior, such as hysteresis and creep.
  - A yield point, where the stress reaches a critical value, and the material begins to deform plastically. The yield strength is the stress at the yield point.
  - A plastic region, where the stress increases with increasing strain, but the material undergoes permanent deformation. The material exhibits work hardening, or strain hardening, where the resistance to deformation increases with increasing strain.
  - An ultimate point, where the stress reaches a maximum value, and the material has the highest resistance to deformation. The ultimate strength is the stress at the ultimate point.
  - A necking region, where the stress decreases with increasing strain, and the material undergoes localized deformation. The cross-sectional area of the material decreases, and the material becomes weaker.
  - A fracture point, where the stress drops to zero, and the material breaks. The fracture strength is the stress at the fracture point.

- The stress-strain diagram for a brittle material typically has the following features:

  - A linear elastic region, where the stress is proportional to the strain, and the material obeys Hooke's law. The slope of this region is the modulus of elasticity, or Young's modulus, of the material.
  - A fracture point, where the stress reaches a critical value, and the material breaks. The fracture strength is the stress at the fracture point.
  - No plastic region, no yield point, no ultimate point, and no necking region, as the material fractures with little or no plastic deformation.

  - The number of valves controls the flow of the working fluid into and out of the cylinder. A two-valve engine has one intake and one exhaust valve per cylinder, while a four-valve engine has two intake and two exhaust valves per cylinder.

  - The cooling of the engine prevents overheating and damage to the components. An air-cooled engine uses air as the cooling medium, while a water-cooled engine uses water or a mixture of water and antifreeze as the cooling medium.
  - The application of the engine determines the design and specifications of the engine. An automobile engine is used to power a vehicle, while a stationary engine is used to power a generator, pump, or other equipment. A marine engine is used to propel a boat or ship, while an aircraft engine is used to propel an airplane or helicopter. - A two stroke engine has only two strokes: suction-compression stroke and expansion-exhaust stroke.
- A two stroke engine uses piston-controlled ports instead of valves to control the intake and exhaust of gases.
- A two stroke engine uses the total loss lubrication method, which means the oil is mixed with the fuel and enters the engine along with it.
- A two stroke engine has a simple and compact construction, but it also has some disadvantages such as low efficiency, high emissions, and poor durability.

- A four stroke engine is an engine that completes one cycle in two revolutions of the crankshaft, which means it has one power stroke for every two revolutions.
- A four stroke engine has four strokes: suction stroke, compression stroke, power stroke, and exhaust stroke.
- A four stroke engine uses valves to control the intake and exhaust of gases, which are operated by a camshaft and a timing belt or chain.
- A four stroke engine uses a separate lubrication system, which means the oil is circulated by a pump and does not enter the combustion chamber.
- A four stroke engine has a complex and bulky construction, but it also has some advantages such as high efficiency, low emissions, and long life.

### Scavenging Process

- Scavenging is the process of replacing the exhaust gas in a cylinder of an internal combustion engine with the fresh air/fuel mixture (or fresh air, in the case of direct-injection engines) for the next cycle.
- Scavenging is necessary for the engine's proper fuel combustion and better power output. If scavenging is incomplete, the remaining exhaust gases can cause improper combustion for the next cycle, leading to reduced power output.
- Scavenging occurs in IC engines during the overlapping of valves (Opens at the same time) in which burnt gases are released out of the cylinder with the entering of fresh charge inside the engine at the same time.
- There are three types of scavenging used in two stroke IC engines:
  - Uniflow scavenging: The fresh charge enters from one end of the cylinder and the exhaust gases leave from the other end. This type of scavenging has the highest efficiency and the least mixing of fresh and exhaust gases.
  - Cross-flow scavenging: The fresh charge enters from one side of the cylinder and the exhaust gases leave from the opposite side. This type of scavenging has a lower efficiency and a higher mixing of fresh and exhaust gases .
  - Loop or reverse scavenging: The fresh charge enters from the bottom of the cylinder and the exhaust gases leave from the top. This type of scavenging has a moderate efficiency and a moderate mixing of fresh and exhaust gases.
- The scavenging efficiency is the ratio of the mass of fresh charge admitted to the cylinder to the mass of charge that would be admitted if the cylinder were filled with fresh charge at the same pressure and temperature. The scavenging efficiency can be improved by increasing the pressure difference between the inlet and the outlet, increasing the duration of the scavenging process, and using a suitable scavenging method.



### Difference between two-stroke and four stroke IC engines and SI and CI Engines

- IC engines are internal combustion engines that convert chemical energy of fuel into mechanical energy by burning it inside a combustion chamber.
- SI engines are spark ignition engines that use a spark plug to ignite the air-fuel mixture in the cylinder.
- CI engines are compression ignition engines that use high pressure and temperature to ignite the fuel injected into the cylinder.

#### Two-stroke IC engines
- A two-stroke engine is an engine that completes a power cycle with two strokes of the piston during only one crankshaft revolution.
- The two strokes are intake-compression and power-exhaust.
- The intake and exhaust ports are located on the cylinder wall and are opened and closed by the movement of the piston.
- The engine can produce power after one cycle because the exhaust and intake of the gas occur simultaneously.
- Two-stroke engines are simpler, lighter, and cheaper than four-stroke engines.
- Two-stroke engines are also easier to start in cold temperatures, making them ideal for use in snowmobiles.
- However, two-stroke engines have lower efficiency, higher emissions, and shorter lifespan than four-stroke engines.
- Two-stroke engines also require a special oil-fuel mixture to lubricate the moving parts, which increases the fuel consumption and pollution.

#### Four-stroke IC engines
- A four-stroke engine is an internal combustion engine in which the piston completes four separate strokes while turning the crankshaft.
- The four strokes are intake, compression, power, and exhaust.
- The intake and exhaust valves are located on the cylinder head and are opened and closed by a camshaft.
- The engine can produce power after two cycles because the power stroke is separated from the exhaust stroke.
- Four-stroke engines are more complex, heavier, and more expensive than two-stroke engines.
- However, four-stroke engines produce more torque at lower rpm, generally have better durability than high-revving two-stroke engines and also provide improved fuel efficiency and lower emissions .
- Four-stroke engines do not require a special oil-fuel mixture and can use regular gasoline or diesel.

#### SI engines
- SI engines are spark ignition engines that use a spark plug to ignite the air-fuel mixture in the cylinder.
- SI engines can use either two-stroke or four-stroke cycles, depending on the design and application.
- SI engines are mostly used in gasoline-powered vehicles, motorcycles, and small engines.
- SI engines have a higher power-to-weight ratio, higher thermal efficiency, and lower compression ratio than CI engines.
- However, SI engines have lower fuel economy, higher octane requirement, and higher tendency to knock than CI engines.

#### CI engines
- CI engines are compression ignition engines that use high pressure and temperature to ignite the fuel injected into the cylinder.
- CI engines can use either two-stroke or four-stroke cycles, depending on the design and application.
- CI engines are mostly used in diesel-powered vehicles, trucks, and generators.
- CI engines have a lower power-to-weight ratio, lower thermal efficiency, and higher compression ratio than SI engines.
- However, CI engines have higher fuel economy, lower octane requirement, and lower tendency to knock than SI engines.



### Electric vehicles and hybrid vehicles

- Electric vehicles (EVs) are vehicles that use an electric motor and a battery pack as the main source of propulsion. EVs can be recharged by plugging into an external power source, such as a wall outlet or a charging station .
- Hybrid vehicles are vehicles that use both an ICE and an electric motor as sources of propulsion. - Hybrid vehicles are a compromise between EVs and conventional vehicles, as they can offer some of the benefits of EVs, such as lower emissions and better fuel economy, while avoiding some of the drawbacks, such as limited range and high cost. However, hybrid vehicles still rely on fossil fuels and emit tailpipe emissions, and they have a more complex and heavier drivetrain than EVs .



### Components of an EV

An electric vehicle (EV) is a vehicle that uses one or more electric motors to propel itself. Unlike conventional vehicles that use an internal combustion engine (ICE) to burn fuel, EVs use electricity stored in a battery pack or generated by a fuel cell to power the electric motor. EVs have several advantages over ICE vehicles, such as lower emissions, higher efficiency, lower maintenance costs, and quieter operation.

The main components of an EV are:

- **Traction battery pack**: This is the source of energy for the electric motor. It consists of a series of cells that store electricity in chemical form. The battery pack can be charged by plugging into an external power supply or by regenerative braking, which converts the kinetic energy of the vehicle into electrical energy. The battery pack also provides power to the auxiliary systems of the vehicle, such as lights, air conditioning, and entertainment. The battery pack is usually the heaviest and most expensive component of an EV  .

- **DC-DC converter**: This is a device that converts the high voltage and low current output of the battery pack into a lower voltage and higher current output that is suitable for the electric motor and the auxiliary systems. The DC-DC converter also regulates the power flow between the battery pack and the electric motor, and protects the battery pack from overcharging or over-discharging  .

- **Electric motor**: This is the main component of an EV that converts the electrical energy from the battery pack into mechanical energy that drives the wheels. The electric motor can be either AC (alternating current) or DC (direct current), depending on the design of the vehicle. The electric motor can also act as a generator during regenerative braking, and send electricity back to the battery pack. The electric motor is usually more efficient, compact, and reliable than an ICE   .

- **Power inverter**: This is a device that converts the DC output of the battery pack into AC output that is compatible with the AC electric motor. The power inverter also controls the speed and torque of the electric motor by varying the frequency and amplitude of the AC output. The power inverter is usually integrated with the electric motor in a single unit  .

- **Charge port**: This is the interface that allows the EV to connect to an external power supply, such as a wall outlet or a charging station, in order to charge the battery pack. The charge port can be either AC or DC, depending on the type of charging system. The charge port also communicates with the external power supply and the onboard charger to regulate the charging process.

- **Onboard charger**: This is a device that converts the AC input from the external power supply into DC output that is suitable for charging the battery pack. The onboard charger also monitors the battery pack's temperature, voltage, and current, and adjusts the charging rate accordingly. The onboard charger is usually located inside the vehicle, near the battery pack .

- **Controller**: This is a device that acts as the brain of the EV. It receives inputs from the driver, such as the accelerator pedal and the brake pedal, and sends commands to the electric motor, the power inverter, the DC-DC converter, and the onboard charger. The controller also receives feedback from the battery pack, the electric motor, and the vehicle's sensors, and adjusts the performance and efficiency of the EV accordingly. The controller is usually a microprocessor or a microcontroller that runs a software program  .

- **Auxiliary battery**: This is a small battery that provides power to the vehicle's accessories, such as the lights, the horn, the windshield wipers, and the dashboard. The auxiliary battery is usually a 12-volt lead-acid battery that is charged by the DC-DC converter. The auxiliary battery is separate from the traction battery pack, and has a lower capacity and voltage .



### EV batteries

- EV batteries are the devices that store electrical energy and power the electric motors in electric vehicles (EVs).
- EV batteries are usually rechargeable and can be charged from external sources or from regenerative braking, which converts kinetic energy into electrical energy when the vehicle decelerates.
- The most common type of EV battery is lithium-ion, which has high energy density, long cycle life, and low self-discharge rate. Lithium-ion batteries are composed of positive and negative electrodes, separated by a porous membrane and immersed in a liquid electrolyte that allows the flow of lithium ions.
- Other types of EV batteries include lead-acid, nickel-cadmium, nickel-metal hydride, zinc-air, sodium nickel chloride, and solid-state batteries. Each type has its own advantages and disadvantages in terms of cost, performance, safety, and environmental impact.
- EV batteries are designed to meet the specific requirements of different types of EVs, such as battery electric vehicles (BEVs), plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs), and hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs). The main factors that affect the design of EV batteries are energy capacity, power output, weight, size, durability, and cost.
- EV batteries are subject to degradation over time and use, which reduces their capacity and performance. The main causes of degradation are temperature, state of charge, depth of discharge, and charging and discharging rates. EV batteries can also suffer from thermal runaway, which is a rapid and uncontrollable increase in temperature that can lead to fire or explosion.
- EV batteries are one of the key components of EVs and have a significant impact on their performance, range, cost, and environmental footprint. Therefore, improving the technology and efficiency of EV batteries is essential for the development and adoption of EVs.



### Chargers for Electric Vehicles

- Electric vehicles (EVs) can be charged using different types of chargers that vary in their power output, charging speed, and connector type.
- The most common types of chargers are Level 1, Level 2, and Level 3 (also known as DC fast charging).
- Level 1 chargers are the slowest and use a standard 120-volt (120V) AC outlet. They can deliver about 1.4 kilowatts (kW) of power and provide 4 miles of driving range per hour of charging. They are suitable for overnight charging or for plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs) that have smaller batteries.
- Level 2 chargers are faster and use a 240-volt (240V) AC outlet. They can deliver up to 19.2 kW of power and provide 10 to 60 miles of driving range per hour of charging. They are commonly installed at homes, workplaces, and public charging stations. They require a dedicated circuit and a special connector .
- Level 3 chargers are the fastest and use a 480-volt (480V) DC outlet. They can deliver up to 350 kW of power and provide 180 to 240 miles of driving range in 15 minutes of charging. They are mainly located along highways and in urban areas. They require a high-power transformer and a special connector .
- The type of connector that an EV charger uses depends on the vehicle model and the charging standard. The most common connectors are J1772, Tesla, CHAdeMO, and CCS.
- J1772 is a universal connector that works with both Level 1 and Level 2 chargers. It is compatible with most EVs except Tesla.
- Tesla has its own proprietary connector that works with both Level 1 and Level 2 chargers. It is compatible only with Tesla vehicles, but Tesla also provides adapters for other connectors.
- CHAdeMO is a connector that works with Level 3 chargers. It is compatible with some EVs such as Nissan Leaf, Mitsubishi Outlander, and Kia Soul.
- CCS is a connector that works with Level 3 chargers. It is compatible with most EVs except Tesla and CHAdeMO vehicles.

### Drives for IC Engines and Electric Vehicles

- A drive is a system that transfers power from a source to a load, such as a vehicle's wheels.
- A drive can be mechanical, electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, or a combination of these.
- A drive can also be classified as direct or indirect, depending on whether the power source is directly connected to the load or not.
- An internal combustion engine (ICE) is a type of drive that uses fuel and air to create a combustion reaction inside a cylinder, which pushes a piston that rotates a crankshaft, which drives a transmission, which drives a differential, which drives the wheels.
- An electric vehicle (EV) is a type of drive that uses one or more electric motors to propel the vehicle, which are powered by a battery pack, which is charged by an external source or by regenerative braking, which recovers some of the kinetic energy of the vehicle.
- The main components of an EV drive are the battery pack, the main inverter, the electric motor, the transmission, and the differential.
- The battery pack stores electrical energy and provides it to the main inverter, which converts the direct current (DC) from the battery to alternating current (AC) that drives the electric motor.
- The electric motor converts electrical energy to mechanical energy and rotates a shaft, which drives a transmission, which drives a differential, which drives the wheels.
- The transmission can be single-speed or multi-speed, depending on the design and performance of the vehicle. A single-speed transmission has a fixed gear ratio, while a multi-speed transmission has variable gear ratios that can be changed by the driver or automatically.
- The differential is a device that allows the wheels to rotate at different speeds when the vehicle turns, which reduces tire wear and improves handling. The differential can be open, limited-slip, or locking, depending on the type and level of traction control.



### Transmission and Power Devices for IC Engines and Electric Vehicles

- Transmission is a machine that transmits power from the energy source (electric motor or internal combustion engine) to the wheels of the vehicle.
- Power devices are components that convert, regulate, or control the power flow in the transmission system.
- Some examples of transmission and power devices for IC engines and electric vehicles are:

  - Power split device: A device that can operate the vehicle with electric motor power and the IC engine separately or can also combine the power from both sources. It also acts as a continuously variable transmission (CVT) and eliminates the need for any manual or automatic transmission.
  - Motor: A device that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. Electric vehicles use motors to drive the wheels, while hybrid vehicles use motors to assist the IC engine or to regenerate electricity during braking .
  - Differential: A device that allows the wheels to rotate at different speeds while receiving power from the transmission. It is essential for smooth turning and cornering of the vehicle.
  - Sensor interface: A device that converts the signals from various sensors (such as temperature, pressure, speed, etc.) into digital or analog signals that can be processed by the engine control unit (ECU) or the battery management system (BMS).
  - Load or motor driver: A device that controls the current or voltage supplied to the loads (such as fuel injectors, ignition coils, etc.) or the motors (such as electric power steering, cooling fan, etc.) according to the commands from the ECU or the BMS.
  - Voltage regulator: A device that maintains a constant output voltage for the loads or the motors, regardless of the variations in the input voltage or the load current.
  - Alternator: A device that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy. It is used to charge the battery and power the electrical system of the vehicle. It is driven by the IC engine or the motor.



### Advantages and disadvantages of EVs

Electric vehicles (EVs) are vehicles that use one or more electric motors for propulsion, instead of internal combustion engines (ICEs) that burn fossil fuels. EVs can be powered by batteries, fuel cells, or other sources of electricity. EVs have several advantages and disadvantages compared to ICE vehicles, which are summarized below.

#### Advantages of EVs

- **Environmental impact**: EVs have lower or zero tailpipe emissions of greenhouse gases and air pollutants, which contribute to climate change and health problems. EVs can also reduce the dependence on fossil fuels and increase the use of renewable energy sources for transportation .
- **Energy efficiency**: EVs are more efficient than ICE vehicles, as they convert a higher percentage of the electrical energy from the grid or the battery to power the wheels. ICE vehicles lose a lot of energy as heat and noise. EVs also have regenerative braking, which recovers some of the kinetic energy that would otherwise be wasted.
- **Cost savings**: EVs have lower operating and maintenance costs than ICE vehicles, as they have fewer moving parts and fluids that need to be replaced or serviced. EVs also have lower fuel costs, as electricity is cheaper than gasoline or diesel in most regions. EVs can also benefit from tax incentives, subsidies, or exemptions in some countries .
- **Performance and comfort**: EVs have better acceleration and torque than ICE vehicles, as they can deliver instant power to the wheels. EVs also have lower noise and vibration levels, which improve the driving experience and reduce noise pollution .

#### Disadvantages of EVs

- **Recharge points**: EVs have a limited range and need to be recharged frequently, which can be inconvenient and time-consuming. The availability and accessibility of charging stations are still limited in many areas, especially in rural or remote locations. EVs also need different types of chargers and connectors, which may not be compatible with all models or networks .
- **Initial investment**: EVs have higher upfront costs than ICE vehicles, mainly due to the expensive batteries that store the electricity. The batteries also degrade over time and need to be replaced or recycled, which can add to the environmental and economic costs. EVs may not be affordable or attractive for all consumers, especially those who drive long distances or have low incomes .
- **Electricity source**: EVs are only as clean as the electricity that powers them. If the electricity is generated from fossil fuels, such as coal or natural gas, EVs may still have a significant carbon footprint and environmental impact. EVs also increase the demand for electricity, which may strain the grid or require more infrastructure and generation capacity .
- **Driving range and speed**: EVs have a shorter driving range than ICE vehicles, as the battery capacity and performance are affected by various factors, such as temperature, terrain, driving style, and load. EVs also have lower top speeds than ICE vehicles, as they are designed to optimize efficiency and battery life. EVs may not be suitable for long trips or high-speed driving .



### Hybrid electric vehicles

- A hybrid electric vehicle (HEV) is a type of hybrid vehicle that combines a conventional internal combustion engine (ICE) system with an electric propulsion system (hybrid vehicle drivetrain)  .
- The presence of the electric powertrain is intended to achieve either better fuel economy than a conventional vehicle or better performance  .
- A hybrid electric vehicle cannot be plugged in to charge the battery. Instead, the battery is charged through regenerative braking and by the internal combustion engine .
- There are different types of hybrid electric vehicles, such as parallel hybrids, series hybrids, power-split hybrids, and plug-in hybrids (PHEVs)  .
- Parallel hybrids have both the ICE and the electric motor connected to the mechanical transmission, and can operate using either or both sources of power  .
- Series hybrids have only the electric motor connected to the wheels, and use the ICE as a generator to charge the battery or provide additional power to the motor  .
- Power-split hybrids have a planetary gear set that allows the ICE and the electric motor to provide power to the wheels in different ratios, depending on the driving conditions  .
- Plug-in hybrids (PHEVs) are similar to power-split hybrids, but have a larger battery that can be recharged by plugging into an external power source, allowing them to run more on electric power and less on gasoline  .
- Hybrid electric vehicles have several advantages over conventional vehicles, such as lower emissions, higher fuel efficiency, reduced dependency on fossil fuels, and improved performance   .
- Hybrid electric vehicles also have some challenges, such as higher cost, complexity, weight, and maintenance, as well as limited battery capacity and range   .
- Some examples of hybrid electric vehicles are the Toyota Prius, the Honda Insight, the Ford Fusion Hybrid, the Kia Niro Plug-In Hybrid, and the Hyundai Ioniq Hybrid   .



### HEV drive train components

- A hybrid electric vehicle (HEV) is a type of hybrid vehicle that combines a conventional internal combustion engine (ICE) system with an electric propulsion system (hybrid vehicle drivetrain) .
- The main components of an HEV drivetrain are  :
  - Electric traction motors/controllers: These are devices that convert electrical energy into mechanical energy to drive the wheels of the vehicle. They also control the speed, torque and direction of the motor. Some HEVs use one motor, while others use two or more motors for different modes of operation.
  - Electric energy storage systems: These are devices that store electrical energy for later use. They can be batteries, ultracapacitors, flywheels or fuel cells. They provide power to the electric motor when needed, and also receive power from the motor or the ICE during regenerative braking or charging.
  - Internal combustion engine (ICE): This is a conventional engine that burns fuel to produce mechanical power. It can be gasoline, diesel, ethanol, natural gas or hydrogen. It can either directly drive the wheels, or act as a generator to charge the electric energy storage system or power the electric motor.
  - Fuel tank: This is a container that holds the fuel for the ICE. It can vary in size and shape depending on the type and amount of fuel used.
  - Control board: This is a device that manages the operation of the HEV drivetrain. ### Construction and Working of Domestic Refrigerator

### Humidity

Humidity is a measure of the amount of water vapor present in the air. Water vapor is the gaseous state of water, which is generally invisible to the human eye. Humidity indicates the likelihood for precipitation, dew, or fog to be present. Humidity depends on the temperature and pressure of the system of interest.

There are three primary measurements of humidity that are widely employed: absolute, relative, and specific.

- **Absolute humidity** describes the water content present in the air and is expressed in either gram per cubic meter or grams per kilogram. The absolute humidity in the atmosphere ranges from near zero to roughly 30 grams per cubic metre. Absolute humidity is not affected by the temperature or pressure of the air, but it changes with the volume of the air.
- **Relative humidity** is the ratio of the partial pressure of water vapor to the equilibrium vapor pressure of water at the same temperature. It is expressed as a percentage and indicates how close the air is to saturation. Relative humidity depends on both the temperature and the pressure of the air, and it changes with the weather conditions. A relative humidity of 100% means that the air is saturated with water vapor and cannot hold any more, creating the possibility of rain.
- **Specific humidity** is the ratio of water vapor mass to total moist air parcel mass. It is expressed in grams of water vapor per kilogram of air. Specific humidity is approximately equal to the mixing ratio, which is defined as the ratio of the mass of water vapor in an air parcel to the mass of dry air for the same parcel. Specific humidity is not affected by the temperature or pressure of the air, but it changes with the addition or removal of water vapor.

Humidity can be measured using various instruments, such as hygrometers, psychrometers, dew point meters, and humidity sensors. These instruments can measure one or more of the humidity parameters mentioned above, depending on their design and principle of operation. Humidity measurement is important for various applications, such as meteorology, agriculture, industry, health, and comfort.



### Dry Bulb Temperature

- Dry bulb temperature (DBT) is the temperature of air measured by a thermometer freely exposed to the air, but shielded from radiation and moisture.
- DBT is the temperature that is usually thought of as air temperature, and it is the true thermodynamic temperature.
- DBT is directly proportional to the mean kinetic energy of the air molecules.
- DBT is an indicator of heat content and is shown along the bottom axis of the psychrometric chart or along the left side of the Mollier diagram.
- DBT is also called "dry bulb" because the air temperature is indicated by a thermometer not affected by the moisture of the air.
- DBT is contrasted with wet bulb temperature and dew point temperature, which are related to the humidity of the air.



### Wet Bulb

- Wet bulb is a term used to describe the temperature of a thermometer whose bulb is covered with a wet cloth and exposed to air flow.
- Wet bulb temperature is the lowest temperature that can be reached by evaporating water into the air at constant pressure.
- Wet bulb temperature is an indicator of how the human body would feel in direct sunlight, as it reflects the cooling effect of sweat evaporation.
- Wet bulb temperature is also an important parameter in psychrometry, the study of moist air and its properties.
- Wet bulb temperature can be measured by using a wet bulb thermometer, a sling psychrometer, or a wet bulb globe temperature device.
- Wet bulb temperature can be calculated from dry bulb temperature (the temperature of the air without any moisture) and relative humidity (the ratio of the amount of water vapor in the air to the maximum amount possible at the same temperature) using formulas or tables.
- Wet bulb temperature is always lower than or equal to dry bulb temperature, and the difference between them is called the wet bulb depression.
- Wet bulb temperature is influenced by the amount of water vapor in the air, the air pressure, and the air flow rate.



### Dew point temperature

- Dew point temperature is important for refrigeration and air-conditioning systems, as it affects the cooling capacity, energy efficiency, and moisture removal of the systems.
- Dew point temperature can be calculated from the relative humidity and dry bulb temperature of the air, using psychrometric charts or equations.

  - Condensation: The refrigerant vapour at high pressure and temperature is supplied by the compressor to the condenser, where it releases heat to the outside air and condenses into a liquid.
  - Expansion: The refrigerant liquid at high pressure and temperature is passed through the capillary tube, where it expands and drops its pressure and temperature.
  - Evaporation: The refrigerant liquid at low pressure and temperature is supplied by the capillary tube to the evaporator, where it absorbs heat from the room air and evaporates into a vapour.
- The cycle repeats until the desired temperature of the room is reached.
- The air circuit of a window air conditioner consists of three major air circulation systems:
  - Room air: The air from the room is drawn over the evaporator fan through a filter, which removes dirt, dust, impurities, etc. The fan blows the cool and dry air back into the room.
  - Outside air: The air from the outside is drawn over the condenser fan through a grille, which prevents the entry of insects, birds, etc. The fan blows the hot and humid air out of the unit.
  - Refrigerant air: The air that carries the refrigerant between the compressor, the condenser, and the evaporator. It is sealed and insulated from the room air and the outside air.
- The advantages of a window air conditioner are :

### Newtonian and Non-Newtonian Fluids

- Fluids are substances that can flow and deform under external forces.
- Fluids can be classified into two major types: **Newtonian** and **non-Newtonian** based on their viscosity behavior.
- Viscosity is a measure of how much a fluid resists deformation or flow under shear stress.
- Shear stress is the force per unit area applied parallel to the fluid surface.
- Shear rate is the rate of change of fluid velocity with respect to the perpendicular distance from the fluid surface.

#### Newtonian Fluids

- Newtonian fluids are the ones that obey **Newton’s law of viscosity**, which states that the shear stress is directly proportional to the shear rate.
- These fluids have a **constant viscosity** that does not depend on the shear stress or shear rate.
- The viscosity of Newtonian fluids only changes with temperature or pressure.
- Examples of Newtonian fluids are water, mineral oil, gasoline, alcohol, etc.

#### Non-Newtonian Fluids

- Non-Newtonian fluids are the ones that do not obey Newton’s law of viscosity, which means that their viscosity varies with the shear stress or shear rate.
- These fluids have a **variable viscosity** that can increase or decrease under different shear conditions.
- Non-Newtonian fluids can be further classified into different types based on their viscosity behavior, such as:
  - **Shear-thinning fluids**: These fluids have a **decreasing viscosity** with an increasing shear rate. They become thinner and more fluid-like when agitated or shaken. Examples are ketchup, blood, paint, shampoo, etc.
  - **Shear-thickening fluids**: These fluids have an **increasing viscosity** with an increasing shear rate. They become thicker and more solid-like when agitated or shaken. Examples are cornstarch and water mixture, quicksand, whipped cream, etc.
  - **Thixotropic fluids**: These fluids have a **decreasing viscosity** over time when subjected to a constant shear stress. They become thinner and more fluid-like when left undisturbed. Examples are yogurt, honey, jelly, etc.
  - **Rheopectic fluids**: These fluids have an **increasing viscosity** over time when subjected to a constant shear stress. They become thicker and more solid-like when left undisturbed. Examples are some types of clay, cream, etc.
  - **Bingham plastic fluids**: These fluids have a **minimum shear stress** required to initiate flow. They behave like solids below this threshold and like fluids above it. Examples are toothpaste, mayonnaise, mustard, etc.

### Pascal’s Law and Continuity Equation

Pascal's law is a principle of fluid mechanics that states that a pressure applied to a fluid in a closed container is transmitted equally to every point of the fluid and the walls of the container. This means that if a force F is applied to a piston with area A, the pressure P exerted by the fluid is given by:

P = F / A

This pressure is the same at any point in the fluid, regardless of the shape or size of the container. Pascal's law can be used to explain how hydraulic systems work, such as brakes, lifts, and presses.

The continuity equation is another principle of fluid mechanics that states that the mass flow rate of a fluid is constant in a steady-state flow system. This means that the product of the cross-sectional area A and the velocity v of the fluid is the same at any point in the system, regardless of the changes in the shape or size of the pipe. The continuity equation can be written as:

A1v1 = A2v2

This equation expresses the conservation of mass in a fluid system. The continuity equation can be used to analyze the flow of fluids in pipes, nozzles, and turbines.

Some applications of Pascal's law and the continuity equation are:

- Hydraulic brakes: When the brake pedal is pressed, a force is applied to a piston in a master cylinder, which increases the pressure in the brake fluid. This pressure is transmitted equally to the pistons in the brake calipers, which apply a force to the brake pads, slowing down the wheels.
- Hydraulic lift: When a force is applied to a small piston in a pump, the pressure in the hydraulic fluid increases. This pressure is transmitted equally to a large piston in a cylinder, which lifts a heavy load. The ratio of the areas of the pistons determines the mechanical advantage of the lift.
- Venturi meter: A venturi meter is a device that measures the flow rate of a fluid in a pipe. It consists of a pipe with a narrow section called a throat. According to the continuity equation, the velocity of the fluid increases and the pressure decreases at the throat. By measuring the pressure difference between the throat and the normal section, the flow rate can be calculated.

  - They have a non-linear output and low voltage, which require amplification and linearization.

#### Optical Pyrometer

- An optical pyrometer is a device that measures the temperature of a hot object by comparing the intensity or the color of the radiation emitted by the object with a known standard source.
- An optical pyrometer consists of an optical system, a detector, a display, and a calibration source.
- The optical system focuses the radiation from the object onto the detector, which converts it into an electrical signal.
- The display shows the temperature of the object, based on the calibration of the detector and the source.
- Optical pyrometers are used for measuring high temperatures from 700°C to 4000°C, with moderate accuracy and fast response time.
- Optical pyrometers are suitable for measuring temperatures of objects that are inaccessible, moving, or hazardous, such as furnaces, flames, and molten metals.
- Some advantages of optical pyrometers are:
  - They are non-contact, which avoids heat loss, damage, or interference with the object.
  - They are immune to electromagnetic interference and ambient temperature variations.
  - They have a high resolution and a wide temperature range.
- Some disadvantages of optical pyrometers are:
  - They require a clear line of sight and a known emissivity of the object, which can vary with the surface condition, wavelength, and angle of view.
  - They are affected by dust, smoke, steam, or other sources of radiation, which can obscure or distort the signal.
  - They are expensive and complex, and require a power supply and a calibration source.



### Mass Flow Rate (Venturi Meter and Orifice Meter)

- Mass flow rate is the amount of mass of a fluid passing through a cross-sectional area per unit time. It is usually denoted by m-dot and has the units of kg/s or lbm/s.
- Venturi meter and orifice meter are devices that reduce the pressure of a flowing fluid to measure its average velocity, volumetric flow rate or mass flow rate. They are based on the principle of Bernoulli's equation, which states that the total energy of a fluid (pressure, kinetic and potential) along a streamline is constant.
- Venturi meter is a device that consists of a converging section, a throat and a diverging section. The fluid velocity increases in the converging section, reaches a maximum at the throat and decreases in the diverging section. The pressure decreases in the converging section, reaches a minimum at the throat and increases in the diverging section. The pressure difference between the inlet and the throat is proportional to the square of the fluid velocity at the throat, and hence to the mass flow rate of the fluid.
- Orifice meter is a device that consists of a thin plate with a hole (orifice) in it. The fluid velocity increases as it passes through the orifice, and the pressure decreases. The pressure difference between the upstream and downstream of the orifice is proportional to the square of the fluid velocity at the orifice, and hence to the mass flow rate of the fluid.
- Venturi meter is more accurate than orifice meter because it has a smoother and gradual contraction and expansion of the fluid, which reduces the energy losses due to friction and turbulence. The experimental measurement by venturi meter is 0.96-0.99 times the theoretical measurement, while the experimental measurement by orifice meter is 0.6-0.62 times the theoretical measurement .
- Venturi meter is also more expensive and bulky than orifice meter, and requires more space for installation. Orifice meter is simpler and cheaper, and can be easily replaced or modified. Both devices require calibration and correction factors to account for the effects of viscosity, compressibility, temperature, density and Reynolds number of the fluid.



### Strain (Bonded and Unbonded Strain Gauge)

- Strain is the measure of the deformation or change in dimensions of a body due to an applied force or stress.
- Strain gauge is a device that converts strain into a measurable electrical signal.
- There are two types of strain gauges: bonded and unbonded.

#### Bonded Strain Gauge

- A bonded strain gauge is a thin metallic foil or wire that is attached or glued to the surface of the specimen whose strain is to be measured.
- When the specimen is subjected to stress, the strain gauge also deforms and changes its resistance due to the change in length and cross-sectional area.
- The change in resistance is proportional to the strain and can be measured by a Wheatstone bridge circuit or a strain indicator.
- Bonded strain gauges are widely used for static and dynamic measurements of stress, force, torque, pressure, etc.
- Bonded strain gauges have the advantages of high sensitivity, accuracy, stability, and compatibility with the specimen material.
- Bonded strain gauges have the disadvantages of being affected by temperature changes, moisture, and surface irregularities.

#### Unbonded Strain Gauge

- An unbonded strain gauge is a resistance wire or foil that is stretched between two frames or supports and is not attached to the specimen whose strain is to be measured.
- The specimen is connected to the strain gauge by thin rods or wires that transmit the strain from the specimen to the gauge.
- When the specimen is subjected to stress, the strain gauge changes its resistance due to the change in length and tension of the wire or foil.
- The change in resistance is proportional to the strain and can be measured by a Wheatstone bridge circuit or a strain indicator.
- Unbonded strain gauges are used for measuring very small strains and forces that are difficult to measure by bonded gauges.
- Unbonded strain gauges have the advantages of being less affected by temperature changes, moisture, and surface irregularities.
- Unbonded strain gauges have the disadvantages of being less sensitive, accurate, stable, and robust than bonded gauges.



### Force (Proving Ring) and Torques (Prony Brake Dynamometer) for the Notes of the Unit 5 - Introduction to Measurement and Mechatronics in the Subject of Fundamentals of Mechanical Engineering

- Force is a physical quantity that causes a change in the motion, shape, or deformation of an object. Force can be measured by various methods, such as using a spring balance, a load cell, or a proving ring.
- A proving ring is a device used to measure force. It consists of an elastic ring of known diameter with a measuring device located in the center of the ring. Proving rings come in a variety of sizes. They are made of a steel alloy.
- The principle of a proving ring is based on the Hooke's law, which states that the deformation of an elastic body is proportional to the applied force. When a force is applied to the ring, it causes a slight change in its diameter, which is detected by the measuring device. The measuring device can be a dial gauge, a strain gauge, or a load cell.
- The advantages of a proving ring are that it is simple, robust, accurate, and easy to calibrate. The disadvantages are that it is sensitive to temperature changes, it has a limited range of measurement, and it requires a correction factor for the effect of gravity.
- Torque is a physical quantity that measures the tendency of a force to rotate an object about an axis. Torque can be measured by various methods, such as using a torsion balance, a torque meter, or a prony brake dynamometer.
- A prony brake dynamometer is a device used to measure the torque and power of a rotating shaft. It consists of a frictional brake that clamps around the shaft and a lever arm that measures the braking force. The brake can be adjusted to vary the amount of resistance on the shaft .
- The principle of a prony brake dynamometer is based on the balance of torques. When the brake is applied to the shaft, it causes a frictional force that opposes the rotation of the shaft. The frictional force is measured by the lever arm, which is attached to a spring balance or a load cell. The torque is calculated by multiplying the frictional force by the radius of the shaft. The power is calculated by multiplying the torque by the angular speed of the shaft .
- The advantages of a prony brake dynamometer are that it is simple, inexpensive, and easy to use. The disadvantages are that it is inaccurate, it causes a lot of heat and wear on the shaft, and it wastes a lot of energy in the form of heat .



### Concepts of accuracy for the notes of the Unit 5 - Introduction to Measurement and Mechatronics in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF MECHANICAL ENGINEERING

- Accuracy is the degree of closeness of a measurement to the true or accepted value of the quantity being measured  .
- Accuracy is affected by systematic errors, which are caused by factors such as calibration, environmental conditions, or instrument design .
- Accuracy can be expressed as a percentage of error, which is the ratio of the absolute error to the true value, multiplied by 100.
- Accuracy can also be expressed as a tolerance, which is the range of values within which the true value is expected to lie .
- Accuracy can be improved by using more precise instruments, reducing systematic errors, and applying correction factors .
- Accuracy is important for ensuring the reliability and validity of measurements and data analysis .
- Accuracy is different from precision, which is the degree of repeatability or consistency of a measurement  .
- Precision is affected by random errors, which are caused by factors such as noise, fluctuations, or human errors .
- Precision can be expressed as a standard deviation, which is a measure of how much the measurements deviate from the mean value .
- Precision can also be expressed as a resolution, which is the smallest change in the quantity being measured that can be detected by the instrument .
- Precision can be improved by using more sensitive instruments, reducing random errors, and increasing the number of measurements .
- Precision is important for ensuring the accuracy and quality of measurements and data analysis .
- Precision and accuracy are related but not equivalent concepts. A measurement can be precise but not accurate, accurate but not precise, both precise and accurate, or neither precise nor accurate .
- Precision and accuracy can be represented by a target diagram, where the bullseye is the true value, the arrows are the measurements, and the distance between the arrows and the bullseye is the error .

### Precision and Resolution

- Precision is the amount of information that is conveyed in terms of digits. It refers to the resolution or limit of the measurement.
- Precision is independent of accuracy. Accuracy denotes how close the measured value is to the true value of a given quantity.
- Precision lets the operator know how well-repeated measurements of the same object will agree with one another.
- Precision is an instrument’s degree of repeatability—how reliably it can reproduce the same measurement over and over.
- Resolution is the total weighing range of a scale divided by the readability of the display.
- Resolution is the smallest increment an instrument can detect and display—hundredths, thousandths, millionths.
- Resolution is a measure of the smallest change in the quantity being measured that causes a perceptible change in the corresponding indication.
- Resolution affects the precision of a measurement, but not the accuracy.



### Introduction to Mechatronic Systems

- A mechatronic system is a system that integrates mechanical, electrical, and computer engineering components to perform a desired function.
- Mechatronic systems can be found in various applications, such as robotics, automotive, aerospace, biomedical, manufacturing, and consumer products.
- Mechatronic systems are designed using a multidisciplinary approach that considers the interactions and trade-offs between different subsystems and components.
- Mechatronic systems typically consist of four main elements: sensors, actuators, controllers, and interfaces.
  - Sensors are devices that measure physical quantities, such as position, velocity, force, temperature, pressure, etc., and convert them into electrical signals.
  - Actuators are devices that convert electrical signals into physical actions, such as motion, force, torque, etc., to manipulate the system or the environment.
  - Controllers are devices that process the sensor signals and generate the actuator signals, using algorithms, logic, or feedback, to achieve the desired system behavior or performance.
  - Interfaces are devices that enable communication and data exchange between the system and the user, other systems, or external devices, using displays, keyboards, touchscreens, wireless networks, etc.
- Mechatronic systems are characterized by the following features:
  - Synergy: the system performance is greater than the sum of the performance of the individual components, due to the integration and coordination of different domains and disciplines.
  - Adaptability: the system can adjust its behavior or parameters according to the changes in the environment, the user input, or the system state, using feedback, learning, or optimization techniques.
  - Intelligence: the system can perform complex tasks, such as perception, decision making, planning, or learning, using artificial intelligence, machine learning, or computer vision methods.
  - Autonomy: the system can operate independently or with minimal human intervention, using self-monitoring, self-diagnosis, or self-repair capabilities.



### Evolution for the notes of the Unit 5 - Introduction to Measurement and Mechatronics in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF MECHANICAL ENGINEERING

- Measurement and mechatronics are two interrelated fields of engineering that deal with the design, development and application of systems that integrate mechanical, electrical, electronic and computer components.
- Measurement is the process of obtaining quantitative information about physical quantities, such as length, mass, force, temperature, etc., using instruments or devices that can sense, convert, display and record the data.
- Mechatronics is the synergistic combination of mechanical engineering, electronics, control engineering and computer science, applied to the design of systems, products and processes that are controlled by microprocessors and software.
- The evolution of measurement and mechatronics can be traced back to the development of various technologies and disciplines over time, such as:
  - The invention of the steam engine and the industrial revolution in the 18th and 19th centuries, which stimulated the need for accurate measurement of pressure, temperature, speed, power, etc., and the emergence of mechanical engineering as a distinct field.
  - The discovery of electricity and magnetism and the advancement of electrical engineering and electronics in the 19th and 20th centuries, which enabled the creation of electrical and electronic devices and circuits that could measure, transmit, process and store information, and the development of electromechanical systems, such as electric motors, generators, transformers, relays, etc.
  - The introduction of the transistor and the integrated circuit in the mid-20th century, which revolutionized the fields of electronics and computing, and led to the miniaturization, digitization and automation of many systems and devices, and the emergence of microelectronics, microprocessors and software engineering.
  - The integration of mechanical, electrical, electronic and computer components and technologies in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, which resulted in the creation of complex, intelligent and adaptive systems and products that can sense, actuate, control and communicate, and the establishment of mechatronics as a multidisciplinary field of engineering.
- Some examples of measurement and mechatronics systems and products are:
  - Sensors and transducers, which convert physical quantities into electrical signals, such as resistive, capacitive, inductive, piezoelectric, Hall effect, optical and digital transducers.
  - Data acquisition systems, which collect, convert, process and store data from sensors and transducers, such as analog-to-digital converters, multiplexers, amplifiers, filters, microcontrollers, etc.
  - Actuators and drives, which convert electrical signals into mechanical motion or force, such as electric motors, solenoids, relays, valves, etc.
  - Control systems, which regulate the behavior and performance of systems and processes, using feedback, feedforward, PID, fuzzy logic, neural networks, etc.
  - Mechatronic products, which incorporate sensors, actuators, control systems and software, such as robots, machine tools, washing machines, cameras, etc.
