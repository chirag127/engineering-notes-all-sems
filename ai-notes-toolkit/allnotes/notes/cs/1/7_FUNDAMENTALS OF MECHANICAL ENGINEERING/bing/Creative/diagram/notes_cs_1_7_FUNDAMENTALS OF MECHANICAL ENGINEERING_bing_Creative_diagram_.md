## Unit 1 - Introduction to Mechanics

Mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion and interaction of objects under the influence of forces.

Some of the main topics in mechanics are:

- Kinematics: the study of how objects move without considering the causes of their motion.
- Dynamics: the study of how forces affect the motion of objects.
- Statics: the study of how objects remain at rest or in equilibrium under the action of forces.
- Work, energy and power: the study of how forces do work on objects and how energy is transferred and transformed.
- Momentum and collisions: the study of how objects conserve their momentum and how they interact during collisions.
- Rotational motion: the study of how objects rotate around an axis and how angular quantities are related to linear quantities.
- Gravitation: the study of how objects attract each other due to their mass and how gravity affects the motion of objects.
- Fluid mechanics: the study of how fluids (liquids and gases) flow and exert pressure on objects.

Some of the main concepts and principles in mechanics are:

- Displacement, velocity and acceleration: the quantities that describe the position, speed and change of speed of an object.
- Force: a push or pull that can cause an object to accelerate or deform.
- Newton's laws of motion: three laws that relate the forces acting on an object to its motion.
- Friction: a force that opposes the relative motion of two surfaces in contact.
- Free-body diagrams: diagrams that show all the forces acting on an object and their directions.
- Equilibrium: a state in which the net force and the net torque acting on an object are zero.
- Work: the product of force and displacement in the direction of the force.
- Energy: the capacity to do work or cause change.
- Kinetic energy: the energy of an object due to its motion.
- Potential energy: the energy of an object due to its position or configuration.
- Conservation of energy: the principle that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed.
- Power: the rate of doing work or transferring energy.
- Momentum: the product of mass and velocity of an object.
- Impulse: the product of force and time interval during which the force acts.
- Conservation of momentum: the principle that the total momentum of a system of objects remains constant unless an external force acts on the system.
- Elastic and inelastic collisions: types of collisions in which the kinetic energy of the system is conserved or not conserved, respectively.
- Angular displacement, velocity and acceleration: the quantities that describe the angle, angular speed and angular change of speed of an object.
- Torque: a force that causes an object to rotate or change its angular momentum.
- Moment of inertia: a measure of an object's resistance to change its angular velocity.
- Angular momentum: the product of moment of inertia and angular velocity of an object.
- Conservation of angular momentum: the principle that the total angular momentum of a system of objects remains constant unless an external torque acts on the system.
- Universal law of gravitation: the law that states that every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force that is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.
- Gravitational field: the region around an object where it exerts a gravitational force on other objects.
- Gravitational potential energy: the energy of an object due to its position in a gravitational field.
- Kepler's laws of planetary motion: three laws that describe the motion of planets around the sun.
- Fluid: a substance that can flow and take the shape of its container.
- Density: the mass per unit volume of a substance.
- Pressure: the force per unit area exerted by a fluid on a surface.
- Pascal's principle: the principle that states that a change in pressure at any point in a confined fluid is transmitted equally to all parts of the fluid and to the walls of the container.
- Archimedes' principle: the principle that states that the buoyant force on an object submerged in a fluid is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object.
- Bernoulli's principle: the principle that states that the pressure in a moving fluid decreases as its speed increases.

### Types of supports (Hinge, Roller) and loads (Point, UDL, UVL)

- Supports are devices that connect a structural member to the ground or to another part of the structure and provide resistance to forces and moments.
- Supports can be classified into four main types: simple, roller, hinge, and fixed.
- Simple supports are free to rotate and translate in any direction. They can only resist a vertical force. They are also called knife-edge supports.
- Roller supports are free to rotate and translate along the surface upon which the roller rests. They can resist a vertical force but not a horizontal force or a moment. They are commonly used for bridges and beams.
- Hinge supports are capable of resisting forces acting in any direction of the plane, but not out of the plane. They can also resist a moment about the axis perpendicular to the plane. They allow rotation about any axis in the plane. They are also called pin supports.
- Fixed supports can resist vertical and horizontal forces as well as moments. They restrain both rotation and translation. They are also called built-in or cantilever supports.
- Loads are external forces or moments that act on a structural member and cause deformation, stress, or displacement.
- Loads can be classified into three main types: point, uniformly distributed (UDL), and uniformly varying (UVL).
- Point loads are concentrated forces or moments that act at a single point on a structural member. They are also called concentrated loads. They can cause high stress and deflection at the point of application.

- The point C is called the ultimate stress or ultimate strength of the material, where the material reaches its maximum load-bearing capacity.
- The region between C and D is called the necking region, where the material undergoes localized deformation and the cross-sectional area decreases rapidly. The stress decreases with increasing strain due to the reduction in area.
- The point D is called the fracture point, where the material breaks or ruptures. The stress at this point is called the fracture stress or fracture strength of the material.
- The strain at this point is called the fracture strain or ductility of the material, which is a measure of how much the material can stretch before breaking.

### Scavenging Process

- Scavenging is the process of replacing the exhaust gas in a cylinder of an internal combustion engine with the fresh air/fuel mixture (or fresh air, in the case of direct-injection engines) for the next cycle.
- Scavenging is necessary for the engine's proper fuel combustion and better power output .
- Scavenging occurs in IC engines during the overlapping of valves (Opens at the same time) in which burnt gases are released out of the cylinder with the entering of fresh charge inside the engine at the same time.
- There are three types of scavenging used in two stroke IC engines:
  - Uniflow scavenging: The fresh charge enters from one end of the cylinder and the exhaust gas exits from the other end. This type of scavenging has the least amount of residual gas and the highest efficiency.
  - Cross-flow scavenging: The fresh charge enters from one side of the cylinder and the exhaust gas exits from the opposite side. This type of scavenging has a simple design but a high amount of residual gas and a low efficiency .
  - Loop or reverse scavenging: The fresh charge enters from the bottom of the cylinder and the exhaust gas exits from the top. This type of scavenging has a moderate amount of residual gas and a moderate efficiency.
- The scavenging efficiency is the ratio of the mass of fresh charge admitted to the cylinder to the mass of charge that would be admitted if the cylinder were completely emptied of residual gas.
- The scavenging process can be improved by increasing the pressure difference between the inlet and the outlet, increasing the duration of the scavenging period, and using a suitable scavenging port or valve design .

- A compressor, which is a device that increases the pressure and temperature of the refrigerant by reducing its volume.
- A condenser, which is a device that transfers heat from the refrigerant to the surroundings, causing the refrigerant to change from gas to liquid.
- An expansion valve, which is a device that reduces the pressure and temperature of the refrigerant by increasing its volume.
- An evaporator, which is a device that absorbs heat from the space or the object to be cooled, causing the refrigerant to change from liquid to gas.

The refrigeration or the air-conditioning cycle consists of four processes:

- Compression: The refrigerant enters the compressor as a low-pressure gas and leaves as a high-pressure gas.
- Condensation: The refrigerant passes through the condenser, where it releases heat to the surroundings and becomes a high-pressure liquid.
- Expansion: The refrigerant passes through the expansion valve, where it expands and becomes a low-pressure liquid.
- Evaporation: The refrigerant passes through the evaporator, where it absorbs heat from the space or the object to be cooled and becomes a low-pressure gas.

The coefficient of performance (COP) is a measure of the efficiency of a refrigeration or an air-conditioning system. It is defined as the ratio of the useful heat transfer (either cooling or heating) to the work input. The higher the COP, the more efficient the system is.

### Pascal’s Law and Continuity Equation

Pascal's law and continuity equation are two important concepts in fluid mechanics that relate to the pressure and velocity of fluids in motion.

#### Pascal's Law

Pascal's law states that a pressure applied to a fluid in a closed container is transmitted equally to every point of the fluid and the walls of the container. Mathematically, this can be expressed as:

P = F / A (Equation 1)

Where P is the pressure, F is the force, and A is the area.

Pascal's law can be used to explain how hydraulic systems work, such as brakes, lifts, and presses. By applying a small force to a small area, a large force can be generated on a large area, as long as the pressure is constant.

#### Continuity Equation

The continuity equation expresses the relationship between mass flow rates at different points in a fluid system under steady-state flow conditions. It is based on the principle of conservation of mass, which states that the mass of a fluid cannot be created or destroyed. Mathematically, this can be expressed as:

ρ1A1v1 = ρ2A2v2 (Equation 2)

Where ρ is the density, A is the cross-sectional area, and v is the velocity of the fluid at points 1 and 2.

The continuity equation can be used to analyze the flow of fluids in pipes, ducts, and channels. It shows that when the cross-sectional area of a pipe decreases, the velocity of the fluid increases, and vice versa. It also shows that when the density of a fluid changes, the velocity of the fluid changes accordingly.

m-dot = A1 * sqrt(2 * rho * (P1 - P2) / (1 - (A2 / A1)^2))

where m-dot is the mass flow rate, A1 and A2 are the cross-sectional areas of the inlet and the throat sections, rho is the density of the fluid, and P1 and P2 are the pressures at the inlet and the throat sections.
