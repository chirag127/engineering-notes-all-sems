## Unit 1 - Introduction to Mechanics
Mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of objects and the forces that cause that motion. It is divided into two main parts: kinematics and dynamics.

1. **Kinematics** is the study of motion without considering the forces that cause it. It describes how objects move in terms of position, velocity, and acceleration.
2. **Dynamics** is the study of motion and the forces that cause it. It explains why objects move the way they do and how to predict their future motion.

Some important concepts in mechanics include:
- **Displacement**: the change in position of an object.
- **Velocity**: the rate of change of displacement with respect to time.
- **Acceleration**: the rate of change of velocity with respect to time.
- **Force**: a push or pull on an object that can cause it to accelerate.
- **Mass**: a measure of the amount of matter in an object.
- **Weight**: the force of gravity on an object.
- **Newton's laws of motion**: three laws that describe the relationship between the motion of an object and the forces acting on it.

Mechanics has many practical applications, including the design of machines, vehicles, and structures, as well as the study of the motion of planets and other celestial bodies. It is a fundamental subject in physics and engineering.



### Force moment and couple

#### Force moment
- The moment of a force is a measure of its tendency to cause a body to rotate about a specific point or axis.
- It is calculated as the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the line of action of the force to the point or axis of rotation.
- The unit of moment of force is newton-meter (N.m) in the SI system.

#### Couple
- A couple is a pair of forces, equal in magnitude, oppositely directed, and displaced by perpendicular distance or moment.
- The effect of a couple is to produce rotation without translation.
- The moment of a couple is the same about any point and is equal to the product of one of the forces and the perpendicular distance between the forces.
- The unit of moment of a couple is also newton-meter (N.m) in the SI system.

The principle of transmissibility states that the external effect of a force on a rigid body is the same, regardless of the point of application, provided the line of action of the force remains unchanged. This principle is important in the study of mechanics as it allows us to analyze the forces acting on a body without having to consider the exact point of application of the force. Instead, we can focus on the line of action of the force and its magnitude.

### Varignon's Theorem

Varignon's Theorem is a statement in mechanics that deals with the moment of a force. It states that the moment of a force about a point is equal to the algebraic sum of the moments of the components of the force about the same point.

In other words, if a force is resolved into two or more components, the moment of the force about a point is equal to the sum of the moments of the components about the same point.

This theorem is useful in solving problems involving the moment of a force, as it allows us to break down a complex force into simpler components and calculate their moments separately.

### Free Body Diagram

A free body diagram is a graphical representation of the forces acting on an object. It is used to analyze the forces acting on an object and to determine the net force and the resulting acceleration.

Here are some key points to remember when constructing a free body diagram:

1. Draw a diagram of the object, representing it as a point mass or a simple shape.
2. Identify all the forces acting on the object and represent them as vectors, with the tail of the vector at the point of application of the force and the head of the vector pointing in the direction of the force.
3. Label each force with its type (e.g. gravitational force, normal force, frictional force, etc.) and its magnitude (if known).
4. The net force acting on the object is the vector sum of all the individual forces. This can be calculated by adding the vectors head-to-tail or by using the Pythagorean theorem and trigonometry.
5. The acceleration of the object is determined by the net force and the mass of the object, according to Newton's second law of motion: F_net = ma.

Free body diagrams are a useful tool in solving problems in mechanics and can help to visualize and understand the forces acting on an object. It is important to carefully identify all the forces and to accurately represent their magnitudes and directions in the diagram.

### Strain
Strain is a measure of deformation representing the displacement between particles in the body relative to a reference length. It is defined as the change in length divided by the original length. Strain is a dimensionless quantity and is usually expressed as a decimal fraction, a percentage, or in parts-per notation.

There are two types of strain: normal strain and shear strain.

- Normal strain: Normal strain occurs when the deformation of a body is caused by an external force that is perpendicular to the surface of the body. It is defined as the change in length divided by the original length.

- Shear strain: Shear strain occurs when the deformation of a body is caused by an external force that is parallel to the surface of the body. It is defined as the change in the angle between two lines that were originally perpendicular.

Strain can be either positive or negative. Positive strain indicates that the body is elongated, while negative strain indicates that the body is compressed.

Strain is an important concept in mechanics, as it is used to determine the amount of deformation a body undergoes when subjected to external forces. It is also used to calculate the stress in a body, which is the force per unit area that arises from the deformation of the body.



### Hookes’ law

Hooke's law is a principle of physics that states that the force needed to extend or compress a spring by some distance is proportional to that distance. It is expressed mathematically as F = kx, where F is the force applied to the spring, x is the displacement of the spring from its equilibrium position, and k is the spring constant, a measure of the stiffness of the spring.

- Hooke's law applies to many materials, not just springs, and is a fundamental principle of elasticity.
- The spring constant, k, is a measure of the stiffness of the spring and is determined by the material and geometry of the spring.
- The displacement, x, is the change in length of the spring from its equilibrium position.
- Hooke's law is an idealization and is only approximately true for small displacements. For larger displacements, the force-displacement relationship may become nonlinear.
- Hooke's law is named after the 17th-century British physicist Robert Hooke, who first formulated the law in 1660.




### Poisson’s ratio

Poisson’s ratio is a measure of the deformation of a material when subjected to an applied stress. It is defined as the ratio of the lateral strain to the axial strain in the direction of the applied stress.

- Poisson’s ratio is denoted by the Greek letter nu (ν).
- It is a dimensionless quantity, meaning it has no units.
- The value of Poisson’s ratio typically ranges between -1 and 0.5 for most materials.
- A value of 0.5 indicates that the material is incompressible, meaning that when it is subjected to an applied stress, its volume remains constant.
- A value of 0 indicates that the material is perfectly compressible, meaning that when it is subjected to an applied stress, its volume changes but its shape remains unchanged.
- A negative value of Poisson’s ratio indicates that the material exhibits auxetic behavior, meaning that when it is subjected to an applied stress, it expands in the direction perpendicular to the applied stress.

Poisson’s ratio is an important property of materials as it is used in the calculation of stress and strain in various engineering applications. It is also used in the design of structures and components to ensure that they can withstand the applied loads without failure.



### Elastic Constants and their Relationship

Elastic constants are the material properties that describe the relationship between stress and strain in a material. There are several elastic constants, including:

1. **Young's Modulus (E):** This is the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain within the elastic limit. It is a measure of the stiffness of a material.

2. **Shear Modulus (G):** This is the ratio of shear stress to shear strain within the elastic limit. It is a measure of the rigidity of a material.

3. **Bulk Modulus (K):** This is the ratio of hydrostatic stress to volumetric strain within the elastic limit. It is a measure of the compressibility of a material.

4. **Poisson's Ratio (ν):** This is the ratio of lateral strain to axial strain within the elastic limit. It is a measure of the degree to which a material tends to expand in directions perpendicular to the direction of compression.

These elastic constants are related to each other through various equations. For example, the relationship between Young's Modulus, Shear Modulus, and Poisson's Ratio is given by the equation:

E = 2G(1 + ν)

The relationship between Bulk Modulus, Shear Modulus, and Poisson's Ratio is given by the equation:

K = 2G(1 + ν) / (3(1 - 2ν))

These relationships can be used to determine the values of one elastic constant if the values of the others are known. They are important in the study of mechanics and the behavior of materials under stress.



### Stress-Strain Diagram for Ductile and Brittle Materials

A stress-strain diagram is a graphical representation of the relationship between the stress and strain of a material. It is used to determine the mechanical properties of a material, such as its strength, ductility, and toughness.

#### Ductile Materials
- Ductile materials are materials that can undergo large plastic deformation before failure.
- The stress-strain diagram for a ductile material typically has a linear elastic region, followed by a non-linear plastic region.
- In the elastic region, the material obeys Hooke's Law, where the stress is proportional to the strain.
- In the plastic region, the material undergoes permanent deformation.
- The point at which the material transitions from elastic to plastic behavior is called the yield point.
- The maximum stress that the material can withstand before failure is called the ultimate tensile strength.

#### Brittle Materials
- Brittle materials are materials that undergo little to no plastic deformation before failure.
- The stress-strain diagram for a brittle material typically has a linear elastic region, followed by a sudden fracture.
- In the elastic region, the material obeys Hooke's Law, where the stress is proportional to the strain.
- The maximum stress that the material can withstand before failure is called the ultimate tensile strength.
- Unlike ductile materials, brittle materials do not have a distinct yield point.




### Factor of Safety

Factor of safety (FoS) is an engineering term that describes how much stronger a system or structure is than it is required to be to fulfill its purpose under expected conditions . There are two definitions for the factor of safety:

1. The ratio of a structure's absolute strength (structural capability) to actual applied load; this is a measure of the reliability of a particular design. This is a calculated value, and is sometimes referred to, for the sake of clarity, as a realized factor of safety .
2. A constant required value, imposed by law, standard, specification, contract or custom, to which a structure must conform .

A simple calculation for safety factor is; Factor of Safety = Actual Breaking Strength (lbs) / Normal Working Load (lbs). The term “actual breaking strength” refers to the amount of load that will cause the system to fail .

Factor of safety exists as a safety measure that is designed to make a product, a system, or a structure more reliable and less likely to fail .

- The construction and operation of a two-stroke and four-stroke engine differ in several ways.
- In a two-stroke engine, the intake and exhaust events occur simultaneously as the piston moves towards the bottom of the cylinder, while in a four-stroke engine, these events occur separately during the intake and exhaust strokes.
- Additionally, two-stroke engines require a method of lubrication, such as oil mixed with the fuel, while four-stroke engines have a separate lubrication system.
- The choice between a two-stroke and four-stroke engine depends on the specific application and the desired balance between power, efficiency, and emissions.

#### Continuity Equation
The continuity equation is a statement of the conservation of mass. It states that the mass flow rate into a control volume must equal the mass flow rate out of the control volume plus the rate of change of mass within the control volume. Mathematically, this is expressed as:

`ρ1A1V1 = ρ2A2V2`

Where:
- `ρ` is the density of the fluid
- `A` is the cross-sectional area of the flow
- `V` is the flow velocity

The continuity equation is used to analyze fluid flow in pipes and other systems where the fluid is confined.
