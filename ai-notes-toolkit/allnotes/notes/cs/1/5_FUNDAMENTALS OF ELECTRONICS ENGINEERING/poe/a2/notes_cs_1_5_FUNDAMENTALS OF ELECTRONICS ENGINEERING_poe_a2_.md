# FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRONICS ENGINEERING

1. Introduction
- Electronics is the study of how electrical devices control the flow of electrons.
- Electronics engineering is the application of physics and mathematics to address problems associated with the transmission of information via electronic devices.

2. Electrical Components
- Resistors: Components that resist the flow of current. They control voltage and current levels.
- Capacitors: Components that store electrical charge. They are used to filter signals and block DC current.
- Inductors: Components that store electrical energy in a magnetic field. They are used in AC circuits to filter signals and block high-frequency alternating current.
- Diodes: Components that allow current to flow in only one direction. They are used to convert alternating current to direct current.
- Transistors: Components that control the flow of electrons. They are the fundamental building blocks of all modern electronic devices.

- Series circuits: Current flows through each component one after the other. Voltage is shared across all components.
- Parallel circuits: Components are arranged in branches so current has multiple paths. Voltage is the same across all components.
- Combination circuits: Include both series and parallel components. The relationships between voltage, current, and resistance must be analyzed for the entire circuit.

## Unit 1 - Semiconductor Diode

1. A semiconductor diode is a two-terminal electronic component that conducts current primarily in one direction (asymmetric conductance). It has two terminals which are called anode and cathode.
2. At the junction of the two semiconductors, a depletion region exists. When the diode is in forward bias, the depletion region width decreases. When the diode is in reverse bias, the depletion region width increases.
3. Forward bias: When the voltage applied across the diode is such that the anode is positive with respect to the cathode, the diode is said to be in forward bias. In forward bias, the current flows through the diode. The diode has a small voltage drop across it.
4. Reverse bias: When the voltage applied across the diode is such that the cathode is positive with respect to the anode, the diode is said to be in reverse bias. In reverse bias, ideally the current through the diode is zero. A small amount of current called reverse saturation current flows due to minority charge carriers.
5. The two materials used to create the semiconductor diode are P-type and N-type semiconductors. The P-N junction is formed between the two semiconductors. The characteristics and performance of the diode are determined by the properties of this P-N junction.

### Depletion layer for the notes of the Unit 1 - Semiconductor Diode in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRONICS ENGINEERING

- When a P-N junction is formed by joining p-type and n-type semiconductors, the free electrons from n-type region diffuse into p-type region and the holes from p-type region diffuse into n-type region.
- This results in the formation of a region across the junction that is depleted of charge carriers. This region is called depletion layer.
- The depletion layer has a built-in potential that opposes the flow of charge carriers. The depletion layer width varies with the amount of doping. Higher the doping, thinner the depletion layer.
- At equilibrium, the depletion layer has a fixed width. When reverse bias is applied, the width increases and when forward bias is applied, the width decreases.
- The depletion layer is responsible for the rectification action and other important characteristics of the P-N junction diode.

### V-I characteristics for the notes of the Unit 1 - Semiconductor Diode in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRONICS ENGINEERING

1. When voltage is applied to a diode in forward bias, the current through the diode increases exponentially with increase in voltage. This region is called the Forward Bias Region. The curve in this region is almost a straight line after certain minimum voltage (around 0.7V for Si and 0.3V for Ge).
2. When the voltage is applied in reverse bias, the reverse saturation current flows through the diode. The reverse current is very small (in the range of μA) and almost constant with increase in reverse voltage. This is called the Reverse Bias Region or Breakdown Region.
3. The voltage at which the reverse current starts increasing rapidly is called the Breakdown Voltage. The diode gets damaged if the reverse voltage exceeds the breakdown voltage.
4. The points where the forward and reverse bias curves meet are called the Cut-in Voltage and Breakdown Voltage respectively.
5. Diodes have nonlinear V-I characteristics and show asymmetry between forward and reverse bias. They act as a rectifier converting AC to DC.

### Ideal and Practical Diodes

- Ideal diode: It is an theoretical model of a diode which assumes the diode to have zero voltage drop and infinite resistance in reverse bias and zero resistance in forward bias. It allows current to flow only in one direction.
- Practical diode: Real diodes do not behave exactly like an ideal diode. They have a small voltage drop in forward bias and a small leakage current in reverse bias. The deviation from ideal behavior is due to the following reasons:
- The resistance of the semiconductor material is not zero.
- The junction of the semiconductor material has a potential barrier which allows some amount of leakage current to flow in reverse bias.
- The junction capacitance causes some additional effects in practical diodes like capacitance effect, transition capacitance effect, etc.

The characteristics of practical diodes can be approximated to the characteristics of an ideal diode using piecewise linear approximations. Practical diodes are widely used in electronics circuits with appropriate biasing and compensation for the non-ideal effects.

### Diode Equivalent Circuits

- A diode can be represented by an ideal equivalent circuit for analysis purposes.
- The ideal diode circuit consists of a voltage-controlled switch that closes when the voltage across the diode is positive (forward-biased) and opens when the voltage across the diode is negative (reverse-biased).
- The switch closure in the ideal diode circuit represents the low resistance of the diode during forward conduction. The switch opening represents the high resistance of the diode during reverse bias.
- The ideal diode equivalent circuit is useful for analyzing the steady-state behavior of diode circuits. However, it does not model the capacitance of a real diode, which becomes important when analyzing transient circuit behavior.
- A more complete diode model is the diode capacitance model, which incorporates the junction capacitance of a real diode. The diode capacitance model provides a more accurate representation of a real diode at high frequencies.

### Zener Diodes breakdown mechanism (Zener and avalanche)

- A Zener diode is a type of diode that allows current to flow in the reverse direction when the voltage is above a certain value known as the breakdown voltage or Zener voltage.
- The Zener breakdown occurs in a heavily doped p-n junction diode. At a particular reverse voltage, known as the Zener voltage (Vz), the depletion region disappears and the reverse saturation current increases rapidly. This causes the diode to conduct in the reverse direction.
- The avalanche breakdown occurs in a lightly doped p-n junction diode. When the reverse voltage is increased beyond a particular value known as the avalanche voltage (Va), the electrons gain sufficient kinetic energy to free additional electrons by collisions with the semiconductor atoms. This leads to an avalanche of carriers resulting in a rapid increase in the reverse current.
- The Zener breakdown occurs at a lower voltage than the avalanche breakdown. However, both these mechanisms cause the diode to conduct in the reverse direction, and the diode is designed to use either the Zener or avalanche breakdown depending on the doping level and required breakdown voltage.
- The Zener diode is mainly used as a voltage reference and to provide voltage regulation. It is widely used in power supply circuits because it has a predictable and stable reverse breakdown voltage.

### Half and Full Wave rectification

**Half Wave Rectification:**

- Only one half of the AC wave is passed, the other half is blocked.
- A diode is connected in series with the load.
- The diode conducts only during the positive half cycle and blocks during the negative half cycle.
- The output is a series of positive pulses.
- The average output voltage is less than the peak voltage of the AC input.
- Low efficiency and high ripple factor.

**Full Wave Rectification:**

- The complete AC wave is rectified.
- A centre-tapped transformer and two diodes are used.
- During the positive half cycle, the top diode conducts and during the negative half cycle, the bottom diode conducts.
- The output is a full wave with magnitude equal to the peak voltage of the input AC wave.
- Higher efficiency and lower ripple factor than half wave rectification.
- The output DC voltage is higher and the ripple factor is lower.

### Zener Diode as Shunt Regulator

- A Zener diode acts as a shunt voltage regulator when it is operated in the breakdown region.
- In shunt voltage regulation, the Zener diode is connected in parallel with the load.
- The voltage across the Zener diode remains constant at the breakdown voltage. Hence, any increase in supply voltage appears across the Zener diode and the voltage across the load remains constant.
- This is suitable when the current drawn by the load is small. The Zener diode can draw high currents in the breakdown region and adequate heat sinking becomes necessary.
- The series resistance Rs is included to limit the short circuit current from the power supply in case of failure of the Zener diode.
- The value of Rs is chosen to allow sufficient current through the Zener diode under normal conditions.
- The value of Rs should be low enough not to cause a significant voltage drop and affect the load regulation.

### Voltage-Multiplier Circuits

- Voltage multipliers are circuits that output a DC voltage that is a multiple of the peak value of the AC input voltage.
- They are commonly used to generate high DC voltages from low AC voltages.
- The basic element of a voltage multiplier is the diode-capacitor combination. The diode allows current to flow only during the positive half-cycle of the input, charging the capacitor. During the negative half-cycle, the diode is reverse-biased and the capacitor is isolated, thereby storing the charge.
- By cascading several stages of diode-capacitor combinations, a higher DC output voltage can be obtained. Each stage outputs approximately the peak voltage of the input, and by putting several stages in series, the final output voltage is the sum of the individual stage outputs.
- The two most popular types of voltage multipliers are the half-wave multiplier and the full-wave multiplier:
-- Half-wave multiplier: Consists of diode-capacitor stages with diodes connected in series, with each diode conducting current only during alternate half-cycles. Output voltage is approximately equal to the peak input voltage multiplied by the number of stages.
-- Full-wave multiplier: Consists of diode-capacitor stages with diodes connected to conduct current during both half-cycles of the input. Output voltage is approximately equal to twice the peak input voltage multiplied by the number of stages. The full-wave multiplier is more efficient than the half-wave multiplier.

### Light-Emitting Diodes

- Light-emitting diodes (LEDs) are semiconductor diodes that emit light when an electric current passes through them.
- They are made of a material that releases photons (light particles) when electrical energy is applied.
- The color of the light emitted depends on the bandgap of the semiconductor material used. Materials such as gallium arsenide and gallium phosphide emit red and yellow light. Indium gallium nitride can emit green, blue, and ultraviolet light.
- LEDs are more energy efficient and durable than incandescent light bulbs. They are used as indicator lights in many devices and are increasingly being used for general lighting applications.
- The current-voltage characteristic of an LED is similar to that of a regular diode. However, no light is emitted until the voltage across the LED exceeds its threshold voltage. The light intensity then increases rapidly with increasing current. If the current is too high, the LED can be damaged due to overheating.
- The voltage drop across an LED depends on the color of light and viewing angle. Red LEDs usually have the lowest voltage drop while blue and white LEDs have the highest voltage drop.

### Photo Diodes

- A photo diode is a PN junction diode that converts light energy into electrical energy.
- When light falls on the PN junction, electron-hole pairs are generated which increase the conductivity of the diode. This results in flow of current through the circuit.
- The amount of current flowing depends on the intensity of light falling on the PN junction. Higher the intensity of light, higher is the current through the circuit.
- Photo diodes are used to detect light and convert light signals into electrical signals. They are used in various optical instruments and circuits like light meters, remote controls, optical communication systems, etc.
- The sensitivity of a photo diode depends on the material used and the area of the PN junction. Larger area and materials like silicon and germanium having lower band gap result in higher sensitivity.
- The response time of a photo diode depends on the recombination time of carriers. Faster recombination results in faster response and vice versa.
- The two modes of operation of a photo diode are:
- Photovoltaic mode: Used to produce a voltage or current in response to light. No external voltage is applied.
- Photoconductive mode: Used to change the conductivity of the diode in response to light. A voltage is applied externally and the change in current is measured.

### Varactor Diodes

- Varactor diodes are semiconductor diodes that are specifically designed to take advantage of the change in capacitance with voltage.
- The junction capacitance of a normal diode varies with the applied voltage - it decreases with increasing reverse voltage. This property can be exploited in varactor diodes to produce a voltage-controlled capacitance.
- Varactor diodes are typically manufactured from silicon or gallium arsenide and have a very low breakdown voltage. This enables the capacitance to vary over a relatively wide range.
- The capacitance of a varactor diode can be varied by a factor of up to 10 or even more. However it is non-linear and this needs to be taken into account in any application.
- Varactor diodes are used as capacitors in a number of tuning applications where a change in frequency is required including in voltage controlled oscillators, radio frequency tuners, and phase locked loops.
- By changing the reverse bias on the diode, it is possible to change the capacitance and hence tune to different frequencies. This makes varactor diodes very useful in many radio frequency tuning applications.

### Tunnel Diodes

- Tunnel diodes are heavily doped p-n junction diodes that exhibit negative resistance.
- They have a very narrow depletion layer (around 10 nm) which allows electrons to tunnel through the forbidden region, giving rise to the name 'tunnel diode'.
- The V-I characteristics of a tunnel diode show a region of negative slope, called the 'negative resistance' region. This is due to the tunneling effect and allows for fast switching applications and high frequency oscillators.
- The peak-to-valley ratio (PVR) is an important parameter that determines the suitability of a tunnel diode for an application. A higher PVR means more stability and less noise.
- Tunnel diodes are commonly used in high speed circuits, especially for generating microwave signals. They can oscillate at frequencies up to about 100 GHz.
- However, tunnel diodes have very high peak currents and therefore tend to heat up significantly. This limits their maximum power handling capacity and junction temperature must be maintained below 150°C.
- Due to their niche applications and complex fabrication process, tunnel diodes are not commonly used today, having been replaced by newer components like IMPATT diodes and Gunn diodes which can achieve even higher frequencies at higher power levels.

## Unit 2 - Bipolar Junction Transistor

1. Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) is a three terminal semiconductor device consisting of two p-n junctions connected back to back.
2. The three terminals are named as Emitter (E), Base (B) and Collector (C).
3. The emitter injects electrons into the base. The base controls the number of electrons entering the collector region. The collector collects the electrons injected from the emitter.
4. The two p-n junctions are - Emitter-base junction and Collector-base junction.
5. BJT has two modes of operation - Forward active / Forward biased mode and Reverse active / Reverse biased mode.
6. In forward active mode, the emitter-base junction is forward biased and collector-base junction is reverse biased. Majority carriers (electrons) flow from emitter to collector.
7. In reverse active mode, the emitter-base junction is reverse biased and collector-base junction is forward biased. Minority carriers (holes) flow from collector to emitter.
8. BJT can act as an amplifier or a switch. The magnitude of current amplification depends on the ratio of emitter and collector doping concentrations.
9. The three regions of BJT are Emitter, Base and Collector. The Base region controls the flow of electrons from Emitter to Collector.
10. BJT has higher input impedance than MOSFET and can handle higher power but has lower switching speed than MOSFET.

### Transistor Construction

- A transistor consists of three layers of semiconductor material.
- The middle layer is thin and is known as the base. The top layer is the emitter and the bottom layer is the collector.
- The emitter and collector are connected to a voltage source. The base is connected to a variable voltage source to control the transistor.
- The emitter-base junction is forward biased and the collector-base junction is reverse biased.
- The emitter injects electrons into the base. The electrons move from the emitter to the collector, flowing through the base. This flow of electrons through the base is controlled by the voltage applied to the base.
- A small change in base voltage can produce a large change in emitter current. This forms the basis for the amplification function of the transistor.
- The transistor has three terminals – emitter (E), base (B) and collector (C). The input is applied between the base and emitter and the output is taken from the collector and emitter.

### Amplification action for the notes of the Unit 2 - Bipolar Junction Transistor in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRONICS ENGINEERING

1. A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a three terminal semiconductor device that can be used for amplification of signals.
2. The three terminals of a BJT are:
- Emitter (E): Injects electrons into the base region.
- Base (B): Thin region between emitter and collector which controls the flow of electrons.
- Collector (C): Collects the electrons from the emitter.
3. The two junctions in a BJT are:
- Emitter-base junction: Forward biased junction (heavily doped)
- Base-collector junction: Reverse biased junction (moderately doped)
4. Working principle: When a voltage is applied across the emitter-base junction, it gets forward biased and electrons flow from emitter to base. The ratio of change in collector and base currents is called current gain which results in amplification.
5. The amplification can be calculated as: Amplification (β) = Change in collector current (ΔIc)/Change in base current (ΔIb)
6. The amplification factor can be varied by changing the doping concentrations and dimensions of the three regions.

### Common Emitter

** Ohmic region: For low drain voltages (V< pinch-off voltage Vp), the curve is a straight line through the origin. The drain current is directly proportional to the drain voltage.
** Saturation region: For drain voltages higher than Vp, the channel is pinched-off. The drain current becomes constant and independent of the drain voltage. The JFET operates in the saturation mode in this region.
** Breakdown region: For very high drain voltages, the junction between the drain and the channel breaks down and the drain current increases rapidly. This leads to the destruction of the JFET. So, the operating region is kept within the Ohmic and saturation regions.
* The transfer characteristic shows the variation of drain current with the gate-source voltage (I-Vg). The drain current decreases with an increase in the reverse bias to the gate. The gate voltage required to pinch-off the channel is known as the pinch-off voltage (Vp).

### Differential and Common-Mode Operation

- An op amp has two inputs - non-inverting (+) and inverting (-) input terminals.

- The output of an op amp also contains signals that depend on the average of the two inputs. This is called common-mode operation and is undesirable.
- To get an op amp to function properly in a circuit, it is important to understand both differential and common-mode operation.

### K Map Minimization upto 6 Variables

**Karnaugh Map (K-Map):**

- It is a graphical technique to minimize Boolean functions.
- It uses a grid to represent a truth table in a compact form.
- It can handle up to 6 variables. For more than 6 variables, it becomes tedious and prone to errors.
- The process of identifying groups that can be combined to simplify an expression is called **minimization**.

**Steps to minimize a K-Map:**

1. Draw the K-Map and represent 1s with dark rectangles.
2. Look for adjacent 1s that can form the largest rectangle possible. These form a group that can be combined.
3. Write the product term for the group and circle it.
4. Repeat step#2 and #3 until no more groups can be identified.
5. The final product terms give the minimized SOP or POS expression.

- Keep the groups as large as possible to get minimum product terms.
- Don't skip a 1 while forming a group.
- Change the K-Map arrangement (top to bottom, left to right) if you get stuck. A different perspective may help in identifying groups.

### Fundamentals of amplitude modulation and demodulation techniques

- Amplitude modulation (AM) is a technique used in electronic communication, most commonly for transmitting information via a radio carrier wave.
- In AM, the amplitude of the carrier wave is varied in proportion to the waveform being transmitted. This results in the modulated carrier having components at the original frequency of the waveform as well as at frequencies above and below the original frequency.
- The demodulation process extracts the original information signal from the modulated carrier wave. This is usually done with an envelope detector which removes the carrier wave, leaving just the varying amplitude envelope.
- Some advantages of AM are:
    - It is relatively inexpensive to implement.
    - AM signals have a smaller bandwidth than FM signals which can be important in some applications where bandwidth is limited.
    - AM is less susceptible to noise than other modulation techniques since noise or interference would have to significantly affect the amplitude of the carrier to have a noticeable effect on the demodulated signal.
- Some disadvantages of AM are:
    - It is sensitive to electromagnetic interference (EMI) and radio frequency interference (RFI).
    - The modulation index cannot be too large without causing interference between adjacent channels. This limits the maximum modulation depth compared to other techniques.
    - AM has poorer audio fidelity than FM for audio signals since the modulation is directly related to changes in amplitude. This can result in audio distortion when the modulating waveform contains significant low-frequency content.

### Cellular Communication

1. Cellular networks are made up of cells, each with a low-power radio transmitter/receiver base station.
2. Cells are typically 0.5 to 5 miles in radius and overlap to provide coverage over a large geographic area.
3. As users move from cell to cell, their call or data session is handed off between base stations to maintain connectivity.
4. Mobiles phones and devices continuously scan for the strongest signal from nearby base stations and connect to the strongest available signal.
5. Cells use different radio frequencies to avoid interference, and frequency reuse patterns are employed to maximize spectrum efficiency.
6. Base stations are connected to a mobile switching center (MSC) or gateway, which manages connectivity between cells, switches calls and data to the PSTN or Internet, and tracks user location to enable call routing and handoffs.
7. 2G/3G/4G cellular networks use a variety of multiple access technologies (FDMA, TDMA, CDMA, OFDMA) to enable multiple users and devices to share the radio spectrum.
8. LTE is the 4G cellular technology that uses OFDMA for downlink and SC-FDMA for uplink and delivers significantly higher peak data rates than previous technologies.

### Fundamentals of Satellite & Radar Communication

- Satellite communication uses artificial satellites placed in the orbit around the earth to establish communication. The satellite acts as a relay station to transmit and receive radio signals.
- The satellite has a microwave transponder that receives, amplifies and retransmits the signals back to the earth.
- The key components of a satellite communication system are the ground station, the uplink, the satellite, and the downlink.
- The ground station sends an uplink signal to the satellite which then sends a downlink signal to the ground station or other terminals.
- Due to the height of the satellite from the earth, a line of sight is present between the ground stations and the satellite which allows long-distance communication.
- The properties of the transmitted signal such as frequency, power level, modulation, etc. depend on the application of satellite communication like transponders, broadcasting, etc.
- Radar or RAdio Detection And Ranging uses radio waves to detect the location and velocity of objects. Radar systems transmit radio signals and listen to their echoes to sense objects and their properties.
- The radar system consists of a transmitter to generate radio signals, an antenna to transmit and receive signals, a modulator to modify signals, a receiver to detect echoes, and a signal processor to analyze received signals.
- The radar measures the time taken by the transmitted signal to bounce off the target and return to the radar to calculate the distance of the target. The change in frequency of the received signal is used to measure the velocity of the target.
- The applications of radar include air traffic control, weather monitoring, autonomous vehicles, etc. The radar technology works effectively even in harsh environmental conditions like fog, heavy rain, etc.
