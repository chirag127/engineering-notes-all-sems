

# FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRONICS ENGINEERING

Electronics engineering is a branch of engineering that deals with the design, development and testing of electronic systems and products. Electronics engineers work with various components and devices that manipulate electric signals, such as resistors, capacitors, diodes, transistors, integrated circuits, microcontrollers, sensors, etc.

Some of the topics that are essential for understanding the fundamentals of electronics engineering are:

- Electricity: The flow of electric charge through a conductor. There are two types of electric signals: alternating current (AC) and direct current (DC). AC signals change their direction and magnitude periodically, while DC signals have a constant direction and magnitude. AC signals are used for power transmission and distribution, while DC signals are used for batteries, logic circuits, and digital devices.
- Circuits: A closed path through which electric current can flow. A circuit consists of a power source, such as a battery or a generator, and one or more components, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, switches, etc. that control the current and voltage in the circuit. A circuit can be analyzed using various methods, such as Kirchhoff's laws, Ohm's law, Thevenin's theorem, etc.
- Resistance: The opposition to the flow of electric current in a conductor. Resistance depends on the material, length, cross-sectional area, and temperature of the conductor. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω). Resistors are components that have a fixed or variable resistance and are used to limit the current, divide the voltage, or create a voltage drop in a circuit.
- Series and parallel circuits: Two ways of connecting components in a circuit. In a series circuit, the components are connected one after another, so that the same current flows through all of them. The total resistance of a series circuit is the sum of the individual resistances. In a parallel circuit, the components are connected across the same two points, so that the voltage across each component is the same. The total resistance of a parallel circuit is the reciprocal of the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances.
- Basic components: Some of the common components that are used in electronic circuits are:

  - Capacitors: Components that store electric charge and energy in an electric field. Capacitors consist of two conductive plates separated by an insulator, such as air, paper, or ceramic. Capacitors are measured in farads (F) and are used to filter, smooth, or store signals in a circuit.
  - Diodes: Components that allow current to flow in one direction only. Diodes consist of a semiconductor material, such as silicon or germanium, that has two terminals: anode and cathode. Diodes are used to rectify, regulate, or switch signals in a circuit.
  - Transistors: Components that amplify or switch electric signals. Transistors consist of three terminals: base, collector, and emitter. Transistors can be classified into two types: bipolar junction transistors (BJT) and field-effect transistors (FET). Transistors are the building blocks of logic gates, amplifiers, and microprocessors.
  - Integrated circuits: Components that contain many transistors and other components on a single chip of silicon or other semiconductor material. Integrated circuits can perform complex functions, such as arithmetic, memory, or communication. Integrated circuits are classified into different categories, such as analog, digital, or mixed-signal.
  - Microcontrollers: Components that contain a microprocessor, memory, and input/output devices on a single chip. Microcontrollers are programmable devices that can control other components or devices in a circuit. Microcontrollers are used for embedded systems, such as robotics, automation, or smart devices.

## Unit 1 - Semiconductor Diode

- A semiconductor diode is a device that allows current to flow in one direction, but blocks it in the opposite direction  .
- A semiconductor diode is made of a p-type and an n-type semiconductor material, which are joined together to form a p-n junction  .
- The p-type material has an excess of holes, which are positively charged carriers, while the n-type material has an excess of electrons, which are negatively charged carriers  .
- When the p-n junction is formed, some electrons from the n-type material diffuse across the junction and fill some of the holes in the p-type material. This creates a region near the junction that has no free carriers, called the depletion region  .
- The depletion region acts as a barrier for the current flow, unless an external voltage is applied across the diode  .

- The characteristics of a semiconductor diode are the relationship between the voltage across the diode and the current through it  .
- The characteristics can be divided into two regions: forward bias and reverse bias  .
- Forward bias is when the anode is connected to the positive terminal of the voltage source and the cathode is connected to the negative terminal. This reduces the barrier of the depletion region and allows current to flow through the diode  .
- Reverse bias is when the anode is connected to the negative terminal of the voltage source and the cathode is connected to the positive terminal. This increases the barrier of the depletion region and blocks the current flow through the diode, except for a very small leakage current  .

- The forward bias region is the part of the graph where the current increases rapidly with the voltage. The reverse bias region is the part of the graph where the current is almost zero, except for a small negative current  .
- The forward voltage is the minimum voltage required to make the diode conduct in the forward direction. It is typically around 0.7 V for silicon diodes and 0.3 V for germanium diodes  .
- The reverse breakdown voltage is the maximum voltage that the diode can withstand in the reverse direction before it starts to conduct a large current. This can damage the diode if the current is not limited by a resistor or another device  .
- The diode can be used for various applications, such as rectification, switching, clamping, voltage regulation, and signal modulation  .
- Rectification is the process of converting alternating current (AC) into direct current (DC) by using one or more diodes  .
- Switching is the process of turning a circuit on or off by using a diode as a switch  .
- Clamping is the process of limiting the voltage of a signal to a certain range by using a diode and a capacitor  .
- Voltage regulation is the process of maintaining a constant output voltage by using a diode and a resistor or a zener diode  .
- Signal modulation is the process of changing the amplitude, frequency, or phase of a signal by using a diode and a transistor



### Depletion layer

- A depletion layer is an insulating region within a conductive, doped semiconductor material where the mobile charge carriers have been diffused away, or have been forced away by an electric field.
- A depletion layer forms when a p-type semiconductor is joined with an n-type semiconductor, creating a p-n junction.
- The p-type semiconductor has an excess of holes (positive charge carriers), while the n-type semiconductor has an excess of electrons (negative charge carriers).
- When the p-n junction is formed, some of the electrons from the n-type region diffuse into the p-type region and recombine with the holes, creating a region of negative ions (ionized acceptors) near the junction.
- Similarly, some of the holes from the p-type region diffuse into the n-type region and recombine with the electrons, creating a region of positive ions (ionized donors) near the junction.
- The regions of ionized acceptors and donors form the depletion layer, which has no net charge, but has an electric potential difference across it, called the built-in potential.
- The depletion layer acts as a barrier that opposes the further diffusion of charge carriers across the junction.
- The width and the potential of the depletion layer depend on the doping concentration of the semiconductors and the applied external voltage.
- The depletion layer can be reduced by applying a forward bias voltage, which lowers the barrier and allows the charge carriers to flow across the junction.
- The depletion layer can be increased by applying a reverse bias voltage, which raises the barrier and prevents the charge carriers from flowing across the junction, except for a small leakage current.
- The depletion layer plays an important role in the operation of semiconductor devices, such as diodes, transistors, solar cells, and light-emitting diodes.



### V-I characteristics of semiconductor diode

- The V-I characteristics of a semiconductor diode is a graph that shows the relationship between the voltage across the diode and the current through it.
- The V-I characteristics can be divided into two regions: forward bias and reverse bias.
- Forward bias is the condition when the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the p-type material and the negative terminal to the n-type material of the diode. In this case, the diode allows the current to flow through it and acts as a closed switch.
- Reverse bias is the condition when the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the n-type material and the negative terminal to the p-type material of the diode. In this case, the diode blocks the current and acts as an open switch.

Avalanche breakdown occurs when the electric field accelerates the minority carriers to such an extent that they collide with the atoms and create more carriers. Zener breakdown occurs when the electric field is strong enough to pull the electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, creating a large number of carriers.

### Diode Equivalent Circuits

A diode is a semiconductor device that allows current to flow in one direction only. It has two terminals, anode and cathode, and a characteristic curve that shows the relationship between the voltage across the diode and the current through it.

An equivalent circuit is a simplified representation of a device or a circuit that preserves its essential behavior and properties. An equivalent circuit can help us to analyze and design circuits that involve diodes.

There are different types of equivalent circuits for diodes, depending on the level of accuracy and complexity required. Some of the common ones are:

- **Ideal diode model**: This is the simplest model, where the diode is replaced by a switch that is closed when the diode is forward biased and open when the diode is reverse biased. This model ignores the voltage drop and the nonlinearity of the diode, and assumes that the diode can switch instantly between the on and off states. This model is useful for qualitative analysis and understanding the basic operation of diode circuits.

- **Simplified equivalent circuit**: This is a slightly more realistic model, where the diode is replaced by a battery and an ideal diode in series. The battery represents the forward voltage drop of the diode, which is typically 0.6 V for silicon diodes and 0.3 V for germanium diodes. This model accounts for the voltage drop, but still ignores the nonlinearity and the switching time of the diode. This model is useful for quantitative analysis and design of simple diode circuits.

- **Piecewise-linear equivalent circuit**: This is a more accurate model, where the diode is replaced by a series combination of a battery, a resistor, and an ideal diode. The battery and the resistor represent the linear approximation of the diode characteristic curve in the forward region, and the ideal diode represents the cutoff region. The values of the battery and the resistor depend on the operating point and the slope of the diode curve. This model accounts for the voltage drop, the nonlinearity, and the resistance of the diode, but still ignores the switching time and the reverse breakdown of the diode. This model is useful for detailed analysis and design of diode circuits.

- **Small-signal equivalent circuit**: This is a more sophisticated model, where the diode is replaced by a series combination of a resistor and a controlled current source. The resistor represents the dynamic resistance of the diode, which is the inverse of the slope of the diode curve at the operating point. The controlled current source represents the variation of the diode current due to the variation of the diode voltage. This model is valid only for small variations of the diode voltage and current around the operating point, and assumes that the diode is always forward biased. This model accounts for the voltage drop, the nonlinearity, the resistance, and the frequency response of the diode, but still ignores the switching time and the reverse breakdown of the diode. This model is useful for analysis and design of diode circuits that involve ac signals and modulation.

### Zener Diodes breakdown mechanism (Zener and avalanche)

- A Zener diode is a special type of diode that can operate in the reverse breakdown region, where the current increases rapidly with a small increase in reverse voltage.
- The reverse breakdown region is where the electric field across the depletion layer becomes so high that it can break the covalent bonds of the semiconductor atoms, creating free electrons and holes that can conduct current.
- There are two mechanisms that can cause reverse breakdown: Zener breakdown and avalanche breakdown.
- Zener breakdown occurs when the electric field is strong enough to pull electrons from the valence band of the semiconductor and make them jump to the conduction band, creating electron-hole pairs. This mechanism dominates at low reverse voltages (below 5V) and in heavily doped diodes, where the depletion layer is thin and the electric field is high.
- Avalanche breakdown occurs when the free electrons in the conduction band gain enough kinetic energy from the electric field to collide with the semiconductor atoms and knock out more electrons, creating a chain reaction of electron multiplication. This mechanism dominates at high reverse voltages (above 5V) and in lightly doped diodes, where the depletion layer is thick and the electric field is moderate.
- The breakdown voltage of a Zener diode is the reverse voltage at which the diode enters the breakdown region and the current starts to increase rapidly. The breakdown voltage is determined by the doping level and the temperature of the diode. The breakdown voltage decreases with increasing temperature and increasing doping level.
- A Zener diode can be used as a voltage regulator, by connecting it in parallel with a load and a series resistor. The Zener diode will maintain a constant voltage across the load, as long as the input voltage is higher than the breakdown voltage and the current through the diode is within its power rating. The series resistor will limit the current through the diode and protect it from overheating.



### Diode Application

A diode is a two-terminal electronic device that allows current to flow in only one direction. It has a low resistance in the forward direction and a high resistance in the reverse direction. Diodes are widely used in various fields of electronics for different purposes. Some of the common applications of diodes are:

- **Rectification**: A diode can convert an alternating current (AC) into a direct current (DC) by blocking the negative or positive half cycles of the AC waveform. This process is called rectification and it is the basic function of a diode. Rectifiers are used in power supplies, battery chargers, radio receivers, and many other devices that require a DC voltage  .

- **Switching**: A diode can act as a switch that can turn on or off a circuit depending on the polarity of the input voltage. Diodes are used in digital logic circuits, relays, transistors, and other switching devices. Switching diodes have a fast response time and can handle high frequencies .

- **Source Isolation**: A diode can isolate a source from a load by preventing the reverse flow of current from the load to the source. This is useful for protecting the source from damage or interference caused by the load. Diodes are used in source isolation in solar panels, batteries, generators, and other power sources .

- **Voltage Reference**: A diode can provide a stable and precise voltage reference for a circuit by exploiting its forward voltage drop characteristic. The forward voltage drop of a diode depends on the type and temperature of the diode, but it is relatively constant for a given current. Diodes are used in voltage reference circuits for regulators, comparators, amplifiers, and other devices that require a fixed reference voltage .

- **Frequency Mixer**: A diode can mix two or more input signals of different frequencies and produce an output signal that contains the sum and difference of the input frequencies. This is useful for modulating and demodulating signals in communication systems. Diodes are used in frequency mixers for radio transmitters, receivers, radars, and other devices that operate on different frequency bands .

- **Diode Detector**: A diode can detect the presence and amplitude of a signal by rectifying it and producing a DC output that is proportional to the signal strength. This is useful for measuring and monitoring signals in various applications. Diodes are used in diode detectors for signal strength meters, envelope detectors, peak detectors, and other devices that require a DC output from an AC input .

- **Light Source**: A diode can emit light when a current passes through it in the forward direction. This type of diode is called a light-emitting diode (LED) and it is widely used as a source of illumination and indication in various devices. LEDs have many advantages over conventional light sources, such as low power consumption, long lifespan, high brightness, and various colors .

- **Temperature and Light Sensor**: A diode can sense the temperature and light intensity by measuring its forward voltage drop and reverse current, respectively. The forward voltage drop of a diode decreases with increasing temperature, while the reverse current of a diode increases with increasing light intensity. Diodes are used in temperature and light sensors for thermometers, photometers, cameras, and other devices that require a measurement of temperature or light .

- **Solar Cell or Photo-Voltaic Cell**: A diode can convert light energy into electrical energy by generating a voltage and a current when exposed to light. This type of diode is called a solar cell or a photo-voltaic cell and it is widely used as a source of renewable energy in various applications. Solar cells are made of semiconductor materials that have a p-n junction, which creates an electric field that separates the electrons and holes generated by light .

- **Clipper and Clamper**: A diode can clip or clamp a signal to a desired level by blocking or shifting the portions of the signal that exceed or fall below the level. This is useful for limiting or modifying the shape of a signal in various applications. Diodes are used in clipper and clamper circuits for waveform generators, signal processors, oscillators, and other devices that require a specific signal shape .

### Diode Configuration

A diode is an electrical device that allows current to flow in one direction only. It has two terminals: an anode and a cathode. The anode is the positive terminal and the cathode is the negative terminal. A diode is made of a semiconductor material, such as silicon, that has a p-n junction. A p-n junction is a boundary between two regions of the semiconductor that have different types of doping. Doping is the process of adding impurities to the semiconductor to change its electrical properties. The p-region has a surplus of holes, which are positive charge carriers, and the n-region has a surplus of electrons, which are negative charge carriers.

A diode can be configured in different ways depending on the polarity of the applied voltage and the desired function. Some common diode configurations are:

- Forward bias: In this configuration, the anode is connected to the positive terminal of the voltage source and the cathode is connected to the negative terminal. This causes the holes in the p-region and the electrons in the n-region to move towards the junction, creating a current. The diode is said to be on or conducting in this configuration. The voltage across the diode is usually small, around 0.7 V for silicon diodes.

- Reverse bias: In this configuration, the anode is connected to the negative terminal of the voltage source and the cathode is connected to the positive terminal. This causes the holes in the p-region and the electrons in the n-region to move away from the junction, creating a depletion region. The diode is said to be off or blocking in this configuration. The voltage across the diode is equal to the applied voltage, unless it exceeds the breakdown voltage of the diode, which can cause permanent damage.

- Half-wave rectifier: In this configuration, the diode is connected to an alternating current (AC) source and a load resistor. The diode allows only the positive half-cycles of the AC voltage to pass through, converting it to a pulsating direct current (DC) voltage. The output voltage is equal to the peak value of the input voltage minus the diode voltage drop.

- Full-wave rectifier: In this configuration, four diodes are arranged in a bridge circuit to convert an AC voltage to a DC voltage. The diodes allow both the positive and negative half-cycles of the AC voltage to pass through, but in opposite directions, creating a continuous DC voltage. The output voltage is equal to the peak value of the input voltage minus two diode voltage drops.

- Zener diode: In this configuration, the diode is reverse biased and operates in the breakdown region. The breakdown region is the region where the diode can withstand a high reverse voltage without being damaged. The diode has a constant voltage across it, called the Zener voltage, which depends on the doping level of the diode. The Zener diode can be used as a voltage regulator, to provide a stable reference voltage for other circuits.

- Light-emitting diode (LED): In this configuration, the diode is forward biased and emits light when current flows through it. The color and intensity of the light depend on the material and the doping level of the diode. The LED can be used as an indicator, a display, or a source of illumination.

### Clippers

- Clippers are circuits that are used to clip off or remove a portion of an input signal without distorting the remaining part of the waveform.
- Clippers are also known as limiters, amplitude selectors, or slicers.
- Clippers can be classified into two types: series and parallel.
- Series clippers are circuits where the diode is connected in series with the load resistor.
- Parallel clippers are circuits where the diode is connected in parallel with the load resistor.
- The diode is a semiconductor device that acts as a one-way switch for current. It allows current to flow easily in one direction, but severely restricts current from flowing in the opposite direction.
- The diode has two terminals: anode and cathode. The anode is the positive terminal and the cathode is the negative terminal.
- The diode has two modes of operation: forward bias and reverse bias. In forward bias, the anode is connected to the positive voltage and the cathode is connected to the negative voltage. In this mode, the diode conducts current and behaves like a closed switch. In reverse bias, the anode is connected to the negative voltage and the cathode is connected to the positive voltage. In this mode, the diode blocks current and behaves like an open switch.
- The diode has a characteristic curve that shows the relationship between the voltage across the diode and the current through the diode. The curve has a knee point, which is the minimum voltage required for the diode to conduct current in forward bias. The knee point is also called the cut-in voltage or the threshold voltage.
- The diode has a maximum reverse voltage that it can withstand without breaking down. This voltage is called the breakdown voltage or the peak inverse voltage. If the reverse voltage exceeds the breakdown voltage, the diode will conduct a large amount of current in reverse bias and may get damaged.
- The diode can be made of different semiconductor materials, such as silicon, germanium, or gallium arsenide. The material affects the properties of the diode, such as the knee point, the breakdown voltage, and the power rating.
- The diode can have different shapes and sizes, depending on the application and the design. Some common types of diodes are signal diodes, rectifier diodes, Zener diodes, light-emitting diodes, and Schottky diodes.

## Unit 3 - Field Effect Transistor

- A field effect transistor (FET) is a type of transistor that uses an electric field to control the flow of current in a semiconductor.
- FETs have three terminals: source, gate, and drain. The source is where the current enters, the gate is where the electric field is applied, and the drain is where the current leaves.
- FETs can be classified into two main types: junction FETs (JFETs) and metal-oxide-semiconductor FETs (MOSFETs).
- JFETs are made of a single type of semiconductor (either n-type or p-type) with two regions of the opposite type (called the gate) forming a pn junction on either side of the channel. The gate voltage controls the width of the channel and thus the current flow.
- MOSFETs are made of a semiconductor (usually silicon) with a thin layer of metal oxide (usually silicon dioxide) on top, forming the gate. The gate is insulated from the channel by the oxide layer, and the voltage applied to the gate creates an electric field that attracts or repels charge carriers in the channel, depending on the type of MOSFET.
- MOSFETs can be further divided into two subtypes: enhancement-mode and depletion-mode. Enhancement-mode MOSFETs have a normally off channel that is turned on by applying a positive (for n-channel) or negative (for p-channel) gate voltage. Depletion-mode MOSFETs have a normally on channel that is turned off by applying a negative (for n-channel) or positive (for p-channel) gate voltage.
- FETs are widely used in electronic circuits for amplification, switching, logic, and memory functions. They have several advantages over bipolar junction transistors (BJTs), such as higher input impedance, lower power consumption, faster switching speed, and better scalability .
- FETs also have some disadvantages, such as lower output impedance, higher noise, and susceptibility to damage by electrostatic discharge .

### Construction and Characteristic of JFETs

- A JFET (Junction Field Effect Transistor) is a semiconductor device with three terminals: gate, source and drain.
- A JFET can be either N-channel or P-channel, depending on the type of charge carriers in the channel .
- A JFET is constructed using a long channel of semiconductor material, which is either N-type or P-type, and two P-N junctions at the sides, which form the gate  .
- The gate is reverse biased, which creates a depletion region around the P-N junctions, narrowing the channel and controlling the current flow from source to drain  .
- The JFET is a voltage-controlled device, meaning that the gate voltage determines the amount of current that can flow through the channel  .
- The JFET has a high input impedance, low noise, and low power consumption, making it suitable for amplification and switching applications  .
- The JFET has two main characteristics: the drain characteristic and the transfer characteristic  .
- The drain characteristic shows the relationship between the drain current (ID) and the drain-source voltage (VDS) for a fixed gate-source voltage (VGS)  .
- The drain characteristic has three regions: ohmic, saturation, and breakdown  .
- In the ohmic region, the JFET behaves like a resistor, and the drain current is proportional to the drain-source voltage  .
- In the saturation region, the drain current reaches a maximum value, called the pinch-off current (IDSS), and becomes independent of the drain-source voltage  .
- In the breakdown region, the drain current increases rapidly as the drain-source voltage exceeds a critical value, called the avalanche voltage (VAV)  .
- The transfer characteristic shows the relationship between the drain current (ID) and the gate-source voltage (VGS) for a fixed drain-source voltage (VDS)  .
- The transfer characteristic has a negative slope, meaning that the drain current decreases as the gate-source voltage becomes more negative (for N-channel JFET) or more positive (for P-channel JFET)  .
- The transfer characteristic has a cut-off point, where the drain current becomes zero, and a saturation point, where the drain current reaches the pinch-off current  .
- The transfer characteristic can be approximated by a quadratic equation: ID = IDSS (1 - VGS / VP)^2, where VP is the pinch-off voltage  .

### Differential and Common-Mode Operation

- A differential amplifier is a circuit that can accept two input signals and amplify the difference between these two input signals.
- An op-amp is a type of differential amplifier that has a very high voltage gain and a very high input impedance.
- The differential input voltage of an op-amp is the difference between the voltages applied to the two input terminals, denoted by Vd = V+ - V-.

- The common-mode operation of an op-amp is when the input signals have the same polarity and the output voltage is proportional to the common-mode input voltage.

- The lower the CMRR, the more the op-amp is affected by common-mode interference or noise.

### Cellular Communication

Cellular communication is the process by which cells send and receive signals to coordinate their functions and activities. Cellular communication can be classified into two types: biological and technological.

#### Biological Cellular Communication

Biological cellular communication involves the transmission of chemical signals between living cells. These signals can be hormones, neurotransmitters, cytokines, growth factors, or other molecules that bind to specific receptors on the target cells. The binding of a signal to a receptor triggers a cascade of events inside the cell, such as changes in gene expression, enzyme activity, membrane potential, or cell division. Biological cellular communication can occur in different ways, depending on the distance and the type of signal. Some of the common forms of biological cellular communication are:

- **Autocrine signaling**: The cell produces and responds to its own signal. For example, some immune cells secrete and bind to cytokines that stimulate their own growth and activation.
- **Paracrine signaling**: The cell produces a signal that affects nearby cells. For example, some nerve cells release neurotransmitters that bind to receptors on adjacent nerve cells or muscle cells.
- **Endocrine signaling**: The cell produces a signal that travels through the bloodstream to reach distant cells. For example, some glands secrete hormones that regulate the metabolism, growth, and development of various organs and tissues.
- **Juxtacrine signaling**: The cell produces a signal that is displayed on its surface and interacts with a receptor on another cell in direct contact. For example, some immune cells express antigens that are recognized by receptors on other immune cells.

#### Technological Cellular Communication

Technological cellular communication involves the transmission of electromagnetic signals between devices that use cellular networks. Cellular networks are systems of interconnected base stations that provide wireless coverage to mobile phones, personal communication systems, wireless networking, etc. The technology is designed to replace high power transmitter/receiver systems for cell radio phones. For data transmission, cellular networks use lower capacity, shorter range, and more transmitters. Technological cellular communication can be classified into different generations, depending on the speed, bandwidth, and technology of the network. Some of the common generations of technological cellular communication are:
