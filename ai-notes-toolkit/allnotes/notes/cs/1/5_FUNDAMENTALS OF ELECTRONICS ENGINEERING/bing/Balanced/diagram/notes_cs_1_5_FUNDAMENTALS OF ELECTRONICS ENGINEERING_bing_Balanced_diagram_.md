# FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRONICS ENGINEERING

Electronics engineering is a branch of engineering that deals with the design, development and testing of electronic systems and products. Electronics engineers work with various components and devices that manipulate electric signals, such as resistors, capacitors, diodes, transistors, integrated circuits, microcontrollers, sensors, etc. Electronics engineering has many applications in various fields, such as telecommunications, science, healthcare, government, and military .

Some of the basic concepts and topics that are essential for electronics engineering are:

- Electricity: Electricity is the flow of electric charge through a conductor. There are two types of electrical signals, those being alternating current (AC), and direct current (DC). AC is the type of electricity that comes from the power grid and changes direction periodically. DC is the type of electricity that comes from batteries and solar cells and flows in one direction. AC and DC have different properties and applications in electronics.
- Circuits: A circuit is a complete and closed path through which electric current can flow. A circuit consists of a power source, such as a battery or a generator, and one or more components, such as resistors, capacitors, diodes, etc. that control the flow and behavior of the current. A circuit can be analyzed using various laws and methods, such as Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws, Thevenin's theorem, etc. to determine the voltage, current, power, and resistance at any point in the circuit .
- Resistance: Resistance is a measure of how much a component or a material opposes the flow of electric current. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω) and depends on factors such as the length, cross-sectional area, temperature, and material of the conductor. Resistance can be used to limit the current, divide the voltage, or generate heat in a circuit. Resistors are components that have a fixed or variable resistance and are used for various purposes in electronic circuits .
- Series Vs. Parallel: Series and parallel are two ways of connecting components in a circuit. In series, the components are connected end to end, so that the same current flows through all of them. In parallel, the components are connected across the same two points, so that the voltage across them is the same. The total resistance, current, and voltage in a series or parallel circuit can be calculated using different formulas. Series and parallel connections have different effects on the circuit performance and behavior .
- Basic Components: There are many types of components that are used in electronic circuits, each with a specific function and symbol. Some of the basic components are:

  - Capacitors: Capacitors are components that store electric charge and energy. Capacitors consist of two conductive plates separated by an insulator, such as air, paper, or ceramic. Capacitors have the ability to block DC and pass AC, and are used for filtering, smoothing, timing, and coupling in electronic circuits. Capacitors are measured in farads (F) and have different types, such as electrolytic, ceramic, film, etc. depending on the material and construction .
  - Diodes: Diodes are components that allow current to flow in one direction only. Diodes consist of a semiconductor material, such as silicon or germanium, that has two terminals, called anode and cathode. Diodes have a threshold voltage, called the forward voltage, that must be applied across them for the current to flow. Diodes are used for rectification, switching, protection, and regulation in electronic circuits. Diodes have different types, such as LED, Zener, Schottky, etc. depending on the function and characteristics .
  - Transistors: Transistors are components that can amplify or switch electric signals. Transistors consist of three terminals, called base, collector, and emitter, and two regions of semiconductor material, called N-type and P-type, that are arranged in different configurations, such as NPN or PNP. Transistors can operate in different modes, such as cut-off, saturation, or active, depending on the voltage and current applied to the terminals. Transistors are used for amplification, switching, oscillation, and logic in electronic circuits. Transistors have different types, such as BJT, FET, MOSFET, etc. depending on the structure and operation .



## Unit 1 - Semiconductor Diode

- A semiconductor diode is a device that allows current to flow in one direction, but blocks it in the opposite direction  .
- A semiconductor diode is made of a p-type and an n-type semiconductor material, which are joined together to form a p-n junction  .
- The p-type material has a surplus of holes (positive charge carriers), while the n-type material has a surplus of electrons (negative charge carriers)  .
- The p-n junction has two terminals: the anode (connected to the p-type material) and the cathode (connected to the n-type material)  .
- The symbol of a semiconductor diode is shown below  :

- The arrow in the symbol indicates the direction of conventional current flow (from positive to negative) when the diode is forward biased  .
- Forward biasing means applying a positive voltage to the anode and a negative voltage to the cathode, which reduces the potential barrier at the p-n junction and allows current to flow  .
- Reverse biasing means applying a negative voltage to the anode and a positive voltage to the cathode, which increases the potential barrier at the p-n junction and blocks current flow  .
- The characteristic curve of a semiconductor diode is shown below  :

diode curve

- The curve shows the relationship between the voltage across the diode (V) and the current through the diode (I)  .
- The curve has two regions: the forward region (where V > 0 and I > 0) and the reverse region (where V < 0 and I < 0)  .
- In the forward region, the current increases exponentially with the voltage, but there is a threshold voltage (Vth) below which the current is negligible  .
- In the reverse region, the current is very small and almost constant, until a breakdown voltage (Vbr) is reached, where the current increases rapidly  .
- The threshold voltage and the breakdown voltage depend on the type and doping level of the semiconductor material  .
- Semiconductor diodes have many applications, such as rectifiers, switches, clippers, clampers, voltage regulators, and logic gates  .



### Depletion layer

- A depletion layer is an insulating region within a conductive, doped semiconductor material where the mobile charge carriers have been diffused away, or have been forced away by an electric field.
- A depletion layer forms when a p-type semiconductor is joined with an n-type semiconductor, creating a p-n junction.
- The p-type semiconductor has a higher concentration of holes, while the n-type semiconductor has a higher concentration of electrons.
- Due to the difference in concentration, holes diffuse from the p-region to the n-region, and electrons diffuse from the n-region to the p-region, causing recombination.
- As a result, a thin layer near the junction becomes depleted of charge carriers, leaving behind fixed and oppositely charged ions.
- The depletion layer acts as a barrier that opposes the flow of electrons from the n-side to the p-side, and vice versa.
- The depletion layer also creates an electric field that points from the n-side to the p-side, which induces a potential difference across the junction, called the built-in potential.
- The width and potential of the depletion layer depend on the doping concentration, the applied voltage, and the temperature.
- The depletion layer plays an important role in the operation of semiconductor devices, such as diodes, transistors, solar cells, and light-emitting diodes.
- The depletion layer can be modulated by applying an external voltage, which can either widen or narrow the depletion layer, and change the direction and magnitude of the electric field.

### Zener Diodes breakdown mechanism (Zener and avalanche) for the notes of the Unit 1 - Semiconductor Diode in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRONICS ENGINEERING

- A Zener diode is a heavily doped semiconductor device that is designed to operate in the reverse direction.
- When the reverse voltage across the Zener diode reaches a certain value, called the Zener voltage, the diode conducts a large current and the voltage across it remains constant.
- The Zener diode can be used for voltage regulation, as a reference element, surge suppressor, and in switching and clipping circuits.
- There are two types of breakdown mechanisms that occur in Zener diodes: Zener breakdown and avalanche breakdown.
- Zener breakdown occurs in heavily doped junctions, which produce a narrow depletion region. The electric field in the depletion region is very high, and it can pull the valence electrons from the atoms and create a large current. The Zener breakdown voltage decreases with increasing temperature, creating a negative temperature coefficient.
- Avalanche breakdown occurs in lightly doped junctions, which produce a wider depletion region. The electric field in the depletion region is not high enough to cause Zener breakdown, but it can accelerate the free electrons to high velocities. These electrons can collide with the atoms and knock out more electrons, creating a chain reaction and a large current. The avalanche breakdown voltage increases with increasing temperature, creating a positive temperature coefficient.
- The Zener diode can operate in both Zener and avalanche breakdown regions, depending on the doping level and the reverse voltage. The Zener diode is usually designed to have a breakdown voltage in the range of 2 to 200 volts. The V-I characteristics of the Zener diode show a sharp knee at the breakdown voltage, where the current increases rapidly and the voltage remains constant.



### Diode Application

A diode is a two-terminal electronic device that allows current to flow in only one direction. It has a low resistance in one direction (forward bias) and a high resistance in the other direction (reverse bias). Diodes are widely used in various fields of electronics for different purposes. Some of the common applications of diodes are:

- **Rectification**: A diode can convert an alternating current (AC) into a direct current (DC) by blocking the negative or positive half cycles of the AC waveform. This process is called rectification and it is the basis of many power supplies, chargers, adapters, etc. A single diode can perform half-wave rectification, while a bridge of four diodes can perform full-wave rectification.
- **Switching**: A diode can act as a switch that can turn on or off a circuit depending on the polarity of the input voltage. This property is useful for logic circuits, digital devices, multiplexers, demultiplexers, etc. A diode can also be used to isolate a signal from a power source or to control the size of a signal by clipping or clamping it.
- **Voltage Reference**: A diode can provide a stable and precise voltage reference for various circuits, such as voltage regulators, comparators, amplifiers, etc. This is because a diode has a characteristic voltage drop across it when it is forward biased, which depends on the type and material of the diode. For example, a silicon diode has a voltage drop of about 0.7 V, while a germanium diode has a voltage drop of about 0.3 V.
- **Frequency Mixer**: A diode can be used to combine or mix two different frequency signals into a single output signal that contains the sum and difference of the input frequencies. This is useful for radio communication, modulation, demodulation, signal processing, etc. A diode can also be used to detect or demodulate an amplitude modulated (AM) signal by rectifying it and filtering out the carrier frequency.
- **Light Source**: A diode can emit light when it is forward biased, if it is made of a suitable material that can produce photons from the recombination of electrons and holes. This type of diode is called a light-emitting diode (LED) and it is widely used for illumination, display, indication, etc. LEDs can produce different colors of light depending on the band gap of the material and the doping impurities.
- **Temperature and Light Sensor**: A diode can sense the temperature or light intensity by measuring the change in its electrical characteristics, such as voltage drop, current, resistance, etc. A diode that is sensitive to temperature is called a thermistor, while a diode that is sensitive to light is called a photodiode. Thermistors are used for temperature measurement, control, compensation, etc. Photodiodes are used for light detection, measurement, conversion, etc.
- **Solar Cell or Photovoltaic Cell**: A diode can generate electricity when it is exposed to light, if it is made of a material that can create an electric field from the absorption of photons. This type of diode is called a solar cell or a photovoltaic cell and it is used for converting solar energy into electrical energy. Solar cells are composed of multiple diodes connected in series or parallel to form a solar panel or a solar array.



### Diode Configuration

A diode is an electrical device that allows current to flow in one direction only. It has two terminals: an anode and a cathode. The anode is the positive terminal and the cathode is the negative terminal. A diode is made of a semiconductor material, such as silicon, that has a p-n junction. A p-n junction is a boundary between two regions of the semiconductor that have different types of impurities. The p-region has an excess of positive charge carriers (holes) and the n-region has an excess of negative charge carriers (electrons).

A diode can be configured in different ways depending on the application and the desired output. Some common diode configurations are:

- Series diode configuration: In this configuration, two or more diodes are connected in series with a DC input voltage. The output voltage is equal to the input voltage minus the voltage drops across the diodes. The diodes must be oriented in the same direction to allow current to flow. This configuration can be used to protect circuits from reverse polarity or to increase the voltage rating of the diodes.

- Parallel diode configuration: In this configuration, two or more diodes are connected in parallel with a DC input voltage. The output voltage is equal to the lowest voltage drop across the diodes. The diodes must be oriented in the opposite direction to prevent current from flowing. This configuration can be used to provide a constant voltage output or to reduce the power dissipation of the diodes.

- Half-wave rectifier: In this configuration, a single diode is connected in series with an AC input voltage and a load resistor. The output voltage is the positive half of the input voltage. The diode blocks the negative half of the input voltage. This configuration can be used to convert AC to DC, but it is inefficient and produces a pulsating output.

- Full-wave rectifier: In this configuration, four diodes are connected in a bridge arrangement with an AC input voltage and a load resistor. The output voltage is the full input voltage with both positive and negative halves. The diodes allow current to flow in alternate paths depending on the polarity of the input voltage. This configuration can be used to convert AC to DC, and it is more efficient and produces a smoother output than the half-wave rectifier.

- Zener diode configuration: In this configuration, a special type of diode called a zener diode is connected in reverse bias with a DC input voltage and a load resistor. The output voltage is equal to the zener voltage, which is the voltage at which the diode breaks down and conducts in reverse. The zener diode can regulate the output voltage by maintaining a constant voltage drop across itself. This configuration can be used to provide a stable reference voltage or to protect circuits from overvoltage.

### Zener diode as shunt regulator

- A Zener diode is a special type of diode that can operate in the reverse breakdown region, where it maintains a nearly constant voltage across its terminals.
- A Zener diode can be used as a shunt voltage regulator, which is a device that regulates the output voltage across a load by diverting excess current to the ground.
- The basic circuit of a Zener diode shunt regulator is shown below:

```
  +V_in
   |
   R
   |
   +----+V_out
   |    |
   Z    R_L
   |    |
   +----+
   |
  GND
```

- In this circuit, R is the series resistor, Z is the Zener diode, and R_L is the load resistor. The input voltage V_in is applied across the series combination of R, Z, and R_L, and the output voltage V_out is taken across Z and R_L.
- The Zener diode is connected in parallel with the load R_L, and it is reverse biased, meaning that the cathode is more positive than the anode. The Zener diode has a breakdown voltage V_Z, which is the minimum reverse voltage required to make it conduct.
- When the input voltage V_in is less than V_Z, the Zener diode does not conduct, and the output voltage V_out is equal to V_in. The current through the circuit is given by:

```
I = V_in / (R + R_L)
```

- When the input voltage V_in is greater than V_Z, the Zener diode starts to conduct, and the output voltage V_out is equal to V_Z. The current through the circuit is given by:

```
I = V_in / R
```

- The excess current (I - V_Z / R_L) is shunted through the Zener diode to the ground, thus regulating the output voltage at V_Z. The Zener diode acts as a constant voltage source in parallel with the load.
- The advantages of using a Zener diode as a shunt regulator are:
  - It provides a better regulation over a wide range of load currents and input voltages.
  - It has a higher current capability than a linear regulator.
  - It is low cost and simple to implement.
- The disadvantages of using a Zener diode as a shunt regulator are:
  - It has a poor efficiency, as the excess power is dissipated as heat in the Zener diode and the series resistor.
  - It has a high output impedance, as the Zener diode resistance varies with the current.
  - It has a limited output voltage range, as the Zener diode breakdown voltage is fixed and depends on the doping level.

#### Voltage Doubler Circuit

- A voltage doubler is a voltage multiplier circuit that produces an output voltage that is twice the peak input voltage.
- A voltage doubler consists of two diodes and two capacitors connected in a specific configuration.
- There are two types of voltage doubler circuits: half-wave and full-wave.
- A half-wave voltage doubler consists of a capacitor connected in parallel with the AC input and a diode connected in series with the output.
- A full-wave voltage doubler consists of two half-wave voltage doublers connected in series, with a common ground.
- The operation of a voltage doubler circuit can be explained by the charging and discharging of the capacitors during the positive and negative half cycles of the AC input.
- The output voltage of a voltage doubler circuit is given by Vout = 2Vp - 2Vd, where Vp is the peak input voltage and Vd is the diode voltage drop.
- The output voltage of a voltage doubler circuit is not exactly twice the input voltage, due to the voltage drop across the diodes and the capacitor leakage.
- The output voltage of a voltage doubler circuit can be increased by adding more stages of half-wave voltage doublers in series.

Two terminal devices are electronic components that have only two terminals, such as anode and cathode, and allow current to flow only in one direction. The most common two terminal device is the diode, which is a semiconductor device that has a p-n junction and a non-linear current-voltage characteristic. Diodes are widely used in various applications, such as rectification, switching, voltage regulation, signal modulation, etc.

However, there are some special types of diodes that have different structures and functions than the ordinary diodes. These are called special purpose two terminal devices, and they include:

- **Tunnel diode**: This is a diode that has a very thin p-n junction and a negative resistance region in its current-voltage characteristic. This means that the current decreases as the voltage increases in a certain range. Tunnel diodes can operate at very high frequencies and are used in oscillators, amplifiers, and logic circuits.
- **Photo diode**: This is a diode that generates a current when exposed to light. Photo diodes have a reverse-biased p-n junction and a large surface area to capture more photons. Photo diodes are used in optical communication, sensors, detectors, and solar cells.
- **Varactor diode**: This is a diode that has a variable capacitance depending on the applied voltage. Varactor diodes have a reverse-biased p-n junction and a thin depletion layer that acts as a dielectric. Varactor diodes are used in tuning circuits, frequency modulators, and filters.
- **Schottky diode**: This is a diode that has a metal-semiconductor junction instead of a p-n junction. Schottky diodes have a lower forward voltage drop and a faster switching speed than ordinary diodes. Schottky diodes are used in power supplies, logic circuits, and radio frequency applications.
- **Light emitting diode (LED)**: This is a diode that emits light when a forward current flows through it. LEDs have a forward-biased p-n junction and a semiconductor material that emits photons of different colors depending on the band gap. LEDs are used in displays, indicators, lighting, and optical communication.
- **Silicon controlled rectifier (SCR)**: This is a device that has four layers of alternating p-type and n-type semiconductors, forming two p-n junctions and three terminals: anode, cathode, and gate. SCR is a switch that can be turned on by a gate signal and turned off by reducing the anode current. SCR is used in power control, motor drives, and protection circuits.

These are some of the special purpose two terminal devices that have different characteristics and applications than the ordinary diodes. They are important components in electronic circuits and systems.



### Light-Emitting Diodes

- A light-emitting diode (LED) is a semiconductor device that emits light when current flows through it.
- LEDs are made of two types of semiconductors: n-type and p-type, which are joined together to form a p-n junction.
- When current passes through the p-n junction in the forward direction, electrons from the n-type region recombine with holes from the p-type region, releasing energy in the form of photons.
- The color and wavelength of the light emitted by an LED depends on the energy band gap of the semiconductors used. For example, red LEDs have a band gap of about 1.8 eV, while blue LEDs have a band gap of about 3.0 eV.
- LEDs have many advantages over conventional light sources, such as low power consumption, long lifetime, high efficiency, small size, and fast switching.
- LEDs have many applications in various fields, such as displays, indicators, lighting, communication, sensors, and medical devices.

### Photo Diodes

- A photo diode is a light-sensitive semiconductor diode that produces current when it absorbs photons.
- A photo diode is designed to operate in reverse bias, meaning that the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the cathode and the negative terminal to the anode.
- A photo diode has a nearly linear relationship of current to received optical power, meaning that the more light falls on the device, the more current flows through it.
- A photo diode can be used to measure light intensity, either for its own sake or as a measure of some other property (such as smoke density, radiation level, etc.).
- A photo diode can also be used to generate electric power from solar radiation, in which case it is called a solar cell.
- A photo diode consists of a p-n junction, where the p-type and n-type materials are doped with impurities to create an electric field across the junction.
- When light falls on the photo diode, it creates electron-hole pairs in the depletion region of the junction. These charge carriers are then swept by the electric field to the respective terminals, creating a photocurrent.

### Tunnel Diodes

- A tunnel diode is a type of semiconductor diode that has effectively "negative resistance" due to the quantum mechanical effect called tunneling.
- Tunneling is the phenomenon where an electron can pass through a potential barrier that is higher than its kinetic energy.
- A tunnel diode is formed by a heavily doped p-n junction, where the doping concentration is about 1000 times higher than a normal diode .
- The heavy doping results in a very narrow depletion region, about 10 nm wide, and a broken band gap, where the conduction band of the n-side overlaps with the valence band of the p-side.
- The following image shows the symbol of a tunnel diode:

Tunnel diode symbol

- The following image shows the energy band diagram of a tunnel diode:

Tunnel diode energy band diagram

- The following image shows the current-voltage (I-V) characteristic curve of a tunnel diode:

Tunnel diode I-V curve

- The I-V curve of a tunnel diode has five regions: forward bias, negative resistance, valley, positive resistance, and reverse bias .
- In the forward bias region, the current increases rapidly as the voltage increases, due to the tunneling of electrons from the valence band of the p-side to the conduction band of the n-side .
- In the negative resistance region, the current decreases as the voltage increases, due to the depletion of available states for tunneling on both sides of the junction .
- In the valley region, the current reaches a minimum value, called the valley current, at a voltage, called the valley voltage .
- In the positive resistance region, the current increases again as the voltage increases, due to the thermionic emission of electrons over the potential barrier .
- In the reverse bias region, the current is very small, due to the high reverse breakdown voltage of the diode .
- The peak current, peak voltage, valley current, valley voltage, and negative resistance are the important parameters of a tunnel diode .
- A tunnel diode can be used as a very fast switching device, an oscillator, an amplifier, a logic gate, and a memory device in computers  .
- A tunnel diode was invented in August 1957 by Leo Esaki, Yuriko Kurose, and Takashi Suzuki when they were working at Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo, now known as Sony.



## Unit 2 - Bipolar Junction Transistor

- A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a type of semiconductor device that can amplify or switch electrical signals.
- A BJT consists of three regions: the emitter, the base, and the collector. The emitter and the collector are doped with the same type of impurities (either n-type or p-type), while the base is doped with the opposite type of impurities. The base region is very thin and lightly doped compared to the emitter and the collector.
- A BJT can operate in three modes: cut-off, active, and saturation. In the cut-off mode, the base-emitter junction is reverse biased and the base-collector junction is either reverse biased or weakly forward biased. There is no current flowing through the transistor. In the active mode, the base-emitter junction is forward biased and the base-collector junction is reverse biased. The current flowing through the transistor is proportional to the base current and the current gain of the transistor. In the saturation mode, both the base-emitter and the base-collector junctions are forward biased. The current flowing through the transistor is limited by the external circuit and the transistor acts as a closed switch.
- A BJT can be classified into two types: npn and pnp. In an npn transistor, the emitter and the collector are n-type regions and the base is a p-type region. The current flows from the emitter to the collector when the base is positive with respect to the emitter. In a pnp transistor, the emitter and the collector are p-type regions and the base is an n-type region. The current flows from the emitter to the collector when the base is negative with respect to the emitter.
- A BJT can be used for various applications, such as amplifiers, oscillators, switches, logic gates, and sensors. The performance of a BJT depends on several parameters, such as the current gain, the input and output resistances, the frequency response, the power dissipation, and the thermal stability.



### Transistor Construction

- A transistor is a three-layer semiconductor device that can act as an amplifier or a switch.
- A semiconductor is a material that can conduct electricity under certain conditions, such as when an electric field or a light is applied to it.
- The three layers of a transistor are made of either silicon or germanium, which are doped with impurities to create two types of semiconductors: P-type and N-type.
- P-type semiconductors have extra holes, which are the absence of electrons, and can accept electrons from other sources.
- N-type semiconductors have extra electrons, which can move freely and donate electrons to other sources.
- The three layers of a transistor are arranged in either NPN or PNP configuration, where the middle layer is called the base, and the outer layers are called the emitter and the collector.
- The base is very thin and lightly doped, while the emitter and the collector are thicker and heavily doped.
- The emitter and the collector are connected to an external voltage source, while the base is connected to an input signal.
- The input signal controls the flow of current from the emitter to the collector, which is the output signal.
- The output signal is amplified or switched depending on the type and biasing of the transistor.



### Operation of Bipolar Junction Transistor

- A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a type of transistor that uses both electron and hole charge carriers. In contrast, unipolar transistors, such as field-effect transistors, only use one kind of charge carrier.
- A BJT has three terminals: the base (B), the collector (C), and the emitter (E). The base is the control terminal, the collector is the output terminal, and the emitter is the input terminal. The base-emitter junction is forward-biased, while the base-collector junction is reverse-biased.
- A BJT can operate in three regions: active, saturation, and cutoff. In the active region, the transistor acts as an amplifier, where the collector current is proportional to the base current. In the saturation region, the transistor is fully on and acts as a switch, where the collector current is equal to the saturation current. In the cutoff region, the transistor is fully off and acts as an open circuit, where the collector current is zero.
- The operation of a BJT can be explained by the movement of charge carriers across the two junctions. In an npn transistor, the majority carriers are electrons, while in a pnp transistor, the majority carriers are holes. The following diagram shows the operation of an npn transistor in the active region:

npn transistor operation

- When a small current flows from the base to the emitter, it causes a large number of electrons to be injected from the emitter into the base. Since the base is very thin and lightly doped, most of these electrons diffuse across the base and reach the reverse-biased base-collector junction. There, they are swept into the collector by the electric field, creating a large collector current. The ratio of the collector current to the base current is called the current gain, and it is typically in the range of 50 to 500.
- The operation of a pnp transistor is similar, but with the roles of electrons and holes reversed. A small current flows from the emitter to the base, causing a large number of holes to be injected from the emitter into the base. Most of these holes diffuse across the base and reach the reverse-biased base-collector junction, where they are swept into the collector by the electric field, creating a large collector current. The current gain is also similar to that of an npn transistor.



### Amplification action of Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)

- A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a type of transistor that uses both electrons and holes as charge carriers in different regions of the device.
- A BJT has three terminals: the base (B), the collector (C), and the emitter (E). The base is the input terminal, the collector is the output terminal, and the emitter is the common terminal for both input and output currents.
- A BJT can operate in three regions: the cut-off region, the active region, and the saturation region. The cut-off region is when the base-emitter junction is reverse biased and the base-collector junction is either reverse or forward biased. In this region, there is no current flow in the device. The saturation region is when both the base-emitter and the base-collector junctions are forward biased. In this region, the collector current is limited by the external circuit and the device acts like a closed switch. The active region is when the base-emitter junction is forward biased and the base-collector junction is reverse biased. In this region, the collector current is proportional to the base current and the device acts like a current amplifier.
- A BJT amplifier is a circuit that uses a BJT in the active region to amplify the input signal. Depending on the configuration of the circuit, the BJT amplifier can be a voltage amplifier, a current amplifier, or a power amplifier.
- A voltage amplifier is a circuit that increases the voltage of the input signal and produces an amplified output voltage. The voltage gain of a voltage amplifier is the ratio of the output voltage to the input voltage. A common-emitter amplifier is an example of a voltage amplifier.
- A current amplifier is a circuit that increases the current of the input signal and produces an amplified output current. The current gain of a current amplifier is the ratio of the output current to the input current. A common-base amplifier is an example of a current amplifier.
- A power amplifier is a circuit that increases the power of the input signal and produces an amplified output power. The power gain of a power amplifier is the ratio of the output power to the input power. A power amplifier is usually a combination of a voltage amplifier and a current amplifier.
- The amplification action of a BJT is based on the principle of current control. By applying a small current at the base terminal, a larger current can be controlled at the collector terminal. The ratio of the collector current to the base current is called the current gain or beta (β) of the BJT. The value of beta depends on the type and characteristics of the BJT.
- The amplification action of a BJT can also be explained by the concept of recombination and injection of charge carriers. When the base-emitter junction is forward biased, electrons from the emitter are injected into the base region, where they recombine with holes. Some of the electrons, however, diffuse across the base region and reach the base-collector junction, which is reverse biased. These electrons are swept into the collector region by the electric field, creating a collector current. The base current is the difference between the emitter current and the collector current. The ratio of the collector current to the emitter current is called the common-emitter current gain or alpha (α) of the BJT. The value of alpha is slightly less than one and depends on the recombination rate of the charge carriers in the base region.



### Common Base Configuration of BJT

- The common base configuration is one of the three basic ways to connect a bipolar junction transistor (BJT) as an amplifier.
- In this configuration, the base terminal of the BJT is a common terminal to both the input and output signals, hence its name common base (CB).
- The input signal is applied between the emitter and the base, and the output signal is taken from the collector and the base.
- The common base configuration is less common as an amplifier than compared to the more popular common emitter (CE) or common collector (CC) configurations, but it is still used due to its unique input/output characteristics.
- Some of the advantages of the common base configuration are:
  - It has a high voltage gain, which is the ratio of output voltage to input voltage.
  - It has a high input impedance, which means it does not load the input source too much.
  - It has a low output impedance, which means it can drive a low resistance load easily.
  - It has a wide bandwidth, which means it can amplify a wide range of frequencies without distortion.
- Some of the disadvantages of the common base configuration are:
  - It has a low current gain, which is the ratio of output current to input current.
  - It has a low power gain, which is the product of voltage gain and current gain.
  - It has a low input-output isolation, which means the output signal can affect the input signal and vice versa.
  - It has a low stability, which means it is sensitive to temperature changes and variations in transistor parameters.
- The common base configuration can be analyzed using the hybrid-pi model of the BJT, which is a small-signal equivalent circuit that approximates the behavior of the BJT around a given operating point.
- The hybrid-pi model consists of a controlled current source that represents the transconductance of the BJT, and a parallel combination of a resistor and a capacitor that represents the output resistance and capacitance of the BJT.
- The hybrid-pi model can be used to derive the voltage gain, input impedance, output impedance, and bandwidth of the common base configuration.
- The following diagram shows the hybrid-pi model of the common base configuration, where:
  - Vi is the input voltage
  - Vo is the output voltage
  - Ii is the input current
  - Io is the output current
  - Vcc is the supply voltage
  - Rc is the collector resistor
  - Re is the emitter resistor
  - gm is the transconductance of the BJT
  - ro is the output resistance of the BJT
  - Cpi is the input capacitance of the BJT
  - Cpo is the output capacitance of the BJT

Common base configuration with hybrid-pi model

- The voltage gain of the common base configuration is given by:

  - Av = Vo / Vi = -gm * Rc / (1 + gm * Re)

- The input impedance of the common base configuration is given by:

  - Zi = Vi / Ii = Re / (1 + gm * Re)

- The output impedance of the common base configuration is given by:

  - Zo = Vo / Io = ro || Rc

- The bandwidth of the common base configuration is given by:

  - Bw = gm / (2 * pi * (Cpi + Cpo))

- The current gain of the common base configuration is given by:

  - Ai = Io / Ii = -gm * Rc / (1 + gm * Re) * Re / (Re + ro || Rc)

- The power gain of the common base configuration is given by:

  - Ap = Vo * Io / Vi * Ii = Av * Ai

### Common Collector Configuration

- In this configuration, the base terminal of the transistor serves as the input, the emitter terminal is the output and the collector terminal is common for both input and output.

- Sometimes common collector configuration is also referred to as emitter follower, voltage follower, common collector amplifier, CC amplifier, or CC configuration .
- The common collector amplifier is often used as a voltage buffer, because it has a high input impedance, a low output impedance and a non-inverting voltage gain of approximately one .
- The input circuit is connected between emitter and base and the output is taken from the collector and emitter.

- The common collector amplifier has a high current gain, a high power gain, but a low voltage gain.

## Unit 3 - Field Effect Transistor

- A field effect transistor (FET) is a type of transistor that uses an electric field to control the flow of current in a semiconductor.
- FETs have three terminals: source, gate, and drain. The source and drain are connected to the semiconductor channel, and the gate is insulated from the channel by a thin layer of oxide .
- The gate voltage determines the conductivity of the channel, and thus the current between the source and drain .
- There are two main types of FETs: junction FETs (JFETs) and metal-oxide-semiconductor FETs (MOSFETs) .
- JFETs have a p-n junction between the gate and the channel, and operate in the depletion mode, meaning that the channel is normally conductive and the gate voltage reduces the current.
- MOSFETs have a metal gate and an oxide layer between the gate and the channel, and can operate in either the enhancement mode or the depletion mode, meaning that the channel can be either normally non-conductive or normally conductive, and the gate voltage can either increase or decrease the current.
- FETs have many advantages over bipolar junction transistors (BJTs), such as high input impedance, low power consumption, high switching speed, and compatibility with integrated circuits .
- FETs can be used for various applications, such as amplifiers, switches, sensors, and logic gates .



### Construction and Characteristic of JFETs

- A JFET is a **Junction Field Effect Transistor** that is a type of unipolar transistor that uses an electric field to control the current flow through a channel of semiconductor material .
- A JFET has three terminals: **source**, **drain** and **gate**. The source and drain are connected to the ends of the channel, and the gate is a PN junction that surrounds the channel and forms a reverse-biased diode with the channel .
- A JFET can be either **N-channel** or **P-channel**, depending on the type of charge carriers in the channel. An N-channel JFET has an N-type channel and a P-type gate, while a P-channel JFET has a P-type channel and an N-type gate .

### Transfer Characteristic of FET

- The transfer characteristic of a FET is a curve that shows the relationship between the input voltage (V<sub>GS</sub>) and the output current (I<sub>D</sub>) for a given value of the drain-source voltage (V<sub>DS</sub>).
- The transfer characteristic can be derived from the drain characteristic by drawing a vertical line at a constant V<sub>DS</sub> level and noting the corresponding I<sub>D</sub> and V<sub>GS</sub> values along this line .
- The transfer characteristic can be expressed by the following equation for a JFET:

  I<sub>D</sub> = I<sub>DSS</sub> (1 - V<sub>GS</sub> / V<sub>P</sub>)<sup>2</sup>

  where I<sub>DSS</sub> is the saturation current and V<sub>P</sub> is the pinch-off voltage.

- The transfer characteristic can also be plotted on a normalized scale by dividing I<sub>D</sub> by I<sub>DSS</sub> and V<sub>GS</sub> by V<sub>P</sub>. This is called the universal transfer characteristic and can be applied to any JFET with known values of I<sub>DSS</sub> and V<sub>P</sub> .
- The transfer characteristic can be used to analyze or design FET circuits by finding the operating point (Q-point) of the device, which is the intersection of the transfer characteristic and the load line .
- The transfer characteristic can also be used to determine the transconductance (g<sub>m</sub>) of the FET, which is the ratio of the change in I<sub>D</sub> to the change in V<sub>GS</sub> at a given operating point. The transconductance is a measure of the gain or amplification of the FET .
- The transfer characteristic can be affected by the temperature, the device geometry, and the channel doping of the FET. The transfer characteristic can also vary from device to device due to manufacturing variations .



### MOSFET (MOS) (Depletion and Enhancement) Type

- MOSFET stands for Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor.
- It is a type of FET that uses an electric field to control the conductivity of a channel between the source and drain terminals.
- The channel is formed by a thin layer of semiconductor material (usually silicon) that is insulated from the gate terminal by a thin layer of metal oxide (usually silicon dioxide) .
- There are two major types of MOSFETs: depletion mode and enhancement mode.
- Depletion mode MOSFETs have a channel that is already present at zero gate-source voltage, and the channel conductivity can be reduced by applying a negative voltage to the gate (for n-channel) or a positive voltage to the gate (for p-channel)  .
- Enhancement mode MOSFETs have no channel at zero gate-source voltage, and the channel conductivity can be increased by applying a positive voltage to the gate (for n-channel) or a negative voltage to the gate (for p-channel)  .
- The main differences between depletion and enhancement MOSFETs are summarized in the table below:

| Depletion MOSFET | Enhancement MOSFET |
| ---------------- | ------------------ |
| The type of MOSFET where the channel depletes when a voltage is applied to the gate | The type of MOSFET where the channel enhances when a voltage is applied to the gate |
| The channel is fabricated during manufacturing | There is no channel during manufacturing |
| It conducts current between the source and drain terminals at zero gate-source voltage | It does not conduct current at zero gate-source voltage |
| Applying a reverse voltage to the gate reduces the channel width and conductivity | Applying a reverse voltage does not affect the channel |
| Applying a forward voltage to the gate increases the channel width and conductivity | Applying a forward voltage creates a channel and increases its conductivity |
| It has a lower input impedance than enhancement MOSFET | It has a higher input impedance than depletion MOSFET |
| It is less preferred for switching applications | It is more preferred for switching applications |

  - Logic gates: MOSFETs can be used to implement digital logic circuits such as AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, etc.
  - Memory: MOSFETs can be used to store binary data in dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) and flash memory devices.
  - Sensors: MOSFETs can be used to sense physical parameters such as temperature, pressure, light, etc.



### Transfer Characteristic of FET

- The transfer characteristic of a FET is a curve that shows the relationship between the input voltage (V<sub>GS</sub>) and the output current (I<sub>D</sub>) for a given value of the drain-source voltage (V<sub>DS</sub>).
- The transfer characteristic can be derived from the drain characteristic by drawing a vertical line at a constant V<sub>DS</sub> level and noting the corresponding I<sub>D</sub> and V<sub>GS</sub> values along this line.
- The shape of the transfer characteristic depends on the type of FET (JFET or MOSFET) and the mode of operation (enhancement or depletion).
- For a JFET, the transfer characteristic is nonlinear and has a negative slope, indicating that the drain current decreases as the gate-source voltage becomes more negative. The transfer characteristic can be approximated by a quadratic equation:

  I<sub>D</sub> = I<sub>DSS</sub> (1 - V<sub>GS</sub> / V<sub>P</sub>)<sup>2</sup>

  where I<sub>DSS</sub> is the saturation current and V<sub>P</sub> is the pinch-off voltage.

- For an enhancement-mode MOSFET, the transfer characteristic is also nonlinear and has a positive slope, indicating that the drain current increases as the gate-source voltage becomes more positive. The transfer characteristic can be approximated by a square-law equation:

  I<sub>D</sub> = k (V<sub>GS</sub> - V<sub>T</sub>)<sup>2</sup>

  where k is a constant and V<sub>T</sub> is the threshold voltage.

- For a depletion-mode MOSFET, the transfer characteristic is similar to that of a JFET, except that the gate-source voltage can be either positive or negative to control the drain current.

- A universal transfer characteristic is a normalized plot of I<sub>D</sub> / I<sub>DSS</sub> versus V<sub>GS</sub> / V<sub>P</sub> that can be applied to any JFET, regardless of its specific values of I<sub>DSS</sub> and V<sub>P</sub>. This can simplify the analysis and design of circuits using JFETs.

- The transfer characteristic of a FET is important for determining its operating point, biasing, amplification, and switching behavior.

### Elements of a Communication System

- A communication system is a system that describes the information exchange between two points. The process of transmission and reception of information is called communication.
- The basic components of a communication system are information source, input transducer, transmitter, communication channel, receiver, output transducer, and destination.
- Information source is the origin of the message or signal that needs to be communicated. It can be a person, a device, or a phenomenon.
- Input transducer is the device that converts the message or signal from the information source into a form suitable for transmission. For example, a microphone converts sound waves into electrical signals.
- Transmitter is the device that modulates the input signal and amplifies it to a suitable level for transmission over the communication channel. Modulation is the process of changing one or more properties of a carrier wave according to the input signal. Amplification is the process of increasing the power or amplitude of a signal.
- Communication channel is the medium or path that carries the transmitted signal from the transmitter to the receiver. It can be a wire, a cable, a fiber optic, a radio wave, or a free space.
- Receiver is the device that demodulates the received signal and amplifies it to a suitable level for output. Demodulation is the process of recovering the original input signal from the modulated carrier wave. Amplification is the process of increasing the power or amplitude of a signal.
- Output transducer is the device that converts the output signal from the receiver into a form suitable for the destination. For example, a speaker converts electrical signals into sound waves.
- Destination is the final point of the communication system where the message or signal is delivered. It can be a person, a device, or a phenomenon.
- The communication system can be classified into different types based on the nature of the information, the mode of transmission, the direction of communication, and the configuration of the system.
- The communication system can be analog or digital based on the nature of the information. ### Need of modulation and typical applications

Modulation is the process of changing the characteristics of a carrier wave (such as amplitude, frequency or phase) according to the information contained in a message signal. Modulation is required for various reasons, such as:

- To increase the range and quality of communication: Modulation enables the transmission of low-frequency message signals over long distances without significant loss or distortion. Modulation also improves the signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver, which enhances the quality of communication.
- To reduce the size and cost of the antenna: Modulation allows the use of high-frequency carrier waves, which have shorter wavelengths and require smaller antennas. A smaller antenna reduces the cost and complexity of the transmitter and receiver.
- To avoid interference and multiplexing: Modulation prevents the mixing of message signals from different sources by assigning different carrier frequencies to each source. This enables the simultaneous transmission and reception of multiple signals over a common channel, which is known as frequency division multiplexing (FDM).
- To facilitate the conversion of signals: Modulation enables the inter-conversion of signals from one form to another, such as digital to analog or vice versa. This is useful for transmitting digital signals over analog baseband or for processing analog signals using digital techniques.

Some of the typical applications of modulation are:

- Radio and television broadcasting: Modulation is used to transmit audio and video signals over radio waves. Different types of modulation, such as amplitude modulation (AM), frequency modulation (FM) or phase modulation (PM), are used for different purposes and quality requirements.
- Mobile and wireless communication: Modulation is used to transmit voice and data signals over cellular networks, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, satellite communication, etc. Different types of modulation, such as quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM), phase shift keying (PSK) or frequency shift keying (FSK), are used for different data rates and reliability requirements.
- Optical communication: Modulation is used to transmit information over optical fibers or free space using light waves. Different types of modulation, such as intensity modulation, phase modulation or polarization modulation, are used for different bandwidth and distance requirements.



### Fundamentals of amplitude modulation and demodulation techniques

- Amplitude modulation (AM) is a technique to transmit information via radio carrier waveform by varying the amplitude of the carrier signal in proportion to the amplitude of the modulation signal that is to be transmitted .
- Amplitude demodulation is the process of recovering the original modulation signal from the amplitude-modulated carrier signal .
- There are different types of amplitude modulation and demodulation techniques, such as:
  - Double-sideband suppressed-carrier (DSB-SC) modulation and demodulation: This technique suppresses the carrier signal and transmits only the two sidebands of the modulation signal. The demodulation is done by multiplying the received signal with a local oscillator that has the same frequency and phase as the carrier signal .
  - Single-sideband (SSB) modulation and demodulation: This technique eliminates one of the sidebands of the modulation signal and transmits only the other sideband along with the carrier signal. The demodulation is done by using a filter to select the desired sideband and then multiplying the received signal with a local oscillator that has the same frequency and phase as the carrier signal .
  - Vestigial-sideband (VSB) modulation and demodulation: This technique reduces the bandwidth of one of the sidebands of the modulation signal and transmits the other sideband along with the carrier signal and the vestigial sideband. The demodulation is done by using a filter to select the desired sideband and then multiplying the received signal with a local oscillator that has the same frequency and phase as the carrier signal .
  - Quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) and demodulation: This technique combines two modulation signals, one in phase and one in quadrature, and transmits them as a single amplitude-modulated carrier signal. The demodulation is done by using two local oscillators, one in phase and one in quadrature, and multiplying the received signal with them to recover the two modulation signals .
- Amplitude modulation and demodulation can be performed using analog or digital circuits, such as common emitter amplifiers, envelope detectors, homodyne detectors, filters, mixers, etc   .
- Amplitude modulation and demodulation have various applications in radio broadcasting, television, telephony, data transmission, etc .



### Introduction to Wireless Communication

Wireless communication is the transmission of voice and data without cable or wires. In place of a physical connection, data travels through electromagnetic signals broadcast from sending facilities to intermediate and end-user devices.

Some of the main features of wireless communication are:

- It does not require any physical medium for the transfer of information.
- It uses radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, or other electromagnetic waves for the transmission.
- It can cover a wide range of distances, from a few meters to thousands of kilometers.
- It can support various applications, such as voice, data, video, multimedia, etc.

- It is susceptible to noise, fading, multipath, interference, and security threats, which can degrade the quality and performance of the communication.

- It is complex, as it requires sophisticated techniques and protocols for modulation, coding, multiplexing, routing, etc.

# Cellular Communication

Cellular communication is the process by which cells send and receive signals to coordinate their activities and functions. Cellular communication can be classified into two types: biological and technological.

## Biological Cellular Communication

Biological cellular communication involves the transmission of chemical signals between living cells. These signals can be hormones, neurotransmitters, cytokines, growth factors, or other molecules that bind to specific receptors on the target cells. The binding of a signal to a receptor triggers a cascade of events inside the cell, such as changes in gene expression, enzyme activity, membrane potential, or cell division. Biological cellular communication can occur over different distances and time scales, depending on the type and mode of signaling.

Some of the common forms of biological cellular communication are:

- **Autocrine signaling**: The cell produces and responds to its own signal. This can be a way of self-regulation or feedback.
- **Paracrine signaling**: The cell produces a signal that affects nearby cells. This can be a way of local coordination or modulation.
- **Endocrine signaling**: The cell produces a signal that travels through the bloodstream to reach distant cells. This can be a way of long-term regulation or integration.
- **Neuronal signaling**: The cell produces a signal that travels along a nerve fiber to reach a specific target cell. This can be a way of fast and precise communication or stimulation.
- **Juxtacrine signaling**: The cell produces a signal that is displayed on its surface and interacts with a receptor on an adjacent cell. This can be a way of direct contact or recognition.

## Technological Cellular Communication

Technological cellular communication involves the transmission of electromagnetic signals between devices that use cellular networks. These signals can be voice, data, text, or multimedia that are encoded and modulated by a transmitter and decoded and demodulated by a receiver. Technological cellular communication can occur over different frequencies and bandwidths, depending on the type and standard of the network.

Some of the common features of technological cellular communication are:

- **Cell**: A geographical area that is covered by a single transmitter/receiver system, also called a base station. Cells can vary in size and shape, depending on the terrain and population density.
- **Frequency reuse**: A technique that allows multiple cells to use the same frequency band without interfering with each other, by assigning different codes or channels to each cell. Frequency reuse increases the capacity and efficiency of the network.
- **Handover**: A process that allows a device to switch from one cell to another while maintaining a continuous connection, by transferring the control from one base station to another. Handover ensures the mobility and reliability of the network.
- **Roaming**: A service that allows a device to access the network of a different operator or country, by establishing a temporary agreement between the operators. Roaming enables the compatibility and interoperability of the network.

The main standards of 1G were Advanced Mobile Phone System (AMPS) in North America, Total Access Communication System (TACS) in Europe, and Nordic Mobile Telephone (NMT) in Scandinavia. The main drawbacks of 1G were low voice quality, poor security, and limited capacity.

It used digital modulation and multiple access techniques such as time division multiple access (TDMA) and code division multiple access (CDMA) to transmit voice and data signals over narrowband channels. The main standards of 2G were Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) in Europe and Asia, Interim Standard 95 (IS-95) in North America, and Personal Digital Cellular (PDC) in Japan. The main advantages of 2G were improved voice quality, higher security, and increased capacity. The main drawbacks of 2G were low data rates, limited multimedia services, and lack of interoperability.

It used wideband channels and multiple access techniques such as wideband CDMA (W-CDMA) and CDMA2000 to transmit voice and data signals. The main advantages of 3G were higher data rates, enhanced multimedia services, and global roaming. The main drawbacks of 3G were high cost, complex infrastructure, and spectrum scarcity.

It used orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM) and multiple access techniques such as orthogonal frequency division multiple access (OFDMA) and single carrier frequency division multiple access (SC-FDMA) to transmit voice and data signals over broadband channels. The main standards of 4G were Long Term Evolution (LTE) and WiMAX. The main advantages of 4G were very high data rates, improved multimedia services, and seamless connectivity. The main drawbacks of 4G were high power consumption, limited coverage, and compatibility issues.   

  - **5G (Fifth Generation Technology)**: This is the fifth generation of wireless cellular technology, launched in the late 2010s and early 2020s. It uses massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO), beamforming, and multiple access techniques such as non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA) and sparse code multiple access (SCMA) to transmit voice and data signals over ultra-wideband channels. The main standards of 5G are New Radio (NR) and 5G NR. The main advantages of 5G are extremely high data rates, ultra-low latency, massive connectivity, and enhanced security. The main challenges of 5G are high complexity, spectrum allocation, and network deployment.
