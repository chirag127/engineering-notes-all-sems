### Methodologies in Software Project Management

- A methodology is a set of principles, tools and techniques that are used to plan, execute and manage projects.
- A methodology helps project managers lead team members and manage work while facilitating team collaboration.
- There are many different methodologies, and they all have pros and cons.
- Some of the most popular methodologies in software project management are:

  - **Agile**: Agile is a flexible and iterative approach that focuses on delivering value to customers in short cycles called sprints. Agile principles include fast responses to feedback and changes, effective communication, and collaborative efforts. Agile methods include Scrum, Kanban, Extreme Programming, and others   .
  - **Waterfall**: Waterfall is a linear and sequential approach that follows a predefined set of phases, such as requirements, design, development, testing, and deployment. Waterfall principles include clear documentation, upfront planning, and strict control. Waterfall is suitable for projects with stable and well-defined requirements    .
  - **Scrum**: Scrum is an agile framework that organizes work into sprints, which are time-boxed periods of usually two to four weeks. Scrum principles include self-organizing teams, frequent inspection and adaptation, and empirical process control. Scrum roles include the product owner, the scrum master, and the development team    .
  - **PRINCE2**: PRINCE2 is a structured and process-based methodology that covers the entire project lifecycle, from initiation to closure. PRINCE2 principles include defined roles and responsibilities, business justification, tailored approach, and continuous improvement. PRINCE2 themes include quality, risk, change, and others  .
  - **Lean**: Lean is a philosophy and a set of practices that aim to eliminate waste and maximize value in software development. Lean principles include optimizing the whole, delivering fast, building quality in, and empowering the team. Lean methods include value stream mapping, pull systems, and others .
  - **Six Sigma**: Six Sigma is a methodology that focuses on improving the quality and efficiency of software processes by reducing defects and variations. Six Sigma principles include customer focus, data-driven decision making, and continuous improvement. Six Sigma tools include DMAIC, DMADV, and others .
  - **Critical path method**: Critical path method is a technique that identifies the longest sequence of activities in a project schedule, which determines the shortest possible duration of the project. Critical path method helps project managers plan and monitor the project progress, identify critical and non-critical tasks, and manage risks and resources .
  - **Gantt chart method**: Gantt chart method is a graphical representation of the project schedule, which shows the start and end dates, durations, dependencies, and progress of each task. Gantt chart method helps project managers visualize and communicate the project scope, timeline, and status .

- Choosing the right methodology for a software project depends on various factors, such as the project size, complexity, scope, budget, quality, and customer expectations. Project managers should evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of each methodology and tailor it to the specific needs and context of the project.

### Setting Objectives in SPM

- Strategic Project Management (SPM) is a process of planning, prioritizing, and executing projects to achieve business objectives.
- SPM creates a direct link between business objectives and everyday work, which helps businesses to meet their goals more efficiently and effectively.
- Setting objectives is a key step in SPM, as it provides clarity, focus, motivation, and alignment for the project team and stakeholders.
- The following are some steps to setting objectives in SPM:

  1. Define the business problem or opportunity that the project aims to address. This should be aligned with the strategic direction and priorities of the organization.
  2. Identify the desired outcomes and benefits of the project, and how they will be measured and evaluated. These should be SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound).
  3. Break down the outcomes and benefits into smaller and more manageable objectives, and assign them to different project phases, deliverables, or tasks.
  4. Communicate the objectives to the project team and stakeholders, and ensure their understanding and commitment. This can be done through a project charter, a project plan, or a project dashboard.
  5. Monitor and track the progress and performance of the objectives, and report any issues or changes to the project team and stakeholders. This can be done through regular meetings, status reports, or dashboards.
  6. Review and evaluate the results and achievements of the objectives, and compare them with the expected outcomes and benefits. This can be done through a project closure report, a lessons learned session, or a post-project review.
  7. Celebrate and recognize the success and contribution of the project team and stakeholders, and share the best practices and learnings for future projects.

- Setting objectives in SPM is a critical and continuous activity that requires collaboration, communication, and coordination among the project team and stakeholders. It helps to ensure that the project delivers value and aligns with the business strategy.



### Management Principles in SPM

SPM stands for Software Project Management or Strategic Portfolio Management, depending on the context. Both are related to the effective planning, execution, and control of projects or portfolios that align with the organizational goals and objectives. Here are some of the common principles of management in SPM:

- Define and document requirements: A clear and detailed specification of the scope, objectives, deliverables, and constraints of the project or portfolio is essential for its success. Requirements should be validated by the stakeholders and updated as needed throughout the project lifecycle.
- Manage changes effectively: Changes are inevitable in any project or portfolio, but they need to be managed carefully to avoid scope creep, cost overruns, or quality issues. Changes should be evaluated for their impact, feasibility, and alignment with the strategy, and approved by the appropriate authority before implementation.
- Prioritize projects: Projects or portfolios should be prioritized based on their strategic value, urgency, risk, and resource availability. Prioritization helps to allocate resources optimally, balance the workload, and focus on the most important initiatives .
- Maintain quality: Quality is the degree to which the project or portfolio meets the expectations and requirements of the stakeholders. Quality should be planned, assured, and controlled throughout the project lifecycle, using appropriate standards, methods, and tools.
- Utilize automation: Automation can help to improve the efficiency, accuracy, and consistency of the project or portfolio processes, such as scheduling, tracking, reporting, testing, and deployment. Automation can also reduce human errors, save time and cost, and enhance collaboration.
- Establish communication protocols: Communication is the exchange of information and feedback among the project or portfolio team, stakeholders, and other parties involved. Communication should be clear, timely, relevant, and consistent, using the appropriate channels, formats, and frequency. Communication protocols should define the roles, responsibilities, and expectations of the communicators, as well as the methods, tools, and standards for communication.
- Manage risk: Risk is the uncertainty or possibility of a negative outcome or event that may affect the project or portfolio objectives. Risk should be identified, assessed, prioritized, and mitigated, using appropriate techniques and tools. Risk management should be proactive, continuous, and integrated with the project or portfolio processes.
- Utilize a systematic approach: A systematic approach is a structured and logical way of planning, executing, and controlling the project or portfolio, using a defined methodology, framework, or model. A systematic approach helps to ensure consistency, quality, and transparency, as well as to facilitate learning and improvement .

### Risk Evaluation in SPM

Risk evaluation is an important part of the Software Project Management (SPM) process. It is used to identify potential risks and to assess their impact on the project. The risk evaluation process consists of four steps:

- Analyzing the project environment: This step involves understanding the internal and external factors that may affect the project, such as the project scope, objectives, constraints, assumptions, stakeholders, resources, technology, market, competition, regulations, etc.
- Identifying risks: This step involves brainstorming, interviewing, surveying, researching, and using other techniques to generate a list of possible risks that may occur during the project. Risks can be categorized into different types, such as technical, operational, financial, legal, environmental, social, etc.
- Assigning risk ratings: This step involves estimating the probability and impact of each risk on the project. Probability is the likelihood of the risk occurring, and impact is the severity of the consequences if the risk occurs. Risk ratings can be expressed using numerical scales, qualitative descriptors, or matrices.
- Creating a risk management plan: This step involves developing strategies and actions to prevent, reduce, transfer, or accept the risks. The risk management plan should also include the roles and responsibilities of the project team, the risk monitoring and reporting mechanisms, and the contingency plans and reserves.

Risk evaluation is a continuous and iterative process that should be performed throughout the project lifecycle. It helps the project manager and the project team to anticipate, prepare for, and respond to the uncertainties and challenges that may arise during the project. Risk evaluation also helps to improve the quality, efficiency, and effectiveness of the project.

### Stepwise Project Planning in SPM

Stepwise project planning in software project management is the process of breaking down the development process into smaller, manageable steps. This makes it easier for the project manager to plan and track progress, as each step is clearly defined. It also allows for tasks to be divided up and delegated to different team members.

The following are the main steps involved in stepwise project planning :

- **Step 0: Select project.** This involves choosing a project that is feasible, beneficial, and aligned with the organization's goals and strategies.
- **Step 1: Identify project scope and objectives.** This involves defining the boundaries, deliverables, and expected outcomes of the project. It also involves identifying the project stakeholders and their needs and expectations.
- **Step 2: Identify project infrastructure.** This involves determining the resources, tools, methods, standards, and procedures that will be used to execute the project. It also involves establishing the project organization and roles and responsibilities of the project team members.
- **Step 3: Analyze project characteristics.** This involves identifying the key features, functions, and requirements of the project product or service. It also involves assessing the complexity, size, and risks of the project.
- **Step 4: Identify project products and activities.** This involves decomposing the project product or service into smaller components and defining the activities and tasks required to produce them. It also involves creating a work breakdown structure (WBS) and a network diagram to show the dependencies and sequence of the activities.
- **Step 5: Estimate effort for each activity.** This involves estimating the time, cost, and resources required to complete each activity. It also involves applying various estimation techniques and tools, such as analogy, expert judgment, parametric, or bottom-up methods.
- **Step 6: Identify activity risks.** This involves identifying the potential threats and opportunities that may affect the project activities and their outcomes. It also involves analyzing the probability and impact of the risks and developing mitigation and contingency plans.
- **Step 7: Allocate resources.** This involves assigning the available resources, such as human, material, financial, or technological, to the project activities. It also involves balancing the resource demand and supply and resolving any conflicts or constraints.
- **Step 8: Review/publicize plan.** This involves reviewing the project plan for completeness, accuracy, and consistency. It also involves communicating the project plan to the project stakeholders and obtaining their feedback and approval.
- **Step 9: Execute plan/lower levels of planning.** This involves implementing the project plan and monitoring and controlling the project performance. It also involves performing lower levels of planning, such as phase, stage, or iteration planning, as the project progresses.

### Project Life Cycle

- A project life cycle is a series of phases that a project goes through from its initiation to its closure.
- The phases are sequential and usually overlapping, depending on the project management approach used.
- The project life cycle provides a framework for managing the project, defining the tasks, deliverables, milestones, and resources required for each phase.
- The project life cycle also helps to monitor and control the project progress, quality, risks, and changes.
- The project life cycle may vary depending on the type, size, complexity, and domain of the project, but a generic model consists of four phases: initiation, planning, execution, and closure.

#### Initiation Phase

- The initiation phase is the first phase of the project life cycle, where the project idea is generated, evaluated, and approved.
- The main activities in this phase are:
  - Identify the project sponsor, stakeholders, and customers
  - Define the project scope, objectives, and constraints
  - Conduct a feasibility study to assess the technical, economic, social, and environmental viability of the project
  - Prepare a business case to justify the project benefits, costs, and risks
  - Obtain the project charter, which is a formal document that authorizes the project and assigns the project manager
  - Establish the project team and the project management office (PMO)

#### Planning Phase

- The planning phase is the second phase of the project life cycle, where the project scope is refined, and the project plan is developed and approved.
- The main activities in this phase are:
  - Define the project requirements, specifications, and deliverables
  - Decompose the project scope into work packages and tasks, and create the work breakdown structure (WBS)
  - Estimate the duration, cost, and resources for each task, and create the project schedule and budget
  - Identify the project risks, and develop the risk management plan
  - Define the project quality standards, and develop the quality management plan
  - Define the project communication channels, and develop the communication management plan
  - Define the project roles and responsibilities, and develop the human resource management plan
  - Define the project procurement strategy, and develop the procurement management plan
  - Define the project change control process, and develop the change management plan
  - Integrate all the subsidiary plans into the project management plan, and obtain the approval from the project sponsor and stakeholders

#### Execution Phase

- The execution phase is the third phase of the project life cycle, where the project deliverables are produced and delivered according to the project plan.
- The main activities in this phase are:
  - Execute the project tasks, and manage the project team, resources, and suppliers
  - Monitor and control the project performance, quality, risks, and changes
  - Communicate the project status, issues, and achievements to the project sponsor, stakeholders, and customers
  - Implement the project quality assurance and quality control activities
  - Implement the project risk response strategies
  - Implement the project procurement activities, such as contract negotiation, award, and administration
  - Implement the project change requests, and update the project plan accordingly

#### Closure Phase

- The closure phase is the fourth and final phase of the project life cycle, where the project is formally completed and closed.
- The main activities in this phase are:
  - Verify that all the project deliverables are completed and accepted by the customers
  - Conduct a project review and evaluation to measure the project performance, outcomes, and lessons learned
  - Document the project closure report, which summarizes the project achievements, challenges, and recommendations
  - Release the project resources, and close the project contracts and accounts
  - Celebrate the project success, and recognize the project team and stakeholders

### Software Process and Process Models

- A software process is a set of activities for designing, implementing, and testing a software system.
- A software process model is an abstraction of the software development process that specifies the stages and order of a process.
- Software process models help provide a visual representation of the development process for both the team and the customer to review.
- Software process models also help project managers plan their processes, estimate costs, identify challenges and communicate with teams and customers.
- There are different types of software process models, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Some of the common software process models are :
  - Waterfall: The waterfall software process model is one of the simplest and most effective software process models. It involves a linear sequence of stages, such as requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, and maintenance. Each stage must be completed before moving on to the next one. The waterfall model is easy to understand and follow, but it does not allow for changes or feedback during the development process.
  - Prototyping: Prototyping is another common type of software process model. Prototyping involves creating a working model of the software system that can be tested and evaluated by the customer. Prototyping allows for early feedback and changes, but it can also be time-consuming and costly to produce multiple prototypes.
  - Incremental: Incremental software process model is a type of software process model that involves developing the software system in small increments. Each increment adds some functionality to the system and is delivered to the customer. Incremental model allows for flexibility and feedback, but it also requires careful planning and coordination of the increments.
  - Spiral: Spiral software process model is a type of software process model that combines the features of the waterfall and prototyping models. It involves a cyclic process of four phases: planning, risk analysis, engineering, and evaluation. Each cycle produces a prototype of the system that is evaluated by the customer. Spiral model allows for risk management and customer involvement, but it also requires a lot of documentation and expertise.
  - Agile: Agile software process model is a type of software process model that emphasizes collaboration, communication, and adaptability. It involves a series of short iterations, each delivering a working product that meets the customer's needs. Agile model allows for rapid delivery and changes, but it also requires a high level of customer involvement and trust.
  - Rational Unified Process (RUP): RUP is a type of software process model that is based on the best practices of software engineering. It involves four phases: inception, elaboration, construction, and transition. Each phase consists of nine disciplines, such as business modeling, requirements, design, implementation, testing, and deployment. RUP is a comprehensive and customizable model, but it also requires a lot of resources and discipline.
  - Extreme Programming (XP): XP is a type of software process model that is a subset of agile methods. It involves a set of values, principles, and practices, such as simplicity, communication, feedback, courage, and respect. XP focuses on delivering high-quality software that satisfies the customer's needs. XP is a flexible and efficient model, but it also requires a high level of skill and commitment.



### Choice of Process Models in Software Project Management

A software process model is an abstraction of the software development process that specifies the stages and order of the activities involved in designing, implementing, and testing a software system. There are different types of software process models, each with its own advantages and disadvantages, depending on the nature, size, and complexity of the software project. Some of the most common software process models are:

- **Waterfall model**: This model represents a breakdown of software project activities in a linear sequential phase, such as requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance. Each phase must be completed before the next one can begin, and there is no overlap or iteration between phases. This model is simple, easy to follow, and suitable for well-defined and stable projects with clear requirements and minimal changes .
- **Iterative model**: This model does not attempt to start with a full specification of the requirements, but rather develops the software incrementally through a series of iterations, each of which produces a working version of the software. The requirements are refined and modified as the software evolves, and feedback from the users and stakeholders is incorporated in each iteration. This model is flexible, adaptive, and suitable for complex and dynamic projects with changing requirements and uncertainties .
- **V model**: This model is an extension of the waterfall model, where each phase of the development process is associated with a corresponding phase of the testing process. For example, the requirements analysis phase is linked with the acceptance testing phase, the design phase is linked with the system testing phase, and the implementation phase is linked with the unit testing phase. This model emphasizes the importance of verification and validation of the software at each stage, and is suitable for projects with well-defined and testable requirements .
- **Incremental model**: This model combines the elements of the waterfall and iterative models, where the software is developed and delivered in increments, each of which adds some functionality to the software. The increments are planned and prioritized based on the user and stakeholder needs, and each increment follows a mini waterfall cycle of analysis, design, implementation, and testing. This model allows for early delivery of the software, frequent feedback, and better risk management .
- **Spiral model**: This model is a risk-driven approach, where the software is developed through a series of spirals, each of which consists of four phases: planning, risk analysis, engineering, and evaluation. The planning phase defines the objectives, alternatives, and constraints of the software. The risk analysis phase identifies and resolves the potential risks and uncertainties of the software. The engineering phase implements and tests the software. The evaluation phase reviews and evaluates the software and the process. This model is suitable for large, complex, and high-risk projects that require extensive prototyping and experimentation .
- **Agile model**: This model is a value-driven approach, where the software is developed through a series of short and iterative cycles, called sprints, each of which delivers a potentially shippable product increment. The requirements are captured and prioritized in a product backlog, and the team collaborates with the customer and stakeholders to deliver the highest value features in each sprint. The team follows the principles and practices of agile software development, such as self-organization, cross-functionality, adaptive planning, continuous improvement, and frequent delivery. This model is suitable for fast-paced, volatile, and customer-centric projects that require high responsiveness and flexibility .

The choice of a software process model for a project depends on various factors, such as:

- The size and complexity of the project
- The requirements and scope of the project
- The customer and stakeholder expectations and involvement
- The available resources and budget
- The project schedule and deadlines
- The project risks and uncertainties
- The quality standards and criteria
- The team skills and experience
- The organizational culture and environment

There is no one-size-fits-all software process model that can suit every project. Therefore, the project manager and the team should carefully analyze the project characteristics and context, and select the most appropriate software process model that can best meet the project goals and objectives.

### Network Planning Models in spm

- Network planning models are used to plan and manage software projects by using graphical representations of activities and events.
- They help to visualize the sequence, duration, order, and dependencies of tasks necessary to complete the project.
- They also help to identify the critical path, which is the longest sequence of tasks that determines the minimum time required to finish the project.
- There are two main types of network planning models: activity-on-node (AON) and activity-on-arrow (AOA).
- In AON, the activities are represented by nodes (boxes) and the dependencies are represented by arrows (lines) between nodes.
- In AOA, the activities are represented by arrows and the nodes represent the start and end points of the activities.
- Both AON and AOA use the same notation to indicate the duration and slack of each activity.
- Duration is the time required to complete an activity, and slack is the amount of time an activity can be delayed without affecting the project completion time.
- The duration and slack of each activity are calculated using the following formulas:

  - Earliest start time (ES) = maximum of the earliest finish times of all predecessor activities
  - Earliest finish time (EF) = ES + duration
  - Latest finish time (LF) = minimum of the latest start times of all successor activities
  - Latest start time (LS) = LF - duration
  - Slack = LF - EF = LS - ES

- The critical path is the sequence of activities with zero slack, meaning they cannot be delayed without delaying the project completion time.
- The critical path can be identified by tracing the activities with zero slack from the start node to the end node of the network.
- The critical path determines the project duration, which is equal to the earliest finish time of the end node.
- The network planning models can be used to perform various analyses, such as:

  - What-if analysis: to evaluate the impact of changes in the project scope, resources, or schedule on the project duration and cost.
  - Risk analysis: to identify and quantify the uncertainties and risks associated with the project activities and outcomes.
  - Resource allocation: to optimize the use of available resources (e.g. staff, equipment, materials) to complete the project within the time and budget constraints.
  - Performance measurement: to monitor and control the progress and performance of the project activities and deliverables.

### Forward Pass & Backward Pass Techniques in SPM

- SPM stands for Software Project Management, which is the process of planning, organizing, executing, monitoring, and controlling software projects.
- Forward pass and backward pass are two techniques used in SPM to analyze the project network diagram and determine the project duration, critical path, and slack/float time of each activity.
- A project network diagram is a graphical representation of the sequence and dependencies of the project activities, usually using nodes to represent activities and arrows to represent dependencies.
- Forward pass is a technique to move forward through the network diagram from the start node to the end node, calculating the early start (ES) and early finish (EF) dates of each activity.
  - ES is the earliest possible date that an activity can start, given the dependencies and constraints.
  - EF is the earliest possible date that an activity can finish, calculated by adding the activity duration to the ES.
  - The formula for forward pass is: EF = ES + duration - 1
  - The start node has an ES of 0 and an EF equal to its duration.
  - For activities with multiple predecessors, the ES is the maximum of the EF values of the predecessors.
- Backward pass is a technique to move backward through the network diagram from the end node to the start node, calculating the late start (LS) and late finish (LF) dates of each activity.
  - LS is the latest possible date that an activity can start without delaying the project completion date.
  - LF is the latest possible date that an activity can finish without delaying the project completion date, calculated by subtracting the activity duration from the LS.
  - The formula for backward pass is: LS = LF - duration + 1
  - The end node has an LF equal to the project completion date and an LS equal to its LF minus its duration.
  - For activities with multiple successors, the LF is the minimum of the LS values of the successors.
- The difference between the early and late dates of an activity is called the slack or float time, which is the amount of time that an activity can be delayed without affecting the project completion date.
  - The formula for slack/float time is: Slack = LF - EF = LS - ES
  - Activities with zero slack are called critical activities, and the sequence of critical activities forms the critical path of the project, which is the longest path in the network diagram and determines the minimum project duration.
  - Activities with positive slack are called non-critical activities, and they have some flexibility in their start and finish dates.
- Forward pass and backward pass techniques are useful for project managers to identify the critical path, optimize the project schedule, allocate resources, monitor progress, and manage risks.



### Critical Path Method (CPM) in SPM

- The critical path method (CPM) is a technique where you identify tasks that are necessary for project completion and determine scheduling flexibilities .
- A critical path in project management is the longest sequence of activities that must be finished on time in order for the entire project to be complete .
- The critical path method helps you to plan, schedule, monitor, and control your project effectively.
- There are six steps in the critical path method:
  - Step 1: Specify Each Activity. List all the activities required to complete the project and assign them unique identifiers.
  - Step 2: Establish Dependencies (Activity Sequence). Determine the logical order of the activities and identify which activities depend on others for their start or finish.
  - Step 3: Draw the Network Diagram. Use a graphical tool to represent the activities and their dependencies as nodes and arcs. The network diagram shows the flow of the project from start to finish.
  - Step 4: Estimate Activity Completion Time. Assign a realistic duration to each activity based on the resources, scope, and quality of the work.
  - Step 5: Identify the Critical Path. Calculate the earliest and latest start and finish times for each activity using the forward and backward pass techniques. The critical path is the path with the longest duration and the least amount of slack or float.
  - Step 6: Update the Critical Path Diagram to Show Progress. Monitor the actual performance of the activities and compare them with the planned estimates. Adjust the network diagram and the critical path accordingly to reflect any changes or delays.
- The critical path method helps you to identify the most important activities, the potential risks, and the opportunities for optimization in your project. It also helps you to communicate the project status and expectations to the stakeholders and the team members.

### Earned Value Analysis in SPM

Earned Value Analysis (EVA) is a project management technique that allows to measure the performance and progress of a project based on the planned and actual costs and the earned value of the work done. EVA can help to identify deviations from the baseline plan and to forecast the future outcomes of the project.

Some of the key concepts and terms used in EVA are:

- **Planned Value (PV)**: The budgeted cost of the work scheduled to be done by a certain point in time. It is also known as the Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled (BCWS).
- **Actual Cost (AC)**: The actual cost of the work performed by a certain point in time. It is also known as the Actual Cost of Work Performed (ACWP).
- **Earned Value (EV)**: The budgeted cost of the work actually completed by a certain point in time. It is also known as the Budgeted Cost of Work Performed (BCWP).
- **Cost Variance (CV)**: The difference between the EV and the AC. It indicates whether the project is over or under budget. CV = EV - AC.
- **Schedule Variance (SV)**: The difference between the EV and the PV. It indicates whether the project is ahead or behind schedule. SV = EV - PV.
- **Cost Performance Index (CPI)**: The ratio of the EV to the AC. It measures the cost efficiency of the project. CPI = EV / AC.
- **Schedule Performance Index (SPI)**: The ratio of the EV to the PV. It measures the schedule efficiency of the project. SPI = EV / PV.
- **Estimate at Completion (EAC)**: The projected total cost of the project at completion based on the current performance. EAC = BAC / CPI, where BAC is the Budget at Completion.
- **Estimate to Complete (ETC)**: The projected cost of the remaining work to be done. ETC = EAC - AC.
- **Variance at Completion (VAC)**: The projected difference between the BAC and the EAC. It indicates whether the project will finish over or under budget. VAC = BAC - EAC.

To perform EVA, the following steps are required  :

- Define the scope and the work breakdown structure (WBS) of the project.
- Assign a budget to each work package or activity in the WBS.
- Establish a baseline plan that shows the PV of each work package or activity over time.
- Track the AC and the EV of each work package or activity over time.
- Calculate the CV, SV, CPI, SPI, EAC, ETC, and VAC for the project or any sub-level of the WBS.
- Plot the PV, AC, and EV on a graph to visualize the project performance over time.
- Analyze the results and take corrective actions if needed.

  - Intrinsic motivation is the motivation that comes from within the individual, such as interest, curiosity, enjoyment, or personal growth. Intrinsic motivation is driven by the inherent value or pleasure of the activity itself, rather than external rewards or pressures.
  - Extrinsic motivation is the motivation that comes from outside the individual, such as money, grades, recognition, deadlines, or feedback. Extrinsic motivation is driven by the expected or actual outcomes of the activity, rather than the activity itself.
  - Hybrid motivation is the combination of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, such as when an individual performs an activity for both internal and external reasons.   - Herzberg's two-factor theory is a theory that distinguishes between two types of factors that affect motivation: hygiene factors and motivators. Hygiene factors are the factors that prevent dissatisfaction, such as salary, working conditions, policies, and supervision. Autonomy is the need to have control and choice over one's actions. Competence is the need to have mastery and skill over one's tasks. Relatedness is the need to have meaningful and supportive relationships with others. According to this theory, people are motivated when they experience satisfaction of these needs in their activities.



### The Oldham-Hackman Job Characteristic Model

The Oldham-Hackman Job Characteristic Model is a framework for designing and enriching jobs to increase employee motivation, satisfaction and performance. It was developed by organizational psychologists J. Richard Hackman and Greg Oldham in the 1970s .

The model proposes that there are five core job characteristics that influence three critical psychological states of the employee, which in turn affect the personal and work outcomes. The model also suggests that the impact of the job characteristics can be moderated by the individual's growth need strength, which is the degree to which the employee values personal development and learning from the job .

The five core job characteristics are:

- **Skill variety**: The degree to which the job requires the employee to use different skills and abilities.
- **Task identity**: The degree to which the job allows the employee to complete a whole and identifiable piece of work.
- **Task significance**: The degree to which the job has a substantial impact on the lives of other people, within or outside the organization.
- **Autonomy**: The degree to which the job gives the employee freedom, independence and discretion in scheduling the work and determining the procedures to carry it out.
- **Feedback**: The degree to which the job provides the employee with clear and direct information about the effectiveness of his or her performance   .

The three critical psychological states are:

- **Experienced meaningfulness of the work**: The degree to which the employee feels that the work is worthwhile and important.
- **Experienced responsibility for the outcomes of the work**: The degree to which the employee feels accountable and in control of the results of the work.
- **Knowledge of the actual results of the work**: The degree to which the employee knows how well he or she is doing the work  .

The personal and work outcomes are:

- **High internal work motivation**: The degree to which the employee is intrinsically motivated and interested in the work.
- **High-quality work performance**: The degree to which the employee performs the work effectively and efficiently.
- **High satisfaction with the work**: The degree to which the employee is happy and satisfied with the work.
- **Low absenteeism and turnover**: The degree to which the employee is present and committed to the work and the organization   .

The model can be used to diagnose and improve the design of jobs by increasing the core job characteristics, which will enhance the psychological states and the outcomes. The model also suggests that the employees who have high growth need strength will benefit more from the enriched jobs than those who have low growth need strength  .

The model has been widely applied and tested in various settings and occupations, and has received empirical support for its validity and usefulness. However, the model also has some limitations, such as:

- It does not consider the social and interpersonal aspects of the job, such as relationships, communication and teamwork, which may also affect the employee's motivation and satisfaction.
- It does not account for the individual differences in personality, values, preferences and abilities, which may influence how the employee perceives and responds to the job characteristics.
- It does not address the dynamic and changing nature of the work environment, such as technology, competition and customer demands, which may require the employee to adapt and learn new skills and tasks .

Therefore, the model should be used as a general guideline and not a rigid formula for designing and enriching jobs. The model should also be complemented by other factors and approaches that can enhance the employee's motivation, satisfaction and performance.

- Decision making in SPM can also be influenced by various factors, such as the type of product, the size and complexity of the project, the stakeholders involved, the available resources, the uncertainty and risk, and the time pressure.
- Decision making in SPM can be based on different sources of information, such as data, research, intuition, and experience.
  - Data and research are objective and evidence-based information that can be collected and analyzed to support decision making. For example, data on customer needs, market trends, competitor analysis, and product performance can be used to guide strategic and tactical decisions.
  - Intuition and experience are subjective and personal information that can be derived from the decision maker's own knowledge, judgment, and feelings. For example, intuition and experience can be used to cope with uncertainty, ambiguity, and complexity in operational decisions.
- Decision making in SPM can be performed by different agents, such as individuals, groups, or algorithms.
  - Individual decision making is when the decision maker acts alone, based on his or her own information, preferences, and criteria. For example, a product manager may make individual decisions on the product features, design, and pricing.
  - Group decision making is when the decision maker acts with others, based on the information, preferences, and criteria of the group members. For example, a product team may make group decisions on the product roadmap, release plan, and quality assurance.
  - Algorithmic decision making is when the decision maker acts with the help of a computer program, based on the information, preferences, and criteria of the algorithm. For example, a product manager may use an algorithm to optimize the product portfolio, prioritize the product backlog, or allocate the product resources.
- Decision making in SPM can be evaluated by different measures, such as the effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction of the decision outcomes.
  - Effectiveness is the degree to which the decision outcomes meet the desired objectives and criteria. For example, a decision outcome can be effective if it increases the customer satisfaction, market share, and revenue of the software product.
  - Efficiency is the degree to which the decision outcomes are achieved with the optimal use of resources and time. For example, a decision outcome can be efficient if it reduces the cost, effort, and duration of the software product development.
  - Satisfaction is the degree to which the decision outcomes are acceptable and pleasing to the decision maker and the stakeholders. For example, a decision outcome can be satisfying if it aligns with the values, expectations, and preferences of the product manager, the product team, and the customers.



### Organizational Structures in SPM

Organizational structures are the ways in which an organization arranges its people, roles, and responsibilities to achieve its goals and objectives. Different organizational structures can have different impacts on the performance, communication, and coordination of projects and programs. In this section, we will discuss some of the common types of organizational structures in SPM (Supplier Performance Management) and their advantages and disadvantages.

- **Functional/Hierarchical Structure**: This is a traditional structure where the organization is divided into functional units or departments, such as marketing, finance, production, etc. Each unit has its own manager and staff, and they report to a higher-level manager or executive. Projects and programs are usually managed within the functional units, and the project manager has limited authority and resources. This structure is suitable for organizations that have stable and predictable environments, and that focus on efficiency and specialization. However, it can also create silos, conflicts, and poor communication across different units, and it can limit the flexibility and innovation of the organization. 

- **Matrix Structure**: This is a hybrid structure where the organization combines the functional and project-based structures. In this structure, the project manager and the functional manager share the authority and responsibility for the project team, and the team members report to both managers. There are different types of matrix structures, such as weak, balanced, and strong, depending on the degree of power and influence of the project manager. This structure is suitable for organizations that have dynamic and complex environments, and that need to balance the functional and project objectives. However, it can also create role ambiguity, power struggles, and duplication of work for the team members and managers. 

- **RACI Structure**: This is a tool or technique that can be used to clarify the roles and responsibilities of the project team and stakeholders. RACI stands for Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed, and it is a matrix that shows who is responsible for performing the tasks, who is accountable for the outcomes, who is consulted for input or feedback, and who is informed of the progress or results. This structure is suitable for organizations that have multiple projects and programs, and that need to improve the communication and coordination among the project team and stakeholders. However, it can also create confusion, overlap, or gaps if the RACI matrix is not well-defined, updated, or communicated. 

- **Agile Structure**: This is a flexible and adaptive structure that is based on the principles and values of agile project management. In this structure, the project team is self-organizing, cross-functional, and collaborative, and they work in short iterations or sprints to deliver incremental and valuable products or services. The project manager acts as a facilitator or coach, and the customer or user is actively involved in the project. This structure is suitable for organizations that have uncertain and changing environments, and that need to deliver fast and high-quality solutions. However, it can also create challenges, such as managing dependencies, scaling up, and aligning with the organizational strategy and culture.



### Dispersed and Virtual Teams in SPM

- Dispersed and virtual teams are groups of people who work together on common projects across different locations, time zones, cultures, and organizational boundaries  .
- Dispersed and virtual teams use information and communication technologies (ICT) to coordinate their tasks, share information, and collaborate effectively .
- Dispersed and virtual teams have some advantages over co-located teams, such as:
  - Access to a larger and more diverse pool of talent, skills, and expertise .
  - Reduced costs of travel, relocation, and office space .
  - Increased flexibility and autonomy for team members to manage their work-life balance .
  - Enhanced creativity and innovation due to exposure to different perspectives and cultures .
- Dispersed and virtual teams also face some challenges, such as:
  - Communication difficulties due to language barriers, cultural differences, and lack of non-verbal cues .
  - Coordination problems due to time differences, task interdependence, and unclear roles and responsibilities .
  - Trust and relationship issues due to limited social interaction, low visibility, and potential conflicts .
  - Motivation and performance issues due to isolation, distraction, and lack of feedback and recognition .
- To manage dispersed and virtual teams effectively, project managers need to:
  - Select team members based on their skills, experience, and suitability for remote work.
  - Establish clear and realistic goals, expectations, and norms for the team.
  - Choose appropriate ICT tools and platforms to facilitate communication and collaboration.
  - Provide regular and constructive feedback and recognition to team members.
  - Foster trust and cohesion among team members through social and emotional support.
  - Monitor and evaluate team progress and performance using relevant metrics and indicators.
  - Resolve any conflicts or issues that arise in a timely and respectful manner.
- Dispersed and virtual teams can be more efficient than co-located teams at successfully completing projects, but they also tend to persist with failing projects longer than co-located teams. Therefore, project managers need to be aware of the potential risks and benefits of dispersed and virtual teams, and adapt their management strategies accordingly .



### Communication Genres in SPM

Communication genres are the methods of communication that are selected and developed to deal with particular needs for project coordination. They are the forms and formats of communication that are appropriate for different purposes, audiences, and situations in software project management (SPM). Communication genres can be classified into four main types: verbal, written, visual, and digital.

- Verbal communication is the use of spoken words to convey information, ideas, opinions, or emotions. Verbal communication can be formal or informal, depending on the context and the relationship between the speakers. Verbal communication can be effective for immediate feedback, clarification, persuasion, or motivation. Some examples of verbal communication genres in SPM are meetings, presentations, phone calls, interviews, and negotiations.
- Written communication is the use of written words to communicate information, ideas, opinions, or emotions. Written communication can be formal or informal, depending on the purpose, audience, and tone of the message. Written communication can be effective for documentation, record-keeping, analysis, or evaluation. Some examples of written communication genres in SPM are proposals, reports, emails, memos, letters, and contracts.
- Visual communication is the use of images, symbols, graphs, charts, diagrams, or other visual elements to communicate information, ideas, opinions, or emotions. Visual communication can be used to complement or supplement verbal or written communication, or to convey complex or abstract concepts in a simple or appealing way. Visual communication can be effective for illustration, comparison, contrast, or emphasis. Some examples of visual communication genres in SPM are flowcharts, wireframes, mockups, prototypes, screenshots, and infographics.
- Digital communication is the use of electronic devices, platforms, or networks to communicate information, ideas, opinions, or emotions. Digital communication can be synchronous or asynchronous, depending on the time and availability of the communicators. Digital communication can be effective for collaboration, coordination, sharing, or dissemination. Some examples of digital communication genres in SPM are video conferencing, instant messaging, social media, blogs, wikis, and podcasts.

Communication genres are not fixed or rigid, but rather dynamic and adaptable. They can be modified, combined, or customized according to the specific needs and preferences of the project stakeholders. Communication genres can also evolve over time, as new technologies, trends, or standards emerge. Therefore, software project managers and team members should be aware of the communication genres available to them, and choose the most suitable ones for their project goals and contexts.



### Communication Plans in SPM

A communication plan is a document that describes how an organization or a project will communicate with its stakeholders, such as customers, employees, partners, and funders. A communication plan typically includes the following elements:

- **Communication objectives:** The specific goals and outcomes of the communication activities, such as increasing awareness, building trust, or influencing behavior.
- **Communication audience:** The groups or individuals who will receive the communication messages, such as internal or external stakeholders, or specific segments or personas.
- **Communication channels:** The methods or tools that will be used to deliver the communication messages, such as email, social media, website, or face-to-face meetings.
- **Communication messages:** The key points or information that will be communicated to the audience, such as the vision, mission, values, or benefits of the organization or the project.
- **Communication frequency:** The timing and frequency of the communication activities, such as daily, weekly, monthly, or quarterly.
- **Communication responsibilities:** The roles and tasks of the communication team or the individuals who will be involved in the communication activities, such as planning, creating, delivering, or evaluating the communication messages.
- **Communication budget:** The estimated cost and resources that will be allocated for the communication activities, such as staff time, materials, or equipment.
- **Communication evaluation:** The methods or metrics that will be used to measure the effectiveness and impact of the communication activities, such as surveys, feedback, or analytics.

A communication plan is an essential part of strategic performance management (SPM), which is a process that guides an organization's leadership in designing and revising a system of strategic performance management. Communication is key to the success of any change initiative, as it helps to:

- Align the organization's vision, mission, and goals with the stakeholders' expectations and needs.
- Engage and motivate the stakeholders to participate and contribute to the organization's or the project's success.
- Inform and educate the stakeholders about the organization's or the project's progress, achievements, and challenges.
- Collaborate and communicate with the stakeholders to solicit feedback, input, and support for the organization's or the project's improvement and innovation.
