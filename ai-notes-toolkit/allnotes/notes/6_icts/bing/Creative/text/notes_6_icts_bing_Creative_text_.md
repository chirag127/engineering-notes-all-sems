### State in Ancient India

- The state in ancient India was a social organization with political power that ensured peace, order and happiness.
- The origin of the state in ancient India is not clear, but there are three main theories: social contract theory, divine origin theory and organic theory.
- Social contract theory: This theory suggests that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among the people to surrender some of their rights and freedoms to a ruler or a group of rulers in exchange for protection and welfare.
- Divine origin theory: This theory claims that the state was established by God or gods, who chose a king or a dynasty to rule over the people. The king was considered as the representative of God or gods on earth and had absolute authority and legitimacy.
- Organic theory: This theory views the state as a natural and inevitable outcome of the growth and development of human society. The state was seen as a living organism that evolved from the family, clan, tribe and village to a larger and more complex political entity.
- The types of states in ancient India were mainly monarchy, republic and oligarchy.
- Monarchy: This was the most common and prevalent type of state in ancient India. The king was the source of all power and authority and was assisted by ministers and provincial governors. The king was also the supreme judge, lawgiver and commander-in-chief of the army.
- Republic: This was a type of state where the people or their representatives had a say in the governance and administration of the state. The republics were usually formed by clans or tribes that had a common ancestry and culture. The republics had a council of elders or an assembly of citizens that elected or appointed the chief executive and other officials.
- Oligarchy: This was a type of state where a few powerful individuals or groups controlled the state and its resources. The oligarchs were usually wealthy landowners, merchants or military leaders who had influence and authority over the people. The oligarchs often competed or cooperated with each other for power and wealth.
- The earliest known state in ancient India was the Indus Valley Civilization, also known as the Harappan Civilization, that flourished from 3300 to 1300 BCE. It was a highly advanced and urbanized civilization that had a centralized administration, standardized weights and measures, seal carving, metallurgy, trade and agriculture.
- The later states in ancient India were influenced by the Vedic culture, which emerged around 1500 BCE. The Vedic texts, such as the Vedas, the Upanishads and the Mahabharata, contain references to various kingdoms, dynasties and empires that ruled over different regions of India. Some of the prominent states in ancient India were the Magadha Empire, the Mauryan Empire, the Gupta Empire, the Kushan Empire and the Chola Empire.



#### Theories of origin of State in India

The origin of state in India is a subject of debate among scholars of political science and history. There are different theories that attempt to explain how the state emerged and evolved in ancient India. Some of the prominent theories are:

- **Social Contract Theory**: This theory holds that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among individuals who gave up some of their natural rights and freedoms in exchange for security and order. The state was seen as a human creation that served the common good of the people. This theory is found in some ancient texts such as the Mahabharata, the Arthashastra and the Manusmriti .
- **Divine Origin Theory**: This theory asserts that the state was established by the will of God or gods who chose a king or a dynasty to rule over the people. The king was considered as the representative of God or gods on earth and had a sacred duty to uphold the divine law and order. The state was seen as a divine institution that derived its legitimacy from the religious authority of the king. This theory is found in some ancient texts such as the Vedas, the Ramayana and the Puranas .
- **Organic Theory**: This theory suggests that the state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the social and economic development of human society. The state was seen as a living organism that grew and changed according to the needs and aspirations of the people. The state was a product of the historical and cultural evolution of the people and reflected their collective identity and values. This theory is found in some ancient texts such as the Upanishads, the Dharmashastras and the Buddhist scriptures .

These theories are not mutually exclusive and may have influenced each other in different ways. They also have some common elements such as the recognition of the importance of law and order, the role of the king and the people, and the ethical and moral dimensions of the state. The theories of origin of state in India provide a rich and diverse perspective on the nature and function of the state in ancient India.



#### Evolutionary Theory of origin of State

- The Evolutionary Theory of origin of State is the theory that explains the state as the product of historical and social evolution over a long period of time  .
- According to this theory, the state is not a sudden or artificial creation, but a natural and gradual development of human society from simple to complex forms of organization  .
- The Evolutionary Theory traces the origin of the state from the primitive stages of human society, such as the family, the clan, the tribe, the village, the city, and the nation, and shows how the state emerged as a result of various factors, such as population growth, economic needs, social differentiation, warfare, religion, and culture   .
- The Evolutionary Theory is regarded as the most scientific and convincing theory of origin of the state, as it is based on historical evidence and anthropological research, and it accounts for the diversity and complexity of the state in different times and places    .
- The Evolutionary Theory also helps to understand the functions and purposes of the state, as it shows the state to be a response to the changing needs and challenges of human society, and a means of achieving social order, justice, and progress    .



#### Force Theory of Origin of State

- The force theory of origin of state is a theory that proposes that the state emerged from the use of force by one person or a small group of people over a larger population in a certain territory  .
- According to this theory, the state is a result of war, conquest, or domination by the strong over the weak .
- The force theory implies that the state is based on coercion, violence, and power, and that the people have no say in the formation or governance of the state.
- Some examples of states that are said to have originated from the force theory are the ancient empires of Egypt, Babylon, Assyria, Persia, Greece, Rome, and China, as well as some modern states such as Nazi Germany, Soviet Union, and North Korea  .
- The force theory has been criticized for being too simplistic, unrealistic, and unethical, as it ignores the role of consent, cooperation, and social contract in the origin and development of the state .



#### Mystical Theory of Origin of State

- The mystical theory of origin of state is also known as the **divine origin theory** or the **theory of divine right of kings** .
- The exponents of this theory believe that the state did not come into being by any effort of man, but by the **will of God** .
- According to this theory, the **king** or the **ruler** of the state is the **representative** or the **vicegerent** of God on earth, and therefore has **absolute authority** and **infallibility** .
- The subjects of the state have the **duty** to **obey** and **revere** the king as the **divine** or the **chosen** one .
- The proponents of this theory include **ancient** and **medieval** thinkers and rulers, such as **Plato**, **Aristotle**, **Augustine**, **Aquinas**, **James I**, **Louis XIV**, etc .
- The main **arguments** in favor of this theory are:

  - It provides a **simple** and **clear** explanation of the origin and nature of the state.
  - It gives a **strong** and **stable** basis for the **legitimacy** and **continuity** of the state.
  - It fosters a **sense** of **loyalty**, **devotion**, and **morality** among the people.
  - It prevents **rebellion**, **anarchy**, and **chaos** by discouraging any **challenge** or **criticism** of the ruler.

- The main **criticisms** of this theory are:

  - It is based on **faith** and **superstition**, not on **reason** and **evidence** .
  - It contradicts the **historical** and **empirical** facts of the origin and development of the state .
  - It ignores the **diversity** and **complexity** of the political and social phenomena .
  - It justifies the **tyranny**, **oppression**, and **exploitation** of the ruler over the people .
  - It violates the **rights**, **freedom**, and **dignity** of the people .

- The mystical theory of origin of state is now **rejected** and **discredited** by most modern political thinkers and scholars, who prefer more **scientific** and **rational** theories, such as the **historical** or **evolutionary** theory  .



#### Contract Theory of Origin of State

- The contract theory of origin of state is a theory that explains how the state or the society came into being by a voluntary agreement or contract among individuals or groups of people.
- The contract theory assumes that there was a state of nature before the state, where people lived in natural conditions without any law or authority, and faced problems of insecurity, violence, and disorder.
- The contract theory also assumes that people are rational and self-interested, and that they have natural rights to life, liberty, and property, which they want to protect and enjoy.
- The contract theory proposes that people decided to leave the state of nature and enter into a social contract, where they agreed to give up some of their natural rights and obey a common authority or government, in exchange for protection, peace, and order.
- The contract theory has different versions and variations, depending on the views of different thinkers and philosophers, such as Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, and Immanuel Kant.
- The contract theory has been influential and controversial in the history of political thought, as it provides a basis for the legitimacy, authority, and obligation of the state and the government, as well as the rights and duties of the citizens.



### Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

- State formation in ancient India is the process of emergence and consolidation of political entities that exercised authority and control over a territory and population.
- State formation is linked with the growth and spread of agriculture, trade, industry and urbanisation, which generated surplus and enabled the creation and appropriation of resources.
- The stages of state formation in ancient India can be broadly divided into four phases: the Vedic period, the Mahajanapada period, the Mauryan period and the post-Mauryan period.

#### The Vedic Period (c. 1500-600 BCE)

- The Vedic period is the earliest phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the arrival and settlement of the Indo-Aryan speakers in the north-western part of the subcontinent.
- The Vedic society was organised into tribes (jana) and clans (vish), which were led by chiefs (raja) and elders (sabha and samiti). The tribes were often engaged in conflicts and alliances with each other over land, cattle and resources.
- The Vedic state was based on kinship, ritual and sacrifice, and had a limited territorial scope and administrative apparatus. The raja was mainly a war leader and a patron of priests, and did not have absolute authority or a permanent bureaucracy.
- The Vedic state gradually evolved into a more complex and hierarchical polity, with the emergence of larger territorial units (janapada), the differentiation of social classes (varna), the codification of laws (dharma) and the expansion of trade and urbanisation.

#### The Mahajanapada Period (c. 600-300 BCE)

- The Mahajanapada period is the second phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the formation and consolidation of sixteen major kingdoms or republics (mahajanapada) in northern and central India.
- The Mahajanapada states were more centralised and organised than the Vedic states, with a well-defined territory, a standing army, a regular taxation system, a coinage system and a network of roads and trade routes.
- The Mahajanapada states were also more diverse and pluralistic than the Vedic states, with different forms of governance (monarchy or oligarchy), different religious traditions (Vedic, Jain, Buddhist or Ajivika) and different cultural and linguistic identities.
- The Mahajanapada states were often in competition and conflict with each other for supremacy and resources, which led to the rise of powerful and ambitious rulers, such as Bimbisara of Magadha, Ajatashatru of Magadha, Prasenjit of Kosala and Udayana of Vatsa.

#### The Mauryan Period (c. 300-185 BCE)

- The Mauryan period is the third phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the establishment and expansion of the first pan-Indian empire by Chandragupta Maurya and his successors, Ashoka and Bindusara.
- The Mauryan empire was the largest and most powerful state in ancient India, extending from Afghanistan to Bengal and from Kashmir to Karnataka. It had a centralised and bureaucratic administration, a professional and disciplined army, a uniform and standardised coinage system, a well-developed infrastructure and a cosmopolitan culture.
- The Mauryan empire was also the most diverse and tolerant state in ancient India, with a policy of religious and cultural pluralism, as exemplified by Ashoka's edicts, which promoted dhamma (moral law), ahimsa (non-violence), welfare (sukha) and harmony (samavaya) among all beings.
- The Mauryan empire declined and disintegrated after the death of Ashoka, due to internal rebellions, external invasions and economic and political instability.

#### The Post-Mauryan Period (c. 185 BCE-300 CE)

- The post-Mauryan period is the fourth and final phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the emergence and coexistence of multiple regional and local states in different parts of the subcontinent.
- The post-Mauryan states were smaller and weaker than the Mauryan empire, but more diverse and dynamic, with different political, economic, social and cultural features. Some of the prominent post-Mauryan states were the Sunga, Kanva, Satavahana, Kushana, Shaka, Indo-Greek, Indo-Parthian, Indo-Scythian and Gupta dynasties.

### Kingship in states in Ancient India

- Kingship in ancient India was a **sovereignty** over a territory by a **King** who functioned as its **protector**, a role which involved both **secular and religious power**.
- Ancient Indians believed that the Kingship arose out of **military requirement**. The Vedic literature has some speculations about the origin of kingship. A king must be a capable general whose leadership is recognized by all.
- There were three main theories of origin of state in ancient India: **divine origin theory**, **social contract theory**, and **organic theory**.
  - Divine origin theory: This theory of origin of kingship as well as the state was not widely acclaimed in the ancient Indian polity. The king, according to this theory, was a subordinate to law, which was made by the society and not him.
  - Social contract theory: This theory of origin of kingship as well as the state was more popular in the ancient Indian polity. The king, according to this theory, was a representative of the people who had voluntarily surrendered their rights and powers to him for the sake of security and order.
  - Organic theory: This theory of origin of kingship as well as the state was based on the analogy of the human body. The king, according to this theory, was the head of the state, which was composed of various organs and limbs, each performing a specific function for the welfare of the whole.
- The ideology and symbolism of kingship have played an important role in Hinduism throughout its history, an influence that continues into the present day. The two great epic texts, the **Mahābhārata** and **Rāmāyaṇa**, are narratives of kingship, and the tensions and ambiguities inherent to the role of the king are fundamental to their plots and themes.
- The Brahmanic theory of kingship was the most influential and dominant theory of kingship in ancient India. It was based on the concept of **dharma**, or the cosmic order, which the king had to uphold and protect. The king was considered to be the **incarnation of a god**, or the **deputy of a god**, who had the divine sanction and authority to rule.



### Council of Ministers in Ancient India

- The council of ministers was a body of advisers who assisted the king in the administration of the state.
- The council of ministers was known by different names, such as Mantri-pariṣad, Mantri-maṇḍala, or Mantri-gaṇa  .
- The council of ministers was composed of various types of ministers, such as the Prime Minister (Mantrimukhya or Mahāmantrī), the Chief Justice (Prādhyāpaka or Prādhyakṣa), the Treasurer (Sannidhāta or Kośādhyakṣa), the Commander-in-Chief (Senāpati or Senānī), the Foreign Minister (Dūta or Sandhivigrahika), and others.
- The council of ministers was selected by the king based on their qualifications, character, and loyalty. The ministers were expected to be well-versed in the scriptures, law, politics, economics, and military affairs. They were also expected to be honest, brave, intelligent, and devoted to the king and the welfare of the people .
- The council of ministers had various functions, such as advising the king on important matters, executing the king's orders, supervising the administration of various departments, maintaining law and order, collecting revenue, conducting diplomacy, and leading the army .
- The council of ministers was not a mere rubber stamp of the king, but had some degree of autonomy and influence. The ministers could express their opinions freely and frankly, and sometimes even oppose the king's views. The king was expected to consult the council of ministers before taking any major decision, and respect their consensus. The council of ministers could also act as a check and balance on the king's power, and prevent him from becoming tyrannical or negligent .



### Administration in Ancient India

- Administration in ancient India refers to the structures, procedures and behaviors that the ruling dynasties or emperors used to govern their respective territories and people.
- Administration in ancient India can be traced back to the Indus Valley civilization, which had a systematic and well-organized government in the cities of Mohenjodaro and Harappa.
- Administration in ancient India varied according to the different periods, regions and cultures, but some common features can be identified, such as:
  - The king was the head of administration and the supreme authority in matters of law, justice, religion, defense and taxation. The king was assisted by ministers, councillors, generals and officials who advised him and carried out his orders .
  - The state was divided into provinces and districts for administrative convenience and local governance. The provinces were governed by governors or viceroys appointed by the king, while the districts were headed by local chiefs or officers who collected revenue and maintained law and order.
  - The administration was based on the principles of dharma (duty, righteousness, morality) and artha (wealth, power, policy). The king and his officials were expected to uphold dharma and protect the interests of the people, while also pursuing artha and expanding the territory and resources of the state.
  - The administration was influenced by the religious and philosophical traditions of ancient India, such as Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and others. The king was often regarded as a representative of God or a divine incarnation, and was expected to perform rituals and sacrifices to please the deities and ensure the welfare of the state.
  - The administration was also influenced by the social and economic conditions of ancient India, such as the caste system, the varna system, the guild system, the village system and others. The administration had to balance the rights and duties of the different classes and groups of society, and also promote trade, agriculture, industry and education.



### Political Ideals in Ancient India and their relevance in modern times

- Ancient India had a rich tradition of political thought and practice that spanned from the Vedic period to the medieval period.

Democracy in ancient India was not based on universal suffrage, but on the principle of consent and consensus.
  - Dharma: Dharma is a Sanskrit term that means duty, law, morality, justice, righteousness, or order. It is the basis of the Vedic ideals and the Indian political thought. Dharma aims at the welfare of all beings and the harmony of the cosmos. The king was the upholder and protector of dharma. He had to follow the dharma himself and ensure that his subjects also followed it. He had to rule with compassion, wisdom, and impartiality.   - Artha: Artha is another Sanskrit term that means wealth, material prosperity, power, or polity. It is one of the four goals of human life, along with dharma, kama (pleasure), and moksha (liberation). Artha deals with the practical aspects of governance, such as administration, taxation, diplomacy, war, law, and trade. Artha also implies the pursuit of self-interest and rationality, as well as the balance between the ends and the means.
  - Ahimsa: Ahimsa is another Sanskrit term that means non-violence, non-injury, or non-harming. It is one of the cardinal virtues of the Indian religions, such as Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism. Ahimsa implies the respect for all forms of life and the avoidance of harm or injury to any living being, either by thought, word, or deed. Ahimsa also implies the promotion of peace, harmony, and compassion in the world. Ahimsa was the basis of the non-violent resistance movements led by Mahatma Gandhi and other freedom fighters against the British colonial rule in India.

### Conditions of the Welfare of Societies in Ancient India

- The concept of social welfare in ancient India was based on the idea of **duty** as opposed to **rights** . The duty of an individual was determined by his or her **dharma**, which was the moral and ethical code of conduct for different classes and stages of life .
- The dharma of the king or the ruler was called **rajadharma**, which included the responsibility of ensuring the welfare of the people, maintaining justice and order, protecting the weak and the oppressed, and promoting the common good . The king was considered as the representative of God on earth and was expected to rule with benevolence and wisdom .
- The welfare of the society was also dependent on the cooperation and harmony among the four **varnas** or social classes: the Brahmins (priests and scholars), the Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), the Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and the Shudras (servants and laborers). Each varna had its own dharma and privileges, and was expected to perform its duties faithfully and respectfully.
- The society was also divided into four **ashramas** or stages of life: the Brahmacharya (student), the Grihastha (householder), the Vanaprastha (retired), and the Sannyasa (renunciant). Each ashrama had its own dharma and obligations, and was meant to help the individual attain spiritual and moral growth.
- The welfare of the individual and the society was also influenced by the performance of various **sanskaras** or sacraments, which were rites of passage that marked the important events and transitions in one's life, such as birth, initiation, marriage, death, etc.. These sanskaras were meant to purify, sanctify, and elevate the individual and the society.
- The welfare of the society was also enhanced by the role of the **family**, which was the basic unit of social organization and the source of moral and emotional support. The family was patriarchal and joint, and the elders were respected and obeyed. The family was also responsible for the education and upbringing of the children, and the transmission of the cultural and religious values.
- The welfare of the society was also affected by the **marriage** system, which was considered as a sacred and lifelong bond between a man and a woman. The marriage was arranged by the parents or the elders, and the consent of the bride and the groom was not necessary. The marriage was also based on the compatibility of the varnas, the gotras (clans), and the horoscopes.
- The welfare of the society was also influenced by the **position of women**, which varied according to the time period, the region, the class, and the religion. In general, women were subordinate to men and had limited rights and freedoms. However, some women also enjoyed high status and respect as queens, warriors, poets, teachers, etc.. Women were also expected to be loyal, virtuous, and devoted to their husbands and families.
- The welfare of the society was also reflected by the **dresses and ornaments** of the people, which indicated their class, status, occupation, and taste. The people wore simple and comfortable clothes made of cotton, silk, or wool, and adorned themselves with various kinds of jewelry made of gold, silver, pearls, gems, etc.. The dresses and ornaments also varied according to the seasons, the occasions, and the regions.
- The welfare of the society was also manifested by the **food habits** of the people, which were influenced by the climate, the geography, the religion, and the culture. The people ate a variety of cereals, pulses, fruits, vegetables, milk, honey, etc., and avoided meat, alcohol, and intoxicants. The food habits also differed according to the varnas, the ashramas, and the regions.

### The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

- The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) is a political concept given by Kautilya in his treatise Arthashastra, which deals with the art of governance and statecraft .
- The seven limbs are: 
  - King (Svamin): The king is the supreme authority and the head of the state. He is responsible for the peace, justice and stability of the state. He is independent and has the power to appoint and dismiss his ministers and officials. He is also the commander-in-chief of the army and the protector of the people .
  - Amatya (Minister or Bureaucrat): The amatya is the second in command and the chief adviser of the king. He assists the king in administration, policy-making and diplomacy. He is selected on the basis of merit, loyalty and experience. He supervises the various departments and officials of the state .
  - Janapada (Territory or Population): The janapada is the third limb of the state and consists of the land and the people living in it. The land should be fertile, well-watered and rich in natural resources. The people should be loyal, obedient and prosperous. The king should protect the janapada from external and internal enemies and ensure its welfare .
  - Durga (Fort or Capital): The durga is the fourth limb of the state and refers to the fortified city or the capital of the state. It is the center of administration, commerce and culture. It should be well-built, well-guarded and well-equipped. It should have strong walls, gates, towers and moats. It should also have ample provisions, weapons and manpower .
  - Kosa (Treasure or Revenue): The kosa is the fifth limb of the state and refers to the financial resources and the income of the state. It is derived from various sources such as taxes, fines, tributes, trade, mines, etc. It should be sufficient, regular and increasing. It should be used for the maintenance of the state, the welfare of the people and the expansion of the territory .
  - Danda (Coercive Authority or Army): The danda is the sixth limb of the state and refers to the military force and the law enforcement of the state. It is the instrument of the king to maintain order, justice and security. It consists of four branches: infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants. It should be well-trained, well-equipped and well-disciplined. It should also be loyal, brave and patriotic  .
  - Mitra (Ally or Friend): The mitra is the seventh and the last limb of the state and refers to the friendly and supportive relations with other states. It is based on mutual trust, respect and benefit. It is achieved through diplomacy, treaties and alliances. It helps the state to counter the enemies, expand the territory and enhance the prestige .
- The seven limbs of the state are interdependent and interrelated. They should be balanced, harmonious and efficient. They should work together for the common good of the state. Kautilya considered the king as the most important limb, followed by the amatya, the janapada, the durga, the kosa, the danda and the mitra  .

#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

- Purusārtha (Sanskrit: पुरुषार्थ) literally means "object of human pursuit" or "human goal".
- It is a key concept in Hinduism, and refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. The four purusārthas are Dharma (righteousness, moral values), Artha (prosperity, economic values), Kama (pleasure, love, psychological values) and Moksha (liberation, spiritual values).
- The concept of purusārtha is a fundamental principle of social ethics in ancient India. It is meant to set an ideal objective and also to convey the right means to achieve them. The four purusārthas are not mutually exclusive, but rather interdependent and complementary. They are also flexible and adaptable to the individual's stage of life, social class, and personal preference.  
- The concept of purusārtha is also closely related to the concept of karma, which means action or deed. Karma is the law of cause and effect that governs the cycle of rebirth (samsara) in Hinduism. According to karma, every action has a consequence, either in this life or in the next. The purusārthas are the means to achieve the desired consequences and to avoid the undesired ones. By following the purusārthas, one can accumulate good karma and attain moksha, the ultimate goal of liberation from samsara. 
- The concept of purusārtha also reflects the diversity and complexity of the ancient Indian society, which was divided into four classes (varnas) and four stages of life (ashramas). Each class and stage had its own duties and responsibilities, as well as its own scope and limitations. The purusārthas provided a framework for the individual to balance the different aspects of life and to fulfill the social and spiritual obligations.

- The varnāshrama system is not a system of oppression or discrimination, but a system of cooperation and mutual respect. It recognizes the diversity and interdependence of the human society, and the need for balance and moderation in life.



#### Āshrama or the Stages of Life in Society in Ancient India

- Āshrama (Sanskrit: आश्रम) is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras.
- The Āshrama system is one facet of the Dharma concept in Hinduism, which prescribes the duties and responsibilities of an individual according to his or her age, gender, occupation and social status .
- The four āshramas are: 
  - Brahmacharya (student), marked by chastity, devotion, and obedience to one’s teacher. This stage lasts until the age of 25, and involves learning the scriptures, the arts, the sciences, and the skills necessary for one's future profession.
  - Gṛhastha (householder), requiring marriage, the begetting of children, sustaining one’s family and helping support priests and holy men, and fulfillment of duties toward gods and ancestors. This stage lasts until the age of 50, and involves performing the rituals, the sacrifices, the charity, and the social service prescribed by the scriptures.
  - Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller), involving gradual withdrawal from worldly affairs, detachment from material possessions, and increased focus on spiritual pursuits. This stage lasts until the age of 75, and involves living in a forest hut or a hermitage, practicing meditation, penance, and self-discipline, and seeking the guidance of a guru or a sage.
  - Sannyasa (renunciate), involving complete renunciation of worldly life, attachment, and identity, and dedication to the quest for liberation (moksha) from the cycle of birth and death (samsara). This stage lasts until the end of one's life, and involves wandering as a mendicant, living on alms, studying the scriptures, and teaching the truth to those who seek it.
- The Āshrama system was meant to provide a framework for the spiritual and moral development of an individual, as well as for the social and economic welfare of the society. It was also meant to reflect the natural stages of human life, and to harmonize the individual's goals with the cosmic order.

#### The representation of Women in Historical traditions in Society in Ancient India

- Women in ancient India had a complex and varied status depending on the time period, the region, the religion, and the caste they belonged to.
- During the Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1500 BCE), women were worshiped as mother goddesses and enjoyed equal honor and freedom as men. They could participate in war, gymnastics, public activities, decision-making, and education.
- During the Vedic period (1500-500 BCE), women also held an honored place in society and were respected as wives, mothers, and scholars. They could perform religious rituals, compose hymns, and study the Vedas. Some of the famous women sages of this period were Gargi, Maitreyi, Lopamudra, and Ghosha  .
- During the later Vedic period (500-200 BCE), the status of women began to decline due to the rise of patriarchy, caste system, and Brahmanical orthodoxy. Women were gradually excluded from education, property rights, and public life. They were subjected to the authority of their fathers, husbands, and sons. They had to follow the norms of chastity, obedience, and loyalty. They were also expected to practice sati (self-immolation on the husband's funeral pyre) or widowhood  .
- During the Mauryan period (321-185 BCE), the status of women improved slightly due to the influence of Buddhism and Jainism, which advocated non-violence, equality, and compassion. Women could join the monastic orders, preach the doctrines, and attain salvation. Some of the prominent women figures of this period were Chandragupta Maurya's chief queen Durdhara, Ashoka's daughter Sanghamitra, and the Buddhist nun Dhammawati  .
- During the Gupta period (320-550 CE), the status of women deteriorated further due to the revival of Hinduism and the codification of laws such as the Manusmriti, which prescribed the ideal duties and behavior of women. Women were confined to the domestic sphere, denied education and independence, and subjected to various restrictions and regulations. They were also exploited and oppressed by the upper castes and classes. However, some women still excelled in the fields of literature, art, and science. Some of the notable women of this period were the poet Kalidasa's wife Malavika, the mathematician Lilavati, and the queen Prabhavatigupta  .
- In conclusion, women in ancient India had a diverse and dynamic role in society, which changed according to the historical, cultural, and religious factors. They were not passive or homogeneous, but active and heterogeneous agents of change and continuity. They contributed to the development and enrichment of the Indian civilization in various ways.



#### Challenges faced by Women in Society in Ancient India

- Women in ancient India had different roles and rights depending on the time period, the region, the religion, and the caste they belonged to. Generally, they faced more challenges and oppression as time went by and patriarchy became stronger.
- Some of the challenges faced by women in ancient India were:
  - Lack of autonomy, mobility, and social freedom. Women were expected to be dependent on their fathers, husbands, or sons for their livelihood, protection, and decision-making. They were confined to the domestic sphere and had limited access to education, property, and public life.
  - Child marriage and widowhood. Women were often married off at a very young age, sometimes even before puberty, to older men. They had no say in choosing their partners and had to bear children and serve their husbands. If their husbands died, they were either forced to commit sati (self-immolation on the funeral pyre of their husbands), or to live a life of misery and social ostracism as widows .
  - Sati, Pardha, and Devadasi systems. These were some of the abusive and oppressive practices that women had to endure in ancient India. Sati was the ritual of burning widows alive with their dead husbands, which was seen as a sign of loyalty and virtue. Pardha was the practice of veiling and secluding women from the outside world, which was seen as a sign of modesty and honor. Devadasi was the practice of dedicating young girls to temples as servants and prostitutes, which was seen as a sign of devotion and sacrifice .
  - Dasi, Niyog, and Polygamy. These were some of the other forms of exploitation and discrimination that women faced in ancient India. Dasi was the term for female slaves who were captured, bought, or sold by men. They had no rights and were subjected to sexual and physical abuse. Niyog was the practice of allowing a widow or a barren woman to have sexual relations with another man, usually a relative of her husband, to produce a child. This was seen as a way of preserving the lineage and inheritance of the husband. Polygamy was the practice of allowing a man to have more than one wife, which was seen as a sign of wealth and power  .
- Women in ancient India also had some positive aspects in their lives, such as the respect and reverence given to some goddesses, the role of some women as sages, teachers, poets, and rulers, and the influence of some religions and philosophies that advocated for women's rights and equality. However, these were exceptions rather than the norm, and the majority of women faced many challenges and hardships in their society .



#### Four-class Classification of Society in Ancient India

- The four-class classification of society in ancient India is also known as the **varna system** or the **caste system**.
- The word varna means **color** or **quality** in Sanskrit, and it was used to categorize people based on their **occupation**, **birth**, and **duty**.
- The varna system is derived from the **Vedas**, the ancient Hindu scriptures, which described four main social classes: **Brahmins**, **Kshatriyas**, **Vaishyas**, and **Shudras** .
- The **Brahmins** were the **priests** and **scholars** who performed religious rituals, taught the Vedas, and advised the rulers. They were considered the highest and purest class in the society .
- The **Kshatriyas** were the **rulers**, **administrators**, and **warriors** who protected the society from external threats, maintained law and order, and enforced justice. They were the second highest class in the society .
- The **Vaishyas** were the **cattle herders**, **agriculturists**, **artisans**, and **merchants** who produced and traded goods and services. They were the third highest class in the society .
- The **Shudras** were the **laborers** and **service providers** who performed menial and unskilled tasks for the other classes. They were the lowest and most impure class in the society .
- The varna system was **rigid** and **hereditary**, meaning that people were born into a certain class and could not change it. The classes were also **hierarchical** and **exclusive**, meaning that people of different classes could not interact or intermarry with each other.
- The varna system was also influenced by the concept of **karma** and **rebirth**, which held that people's actions in their current life determined their status in their next life. Therefore, people had to follow their **dharma**, or their moral duty, according to their class, in order to achieve **moksha**, or liberation from the cycle of birth and death.
- The varna system was a dominant feature of the social structure of ancient India, and it influenced the political, economic, religious, and cultural aspects of the society. However, it also faced criticism and challenges from various movements and thinkers, such as Buddhism, Jainism, and the Bhakti movement, which advocated for equality and social justice.



#### Slavery in Society in Ancient India

- The early history of slavery in India is contested because it depends on the translations of terms such as dasa and dasyu, which could mean either enemies or servants.
- Greek writer Megasthenes, who visited India in the 4th century BCE, stated that slavery was banned in Indian society under the Mauryan Empire.
- However, slavery was practiced in various forms in ancient India, such as debt slavery, domestic slavery, military slavery, and temple slavery.
- Slavery system in India was very mild and most of the slaves were domestic slaves who were treated as members of the family. The Dharma Shastras of the 4th century BCE were in a protesting mood against slavery. Slave trade was prohibited in the Shastras.
- Slavery existed in ancient India, where it is recorded in the Sanskrit Laws of Manu of the 1st century BCE. The institution was little documented until the British colonials in the 19th century made it an object of study because of their desire to abolish it.

### Evolution of script in India

- A script is a written symbolisation of speech. It consists of a set of symbols or characters that represent the sounds of a language.
- India has a long and rich history of writing, dating back to the third millennium BCE. India has also been a multilingual and multicultural society, with different scripts used for different languages and regions.
- The earliest known script in India is the Indus script, which was used by the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE). The Indus script is still undeciphered and its linguistic affiliation is unknown.
- The next major script in India is the Brahmi script, which was used to write the Prakrit languages, such as Pali and Magadhi, as well as Sanskrit. The Brahmi script is derived from the Aramaic script, which was brought to India by the Persian Empire in the sixth century BCE.
- The Brahmi script is the source of most modern Indian scripts, as well as the scripts of Sri Lanka, Tibet, Southeast Asia, and some parts of Central Asia. The Brahmi script evolved into different regional variants, such as Kharosthi, Gupta, Nagari, Siddham, and Grantha, depending on the local culture, geography, and writing material.
- The modern Indian scripts are classified into four main groups: the Northern group, the Southern group, the Eastern group, and the Western group. The Northern group includes Devanagari, Gujarati, Gurmukhi, and Nandinagari. The Southern group includes Kannada, Malayalam, Tamil, and Telugu. The Eastern group includes Assamese, Bengali, Odia, and Tibetan. The Western group includes Sindhi, Khojki, and Sharada.
- The modern Indian scripts have some common features, such as being alphasyllabic, meaning that each symbol represents a consonant-vowel syllable, and having a horizontal line at the top of the symbols, called the headstroke or shirorekha. The modern Indian scripts also have some differences, such as the shape and direction of the symbols, the presence or absence of vowel diacritics, and the use of conjuncts or ligatures for complex syllables.
- The evolution of script in India reflects the diversity and dynamism of the Indian culture and languages. The script is not only a means of communication, but also a marker of identity, history, and art.



### Evolution of languages in India

- India is a multilingual country with over 780 languages spoken by its people.

They are believed to be the oldest languages in India and have influenced the Indo-Aryan and the Dravidian languages.

#### Harappan Script

- Harappan script is the name given to the symbols found on the inscriptions and seals of the Indus Valley Civilisation, which flourished in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent from c. 2500 to 1900 BCE .
- Harappan script is not yet deciphered, and its underlying language(s) and meaning are unknown . Some scholars have proposed that it was a logo-syllabic system, where some symbols represented words or concepts, while others represented sounds. Others have suggested that it was a non-linguistic symbol system, used for religious or administrative purposes.
- Harappan script consists of about 400 signs, which vary in shape, size, orientation, and complexity . Some signs are pictographic, resembling objects or animals, while others are abstract or geometric. The signs are usually written from right to left, sometimes in a boustrophedon manner (alternating direction in each line) .
- Harappan script is mostly found on small objects, such as seals, tablets, pottery, and copper plates . The inscriptions are usually very short, ranging from one to 17 signs, with an average of five signs . The longest inscription, found on a signboard at Dholavira, has 10 signs. The inscriptions are often accompanied by images of animals, humans, or religious symbols .
- Harappan script is one of the oldest writing systems in the world, and a major source of information about the Indus Valley Civilisation . However, its interpretation remains a challenge for scholars, and many attempts to decipher it have been controversial or inconclusive . The discovery of a bilingual inscription or a Rosetta Stone-like object could help to unlock the secrets of Harappan script in the future .



#### Brahmi Script

- Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script.
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi .
- Brahmi script appeared as a fully developed script in the third century BCE .
- Brahmi script is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations.
- Brahmi script was already divided into regional variants at the time of the earliest surviving epigraphy around the third century BC.
- Brahmi script is thought to have been modelled on the Aramaic or Phoenician alphabets, or possibly derived from the Indus or Harappa script .
- Brahmi script consists of 33 consonants and 9 vowels, and is written from left to right.
- Brahmi script has a distinctive horizontal line at the top of each character, called the headstroke.
- Brahmi script uses diacritical marks to indicate vowel sounds, and conjuncts to indicate combinations of consonants.
- Brahmi script has several numerical symbols for 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 20, 100, and 1000.



### The Vedas

- The Vedas are a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India.
- The Vedas are composed in Vedic Sanskrit, the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the oldest scriptures of Hinduism.
- The word Veda means "knowledge" in Sanskrit, and the Vedas are thought to contain the fundamental knowledge relating to the underlying cause of, function of, and personal response to existence.
- The Vedas are divided into four collections: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda.
- The Rigveda is the oldest and most important of the Vedas, containing 1,028 hymns to various gods and natural phenomena.
- The Yajurveda contains the formulas and rituals for performing sacrifices and ceremonies.
- The Samaveda consists of musical chants and melodies for the Rigvedic hymns.
- The Atharvaveda contains spells, charms, and magical incantations for various purposes.
- The Vedas have regulated the social, legal, domestic and religious customs of Hindus up to the present day.
- The Vedas are considered to be revealed by the gods to the sages, and are therefore regarded as sacred and authoritative.



### The Upanishads

- The Upanishads are ancient Hindu scriptures that explore the nature of reality, the self, and the supreme being.
- The word Upanishad means "to sit down closely", implying the intimate relationship between the teacher and the student who receive the secret teachings.
- The Upanishads are considered the end or the essence of the Vedas, the oldest and most authoritative Hindu texts .
- There are more than 200 Upanishads, but only 13 are considered the principal or the most important ones. They are: Isha, Kena, Katha, Prashna, Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Chandogya, Brihadaranyaka, Kaushitaki, Shvetashvatara, and Maitri.
- The Upanishads contain various philosophical concepts and doctrines, such as the idea of Brahman, the ultimate reality that pervades everything; the idea of Atman, the true self or soul that is identical with Brahman; the idea of Maya, the illusion or ignorance that prevents one from realizing one's true nature; the idea of Karma, the law of cause and effect that determines one's destiny; the idea of Samsara, the cycle of birth and death that traps one in suffering; the idea of Moksha, the liberation or freedom from Samsara; and the idea of Dharma, the moral duty or righteousness that guides one's actions  .
- The Upanishads also present various methods and practices to attain Moksha, such as meditation, yoga, renunciation, ethical conduct, devotion, and self-inquiry  .
- The Upanishads have influenced many schools of Hindu philosophy, such as Vedanta, Yoga, Samkhya, Nyaya, and Mimamsa, as well as other religions and traditions, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and Neo-Vedanta .

### The Mahabharata

- The Mahabharata is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of India, along with the Ramayana .
- It is a long and complex narrative that covers the history, culture, religion, and philosophy of ancient India.
- It tells the story of the conflict between two groups of cousins, the Kauravas and the Pandavas, who fight for the throne of Hastinapura, a kingdom in northern India .
- The main plot of the Mahabharata is the Kurukshetra War, a 18-day battle that involves various kings, gods, and warriors.
- The Mahabharata also contains many sub-stories, such as the Bhagavad Gita, a dialogue between the Pandava prince Arjuna and the god Krishna, who advises him on his duty and dharma.
- The Mahabharata is considered to be both a historical account of the origins of Hinduism and a code of ethics for the faithful.
- The Mahabharata is divided into 18 books, or parvas, that contain about 74,000 verses and 1.8 million words in total.
- The Mahabharata is attributed to the sage Vyasa, who is also a character in the story. However, it is likely that the Mahabharata was composed by multiple authors over centuries.
- The Mahabharata is a rich source of literature, art, and culture for India and the world. It has been translated, adapted, and retold in various languages and media.



### Puranas

- Puranas are a genre of ancient Indian literature that contain stories, legends, myths, cosmology, genealogy, and other topics related to Hinduism    .
- Puranas are written in Sanskrit verse and are considered as the fifth Veda, after the four Vedas and the Upanishads .
- Puranas are classified into two categories: Mahapuranas (major Puranas) and Upapuranas (minor Puranas). There are 18 Mahapuranas and 18 Upapuranas, each devoted to a different deity, such as Vishnu, Shiva, Brahma, Devi, etc   .
- Puranas are not uniform in content, style, or date of composition. They vary widely in their length, scope, and quality. Some Puranas are very ancient, dating back to the 4th century BCE, while others are relatively recent, dating to the medieval or modern period  .
- Puranas are known for their symbolic and allegorical interpretation of Hindu philosophy, ethics, and cosmology. They also contain historical information, such as the dynasties of kings, the lineage of sages, the description of sacred places, etc  .
- Puranas are popular and influential sources of Hindu culture, religion, and spirituality. They are widely read, recited, and performed by Hindus of different sects, regions, and languages  .



### Buddhist Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Buddhist literature is the collection of writings that preserve the teachings and traditions of Buddhism.
- Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit are three ancient languages that were used to compose Buddhist texts in different regions and periods of history.
- Pali is a Middle Indo-Aryan language that is closely related to the language spoken by the Buddha in the 6th century BCE. It is the canonical language of Theravada Buddhism, the oldest surviving branch of Buddhism. Pali literature includes the Tipitaka (the three baskets of scriptures), the commentaries, the sub-commentaries, and the chronicles.
- Prakrit is a generic term for any Middle Indo-Aryan language that is derived from Sanskrit. There are many varieties of Prakrit, such as Sauraseni, Maharastri, Magadhi and Gandhari. Prakrit literature includes the canonical texts of Mahayana Buddhism, the heterodox sects of Buddhism, and the Jaina tradition. Prakrit literature also includes secular works such as poetry, drama, and inscriptions.
- Sanskrit is a classical Indo-Aryan language that is the primary language of Hinduism and a major literary language of India. Sanskrit literature includes the later developments of Mahayana Buddhism, such as the Mahayana sutras, the tantras, the shastras, and the biographies of Buddhist masters. Sanskrit literature also includes the works of Buddhist philosophers, poets, and scholars, such as Nagarjuna, Asvaghosa, and Dignaga.



### Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Jain literature refers to the literature of the Jain religion, which is one of the oldest and major religions of India.
- Jain literature is a vast and ancient literary tradition, which was initially transmitted orally by the teachers and disciples of Jainism.
- Jain literature is composed in various languages, such as Prakrit, Pali, Sanskrit, Apabhramsha, Hindi, Gujarati, Tamil, Kannada, and Rajasthani.
- The most important and authoritative Jain literature is the canonical literature, which consists of the sacred scriptures of the Jains, known as the Agamas or Siddhantas.
- The canonical literature is written in Ardhamagadhi, a Prakrit language that was spoken by Mahavira, the 24th and last Tirthankara (ford-maker) of Jainism.
- The canonical literature is divided into two main categories: the Angas (limbs) and the Upangas (subsidiary limbs). The Angas are 12 in number and contain the core teachings of Jainism, such as the doctrine of karma, the theory of non-violence, the code of conduct, and the cosmology. The Upangas are 12 in number and contain supplementary teachings, such as the legends of the Tirthankaras, the stories of the previous lives of Mahavira, the description of the six substances, and the classification of living beings.
- The canonical literature also includes other texts, such as the Prakirnas (miscellaneous), the Chhedasutras (disciplinary), the Mulasutras (fundamental), and the Nandisutras (introductory).
- The canonical literature was compiled and edited by the councils of Jain monks, who also added commentaries and sub-commentaries to the original texts. The first council was held at Pataliputra (modern Patna) in the 4th century BCE, the second at Mathura in the 2nd or 3rd century CE, and the third at Valabhi in Gujarat in the 5th or 6th century CE.
- The canonical literature is preserved by two main sects of Jainism: the Svetambaras (white-clad) and the Digambaras (sky-clad). The Svetambaras accept all the 45 texts of the canonical literature as authentic, while the Digambaras reject some of them and consider only 32 texts as genuine. The Digambaras also claim that some of the original texts were lost or corrupted over time, and therefore they rely more on the later works of their scholars and saints.
- Jain literature also includes non-canonical literature, which consists of various works of literature, philosophy, grammar, poetry, drama, history, biography, art, and science, written by Jain authors in different languages and periods. Some of the prominent Jain authors are Kundakunda, Umasvati, Haribhadra, Hemacandra, Jinasena, Somadeva, Banarasidas, and Jnaneshvara.
- Jain literature in Pali and Prakrit is mostly related to the canonical literature and its commentaries, as well as some works of grammar, lexicography, and poetry. Jain literature in Sanskrit is more diverse and covers a wide range of topics, such as logic, metaphysics, ethics, aesthetics, mathematics, astronomy, astrology, medicine, and alchemy. Jain literature in other languages reflects the regional and cultural influences of Jainism and its interaction with other traditions and communities.



### Kautilya's Arthashastra

- Kautilya's Arthashastra is an ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, politics, economics, military strategy, and social organization.
- It is attributed to Kautilya, also known as Chanakya, Vishnugupta, or the Prime Minister of the Mauryan Empire under Chandragupta Maurya (c. 321-c. 297 BCE).
- The Arthashastra consists of 15 books, 150 chapters, and about 6000 verses. It covers topics such as the nature and duties of the king, the structure and functions of the government, the principles of foreign policy, the methods of warfare and espionage, the laws and administration of justice, the taxation and revenue system, the welfare and protection of the people, and the ethical and moral values of the society.
- The Arthashastra is considered one of the earliest and most comprehensive works on political science and public administration in the world. It reflects the political, social, and economic conditions of ancient India, as well as the influence of other cultures and civilizations, such as Persia, Greece, and China.
- The Arthashastra is based on the concept of artha, which means material wealth, prosperity, and security. Kautilya advocates a pragmatic and rational approach to governance, with the aim of maximizing the welfare and happiness of the people, while maintaining the stability and sovereignty of the state. He also emphasizes the importance of ethics, morality, and dharma (duty) in the conduct of the ruler and the citizens.
- The Arthashastra is a source of inspiration and guidance for many modern scholars, leaders, and thinkers, who have applied its principles and insights to various fields and contexts, such as political science, economics, law, management, diplomacy, and strategy.



### Famous Sanskrit Authors in India

Sanskrit is one of the oldest and most influential languages in India. It has a rich literary tradition that spans over two millennia. Many Sanskrit authors have contributed to various genres of literature, such as poetry, drama, philosophy, grammar, and religious texts. Here are some of the famous Sanskrit authors in India:

- **Kalidasa**: He is considered to be the greatest Sanskrit poet and dramatist in India. He lived in the 4th or 5th century CE, during the Gupta dynasty. He wrote three epic poems (Mahakavyas), namely Raghuvamsa, Kumarasambhava, and Meghaduta. He also wrote three plays, namely Malavikagnimitra, Vikramorvasiya, and Abhijnanasakuntala. His works are known for their lyrical beauty, romantic charm, and cultural sophistication .
- **Bhasa**: He is one of the earliest and most prolific Sanskrit dramatists in India. He lived around the 3rd century CE, but his works were rediscovered in the 20th century. He wrote thirteen plays, mostly based on the epics Mahabharata and Ramayana. His plays are known for their dramatic intensity, psychological realism, and tragic endings. Some of his famous plays are Urubhanga, Madhyamavyayoga, and Svapnavasavadatta.
- **Bhartrihari**: He is a renowned Sanskrit poet and philosopher in India. He lived in the 5th or 6th century CE, and is said to be the brother of King Vikramaditya. He wrote three collections of verses (Satakas), namely Nitisataka, Sringarasataka, and Vairagyasataka. His verses deal with ethical, erotic, and spiritual themes, and are known for their wit, wisdom, and eloquence.
- **Banabhatta**: He is a celebrated Sanskrit poet and biographer in India. He lived in the 7th century CE, and was the court poet of King Harshavardhana. He wrote two epic poems, namely Harshacharita and Kadambari. His works are known for their complex plot, elaborate style, and vivid description of the contemporary society.
- **Gajapati Purusottama Deva**: He is a rare example of a Sanskrit poet who was also a powerful king in India. He ruled over Orissa in the 15th century CE, and was a patron of art and culture. He wrote five works, namely Muktichintamani, Gopalarchana vidhi, Nama Malika, Durogotsava, and Bhubaneswari Pujapallava. His works are devoted to the worship of Lord Krishna and Goddess Bhubaneswari.
- **Satyavrat Shastri**: He is a modern Sanskrit poet and scholar in India. He was born in 1930, and is still active in the field of Sanskrit literature. He has written two epic poems, namely Ramakirtimahakavyam and Brahattaram Bharatam. He has also written several shorter poems, plays, and essays on various topics. He is the first and only Sanskrit author to win the Jnanpith Award, India's highest literary honor, in 2009.

## Module 3- Indian Religion, Philosophy, and Practices

- India is a diverse and pluralistic country, where many religions and philosophies coexist and influence each other.
- The major religions in India are Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, Islam, and Christianity. There are also smaller communities of Jews, Zoroastrians, Baháʼís, and others.
- Hinduism is the oldest and largest religion in India, with about 80% of the population identifying as Hindus. Hinduism is a complex and diverse tradition, with many sects, schools, and practices. Hinduism is based on the Vedas, the ancient scriptures that contain hymns, rituals, and philosophy. Hinduism believes in the concepts of karma, dharma, reincarnation, and moksha (liberation from the cycle of birth and death).
- Buddhism originated in India in the 6th century BCE, when Siddhartha Gautama, known as the Buddha, attained enlightenment and taught the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path. Buddhism spread throughout Asia and beyond, and developed into various branches, such as Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana. Buddhism teaches the principles of non-violence, compassion, wisdom, and meditation. Buddhism has about 7% of the population in India, mostly in the northeastern states and among the Dalits (formerly known as untouchables).
- Jainism is another ancient religion that emerged in India around the same time as Buddhism. Jainism emphasizes the practice of ahimsa (non-violence), anekantavada (non-absolutism), and aparigraha (non-attachment). Jainism believes in the existence of jivas (souls) and ajivas (non-souls), and the possibility of attaining kevala (omniscience) and nirvana (liberation) by following the five vows of non-violence, truth, non-stealing, celibacy, and non-possession. Jainism has about 0.4% of the population in India, mostly concentrated in the western and northern states.
- Sikhism is a monotheistic religion that originated in the 15th century CE, when Guru Nanak and his nine successors, known as the Gurus, preached the message of one God, equality, service, and justice. Sikhism is based on the Guru Granth Sahib, the holy scripture that contains the teachings of the Gurus and other saints. Sikhism believes in the concepts of naam (divine name), shabad (divine word), and sangat (congregation). Sikhism has about 2% of the population in India, mostly in the state of Punjab and the national capital region of Delhi.
- Islam is the second-largest religion in India, with about 14% of the population identifying as Muslims. Islam was introduced to India by Arab traders, invaders, and Sufi saints, and flourished under various Muslim dynasties and empires. Islam is a monotheistic religion that follows the Quran, the holy book revealed to Prophet Muhammad, and the Sunnah, the example of his life and teachings. Islam believes in the concepts of tawhid (oneness of God), sharia (Islamic law), and akhirah (afterlife). Islam has many sects and schools, such as Sunni, Shia, Sufi, and Ahmadiyya.
- Christianity is the third-largest religion in India, with about 2.3% of the population identifying as Christians. Christianity was brought to India by various missionaries, such as Thomas the Apostle, Saint Francis Xavier, and William Carey. Christianity is a monotheistic religion that follows the Bible, the holy book that contains the Old and New Testaments. Christianity believes in the concepts of Trinity (Father, Son, and Holy Spirit), incarnation (God becoming human in Jesus Christ), and salvation (forgiveness of sins and eternal life). Christianity has many denominations and traditions, such as Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and Pentecostal.



### Pre-Vedic Religion in India

- Pre-Vedic religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the people who lived in the Indus Valley or Harappan civilization, before the arrival of the Indo-Aryan speakers who brought the Vedic religion to India.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was mainly based on the worship of natural forces and fertility deities, such as the Mother Goddess, the Bull, the Pashupati (Lord of Animals), and the Pipal tree.
- The Pre-Vedic religion also involved the use of seals, amulets, terracotta figurines, and fire altars, which suggest some form of ritual and magic.
- The Pre-Vedic religion did not have a written scripture, but relied on oral transmission and symbolism.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was largely replaced by the Vedic religion, which was brought by the Indo-Aryan speakers who migrated to India from the Central Asian steppes around 1500 BCE.
- The Vedic religion was based on the worship of many gods, such as Indra, Varuna, Agni, Surya, and Rudra, who represented different aspects of nature and cosmic order .
- The Vedic religion also involved the performance of sacrifices (yajna) and the recitation of hymns (mantra) from the sacred texts known as the Vedas, which are the oldest scriptures of Hinduism.
- The Vedic religion evolved over time and gave rise to various schools of thought and sects, such as the Upanishads, the Brahmanas, the Sutras, and the Vedanta.
- The Vedic religion is considered the oldest stratum of religious activity in India, and the foundation of Hinduism.



### Vedic Religion in India

- Vedic religion was the religion of the ancient Indo-European-speaking peoples who entered India about 1500 BCE from the region of present-day Iran.
- It takes its name from the collections of sacred texts known as the Vedas, which are the oldest stratum of religious activity in India for which there exist written materials.
- The Vedas are composed in Vedic Sanskrit and consist of four collections: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda.
- The Vedic religion was based on the performance of sacrifices (yajna) to various gods (devas) who represented natural forces or aspects of the cosmic order.
- The Vedic religion also involved the worship of fire (agni), the sun (surya), the dawn (ushas), the storm (indra), the soma plant (soma), and other deities.
- The Vedic religion was characterized by a hierarchical social structure, with priests (brahmins) at the top, followed by warriors (kshatriyas), commoners (vaishyas), and servants (shudras).
- The Vedic religion also developed the concepts of karma, reincarnation, dharma, and the four stages of life (ashrama).
- The Vedic religion gradually evolved into the later forms of Hinduism, such as Brahmanism, Upanishadism, and Vedanta.



### Buddhism in India

- Buddhism is an ancient Indian religion that originated in the 6th century BCE in the region of Magadha (now Bihar) and spread throughout northern India and beyond  .
- Buddhism is based on the teachings and life experiences of Siddhartha Gautama, who became known as the Buddha (the awakened one) after attaining enlightenment under a bodhi tree .
- Buddhism teaches the four noble truths: the truth of suffering, the truth of the cause of suffering, the truth of the end of suffering, and the truth of the path that leads to the end of suffering .
- Buddhism also teaches the eightfold path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration .
- Buddhism is divided into two main branches: Theravada and Mahayana. Theravada Buddhism follows the Pali canon, the oldest collection of Buddhist scriptures, and emphasizes the individual attainment of nirvana, the state of liberation from the cycle of rebirth. Mahayana Buddhism follows the Sanskrit scriptures, which include additional teachings and stories, and emphasizes the bodhisattva ideal, the aspiration to become a compassionate being who helps others to attain enlightenment  .
- Buddhism flourished in India for over a thousand years, from the 3rd century BCE to the 12th century CE, and produced many schools, sects, and traditions, such as the Madhyamaka, Yogacara, Pure Land, Zen, and Vajrayana. Buddhism also influenced Indian art, architecture, literature, philosophy, and culture   .
- Buddhism spread from India to other parts of Asia, such as Central Asia, China, Tibet, Japan, Korea, Sri Lanka, and Southeast Asia, through trade, pilgrimage, and missionary activities. India was regarded as the sacred land of Buddhism by many Asian Buddhists   .
- Buddhism declined in India from the 8th century CE onwards, due to various factors, such as the rise of Hinduism, the invasions of Muslim rulers, the corruption and degeneration of Buddhist monasteries, and the loss of patronage and support from the laity. Buddhism survived in some regions, such as Ladakh, Sikkim, and Arunachal Pradesh, and was revived in the 20th century by the Dalit Buddhist movement and the Tibetan refugees   .
- Buddhism is currently the fourth largest religion in India, with about 8.4 million followers, or 0.7% of the population, according to the 2011 census. Most Indian Buddhists belong to the Dalit, Tibetan, or Nepali communities. Buddhism is also practiced by some members of other religions, such as Hinduism, Jainism, and Sikhism, as a form of spirituality or philosophy  .



### Jainism in India

- Jainism is an ancient Indian religion that teaches the path to spiritual purity and enlightenment through nonviolence and reducing harm to all living beings .
- Jainism originated in India at least 2,500 years ago and is based on the teachings of 24 spiritual leaders called Tirthankaras, the last of whom was Mahavira  .
- Jains believe in the existence of an eternal soul that can be liberated from the cycle of rebirth and attain a state of omniscience and bliss called moksha  .
- Jains follow five main principles: ahimsa (nonviolence), satya (truthfulness), asteya (non-stealing), brahmacharya (celibacy or chastity), and aparigraha (non-attachment or non-possessiveness)  .
- Jains practice various rituals and ceremonies, such as fasting, meditation, worship, pilgrimage, festivals, and rites of passage  .
- Jains are divided into two major sects: Digambara (sky-clad) and Svetambara (white-clad), which differ in their doctrines, practices, and traditions  .
- Jains are a small but influential minority in India, constituting about 0.4% of the population, or about 4.5 million people .
- Jains are mostly concentrated in the states of Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, and Karnataka, but also have a presence in other parts of India and abroad .
- Jains are the most educated and affluent religious group in India, with high levels of literacy, employment, income, and social status .
- Jains have contributed significantly to the fields of art, architecture, literature, philosophy, science, and social reform in India and beyond  .



### Six System Indian Philosophy

The six systems of Indian philosophy are Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva Mimamsa and Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta. They are also known as the orthodox schools of Hindu philosophy, as they accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of India. Each system has its own founder, texts, concepts and methods of inquiry. Here is a brief overview of each system:

- **Nyaya**: The founder of Nyaya is Akshapada Gautama and it dates back to 2nd Century BCE. It is a school that deals with logic and epistemology, or the theory of knowledge. It aims to establish the validity of sources of knowledge, such as perception, inference, analogy and testimony. It also develops rules of reasoning and debate to refute false views and establish true ones. Nyaya is often paired with Vaisheshika, as they share many concepts and categories .
- **Vaisheshika**: The founder of Vaisheshika is Kanada and it dates back to 6th Century BCE. It is a school that deals with metaphysics, or the nature of reality. It proposes that reality is composed of six categories of substances: atoms, qualities, actions, universals, inherence and absence. It also postulates that atoms are the ultimate indivisible particles of matter, and that they combine to form complex substances. Vaisheshika is often paired with Nyaya, as they share many concepts and categories .
- **Samkhya**: The founder of Samkhya is Kapila and it dates back to 6th Century BCE. It is a school that deals with cosmology, or the origin and evolution of the universe. It proposes that reality is composed of two principles: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter). It also explains how prakriti evolves into various elements, senses, mind and ego, and how purusha becomes bound and liberated from prakriti. Samkhya is often paired with Yoga, as they share many concepts and methods of liberation .
- **Yoga**: The founder of Yoga is Patanjali and it dates back to 2nd Century BCE. It is a school that deals with psychology, or the control and transformation of the mind. It prescribes an eightfold path of discipline and meditation to attain the state of samadhi, or the union of the individual self with the supreme self. It also describes various types of yoga, such as karma, bhakti, jnana and raja. Yoga is often paired with Samkhya, as they share many concepts and methods of liberation .
- **Purva Mimamsa**: The founder of Purva Mimamsa is Jaimini and it dates back to 3rd Century BCE. It is a school that deals with hermeneutics, or the interpretation of the Vedas. It aims to establish the authority and validity of the Vedas, and to derive the rules of dharma, or the moral and religious duty. It also analyzes the language and grammar of the Vedas, and the rituals and sacrifices prescribed by them. Purva Mimamsa is often paired with Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta, as they are both based on the Vedas .
- **Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta**: The founder of Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta is Badarayana and it dates back to 4th Century BCE. It is a school that deals with ontology, or the nature of being. It is based on the Upanishads, the philosophical portions of the Vedas, and it aims to reveal the ultimate truth of Brahman, or the supreme reality. It also develops various sub-schools, such as Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita, that differ in their views on the relation between Brahman, the individual self and the world. Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta is often paired with Purva Mimamsa, as they are both based on the Vedas .

These are the six systems of Indian philosophy, each with its own distinctive features and contributions to the intellectual and spiritual heritage of India. They are not mutually exclusive, but rather complementary and interrelated, as they address different aspects of reality and knowledge. They also influence and interact with each



### Shankaracharya and Advaita Vedanta

- Shankaracharya, also known as Adi Shankara, was an Indian philosopher and theologian who expounded the doctrine of Advaita Vedanta, one of the six orthodox schools of Hindu philosophy .
- Advaita Vedanta is a non-dualistic system of thought that asserts the identity of the individual self (atman) and the supreme reality (brahman), and denies the existence of any independent reality other than brahman.
- Shankaracharya was born in a Brahmin family in Kaladi, Kerala, around the 8th century CE. He renounced the worldly life at a very young age and became a wandering ascetic. He studied under various teachers, including Govinda Bhagavatpada, who initiated him into the Advaita Vedanta tradition .
- Shankaracharya wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras, and the Bhagavad Gita, which are considered the foundational texts of Advaita Vedanta. He also composed original works, such as the Vivekachudamani, the Upadesasahasri, and the Crest-Jewel of Discrimination, which explain the main tenets of his philosophy .
- Shankaracharya established four monastic centers (mathas) in the four corners of India, namely Sringeri in the south, Dwarka in the west, Puri in the east, and Badrinath in the north. He appointed four of his disciples as the heads of these mathas, who continued his lineage and teachings. He also debated and defeated various rival schools of thought, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Mimamsa, and Nyaya  .
- Shankaracharya is regarded as one of the most influential and revered figures in Hinduism, especially by the followers of Advaita Vedanta. He is credited with reviving and reforming the Vedic tradition, and reconciling the various sects and schools of Hinduism. He is also considered a great poet, mystic, and devotee, who composed many hymns and songs in praise of various deities  .
- The cultural influence of Shankara and Advaita Vedanta started only centuries later, in the Vijayanagara Empire in the 14th century, when Sringeri matha started to receive patronage from the kings of the Vijayanagara Empire and became a powerful institution. Advaita Vedanta also influenced the Bhakti movement, the Sikh religion, and the modern Hindu reform movements.
- Advaita Vedanta remains one of the most popular and influential schools of Hindu philosophy today, with many contemporary teachers and followers. It also has attracted the interest of Western scholars and philosophers, who have compared and contrasted it with other systems of thought, such as Platonism, Kantianism, and Phenomenology .



### Various Philosophical Doctrines in India

- Indian philosophy refers to the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent.
- Indian philosophy is characterized by its diversity, continuity, synthesis, and spiritual orientation .
- Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought.
- Indian philosophy shares many common themes such as the self or soul (atman), works (karma), liberation (moksha), and the cycle of birth and death (samsara).
- Indian philosophy can be broadly divided into two categories: orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) .
- Orthodox schools accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, while unorthodox schools reject or question the Vedas .
- The six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa, and Vedanta .
- Sankhya is a dualistic system that posits two independent realities: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter).
- Yoga is a system that is similar to Sankhya but accepts a personal god and focuses on yogic practices for attaining liberation.
- Nyaya is a system that emphasizes logic and epistemology as the means of acquiring valid knowledge.
- Vaisheshika is a system that analyzes reality into six categories: substance, quality, action, universality, particularity, and inherence.
- Mimamsa is a system that interprets the Vedas and the rituals prescribed in them as the means of achieving happiness and liberation.
- Vedanta is a system that expounds the metaphysical and ethical teachings of the Upanishads, the philosophical portions of the Vedas.
- The three unorthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: Buddhism, Jainism, and Charvaka .
- Buddhism is a system that rejects the notion of a permanent self and teaches the four noble truths and the eightfold path as the way to end suffering and attain nirvana.
- Jainism is a system that emphasizes non-violence, asceticism, and the multiplicity of viewpoints as the means of achieving liberation from karma and samsara.
- Charvaka is a system that denies the existence of anything beyond the material world and advocates hedonism and skepticism as the only rational way of life.



### Heterodox Sects in India

- Heterodox sects are those that do not accept the authority of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, and challenge the orthodox Brahmanical traditions and rituals.
- Heterodox sects emerged in the 6th century BCE as part of the Sramana movement, which was a cultural and religious phenomenon that advocated renunciation, asceticism, and liberation from the cycle of rebirth.
- Some of the prominent heterodox sects in India are:
  - Jainism: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, Jainism teaches the doctrine of ahimsa (non-violence), anekantavada (many-sidedness of truth), and aparigraha (non-attachment). Jainism rejects the concept of a creator god and believes in the existence of jivas (souls) and ajivas (non-souls) that are bound by karma and can attain moksha (liberation) by following the five vows of conduct.
  - Buddhism: Founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, Buddhism teaches the four noble truths, the eightfold path, and the concept of nirvana (extinction of suffering). Buddhism rejects the authority of the Vedas, the caste system, and the ritual sacrifices. Buddhism also denies the existence of a permanent self or soul and advocates the doctrine of anatman (no-self) and pratityasamutpada (dependent origination).
  - Ajivika: Founded by Makkhali Gosala, Ajivika is a materialist and fatalist sect that believes in the doctrine of niyati (determinism) and the inevitability of the cycle of rebirth. Ajivika denies the existence of free will, moral responsibility, and the efficacy of human action. Ajivika also rejects the concept of a god, karma, and liberation.
  - Carvaka: Also known as Lokayata, Carvaka is a radical materialist and atheist sect that believes in the doctrine of svabhava (nature) and the primacy of the four elements (earth, water, fire, and air). Carvaka denies the existence of anything beyond the material world, such as the soul, the afterlife, the Vedas, the gods, and the supernatural. Carvaka also advocates the pursuit of sensual pleasure and happiness in this life.



### Bhakti Movement in India

- The Bhakti movement was a significant religious movement in medieval Hinduism that sought to bring religious reforms to all strata of society by adopting the method of devotion to achieve salvation.
- The movement preached against the caste system, ritualism, idolatry, and social inequalities, and emphasized the personal and emotional relationship between the devotee and the deity .
- The movement originated in South India in the 7th-8th centuries CE with the Alvars (Vaishnavite saints) and the Nayanars (Shaivite saints), who composed devotional hymns in Tamil and visited various sacred shrines .
- The movement spread to North India in the 12th-13th centuries CE with the influence of the Bhakti saints like Ramananda, Kabir, Nanak, Tulsidas, Surdas, Mirabai, and Chaitanya, who propagated their teachings in the regional languages like Hindi, Punjabi, Bengali, and Marathi .
- The movement had a profound impact on the Indian society, culture, and literature. It fostered a spirit of tolerance, harmony, and universal brotherhood among the people of different faiths and sects. It also enriched the Indian languages and literature with a vast corpus of devotional poetry, songs, and stories  .



### Sufi movement in India

- Sufism is a mystical branch of Islam that emphasizes the personal and spiritual experience of God.
- Sufism originated in the Middle East in the 8th and 9th centuries and spread to various regions, including India, through the travels and teachings of Sufi saints or sheikhs.
- Sufism in India began in the 13th century with the arrival of the Chishti order, founded by Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, who settled in Ajmer and preached a message of love, tolerance and service to humanity.
- Sufism in India developed into various orders or silsilas, such as the Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi, Shattari and others, each with its own doctrines, rituals and practices.
- Sufism in India attracted people from different backgrounds, religions and castes, as it offered a direct and personal connection with God, without the mediation of priests or rituals.
- Sufism in India also influenced the development of the Bhakti movement, a devotional movement that emerged in the 15th and 16th centuries and emphasized the worship of one God through love, devotion and surrender.
- Sufism in India contributed to the cultural, social and artistic life of the country, as Sufi saints composed poetry, music, literature and art that expressed their mystical experiences and teachings.
- Sufism in India also played a role in the political and historical events of the country, as some Sufi saints supported or opposed the rulers of the Delhi Sultanate, the Mughal Empire and the British Raj.
- Sufism in India continues to exist today as a living tradition that attracts followers from various walks of life and promotes the values of peace, harmony and pluralism.



### Socio religious reform movement of 19th century in India

- Socio religious reform movement of 19th century in India refers to the wave of reforming activities that aimed to reform the Indian society and religion in the light of western education and liberal ideas.
- The main causes of the reform movement were:
  - The impact of British colonialism and western culture on the Indian society, which exposed the evils and superstitions of the traditional social and religious practices.
  - The rise of modern education, science, rationalism, humanism and democracy, which challenged the authority of the orthodox and conservative elements.
  - The influence of the Christian missionaries, who criticized the Hindu religion and tried to convert the Indians to Christianity.
  - The emergence of a new class of educated and enlightened Indians, who felt the need to reform their society and religion from within.
- The main types of the reform movement were:
  - The Hindu reform movements, such as the Brahmo Samaj, the Arya Samaj, the Prarthana Samaj, the Ramakrishna Mission, the Theosophical Society, etc., which aimed to reform the Hindu religion and society by removing the evils of casteism, idolatry, polytheism, rituals, superstitions, etc., and promoting the ideals of monotheism, rationalism, social equality, women's education, etc.   .
  - The Muslim reform movements, such as the Aligarh movement, the Deoband movement, the Wahabi movement, the Ahmadiyya movement, etc., which aimed to reform the Muslim religion and society by removing the degeneration and corruption of the Islamic faith and culture, and promoting the ideals of modern education, social reform, religious revival, etc.  .
  - The Sikh reform movements, such as the Singh Sabha movement, the Akali movement, the Nirankari movement, etc., which aimed to reform the Sikh religion and society by removing the influence of Hinduism and Islam on the Sikh faith and culture, and promoting the ideals of Khalsa identity, Sikh unity, religious purity, etc..
  - The Parsi reform movements, such as the Rehnumai Mazdayasan Sabha, the Parsi Pragati Mandal, the Parsi Panchayat, etc., which aimed to reform the Parsi religion and society by removing the rigid and orthodox practices of the Parsi faith and culture, and promoting the ideals of social reform, religious tolerance, women's rights, etc..
- The main impacts of the reform movement were:
  - The reform movement contributed to the awakening of the Indian people and the growth of Indian nationalism, as it fostered a sense of pride and self-respect among the Indians and challenged the colonial domination and exploitation .
  - The reform movement stimulated the social and religious changes in the Indian society, as it led to the abolition or reform of some of the social evils and religious practices, such as sati, child marriage, widow remarriage, untouchability, etc., and the promotion of some of the social and religious values, such as education, equality, human rights, etc. .
  - The reform movement also faced some limitations and challenges, such as the resistance from the orthodox and conservative sections of the society, the lack of mass participation and support, the emergence of communalism and sectarianism, the diversity and complexity of the Indian society and culture, etc. .

### Astronomy in India and Ancient India

- Astronomy in India has a long history stretching from pre-historic to modern times. Some of the earliest roots of Indian astronomy can be dated to the period of Indus Valley civilisation or earlier.
- Astronomy in ancient India developed to a sophisticated level as the sages of ancient India were keen observers of the sky generation after generation. They recorded their observations and calculations in various texts, such as the Vedas, the Puranas, the Siddhantas, the Aryabhatiya, the Surya Siddhanta, and the Brihat Samhita.
- Astronomy in ancient India can be divided into two major classes. From the third-century BCE, India started having contact with Greece and other nations outside the subcontinent. So, the basic structure of Indian astronomy had outside influence although the astronomical concepts and the major parameters were India’s own.
- The first class of Indian astronomy is called Vedanga Jyotisha, which is based on the Vedas and deals with the calendrical and ritual aspects of astronomy. The second class is called Siddhantic astronomy, which is based on the Siddhantas and deals with the mathematical and predictive aspects of astronomy.
- Some of the notable achievements of ancient Indian astronomers include:
  - The concept of zero and the decimal system of numeration.
  - The accurate determination of the length of the solar year, the lunar month, the sidereal day, and the precession of the equinoxes.
  - The discovery of the heliocentric model of the solar system by Aryabhata in the 5th century CE.
  - The calculation of the value of pi and the circumference of the earth by Aryabhata.
  - The use of trigonometric functions and algebraic methods to solve astronomical problems.
  - The invention of the sine table by Aryabhata and its improvement by Bhaskara II.
  - The formulation of the law of gravity by Brahmagupta in the 7th century CE.
  - The development of various astronomical instruments, such as the gnomon, the water clock, the armillary sphere, and the astrolabe.
  - The observation and explanation of various astronomical phenomena, such as eclipses, comets, meteors, planets, stars, and constellations.
  - The classification of the stars into 27 nakshatras or lunar mansions and 12 rasis or zodiac signs.
  - The creation of various astrological systems, such as horoscopy, natal astrology, electional astrology, and mundane astrology.
- Astronomy in ancient India had a profound and direct influence on the astronomy of other cultures, such as Islamic, Byzantine, Chinese, and Tibetan astronomy . Indian astronomy also contributed to the fields of mathematics, medicine, art, literature, and philosophy.

### Metallurgy in India and Ancient India

Metallurgy is the science and art of extracting and processing metals from ores and other sources. Metallurgy in India and ancient India has a long and rich history, dating back to the prehistoric times. Some of the main aspects of metallurgy in India and ancient India are:

- **Copper metallurgy**: Copper was one of the first metals to be used by humans, and copper metallurgy in India dates back to the Chalcolithic societies in the subcontinent, around the 4th millennium BCE . Copper was used for making tools, weapons, ornaments, coins, and other objects. Copper was also alloyed with tin to produce bronze, which was harder and more durable than pure copper. The Harappan civilization (2500-1900 BCE) was known for its advanced copper and bronze metallurgy, as evidenced by the artefacts found at various sites, such as Mitathal in Haryana.
- **Iron metallurgy**: Iron is the most widely used metal in the world, and iron metallurgy in India has a long and distinguished history, starting from the early Vedic period (1500-500 BCE). Iron was mentioned in various Vedic texts, such as the Rigveda, the Atharvaveda, and the Yajurveda, as a symbol of strength, power, and wealth. Iron was used for making weapons, tools, agricultural implements, and other items. Iron was also smelted and forged into steel, which was superior to iron in terms of hardness, toughness, and resistance to corrosion. Ancient Indians were masters in the production of iron and steel, and developed various methods of extraction, classification, and microstructural analysis of iron and steel. Some of the famous examples of ancient Indian iron and steel are the iron pillar of Delhi (4th century CE), the wootz steel (6th century BCE onwards), and the Damascus steel (11th century CE onwards).
- **Zinc metallurgy**: Zinc is a metal that is essential for various biological and industrial processes, and zinc metallurgy in India is one of the oldest and most sophisticated in the world. Zinc was extracted in India as early as in the 4th to 3rd century BCE, using a distillation technique that was developed at Zawar in Rajasthan . Zinc was used for making brass (an alloy of copper and zinc), coins, medicine, cosmetics, and other products. Zinc was also exported to other countries, such as China, Europe, and the Middle East, where it was highly valued for its properties and applications.
- **Other metals**: Apart from copper, iron, and zinc, ancient Indians also knew and used various other metals, such as gold, silver, lead, tin, mercury, and arsenic. These metals were mentioned in various ancient texts, such as the Vedas, the Arthashastra, the Rasaratnakara, and the Charaka Samhita, and were used for various purposes, such as jewellery, coinage, medicine, alchemy, and metallurgical experiments . Ancient Indians also developed various methods of testing, purifying, and alloying metals, and were aware of the properties and effects of different metals and their combinations.

### Harappan Technologies in India and Ancient India

- The Harappan Civilization, also known as the Indus Valley Civilization, was one of the earliest urban cultures of the Indian subcontinent, dating from 3300 to 1300 BCE .
- The Harappans developed various technologies and innovations in various fields, such as:
  - **Metallurgy**: The Harappans used copper, bronze, lead, and tin to make tools, weapons, ornaments, and seals. They also knew how to smelt iron, but did not use it extensively .
  - **Standardized weights and measures**: The Harappans used a system of uniform weights and measures based on a decimal scale. They also used a ruler with markings of 1.32 inches, which corresponds to one Indus inch .
  - **Seal carving**: The Harappans carved seals out of steatite, a soft stone, and engraved them with various motifs, such as animals, plants, and symbols. The seals were used for trade, administration, and ritual purposes. Some of the seals also have inscriptions in an undeciphered script .
  - **Hydraulic engineering**: The Harappans built sophisticated systems of water management, such as wells, reservoirs, canals, drains, and baths. They also had a sanitation system that included flush toilets and covered sewers .
  - **Urban planning**: The Harappans built well-planned cities with grid-like layouts, fortified walls, and standardized bricks. They also had public buildings, such as granaries, warehouses, and citadels. The cities were divided into two parts: the upper town, which was the administrative and religious center, and the lower town, which was the residential and commercial area.

### Indian Architecture in Ancient India and present times

- Indian architecture is rooted in the history, culture, and religion of India. It reflects the diversity and richness of Indian civilization and has influenced the architecture of other regions as well.
- Indian architecture can be broadly classified into two main categories: Hindu temple architecture and Indo-Islamic architecture. Both of these styles have evolved over time and have various sub-styles and regional variations.
- Hindu temple architecture is the most prominent and distinctive form of Indian architecture. It is based on the principles of Vastu Shastra, which is a traditional system of architecture that prescribes the layout, design, and construction of buildings in harmony with the natural and cosmic forces.
- Hindu temple architecture can be divided into three main types: Nagara, Dravida, and Vesara. Nagara temples are characterized by a curvilinear tower (shikhara) over the sanctum (garbhagriha), a pillared hall (mandapa), and an entrance porch (ardhamandapa). Dravida temples have a pyramidal tower (vimana) over the sanctum, a pillared hall, and a gateway tower (gopuram). Vesara temples are a hybrid of Nagara and Dravida styles, with features of both.
- Some of the famous examples of Hindu temple architecture are the Kailasa temple at Ellora, the Khajuraho temples, the Brihadeeswarar temple at Thanjavur, the Meenakshi temple at Madurai, the Sun temple at Konark, and the Somnath temple at Gujarat.
- Indo-Islamic architecture is the result of the fusion of Islamic and Indian architectural elements. It emerged after the arrival of Muslim invaders and rulers in India, who brought with them their own styles and techniques of building. Indo-Islamic architecture can be divided into two main types: Sultanate architecture and Mughal architecture.
- Sultanate architecture is the style of architecture that developed under the Delhi Sultanate and other regional Muslim kingdoms in India from the 12th to the 16th century. It is characterized by the use of arches, domes, minarets, and decorative motifs such as calligraphy, geometric patterns, and floral designs. Sultanate architecture also incorporated elements of Hindu and Jain architecture, such as pillars, brackets, and carvings.
- Some of the famous examples of Sultanate architecture are the Qutub Minar, the Alai Darwaza, the Tughlaqabad fort, the Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra, the Gol Gumbaz, and the Charminar.
- Mughal architecture is the style of architecture that developed under the Mughal Empire from the 16th to the 18th century. It is considered to be the most refined and sophisticated form of Indo-Islamic architecture. It is characterized by the use of white marble, red sandstone, inlay work, pietra dura, cusped arches, bulbous domes, chhatris, and gardens.
- Some of the famous examples of Mughal architecture are the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort, the Humayun's Tomb, the Jama Masjid, the Fatehpur Sikri, and the Shalimar Gardens.
- Indian architecture in present times is a blend of traditional and modern styles, influenced by various factors such as globalization, urbanization, technology, and sustainability. Indian architecture today reflects the diversity and dynamism of the contemporary Indian society and culture.
- Some of the famous examples of Indian architecture in present times are the Lotus Temple, the Akshardham Temple, the Chandigarh Capitol Complex, the Bahá'í House of Worship, the Infosys Building, and the Antilia.

### Sculptures as Cultural Heritage of India

### Seals as Cultural Heritage of India

- Seals are small objects made of stone, metal, or other materials that have a carved or engraved design on one side and are used to stamp or seal documents, goods, or containers.
- Seals have been used in India since ancient times, especially by the Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1900 BCE), one of the earliest urban civilizations in the world.
- The Indus seals are mostly made of steatite, a soft stone that can be easily carved, and are usually square or rectangular in shape, with a perforated knob on the back for holding or attaching a cord.
- The Indus seals have various designs on the front, such as animals, humans, mythical creatures, geometric patterns, or symbols. Some of the common animals depicted are elephants, tigers, water buffaloes, rhinoceroses, and unicorns. Some of the human figures are shown in a seated posture, wearing a horned headdress, and are interpreted as deities or rulers.
- The Indus seals also have a script on the front, which is still undeciphered. The script consists of about 400 signs, some of which are pictographic and some of which are abstract. The script is written from right to left, and may represent a language or a system of symbols.
- The Indus seals were used for various purposes, such as identifying property, marking trade goods, sealing containers, or expressing religious beliefs. The seals have been found in many sites of the Indus Valley Civilization, as well as in other regions, such as Mesopotamia, Afghanistan, Iran, and Bahrain, indicating the extensive trade and cultural contacts of the Indus people.
- The Indus seals are valuable sources of information about the Indus Valley Civilization, its culture, economy, religion, and society. They also reflect the artistic and technological achievements of the Indus people, who were skilled in carving, engraving, and firing steatite. The seals are considered as cultural heritage of India, as they represent one of the oldest and most sophisticated civilizations of the ancient world.

- The coins of India are a rich and valuable cultural heritage that reveal the diversity and dynamism of the Indian civilization over the centuries   .

### Puppetry as Cultural Heritage of India

- Puppetry is one of the most imaginative and ancient ways of telling a story in the society.
- Puppetry has been in India a means of entertainment and a form of cultural and religious dissemination.
- Several regions in India have their own distinct styles and genres of puppetry, making it one of the richest heritages of the country.
- The origin of puppetry in India cannot be dated, but mythology provides at least two stories that are witness to the importance of this art.
- One story is from the Mahabharata, where Arjuna creates a puppet of his son Abhimanyu and performs his funeral rites with it.
- Another story is from the Ramayana, where Ravana uses a puppet of Sita to deceive Rama and Lakshmana.
- There are four main types of puppetry in India: string puppets, rod puppets, glove puppets and shadow puppets.
- String puppets are controlled by strings attached to different parts of the puppet's body. They are also known as marionettes. The most popular and predominant form of string puppetry in India is Kathputli, which is native to Rajasthan .
- Rod puppets are manipulated by rods attached to the puppet's limbs or head. They are usually larger and more expressive than string puppets. The most famous form of rod puppetry in India is Putul Nautch, which is practiced in West Bengal.
- Glove puppets are worn on the hand of the puppeteer, who moves the fingers to animate the puppet's mouth and arms. They are also called hand puppets. The most well-known form of glove puppetry in India is Pavakoothu, which is performed in Kerala.
- Shadow puppets are flat cut-out figures that are held against a translucent screen and illuminated from behind. They create silhouettes and shadows on the screen. The most renowned form of shadow puppetry in India is Tholu Bommalata, which is prevalent in Andhra Pradesh.
- Puppetry in India is not only a form of art, but also a medium of education, social awareness and cultural preservation.
- Puppetry in India reflects the diversity, creativity and richness of the Indian culture and heritage.

### Theatre as Cultural Heritage of India

These forms were influenced by local cultures, languages, religions, and folk traditions, and were performed in rural and urban settings for the masses. They used music, dance, masks, costumes, and props to create colorful and lively spectacles  .
  - The modern period (from 1800 AD to present) was initiated by the introduction of Western theatre and influences, such as realism, naturalism, social drama, and experimental theatre. The colonial rule, the nationalist movement, the independence struggle, and the post-independence issues shaped the themes and styles of modern Indian theatre. Some of the prominent playwrights and directors of this period are Rabindranath Tagore, Mohan Rakesh, Vijay Tendulkar, Girish Karnad, Badal Sircar, Habib Tanvir, and Satyadev Dubey .
- Theatre in India is a cultural heritage that reflects the diversity, creativity, and identity of the Indian people. It is a medium of expression, education, and entertainment that has survived and adapted to the changing times and contexts. It is a living tradition that continues to evolve and innovate, and to contribute to the global theatre scene .

### Painting as Cultural Heritage of India

- Painting is one of the oldest and most diverse forms of art in India, dating back to prehistoric times.
- Painting reflects the cultural, religious, and political aspects of Indian society, as well as the aesthetic and creative expressions of the artists.
- Painting in India can be classified into various styles, such as mural, miniature, folk, tribal, modern, and contemporary, each with its own characteristics and influences.
- Some of the most famous and distinctive types of Indian paintings are:

  - Madhubani: A folk painting style from Bihar, characterized by bright colors, geometric patterns, and symbolic motifs depicting gods, goddesses, animals, plants, and scenes from mythology and daily life. Madhubani paintings are usually done on walls, floors, or paper using natural dyes and brushes made from bamboo or cotton.
  - Warli: A tribal painting style from Maharashtra, characterized by simple and monochromatic hues, linear figures, and an elementary style of execution that resembles cave painting. Warli paintings depict the social and cultural life of the Warli tribe, such as farming, hunting, dancing, and rituals. Warli paintings are usually done on mud walls using rice paste or white paint .
  - Mughal: A miniature painting style that flourished under the Mughal dynasty from the 16th to the 19th century, influenced by Persian, Indian, and European art. Mughal paintings are known for their realism, rich colors, intricate details, and delicate brushwork. Mughal paintings depict scenes from history, literature, court life, nature, and portraits of rulers and nobles. Mughal paintings are usually done on paper, silk, or ivory using watercolors, gold, and silver .
  - Rajasthani: A miniature painting style that originated in Rajasthan, influenced by Mughal, Persian, and local art. Rajasthani paintings are known for their vibrant colors, elaborate costumes, expressive faces, and romantic themes. Rajasthani paintings depict scenes from Hindu epics, legends, folklore, and court life. Rajasthani paintings are usually done on paper, cloth, or wood using watercolors, gold, and silver .
  - Tanjore: A classical painting style from Tamil Nadu, influenced by Hindu and South Indian art. Tanjore paintings are known for their rich colors, gold foils, precious stones, and relief work. Tanjore paintings depict gods, goddesses, saints, and scenes from Hindu scriptures. Tanjore paintings are usually done on wood, cloth, or paper using watercolors, gold, and silver .

- Painting in India is a living and evolving art form that continues to adapt to changing times and tastes, while preserving its unique identity and heritage. Painting in India is a source of pride, inspiration, and joy for the people of India and the world.

- India has an ancient tradition in diverse martial arts, dating back to the Vedic period and influenced by the geography, culture, and history of the subcontinent.
- Some of the most famous martial arts in India are    :
  - Kalaripayattu: Often known as the ‘Mother Of All Martial Arts’, this 3000-year old art form originating from Kerala, draws inspiration from the raw power, swift movements and sinuous strength of majestic animals – the lion, tiger, elephant, wild boar, snake and crocodile. It involves strikes, kicks, grappling, weaponry, and healing methods.
  - Silambam: A weapon-based martial art from Tamil Nadu, Silambam uses a bamboo staff as the primary weapon, along with other secondary weapons such as swords, daggers, and spears. It is known for its fast and fluid movements, and its use of circular patterns and footwork.
  - Gatka: A Sikh martial art that originated in Punjab, Gatka is mainly focused on the use of swords, sticks, and shields. It is also a sport that involves sparring with wooden sticks and protective gear. Gatka is based on the principles of spirituality, discipline, and service.
  - Thang-Ta: The ancient Manipuri martial art, also known as Huyen Lallong, Thang-Ta involves the use of swords and spears, as well as unarmed combat. It is a powerful yet gracefully complex technique that reflects the culture and history of Manipur.
  - Lathi: Lathi is India’s oldest armed martial art, and also regarded as one of the world’s earliest martial arts weapons. It involves the use of a long bamboo stick, often tipped with metal, to strike, block, and defend. Lathi is widely practised in rural areas of India, especially in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh.
  - Kushti: Kushti is one of the most popular martial arts in India, and also known as traditional wrestling. It started off during the Mughal times, where Malla Yuddha, a local sport, was combined with a Persian sport named Varzesh-e-Bastani. Kushti involves grappling, takedowns, locks, and holds, and is practised on a clay or dirt pit.
  - Mardani Khel: Mardani Khel is a martial art from Maharashtra, that was developed by the Maratha warriors in the 17th century. It involves the use of various weapons, such as swords, daggers, axes, spears, and whips, as well as unarmed combat. Mardani Khel is known for its agility, strength, and courage.
  - Sqay: Sqay is a martial art from Kashmir, that is influenced by Central Asian and Persian styles. It involves the use of swords, shields, and daggers, as well as kicks, punches, and blocks. Sqay is also a sport that has rules and regulations, and is recognised by the Indian Olympic Association.



### Fairs and Festivals as Cultural Heritage of India

- India is a land of diverse cultures, religions, languages, and traditions. It is also a land of vibrant celebrations, where various fairs and festivals are held throughout the year to mark important occasions, honor deities, commemorate saints, express gratitude, and showcase the rich and colorful heritage of the country .
- Fairs and festivals in India reflect the diversity and unity of the people, as well as the values and beliefs of the society. They also provide an opportunity for people to interact, exchange, and enjoy with each other, as well as to showcase their arts, crafts, cuisines, and lifestyles .
- Some of the most famous fairs and festivals in India are:

  - **Makar Sankranti**: This is a harvest festival celebrated in January, when the sun enters the zodiac sign of Capricorn. It is also known as Pongal, Lohri, Bihu, and Maghi in different regions. It is a time to thank the sun god for the bountiful crops, and to fly kites, light bonfires, and share sweets .
  - **Holi**: This is the festival of colors, celebrated in March, to mark the arrival of spring and the victory of good over evil. It is a time to play with colors, water, and flowers, and to enjoy music, dance, and delicacies. It is also a time to forgive and forget, and to spread joy and love.
  - **Diwali**: This is the festival of lights, celebrated in October or November, to mark the return of Lord Rama from exile and the triumph of light over darkness. It is a time to decorate homes and streets with lamps, candles, and rangoli, and to worship Goddess Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth and prosperity. It is also a time to exchange gifts, sweets, and greetings, and to burst crackers.
  - **Dussehra**: This is the festival of victory, celebrated in September or October, to commemorate the defeat of the demon king Ravana by Lord Rama. It is a time to watch the dramatic reenactment of the epic Ramayana, and to burn the effigies of Ravana, Kumbhakarna, and Meghnad. It is also a time to worship Goddess Durga, the goddess of power and courage.
  - **Ganesh Chaturthi**: This is the festival of the elephant-headed god, celebrated in August or September, to mark his birthday. It is a time to install and worship the idols of Lord Ganesh, the god of wisdom and success, and to immerse them in water after a few days. It is also a time to enjoy modak, a sweet dumpling, and to chant "Ganpati Bappa Morya".
  - **Pushkar Mela**: This is the largest camel fair in the world, held in November, in the holy town of Pushkar in Rajasthan. It is a time to witness the trading and racing of thousands of camels, horses, and cattle, and to enjoy the cultural and musical performances, competitions, and exhibitions. It is also a time to take a dip in the sacred Pushkar lake, and to visit the only temple of Lord Brahma in India .
  - **Jaisalmer Desert Festival**: This is a three-day extravaganza of culture, art, and adventure, held in February, in the golden city of Jaisalmer in Rajasthan. It is a time to experience the desert life, and to enjoy the camel rides, polo, and safaris, as well as the folk dances, music, and puppet shows. It is also a time to witness the contests of turban tying, moustache growing, and Mr. Desert .
  - **Goa Carnival**: This is a four-day celebration of fun, frolic, and festivity, held in February or March, in the coastal state of Goa. It is a time to join the colorful parades, floats, and masks, and to enjoy the live bands, dances, and parties. It is also a time to relish the Goan cuisine, especially the seafood, and to indulge in the spirit of "Khush Hali" or happiness .

### Current developments in Arts and Culture in India

- India is a diverse country with a rich and varied cultural heritage. India has one of the world’s largest collections of songs, music, dance, theatre, folk traditions, performing arts, rites and rituals, paintings and writings that are known, as the ‘Intangible Cultural Heritage’ (ICH) of humanity.
- India has also contributed significantly to the global art scene with its ancient and modern forms of art, such as sculpture, architecture, painting, literature, cinema, and more. India has 37 world heritage sites, two thriving film industries and many smaller ones as well.
- In recent years, India has witnessed some new trends and developments in its arts and culture sector, such as:
  - The emergence of contemporary Indian art, which explores new themes, mediums, and styles, influenced by both the local and the global contexts. Contemporary Indian artists have gained recognition and appreciation both nationally and internationally for their innovative and diverse works.
  - The inclusion of three Indian cities, Thrissur and Nilambur in Kerala and Warangal in Telangana, in the UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities, which aims to promote lifelong learning and sustainable development through education, culture, and social inclusion.
  - The nomination of Hoysala Temples, a group of 13th-century temples in Karnataka, for inscription in the World Heritage List, for their outstanding artistic and architectural value and their representation of the Hoysala style of temple building.
  - The repatriation of a 10th-century Goat-Headed Yogini Statue, which was stolen from a temple in Uttar Pradesh and smuggled to France, after a legal battle of 24 years. The statue is a rare example of the Tantric cult of Yoginis, which worshipped female deities associated with power and fertility.
  - The celebration of the Republic Day Parade 2022, which showcased the cultural diversity and unity of India, with various tableaux, cultural performances, and military displays. The parade also featured some new elements, such as the first-ever contingent of women fighter pilots, the first-ever tableau of Ladakh, and the first-ever flypast of the indigenously developed Light Combat Helicopter.
  - The launch of ‘Dharohar’, the National Museum of Customs and GST, which showcases the history and evolution of the customs and taxation system in India, from the ancient to the modern times. The museum also displays various artefacts, documents, uniforms, and stamps related to the customs and GST department.
  - The publication of ‘Devayatanam’, a book that explores the odyssey of Indian temple architecture, from the prehistoric to the contemporary times. The book covers various aspects of temple building, such as the symbolism, the aesthetics, the engineering, and the rituals, and also highlights the diversity and continuity of the Indian temple tradition.

These are some of the current developments in arts and culture in India, which reflect the dynamism, creativity, and resilience of the Indian people and their culture.



### Indian’s Cultural Contribution to the World

India is a country of diverse and rich culture, with a long and ancient history. India has made many contributions to the world in various fields, such as mathematics, architecture, textiles, medicine, language, art, and religion. Some of the most notable contributions are:

- Mathematics: India is the birthplace of the concept of zero, the decimal system, and the place-value system. India also developed algebra, trigonometry, geometry, calculus, and astronomy. Some of the famous Indian mathematicians are Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, Bhaskara, and Ramanujan .
- Architecture: India has a unique and diverse architectural heritage, influenced by various cultures and religions. India is known for its temples, palaces, forts, monuments, and urban planning. Some of the examples of Indian architecture are the Taj Mahal, the Khajuraho temples, the Qutub Minar, the Hampi ruins, and the Harappan civilization .
- Textiles: India is one of the largest producers and exporters of cotton, silk, and other fabrics in the world. India is also the origin of many techniques and styles of weaving, dyeing, printing, and embroidering. India is famous for its sarees, kurtas, shawls, carpets, and bedspreads. India also invented the spinning wheel, the charkha, and the muslin cloth .
- Medicine: India has a rich tradition of medicine, dating back to the ancient times. India developed the system of Ayurveda, which is based on the balance of the body, mind, and spirit. India also pioneered the fields of surgery, dentistry, plastic surgery, and pharmacology. Some of the notable Indian physicians are Charaka, Sushruta, Nagarjuna, and Vagbhata .
- Language: India is a multilingual country, with 22 official languages and hundreds of regional languages and dialects. India is also the source of many languages and scripts that are spoken and written in other parts of the world, such as Sanskrit, Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Tamil, and Devanagari. India also influenced the development of English, Persian, Arabic, and other languages through trade, migration, and colonization .
- Art: India has a vibrant and diverse artistic culture, spanning various forms and genres. India is known for its painting, sculpture, literature, music, dance, and theatre. India has produced many masterpieces and legends in the field of art, such as the Ajanta and Ellora caves, the Ramayana and Mahabharata epics, the Carnatic and Hindustani music, the Bharatanatyam and Kathak dance, and the Rabindranath Tagore and Raja Ravi Varma .
- Religion: India is the birthplace of four major religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. India is also home to many other faiths, such as Islam, Christianity, Judaism, Zoroastrianism, and Baha'i. India has contributed to the world's spiritual and philosophical thought, through concepts such as karma, dharma, yoga, meditation, reincarnation, and non-violence. India has also spread its religious influence to other regions, such as Southeast Asia, China, Japan, and Tibet .

These are some of the major contributions of India to the world, but there are many more. India is a country of diversity, creativity, and innovation, and has enriched the world with its culture.



### Indian Cinema as a Performing Art in India

- Indian cinema is a form of artistic expression that combines storytelling, performing arts, and science to create audio-visual narratives that reflect the socio-cultural and political realities of India.
- Indian cinema has a history of more than a century, starting with the silent era in the early 1900s and evolving into the diverse and vibrant industry that it is today, producing films in various languages, genres, and styles.
- Indian cinema is influenced by various forms of Indian performing arts, such as theater, dance, music, and folk arts, that have their roots in the ancient traditions of India, such as the Natyashastra, a treatise on drama and aesthetics written by Bharata Muni around 200 BC.
- The Natyashastra is considered the foundational text of Indian performing arts, as it lays down the principles of drama, dance, music, and poetry, and introduces the concept of rasa, or the aesthetic emotion, that is the essence of any artistic expression. The Natyashastra identifies nine rasas: shringara (love), hasya (humor), karuna (compassion), raudra (anger), vira (heroism), bhayanaka (fear), bibhatsa (disgust), adbhuta (wonder), and shanta (peace).
- Indian cinema has adopted and adapted the rasa theory to create films that evoke various emotions in the audience, and to explore the themes of love, social justice, nationalism, identity, morality, and spirituality, among others.
- Indian cinema has also been influenced by the Indian People's Theatre Association (IPTA), a cultural and political movement that emerged in the 1940s as a response to the colonial oppression and the freedom struggle. IPTA aimed to use theater and other performing arts as a medium of social change and awareness, and to promote the values of secularism, democracy, and socialism. IPTA produced plays and films that highlighted the issues of the oppressed classes, such as the peasants, the workers, the women, and the minorities, and challenged the feudal, communal, and imperialist forces.
- Indian cinema has also been influenced by the global trends and movements in cinema, such as realism, expressionism, surrealism, neorealism, new wave, and postmodernism, and has experimented with various techniques, styles, and genres, such as documentary, animation, musical, thriller, comedy, horror, and fantasy, to create films that appeal to a wide range of audiences, both domestic and international.
- Indian cinema, as a performing art, has contributed to the cultural and artistic heritage of India, and has also been a source of entertainment, education, and inspiration for millions of people. Indian cinema has also been a platform for showcasing the talents and skills of various artists, such as actors, directors, writers, composers, singers, dancers, cinematographers, editors, and designers, who have enriched the field of cinema with their creativity and innovation.
