### State in Ancient India

- States in ancient India were loosely categorized into monarchy, republican, and oligarchy. Monarchy was the most prevalent type of state in ancient India.
- The state in ancient India was considered necessary, for it ensured peace, order, and happiness. It was a social organization with political power.
- Ancient scholars were not unanimous in their opinion with regard to the origin of the state. The three theories of the origin of the state in ancient India are as follows: 1. Social Contract Theory 2. Divine Origin Theory 3. Organic Theory.
- The core issues in the study of political science are the state and the government. The institution of the state is studied in relation to its origin, nature, aims, and functions of the state in ancient India.




#### Theories of origin of State in India

The origin of the state in ancient India has been a topic of interest for political scientists and historians. There are three main theories that have been proposed to explain the origin of the state in ancient India:

1. **Social Contract Theory**: This theory suggests that the state originated from an agreement between individuals to form a society and establish a government to protect their rights and maintain order.

2. **Divine Origin Theory**: This theory proposes that the state was created by a divine power and that the rulers were chosen by the gods to govern the people.

3. **Organic Theory**: This theory suggests that the state is a natural and organic growth from the family and the clan, and that it evolved over time to become a more complex and organized institution.

These theories provide different perspectives on the origin of the state in ancient India and the role of the government in society .



#### Evolutionary Theory of origin of State
The Evolutionary Theory of the origin of the state is one of the several theories that attempt to explain how states or governments came into existence. According to this theory, the state evolved from the family, which gradually grew in size and complexity as population increased and societies became more organized.

1. The theory suggests that the earliest form of human organization was the family, which was headed by the father or the eldest male member.
2. As families grew in size and number, they formed clans or tribes, which were larger kinship groups.
3. As population increased and societies became more complex, the need for a more formal system of governance arose.
4. The theory proposes that the state emerged as a natural progression from the family and clan/tribe system, as a means of providing for the common defense and welfare of the people.
5. The state, according to this theory, evolved gradually over time, as societies became more complex and the need for a more formal system of governance became apparent.

This theory is based on the idea that the state is a natural and necessary part of human society, and that it evolved gradually over time as societies became more complex. It is one of several theories that attempt to explain the origin of the state, and is often contrasted with other theories such as the Divine Right Theory, the Force Theory, and the Social Contract Theory.



#### Force Theory of origin of State

The Force Theory of the origin of the state is one of the several theories that attempt to explain how states came into existence. According to this theory, the state emerged as a result of the use of force by one group to subjugate another group. Here are some key points to consider:

1. The theory suggests that the state was created when one group or tribe conquered another group or tribe and established its rule over the conquered people.
2. The conquerors used force to maintain their control over the conquered people and to establish a system of government.
3. The use of force was necessary to maintain order and to protect the interests of the ruling group.
4. The ruling group established laws and institutions to govern the conquered people and to maintain their control over them.
5. Over time, the conquered people accepted the rule of the conquerors and the state became an established institution.

This theory has been criticized for its simplicity and for its failure to account for the complex social, economic, and political factors that contributed to the emergence of the state. Nonetheless, it remains an important theory in the study of the origin of the state.



#### Mystical Theory of origin of State

The mystical theory of the origin of the state is one of the oldest theories that explains the origin of the state. According to this theory, the state is of divine origin and is created by God or gods. The ruler is considered to be the representative of God on earth and is therefore given the divine right to rule. The subjects are expected to obey the ruler as they would obey God.

Some key points of this theory are:

1. The state is of divine origin and is created by God or gods.
2. The ruler is considered to be the representative of God on earth and is therefore given the divine right to rule.
3. The subjects are expected to obey the ruler as they would obey God.
4. This theory is one of the oldest theories that explains the origin of the state.

This theory has been used to justify the rule of monarchs and emperors throughout history. However, it has been criticized for being unscientific and for not providing a rational explanation for the origin of the state. It is also considered to be outdated in modern times, where the legitimacy of the state is based on the consent of the governed rather than on divine right.



#### Contract Theory of origin of State

Contract theory is a theory that explains the origin of the state as a result of a social contract between individuals in a society. According to this theory, individuals in a society voluntarily give up some of their rights and freedoms to a governing body in exchange for protection and the enforcement of social order.

1. The theory is based on the idea that individuals have natural rights and freedoms that they are entitled to, but that living in a society requires some limitations on these rights in order to maintain social order and protect the rights of others.
2. The social contract is an agreement between individuals to form a government and abide by its rules in exchange for the benefits of living in a society with laws and protections.
3. The theory suggests that the state is formed as a result of this agreement and that its power and authority are derived from the consent of the governed.
4. The theory has been influential in the development of modern political thought and has been used to justify the existence of government and the need for individuals to obey its laws.
5. Some of the most well-known proponents of contract theory include Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau, each of whom had their own unique interpretation of the theory.




### Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

The formation of the state in ancient India is linked with the growth and spread of agriculture, trade, industry, and urbanization. This process began with the spread of agriculture along river valleys, which generated an agricultural surplus that led to trade, commerce, and urbanization. The first states, known as Janapadas, emerged in these river valleys, and this pattern was replicated in the South and East .

According to ancient Indian texts, a kingdom (rajya) was constituted of seven elements (sapta-prakrtayah) or seven limbs (sapta-anga). These were the King (swamin), Minister (amatya), City (pura), Domain/territory (rastra), Treasury (kosa), Army (danda), and Ally (suhrd/mitra) .

In the course of the evolution of the state in Indian history, monarchies gradually conquered the republics in ancient India. Monarchies remained the mode of government in ancient, medieval, and modern India until the British Raj, without any revival of republicanism. The republican form could only be revived after India's Independence in 1947 .

The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate in 1206 AD changed the political landscape of northern India, with the strategically placed capital of Delhi becoming the new seat of central power. India's next imperial unification was under the Great Mughals, from Babur to Akbar (1526–1605 AD) .

### The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

The Seven Limbs of the State, also known as the Saptanga Theory, was formulated by Kautilya in his work, the Arthashastra. According to this theory, a state has seven elements or constituents, namely:

1. **Swamin** - the King: The King is independent and his responsibility for peace, justice, and stability is stressed.
2. **Amatya** - the Minister or Bureaucrats: The Amatya is responsible for the administration of the state.
3. **Janapada** - the Land and the People or territory: The Janapada refers to the territory and the people of the state.
4. **Durga** - the Fortress or Fort: The Durga refers to the fortifications of the state.
5. **Kosha** - the Treasury or treasure: The Kosha refers to the treasury of the state.
6. **Danda** - the Army or coercive authority: The Danda refers to the coercive authority of the state.
7. **Mitra** - the Allies or ally: The Mitra refers to the allies of the state.

This entire set-up of the kingdom was described as the Saptanga theory in ancient India. The Saptanga theory categorically identified the ideal type qualities/excellencies of the state, determined whether a state would evolve into an inferior entity or a superior entity.

#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

Purusārtha is a fundamental principle of social ethics in ancient Indian society. It refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. These goals are not only meant to set an ideal objective but also to convey the right means to achieve them. The four Purusārthas are Dharma, Artha, Kama, and Moksha.

- **Dharma** refers to righteousness and moral values.
- **Artha** refers to prosperity and economic values.
- **Kama** refers to pleasure, love, and emotional fulfillment.
- **Moksha** refers to liberation and spiritual values.

These four goals were considered essential for leading a balanced and fulfilling life. Each goal was given importance in varying degrees, depending on the individual's stage in life and personal circumstances.



#### Varnāshrama System in Society in Ancient India

- The Varnāshrama system, also known as Varnashramadharma, developed in ancient Hindu society and refers to the segmental division of the Hindu society into four classes or varnas  .
- The term ‘Varnashramadharma’ consists of three words - ‘varna’, ‘asrama’, and ‘dharma’ .
- The four varnas are Brahman, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Sudra .
- Brahman is the class of the priests and teachers .
- Kshatriya is the class of the kings, warriors, and aristocrats .
- Vaishya is the class of traders, merchants, and people engaged in other professions .
- Sudra is the class of laborers and service providers .
- Ashrama is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras .
- The four ashramas are Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller), and Sannyasa (renunciate) .

#### Four-class Classification of Society in Ancient India

The four-class classification of society in ancient India, also known as the caste system, was a strict social hierarchy imposed by Hinduism. The Vedas, ancient Indian texts, described four main social classes:

1. **Brahmins**: This class consisted of priests and scholars. Their task was to study and advise.
2. **Kshatriyas**: This class consisted of kings, governors, and warriors. Their task was to protect.
3. **Vaishyas**: This class consisted of cattle herders, agriculturists, artisans, and merchants. Their task was to cultivate.
4. **Shudras**: This class consisted of laborers and service providers. Their task was to serve.

The Dalit, formerly known as "untouchables," were considered outside the system and were treated poorly.

This system helped in achieving greater vocational specialization and gave rise to the four classes of Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras.

#### Harappan Script

- The Harappan Script, also known as the Indus Script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilization .
- Most inscriptions containing these symbols are extremely short, making it difficult to judge whether or not they constituted a writing system used to record the as-yet unidentified language(s) of the Indus Valley Civilization .
- The script found in the Indus Valley from Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro is the oldest known script of India. Unfortunately, till now it has not been read satisfactorily. This has made the problem of the Indus Valley script even more intractable .
- The Harappan script has long defied attempts to read it, and therefore the language remains unknown .
- The Indus Script combined both word signs and symbols with phonetic value. This type of writing system is known as "logo-syllabic", where some symbols express ideas or words while others represent sounds. This view is based on the fact that roughly 400 signs have been identified, which makes it unlikely that the Indus Script was solely phonetic .



#### Brahmi Script
- The Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script.
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi.

### The Vedas

### The Upanishads
- The Upanishads are late Vedic Sanskrit texts that supplied the basis of later Hindu philosophy.
- They are commonly referred to as Vedānta.
- Vedanta has been interpreted as the "last chapters, parts of the Veda" and alternatively as "object, the highest purpose of the Veda".
- The aim of all Upanishads is to investigate the nature of Ātman (self), and "direct [ing] the enquirer toward it".
- There are more than 200 known Upanishads, one of which, the Muktikā Upanishad, predates 1656 CE and contains a list of 108 canonical Upanishads, including itself as the last.
- These are further divided into Upanishads associated with Shaktism (goddess Shakti), Sannyasa (renunciation, monastic life), Shaivism (god Shiva), Vaishnavism (god Vishnu).
- The Upanishads are the philosophical-religious texts of Hinduism (also known as Sanatan Dharma meaning “Eternal Order” or “Eternal Path”) which develop and explain the fundamental tenets of the religion.
- The name is translated as to “sit down closely” as one would to listen attentively to instruction by a teacher or other authority figure.
- The Upanishads present a vision of an interconnected universe with a single, unifying principle behind the apparent diversity in the cosmos, any articulation of which is called brahman.
- Within this context, the Upanishads teach that brahman resides in the atman, the unchanging core of the human individual.

### Various Philosophical Doctrines in India

Indian philosophy refers to the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. These include both orthodox (astika) systems and unorthodox (nastika) systems. The orthodox systems are the Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva-Mimamsa (or Mimamsa), and Vedanta schools of philosophy, while the unorthodox systems include Buddhism and Jainism .

Indian philosophy is characterized by its acceptance of the authority of ancient philosophers, with innovations being represented as extensions of older systems of thought. It is also characterized by its flexibility and tendency to synthesize . Indian schools generally embody the doctrines of karma and rebirth, with the belief that man must be perfected before he can achieve salvation .

Indian thought has been concerned with various philosophical problems, including the nature of the world (cosmology), the nature of reality (metaphysics), logic, the nature of knowledge (epistemology), ethics, and the philosophy of religion . Indian philosophies share many concepts such as dharma, karma, samsara, reincarnation, dukkha, renunciation, and meditation, with almost all of them focusing on the ultimate goal of liberation of the individual through a diverse range of spiritual practices (moksha, nirvana)  .



### Heterodox Sects in India

Heterodox sects refer to the various religious groups and movements that emerged in India as a response to the ritual-ridden religion of the Vedic period. These sects were founded by ascetic wanderers and teachers who attracted groups of followers and established their own philosophies, which encompassed antinomian, materialist, and fatalist elements.

Some of the major heterodox sects that emerged in India include:

1. **Jainism**: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, in 540 BC. Jainism is an ancient Indian religion that teaches the path to liberation and bliss is to live a life of harmlessness and renunciation.

2. **Ājīvika**: A nāstika or "heterodox" school of Indian philosophy believed to be founded in the 5th century BCE by Makkhali Gosāla. It was a Śramaṇa movement and a major rival of Vedic religion, early Buddhism, and Jainism.

3. **Cārvāka**: Also known as Materialism, this philosophical movement condemned the Vedas as ‘a pious fraud’. The Cārvākas believed in the materialistic view of life and rejected the existence of an afterlife, the soul, and the supernatural.

These are just a few examples of the many heterodox sects that emerged in India. These sects challenged the orthodox beliefs and practices of the time and offered alternative paths to spiritual enlightenment.

- Urdu Drama evolved from the prevailing dramatic traditions of North India shaping Rahas or Raas as practiced by exponents like Wajid Ali Shah, Nawab of Awadh. His dramatic experiments led to the famous Inder Sabha of Agha Hasan Amanat and later this tradition took the shape of Parsi theatre.



### Painting as Cultural Heritage of India

- Indian art is among the oldest in the world, with archaeologists discovering paintings that were created over 70,000 years ago .
- Indian paintings hold a special place in history and continue to influence artists to this day .
- Traditional art in India depicted cultural, religious, and political developments through clever metaphors .
- Indian art forms, including paintings, are a part of the country's rich culture and heritage .
- Beautiful bursts of color with vivid patterns and symbolic images capture Indian culture beautifully on the canvas .
- There are many different types of Indian paintings, including Madhubani, Warli, and Murals, each with its own unique style and history .
- Indian paintings have a rich history and diversity, with the earliest known Indian paintings dating back to pre-historic times, such as those found in places like Bhimbetka rock shelters in Madhya Pradesh .
- India has one of the world’s largest collections of songs, music, dance, theatre, folk traditions, performing arts, rites and rituals, paintings and writings that are known as the ‘Intangible Cultural Heritage’ (ICH) of humanity .



### Martial Arts Traditions of India

- Martial arts are codified systems and traditions of combat practices, which are practised for a variety of reasons – self-defence, competition, physical health and fitness, entertainment, as well as mental, physical, and spiritual development.
- India has an ancient tradition in diverse martial arts.
- India's Martial traditions originated in South India.
- India has its own wrestling art called Khusti.
- Indian Martial arts have influenced Asian martial arts in significant ways.
- Kushti is one of the most popular martial arts in India.
- Kalaripayattu is often known as the ‘Mother Of All Martial Arts’.
- This 3000-year old art form originating from Kerala, draws inspiration from the raw power, swift movements and sinuous strength of majestic animals – the lion, tiger, elephant, wild boar, snake and crocodile.
- The ancient Manipuri Martial Art, also known as Huyen Lallong, is Thang-Ta.
- Swords and spears are used in Manipuri Martial arts, a powerful yet gracefully complex technique.
- Lathi is India’s oldest armed martial art.
- It is also regarded as one of the world’s earliest martial arts weapons.



### Fairs and Festivals as Cultural Heritage of India

India is known for its vibrant and diverse culture, and fairs and festivals are an integral part of this culture. These celebrations offer a unique way of experiencing Indian culture at its best. Some of the most famous fairs and festivals of India include:

1. **Modhera Dance Festival of Gujarat**: Also called the Uttarardh Mahotsavaor, this festival is a cultural celebration inside the premises of Modhera Sun Temple.
2. **Pongal of South India**: Pongal is the harvest festival of the land.
3. **Konark Dance Festival of Odisha**.
4. **Jaisalmer Desert Festival**.
5. **Goa Carnival**.
6. **Nehru Trophy Boat Race of Kerala**.
7. **Pushkar Mela**.
8. **Bikaner Camel Festival**.

These are just a few examples of the numerous fairs and festivals celebrated in India. Each festival has its own unique customs and traditions, and they are celebrated with great enthusiasm and spirit by the people of India. These celebrations provide a glimpse into the rich cultural heritage of India.
