### State in Ancient India

- States in ancient India were loosely categorized into monarchy, republican, and oligarchy. Monarchy was the most prevalent type of state in ancient India.
- The state in ancient India was considered necessary, for it ensured peace, order, and happiness. It was a social organization with political power.
- Ancient scholars were not unanimous in their opinion with regard to the origin of the state. The three theories of the origin of the state in ancient India are as follows: 1. Social Contract Theory 2. Divine Origin Theory 3. Organic Theory.
- The core issues in the study of political science are the state and the government. The institution of the state is studied in relation to its origin, nature, aims, and functions of the state in ancient India.



#### Theories of origin of State in India

There are three main theories of the origin of the state in ancient India. These are:

1. **Social Contract Theory**: This theory suggests that the state was formed as a result of an agreement between individuals to give up some of their rights and freedoms in exchange for protection and security provided by the state.

2. **Divine Origin Theory**: This theory suggests that the state was created by a divine power and that the ruler of the state was chosen by the gods to govern the people.

3. **Organic Theory**: This theory suggests that the state is a natural and organic growth that emerged from the family and the clan as a result of the need for protection and security.

These theories provide different perspectives on the origin and nature of the state in ancient India  .



#### Evolutionary Theory of origin of State

The Evolutionary Theory of the origin of the state is one of the several theories that attempt to explain how states or governments came into existence. According to this theory, the state is a natural development that emerged from the gradual evolution of human society.

1. The theory suggests that the state evolved from the family, which was the earliest form of human organization. The head of the family was the authority figure, and the family members were bound by ties of kinship and obedience.

2. As the family grew in size and complexity, it evolved into a clan or tribe. The clan or tribe was a larger group of people who were related by blood or marriage and who lived together in a specific territory.

3. The clan or tribe was governed by a council of elders or a chief, who was responsible for making decisions and enforcing rules. The members of the clan or tribe were bound by common customs, traditions, and beliefs.

4. As the population grew and the territory expanded, the clan or tribe evolved into a state. The state was a political organization that had a defined territory, a government, and a system of laws.

5. The state had the power to enforce its laws and to protect its citizens from external threats. The citizens of the state were bound by a social contract, in which they agreed to obey the laws of the state in exchange for protection and other benefits.

In summary, the Evolutionary Theory of the origin of the state suggests that the state is a natural development that emerged from the gradual evolution of human society, from the family to the clan or tribe, and finally to the state. This theory emphasizes the role of social, economic, and political factors in the development of the state.



#### Force Theory of origin of State
- The Force Theory is one of the theories that explains the origin of the state.
- According to this theory, the state was created when one group or individual was able to establish control over a territory and its inhabitants through the use of force.
- The group or individual then used this control to establish a government and enforce laws.
- This theory suggests that the state originated from the need for protection and security, and that the use of force was necessary to establish and maintain order.
- Critics of this theory argue that it oversimplifies the complex process of state formation and does not account for the role of cooperation and consent in the establishment of a state.



#### Mystical Theory of origin of State

The mystical theory of the origin of the state is one of the oldest theories of state formation. According to this theory, the state is a divine institution, created by God or the gods, and its rulers are divinely ordained to govern. The theory holds that the state is not a human creation, but rather a supernatural entity that exists to fulfill a divine purpose.

Some key points of the mystical theory of the origin of the state are:

1. The state is a divine institution, created by God or the gods.
2. The rulers of the state are divinely ordained to govern.
3. The state is not a human creation, but rather a supernatural entity.
4. The state exists to fulfill a divine purpose.

This theory has been influential in many societies throughout history, particularly in those with strong religious traditions. It has been used to justify the rule of monarchs and other leaders, who were seen as having a divine right to govern. However, the theory has also been criticized for its lack of empirical evidence and for its potential to justify authoritarian rule.

### The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

The Seven Limbs of the State, also known as the Saptanga Theory, was formulated by Kautilya in his work, the Arthashastra. According to this theory, a state has seven elements or constituents, namely:

1. **Swamin** - the King: The King is independent and his responsibility for peace, justice, and stability is stressed.
2. **Amatya** - the Minister or Bureaucrats: The Amatya is responsible for the administration of the state.
3. **Janapada** - the Land and the People or territory: The Janapada refers to the territory and its inhabitants.
4. **Durga** - the Fortress or Fort: The Durga refers to the fortifications of the state.
5. **Kosha** - the Treasury or treasure: The Kosha refers to the state's treasury.
6. **Danda** - the Army or coercive authority: The Danda refers to the state's military and its ability to enforce its authority.
7. **Mitra** - the Allies or ally: The Mitra refers to the state's allies.

This entire set-up of the kingdom was described as the Saptanga theory in ancient India. These seven limbs were considered the ideal qualities or excellencies of the state, and determined whether a state would evolve into an inferior or superior entity.

#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

Purusārtha is a fundamental principle of social ethics in ancient Indian society. It refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life, which are Dharma (righteousness, moral values), Artha (prosperity, economic values), Kama (pleasure, love), and Moksha (liberation, spiritual values)  .

- **Dharma** refers to the moral and ethical duties of an individual, which includes both personal and social responsibilities. It is considered the foundation of the other three Purusārthas.
- **Artha** refers to the pursuit of wealth and material prosperity, which is necessary for the fulfillment of one's duties and responsibilities.
- **Kama** refers to the pursuit of pleasure and enjoyment, which includes both sensual and aesthetic pleasures.
- **Moksha** refers to the ultimate goal of liberation from the cycle of birth and death, and the attainment of spiritual enlightenment.

These four Purusārthas are not only meant to set an ideal objective but also to convey the right means to achieve them . They provide a framework for leading a balanced and fulfilling life, and for the attainment of both material and spiritual goals.

- From early times Indian Society is divided into four, with specific duties assigned to each other. The term ‘Varnashramadharma’ consists of three words - ‘varna’, asrama, ‘dharma’. Theoretically, the Varna system refers to the segmental division of the Hindu society into four classes.

- In the Varnashramadharma system, there are four varnas ie Brahman, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Sudra. Brahman is the class of the priests and teachers. Kshatriya is the class of the kings, warriors, and aristocrats. Vaishya is the class of traders, merchants, and people engaged in other professions.

- Ashrama is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras. The four ashramas are: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller), and Sannyasa (renunciate).

- Ancient India in the Vedic Period (c. 1500-1000 BCE) did not have social stratification based on socio-economic indicators; rather, citizens were classified according to their Varna or castes. 'Varna' defines the hereditary roots of a newborn, it indicates the color, type, order, or class of people.

#### Four-class Classification of Society in Ancient India

In ancient India, society was divided into four classes, or varnas, based on occupation and social status. These four classes were:

1. **Brahmins**: The priestly class, responsible for performing religious rituals and ceremonies, and for teaching and preserving sacred knowledge.
2. **Kshatriyas**: The warrior class, responsible for protecting the people and maintaining law and order.
3. **Vaishyas**: The merchant class, responsible for trade and commerce.
4. **Shudras**: The laborer class, responsible for performing manual labor and serving the other three classes.

This system of classification was based on the principle of division of labor and was intended to ensure the smooth functioning of society. However, over time, it became rigid and hereditary, leading to social inequality and discrimination.

#### Harappan Script

- The Harappan Script, also known as the Indus Script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilization .
- Most inscriptions containing these symbols are extremely short, making it difficult to judge whether or not they constituted a writing system used to record the as-yet unidentified language(s) of the Indus Valley Civilization .
- The script found in the Indus Valley from Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro is the oldest known script of India. Unfortunately, till now it has not been read satisfactorily. This has made the problem of the Indus Valley script even more intractable .
- The Harappan script has long defied attempts to read it, and therefore the language remains unknown .
- The Indus Script combined both word signs and symbols with phonetic value. This type of writing system is known as "logo-syllabic", where some symbols express ideas or words while others represent sounds. This view is based on the fact that roughly 400 signs have been identified, which makes it unlikely that the Indus Script was solely phonetic .



#### Brahmi Script

- The Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script .
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi .
- The Brahmi writing system, or script, appeared as a fully developed universal one in South Asia in the third century BCE .
- It is a forerunner of all writing systems that have found use in South Asia with the exception of the Indus script of the third millennium BCE, the Kharosthi script, which originated in what today is northwestern Pakistan in the fourth or possibly fifth century BCE, the Perso-Arabic Scripts of the medieval period, and the Latin scripts of the modern period .
- The Brahmi script is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations .
- For example, the Lipisala samdarshana parivarta lists 64 lipi (scripts), with the Brahmi script starting the list .
- The Brahmi script was already divided into regional variants at the time of the earliest surviving epigraphy around the 3rd century BC .
- Cursives of the Brahmi script began to diversify further from around the 5th century AD and continued to give rise to new scripts throughout the Middle Ages .
- The Brāhmī alphabet is the ancestor of most of the 40 or so modern Indian alphabets Khmer and Tibetan .
- It is thought to have been modelled on the Aramaic or Phoenician alphabets, and appeared in India sometime before 500 BC .
- Another theory is that Brāhmī developed from the Indus or Harappa script, which was used in the Indus valley until around 1300 BC .



### The Vedas

- The Vedas are a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India.
- Composed in Vedic Sanskrit, the texts constitute the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the oldest scriptures of Hinduism.
- There are four Vedas: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda.
- The term veda means “knowledge” in that they are thought to contain the fundamental knowledge relating to the underlying cause of, function of, and personal response to existence.
- The Vedas inform the religion of Hinduism (also known as Sanatan Dharma meaning “Eternal Order” or “Eternal Path”).
- The word Veda means wisdom, knowledge or vision, and it serves to manifest the language of the gods in human speech.
- The laws of the Vedas have regulated the social, legal, domestic and religious customs of Hindus up to the present day.
- All the obligatory duties of Hindus at birth, marriage, death etc. are guided by Vedic rituals.



### The Upanishads
- The Upanishads are late Vedic Sanskrit texts that supplied the basis of later Hindu philosophy.
- They are commonly referred to as Vedānta, which has been interpreted as the "last chapters, parts of the Veda" and alternatively as "object, the highest purpose of the Veda".
- The aim of all Upanishads is to investigate the nature of Ātman (self), and "direct [ing] the enquirer toward it".
- There are more than 200 known Upanishads, one of which, the Muktikā Upanishad, predates 1656 CE and contains a list of 108 canonical Upanishads, including itself as the last.
- These are further divided into Upanishads associated with Shaktism (goddess Shakti), Sannyasa (renunciation, monastic life), Shaivism (god Shiva), Vaishnavism (god Vishnu).
- The Upanishads present a vision of an interconnected universe with a single, unifying principle behind the apparent diversity in the cosmos, any articulation of which is called brahman.
- Within this context, the Upanishads teach that brahman resides in the atman, the unchanging core of the human individual.



### The Ramayana

- The Ramayana is an ancient Indian epic, composed some time in the 5th century BCE, about the exile and then return of Rama, prince of Ayodhya.
- It was composed in Sanskrit by the sage Valmiki, who taught it to Rama's sons, the twins Lava and Kush.
- At about 24000 verses, it is a rather long poem and, by tradition, is known as the Adi Kavya.
- The Ramayana belongs to the genre of Itihasa, narratives of past events (purāvṛtta), which includes the Mahabharata, the Puranas, and the Ramayana. The genre also includes teachings on the goals of human life.
- The Ramayana was an important influence on later Sanskrit poetry and the Hindu life and culture, and its main characters were fundamental to the cultural consciousness of a number of nations, both Hindu and Buddhist.



### The Mahabharata
- The Mahabharata is an ancient Sanskrit epic poem that tells the story of the kingdom of Kurus .
- It is based on a real war that took place in the 13th or 14th century B.C. between the Kuru and Panchala tribes of the Indian subcontinent .
- It is regarded as both a historical account of Hinduism's birth and a code of ethics for the faithful .
- The Mahabharata is an important source of information on the development of Hinduism between 400 BCE and 200 CE .
- It is regarded by Hindus as both a text about dharma (Hindu moral law) and a history (itihasa, literally “that’s what happened”) .
- The Mahabharata is one of the two Sanskrit epic poems of ancient India (the other being the Ramayana) .
- The Mahabharata is the longest epic poem known and has been described as "the longest poem ever written" .
- Its longest version consists of over 100,000 śloka or over 200,000 individual verse lines (each shloka is a couplet), and long prose passages .
- The first Bengali translations of the Mahabharata emerged in the 16th century .
- It is disputed whether Kavindra Parameshwar of Hooghly (based in Chittagong during his writing) or Sri Sanjay of Sylhet was the first to translate it into Bengali .



### Puranas

- Puranas are a vast genre of Indian literature about a wide range of topics, particularly about legends and other traditional lore .
- The word "Purana" literally means "ancient" or "old" in Sanskrit  .
- Puranas are known for the intricate layers of symbolism depicted within their stories .
- Puranas are considered one of the most ancient Indian literature about Hinduism and are post-Vedic Texts .
- Puranas are regarded as the fifth Veda .
- Puranas are a class of literary texts, all written in Sanskrit verse .
- The composition of Puranas dates from the 4th century BCE to about 1,000 A.D .
- Puranas are popular encyclopedic collections of myth, legend, and genealogy .
- Puranas were written almost entirely in narrative couplets, in much the same easy flowing style as the two great Sanskrit epic poems, the Mahabharata and the Ramayana .
- Puranas are ancient Hindu texts eulogizing various deities of the Hindu pantheon through divine stories .
- Puranas can be categorized under the same class as the 'Itihasas' or Histories - the Ramayana and the Mahabharata .
- Puranas are believed to have been derived from the same religious system as the Ramayana and the Mahabharata .



### Buddhist Literature in Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit

- **Pali** is an old Prakrit dialect that combines several dialects. Lord Buddha delivered his discourses in Pali, and as a result, early Buddhist literature was written in Pali.
- **Prakrit** is a catch-all name for any language that differs from the mainstream one, Sanskrit.
- The first Buddhist works were written in Pali, the language spoken in Magadha and South Bihar. It is said that Lord Buddha delivered his sermons in Pali, and they have been recorded in the same language.
- Pali still serves as the canonical language of Buddhism in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Other Prakrit languages such as Sauraseni, Maharastri, Magadhi, and Gandhari embody various facets of the literatures of both the Brahmanical/Hindu and Buddhist traditions.
- Early Buddhist texts are therefore often in Prakrit (especially Gāndhārī) or some nonstandard form of Sanskrit, sometimes called Buddhist Sanskrit, an intermediate stage between some Prakrit and standard Sanskrit.




### Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit

- Jain literature refers to the literature of the Jain religion. It is a vast and ancient literary tradition, which was initially transmitted orally.
- The oldest surviving material is contained in the canonical Jain Agamas, which are written in Ardhamagadhi, a Prakrit (Middle-Indo Aryan) language.
- Early Jain literature is mostly written in the Prakrit language, which was a form of the Sanskrit language.
- Pali language was a form of the Prakrit language which was used in Magadha.
- Pali and Prakrit were the first languages to emerge from Sanskrit. Pali was used for the dissemination of Buddhistic ideas, whereas Prakrit was used for the dissemination of Jain doctrines.
- The majority of Buddhist literature is written in Pali, while Jain literature is written in Prakrit.



### Kautilya’s Arthashastra

- The Arthashastra is an Indian treatise on politics, economics, military strategy, the function of the state, and social organization .
- It is attributed to the philosopher and Prime Minister Kautilya (also known as Chanakya, Vishnugupta, l. c. 350-275 BCE) .
- Kautilya was instrumental in establishing the reign of the great king Chandragupta Maurya (r. c. 321-c.297 BCE), founder of the Mauryan Empire (322-185 BCE) .
- The Arthashastra refers to a practice of political diplomacy that emerged in India .
- Kautilya was a professor at Taxila University and later the Mauryan Empire’s Prime Minister .
- The Arthashastra is also spelled Artha-śāstra and means “The Science of Material Gain” in Sanskrit .
- Kautilya's Arthashastra depicts a bureaucratic welfare state, in fact some kind of socialized monarchy, in which the central government administers the details of the economy for the common good .
- In addition, Kautilya offers a work of genius in matters of foreign policy and welfare, including key principles of international relations .

### Sangama Literature in India

- Sangama literature, also spelled as cankam, chankam, or shangam, refers to the earliest writings in the Tamil language.

### Six System Indian Philosophy

The Six Systems of Indian Philosophy refers to several traditions of philosophical thought that originated in the Indian subcontinent. Over centuries, India’s intellectual exploration of truth has come to be represented by six systems of philosophy. These are known as:

1. **Nyaya**: Founded by Akshapada Gautama and dated back to 2nd Century BCE, it is a school that deals with logic and epistemology.
2. **Vaisheshika**: Popularly understood as the atomistic school of Indian Philosophy.
3. **Samkhya**: An extremely complex school of philosophy that goes deep into the understanding of the creator and the creation.
4. **Yoga**: Also known as Ashtanga Yoga.
5. **Purva Mimamsa**: One of the six systems of Indian Philosophy.
6. **Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta**: The term Vedanta means in Sanskrit the “conclusion” (anta) of the Vedas, the earliest sacred literature of India. It applies to the Upanishads, which were elaborations of the Vedas, and to the school that arose out of the study (mimamsa) of the Upanishads.

Each of these systems differs in one way or the other in terms of its concepts, phenomena, laws and dogmas.



### Shankaracharya and Advaita Vedanta

- Shankaracharya, also known as Adi Shankara, was an Indian philosopher and theologian who expounded the doctrine of Advaita Vedanta.
- He renounced worldly pleasures at a very young age and amalgamated the ideologies of ancient Advaita Vedanta, explaining the basic ideas of the Upanishads.
- Shankaracharya is the most renowned exponent of the Advaita Vedanta school of philosophy, from whose doctrines the main currents of modern Indian thought are derived.
- He established mathas, which are thought to have aided in the historical growth, renewal, and dissemination of his philosophy.
- Shankara is one of the most widely known and influential Indian philosophers from the classical period, and the most authoritative philosopher of Advaita Vedānta.
- The cultural influence of Shankara and Advaita Vedanta started only centuries later, in the Vijayanagara Empire in the 14th century, when Sringeri matha started to receive patronage from the kings of the Vijayanagara Empire and became a powerful institution.



### Various Philosophical Doctrines in India

- Indian philosophy refers to the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent .
- These systems include both orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) schools of philosophy .
- The orthodox systems are the Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva-Mimamsa (or Mimamsa), and Vedanta schools of philosophy .
- The unorthodox systems include Buddhism and Jainism .
- Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought .
- Flexibility and the tendency to synthesize are also characteristic of Indian philosophy .
- Indian schools generally embody the doctrines of karma and rebirth; man must be perfected before he can achieve salvation .
- Indian thought has been concerned with various philosophical problems, significant among which are the nature of the world (cosmology), the nature of reality (metaphysics), logic, the nature of knowledge (epistemology), ethics, and the philosophy of religion .
- Indian philosophies share many concepts such as dharma, karma, samsara, reincarnation, dukkha, renunciation, meditation, with almost all of them focussing on the ultimate goal of liberation of the individual through a diverse range of spiritual practices (moksha, nirvana)  .



### Heterodox Sects in India

Heterodox sects refer to the religious movements that emerged in ancient India as a response to the ritualistic and orthodox practices of the Vedic period. These sects were founded by ascetic wanderers and teachers who attracted groups of followers and established various sects. Their philosophies encompassed antinomian, materialist, and fatalist elements .

Some of the major heterodox sects that emerged in ancient India include:

1. **Jainism**: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, in the 6th century BCE. Mahavira was born to Siddhartha, head of the Jnatrika clan, and Trishala, in Kundagrama .
2. **Ajivika**: Founded by Makkhali Gosala in the 5th century BCE, Ajivika was a major rival of Vedic religion, early Buddhism, and Jainism. It was a Śramaṇa movement .
3. **Cārvāka**: Also known as the Materialist school of thought, Cārvāka philosophy condemned the Vedas as a "pious fraud" .

These sects challenged the orthodox practices of the time and offered alternative philosophical and religious beliefs to the people. They played a significant role in shaping the religious landscape of ancient India.



### Bhakti Movement in India

- Bhakti movement preached against the caste system using the local languages so that the message reached the masses. One who practices bhakti is called a bhakta.
- The Bhakti movement, believed to have originated in Tamil Nadu sometime around the 7th and 8th centuries was a cultural, social and religious revolution, the ramifications of which are felt to this day as the movement gained impetus, galvanised the nation in waves and manifested its influence in India’s freedom struggle.
- Bhakti, (Sanskrit: “devotion”) in Hinduism, is a movement emphasizing the mutual intense emotional attachment and love of a devotee toward a personal god and of the god for the devotee. According to the Bhagavadgita, a Hindu religious text, the path of bhakti, or bhakti-marga, is superior to the two other religious approaches, the path of knowledge (jnana) and the path of ritual and good works (karma).

- India's Martial traditions originated in South India.
- India has its own wrestling art called Khusti.
- Indian Martial arts have influenced Asian martial arts in significant ways.
- Kushti is one of the most popular martial arts in India. It started off during the Mughal times where Malla Yuddha, a local sport was combined with a Persian sport named Varzesh-e-Bastani.
- Indian martial arts refers to the fighting systems of the Indian subcontinent. A variety of terms are used for the English phrases “Indian martial arts”, deriving from ancient sources.
- Kalaripayattu is often known as the ‘Mother Of All Martial Arts’, this 3000-year old art form originating from Kerala, draws inspiration from the raw power, swift movements and sinuous strength of majestic animals – the lion, tiger, elephant, wild boar, snake and crocodile.
- The ancient Manipuri Martial Art, also known as Huyen Lallong, is Thang-Ta. Swords and spears are used in Manipuri Martial arts, a powerful yet gracefully complex technique.
- Lathi is India’s oldest armed martial art. It is also regarded as one of the world’s earliest martial arts weapons.



### Fairs and Festivals as Cultural Heritage of India

India is known for its vibrant and diverse culture, which is reflected in the numerous fairs and festivals celebrated throughout the country. These events offer a unique way to experience Indian culture at its best and are an integral part of the country's cultural heritage.

Some of the most famous fairs and festivals in India include:
- **Modhera Dance Festival of Gujarat**: Also known as Uttarardh Mahotsavaor, this festival is a cultural celebration held within the premises of the Modhera Sun Temple.
- **Pongal of South India**: Pongal is a harvest festival celebrated in the southern part of India.
- **Konark Dance Festival of Odisha**: This festival celebrates the classical dance forms of India.
- **Jaisalmer Desert Festival**: This festival is held in the desert city of Jaisalmer and celebrates the rich culture and heritage of Rajasthan.
- **Goa Carnival**: This festival is a celebration of the vibrant culture of Goa and is known for its colorful parades and lively music.
- **Nehru Trophy Boat Race of Kerala**: This boat race is held in the backwaters of Kerala and is a popular event among locals and tourists alike.
- **Pushkar Mela**: This fair is held in the city of Pushkar and is one of the largest camel fairs in the world.
- **Bikaner Camel Festival**: This festival celebrates the camel, an important animal in the desert state of Rajasthan.

These are just a few examples of the many fairs and festivals celebrated in India. Each event is unique and offers a glimpse into the rich cultural heritage of the country.



### Current developments in Arts and Cultural in India

- India has a rich and diverse cultural heritage, with one of the world’s largest collections of songs, music, dance, theatre, folk traditions, performing arts, rites and rituals, paintings and writings that are known as the ‘Intangible Cultural Heritage’ (ICH) of humanity.
- The country is actively working towards creating a unique cultural identity for itself, with a number of tier 1 and tier 2 cities actively working towards this goal.
- In addition to the annual Indian Art Fair, various new museums and exhibitions are emerging. The Kiran Nadar Museum of Art is developing a new, forthcoming institution; the Museum of Art and Photography will open in Bangalore; the Gujral Foundation is commissioning a public pavilion series in Delhi; and the Kochi Biennale will have its fifth edition this December.
- Recently, three cities in India have joined the UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities. These include two cities Thrissur and Nilambur in Kerala and Warangal in Telangana.
- The Ministry of Culture, Government of India, provides financial assistance for cultural activities in performing arts, for building grants including studio theatres, for allied cultural activities, and for Tagore Cultural Complexes (TCC). The ministry also has a scheme of scholarship and fellowship for the promotion of art and culture.



### Indian’s Cultural Contribution to the World

India has made numerous contributions to the world in various fields such as mathematics, architecture, textiles, medicine, language, and art. Here are some of the most significant contributions:

1. **Mathematics**: Hindus were precursors to mathematics. They made significant contributions to the field of mathematics, including the invention of the number zero and the decimal system.

2. **Architecture**: India is known for its picturesque and quirky urban architecture. The country has a rich architectural heritage, with numerous historical monuments and buildings that showcase its architectural prowess.

3. **Textiles**: The cotton fabrics produced in India are among the best in the world. India has a long history of textile production and has been a major exporter of textiles for centuries.

4. **Medicine**: Hindus knew the technique to sterilize and use drugs to heal the sick. India has a rich tradition of medicine, with ancient medical texts such as the Charaka Samhita and the Sushruta Samhita that laid the foundations for Ayurveda, a system of medicine that is still widely practiced in India today.

5. **Language**: India is the richest linguistic nation in the world, with a diverse range of languages spoken throughout the country. Indian languages have had a significant impact on other languages, with numerous words of Indian origin found in languages such as English.

6. **Art**: India has a rich tradition of art, with various art forms such as painting, sculpture, literature, music, dance, and theatre. Indian art has had a significant impact on the world, with Indian art forms being appreciated and practiced globally.

These are just a few of the many contributions that India has made to the world. India's rich cultural heritage and diverse population have resulted in a wealth of knowledge and creativity that has enriched the world in numerous ways.
