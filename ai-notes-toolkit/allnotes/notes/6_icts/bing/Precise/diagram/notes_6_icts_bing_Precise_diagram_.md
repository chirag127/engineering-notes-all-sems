#### Theories of origin of State in India

There are three main theories of the origin of the state in ancient India. These are:

1. **Social Contract Theory**: This theory suggests that the state was formed as a result of an agreement between individuals to give up some of their rights in exchange for protection and security provided by the state.

2. **Divine Origin Theory**: This theory suggests that the state was created by a divine power and that the rulers of the state were chosen by the gods.

3. **Organic Theory**: This theory suggests that the state is a natural and organic growth, arising from the needs of society and evolving over time.

The institution of the state is studied in relation to its origin, nature, aims, and functions in ancient India . These theories provide different perspectives on the formation and role of the state in ancient Indian society.



#### Evolutionary Theory of origin of State

The Evolutionary Theory of the origin of the state is one of the major theories concerning the origin of the state. According to this theory, the state is a natural development from the family and social organization of early human societies.

1. The theory suggests that the state originated from the family, with the head of the family being the first ruler.
2. As families grew and formed clans, the head of the clan became the ruler.
3. As clans joined together to form tribes, the head of the tribe became the ruler.
4. Eventually, tribes joined together to form a state, with the head of the state being the ruler.
5. The state, according to this theory, is a natural progression from the family, clan, and tribe, with the ruler being the head of the state.

This theory is based on the idea that the state is a natural development from the social organization of early human societies. It suggests that the state is a necessary and inevitable institution that arose as human societies became more complex.



#### Force Theory of Origin of State

The Force Theory of Origin of State is one of the several theories that explain the origin of the state. According to this theory, the state came into existence as a result of the use of force. Here are some key points about this theory:

1. The theory suggests that the state was created when one group or individual was able to gain control over a territory and its inhabitants through the use of force.
2. The group or individual that gained control then established a government to maintain their power and control over the territory and its inhabitants.
3. The use of force could have taken many forms, including warfare, conquest, or subjugation.
4. The theory is often associated with the idea of "might makes right," where the group or individual with the most power is able to establish and maintain control.
5. Critics of the theory argue that it oversimplifies the complex processes involved in the formation of states and that it ignores other factors such as economic, social, and cultural developments.




#### Mystical Theory of origin of State

The mystical theory of the origin of the state is one of the oldest theories that explains the formation of the state. According to this theory, the state is of divine origin and is created by God or gods. The ruler is considered to be the representative of God on earth and is responsible for maintaining order and justice in society. Here are some key points about this theory:

1. The state is considered to be of divine origin and is created by God or gods.
2. The ruler is considered to be the representative of God on earth.
3. The ruler is responsible for maintaining order and justice in society.
4. The theory is based on the belief in the divine right of kings, where the monarch is considered to be chosen by God to rule.
5. This theory was prevalent in ancient civilizations such as Egypt, Mesopotamia, and China, where the rulers were considered to be divine or semi-divine.
6. The theory has been criticized for being unscientific and for justifying the absolute power of the ruler.




#### Contract Theory of origin of State

The Contract Theory of the origin of the state is a concept in political philosophy that explains the formation of states and governments. According to this theory, the state is formed as a result of a social contract between individuals in a society.

1. The theory suggests that individuals in a society come together and agree to give up some of their individual rights and freedoms in exchange for protection and security provided by the state.
2. This social contract is seen as a voluntary agreement between individuals and the state, where the state is given the power to govern and enforce laws in order to maintain order and protect the rights of its citizens.
3. The Contract Theory is often associated with philosophers such as Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau, who each had their own interpretations of the concept.
4. Hobbes, for example, believed that the state of nature was a state of war and that individuals needed to surrender their rights to a sovereign in order to ensure their own survival.
5. Locke, on the other hand, believed that individuals had natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and that the state was formed to protect these rights.
6. Rousseau believed that the social contract was not just between individuals and the state, but also between individuals themselves, and that the state was formed to ensure the common good of all its citizens.
7. The Contract Theory has been influential in shaping modern political thought and has been used to justify the existence of states and governments.




### Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

The formation of states in ancient India was a gradual process that involved the creation and appropriation of surplus. This process was closely linked with the growth and spread of agriculture, trade, industry, and urbanization. The spread of agriculture along river valleys led to the generation of agricultural surplus, which in turn led to the development of trade, commerce, and urbanization. The first states, known as Janapadas, emerged in these river valleys, and this pattern was replicated in the South and East of India.

According to ancient Indian texts, a kingdom, or rajya, was constituted of seven elements, or sapta-prakrtayah, also known as the seven limbs, or sapta-anga. These were the king (swamin), minister (amatya), city (pura), domain/territory (rastra), treasury (kosa), army (danda), and ally (suhrd/mitra).

In the course of the evolution of the state in Indian history, monarchies gradually conquered the republics in ancient India. Monarchies remained the mode of government in ancient, medieval, and modern India until the British Raj, without any revival of republicanism. The republican form of government could only be revived after India's independence in 1947.

The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate in 1206 AD changed the political landscape of northern India, with the strategically placed capital of Delhi becoming the new seat of central power. India's next imperial unification occurred under the Great Mughals, from Babur to Akbar (1526-1605 AD).

### The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

According to Kautilya, a state has seven elements or constituents, namely, Swamin— the King, Amatya—the Minister, Janapada—the Land, and the People, Durga—the Fortress, Kosha—the Treasury, Danda—the Army and Mitra—the Allies. This entire set-up of the kingdom was described as Saptanga theory in ancient India .

1. **Swamin (The King)**: The King is independent and his responsibility for peace, justice and stability is stressed .
2. **Amatya (The Minister)**: The Minister or Bureaucrats are responsible for the administration of the state .
3. **Janapada (The Land and the People)**: The Land and the People are the territory of the state .
4. **Durga (The Fortress)**: The Fortress is responsible for the defense of the state .
5. **Kosha (The Treasury)**: The Treasury is responsible for the financial resources of the state .
6. **Danda (The Army)**: The Army is responsible for the coercive authority of the state .
7. **Mitra (The Allies)**: The Allies are responsible for the diplomatic relations of the state .

This theory was formulated by Kautilya in Arthashastra and refers to the seven organs of the state . The Saptanga theory, which categorically identified the ideal type qualities/excellencies of the state, determined whether a state would evolve into an inferior entity or a superior entity .

#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

Purusārtha is a fundamental principle of social ethics in ancient Indian society. It refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. These goals are not only meant to set an ideal objective but also to convey the right means to achieve them. The four Purusārthas are Dharma, Artha, Kama, and Moksha.

- **Dharma** refers to righteousness and moral values.
- **Artha** refers to prosperity and economic values.
- **Kama** refers to pleasure, love, and emotional fulfillment.
- **Moksha** refers to liberation from the cycle of birth and death.

The concept of Purusārtha is closely related to the four classes in society and the four stages in the life of an individual. The eventual goal of the varying darsanas is not only to achieve these four aims but also to lead a balanced life through the pursuit of Purusārtha   .



#### Varnāshrama System in Society in Ancient India

The Varnāshrama system, also known as Varnashramadharma, was a social structure that developed in ancient Hindu society. The term ‘Varnashramadharma’ consists of three words - ‘varna’, ‘asrama’, and ‘dharma’. Theoretically, the Varna system refers to the segmental division of the Hindu society into four classes.

The four varnas are:
1. **Brahman**: the class of priests and teachers.
2. **Kshatriya**: the class of kings, warriors, and aristocrats.
3. **Vaishya**: the class of traders, merchants, and people engaged in other professions.
4. **Sudra**: the class of laborers.

In addition to the Varna system, the Ashrama system was also a part of the Varnāshrama system. The Ashrama system is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras. The four ashramas are: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller), and Sannyasa (renunciate).

The Varnāshrama system was a way to organize society and assign specific duties to each class. It was based on the idea of dharma, or the moral and religious duties of an individual. The system was meant to ensure the smooth functioning of society and the fulfillment of an individual's dharma. However, over time, the system became rigid and was often used to justify discrimination and social inequality.

#### Four-class Classification of Society in Ancient India

In ancient India, society was divided into four classes, or varnas, based on occupation and social status. These classes were:

1. **Brahmins**: The priestly class, responsible for performing religious rituals and providing spiritual guidance.
2. **Kshatriyas**: The warrior class, responsible for protecting the people and maintaining law and order.
3. **Vaishyas**: The merchant class, responsible for trade and commerce.
4. **Shudras**: The laboring class, responsible for performing manual labor and serving the other classes.

This system of classification was rigid and hereditary, with individuals born into a particular class and expected to follow the occupation of their parents. The system also included a fifth group, known as the "untouchables," who were considered outside the varna system and performed tasks considered impure or unclean.

The varna system was an integral part of ancient Indian society and influenced many aspects of daily life, including marriage, occupation, and social interactions. However, it has been criticized for perpetuating social inequality and discrimination. Today, the caste system is illegal in India, but its influence can still be seen in some aspects of society.

#### Harappan Script

- The Harappan Script, also known as the Indus Script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilization.
- Most inscriptions containing these symbols are extremely short, making it difficult to judge whether or not they constituted a writing system used to record the as-yet unidentified language(s) of the Indus Valley Civilization.
- The script found in the Indus Valley from Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro is the oldest known script of India. Unfortunately, till now it has not been read satisfactorily. This has made the problem of the Indus Valley script even more intractable.
- The Harappan script has long defied attempts to read it, and therefore the language remains unknown.
- The Indus Script combined both word signs and symbols with phonetic value. This type of writing system is known as "logo-syllabic", where some symbols express ideas or words while others represent sounds. This view is based on the fact that roughly 400 signs have been identified, which makes it unlikely that the Indus Script was solely phonetic.



#### Brahmi Script

- The Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script .
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi .
- The Brahmi writing system, or script, appeared as a fully developed universal one in South Asia in the third century BCE .
- It is a forerunner of all writing systems that have found use in South Asia with the exception of the Indus script of the third millennium BCE, the Kharosthi script, which originated in what today is northwestern Pakistan in the fourth or possibly fifth century BCE, the Perso-Arabic Scripts of the medieval period, and the Latin scripts of the modern period .
- The Brahmi script is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations .
- For example, the Lipisala samdarshana parivarta lists 64 lipi (scripts), with the Brahmi script starting the list .
- The Brahmi script was already divided into regional variants at the time of the earliest surviving epigraphy around the 3rd century BC .
- Cursives of the Brahmi script began to diversify further from around the 5th century AD and continued to give rise to new scripts throughout the Middle Ages .
- The Brāhmī alphabet is the ancestor of most of the 40 or so modern Indian alphabets, Khmer and Tibetan .
- It is thought to have been modelled on the Aramaic or Phoenician alphabets, and appeared in India sometime before 500 BC .
- Another theory is that Brāhmī developed from the Indus or Harappa script, which was used in the Indus valley until around 1300 BC .



### The Vedas

- The Vedas are a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India.
- Composed in Vedic Sanskrit, the texts constitute the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the oldest scriptures of Hinduism.
- The term "veda" means "knowledge" and they are thought to contain the fundamental knowledge relating to the underlying cause of, function of, and personal response to existence.
- There are four Vedas: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda, and the Atharvaveda.
- The Vedas have regulated the social, legal, domestic, and religious customs of Hindus up to the present day.
- All the obligatory duties of Hindus at birth, marriage, death, etc. are guided by Vedic rituals.
- The word Veda means wisdom, knowledge, or vision, and it serves to manifest the language of the gods in human speech.




### The Upanishads

- The Upanishads are late Vedic Sanskrit texts that supplied the basis of later Hindu philosophy.
- They are commonly referred to as Vedānta, which has been interpreted as the "last chapters, parts of the Veda" and alternatively as "object, the highest purpose of the Veda".
- The aim of all Upanishads is to investigate the nature of Ātman (self), and "direct [ing] the enquirer toward it".
- There are more than 200 known Upanishads, one of which, the Muktikā Upanishad, predates 1656 CE and contains a list of 108 canonical Upanishads, including itself as the last.
- These are further divided into Upanishads associated with Shaktism (goddess Shakti), Sannyasa (renunciation, monastic life), Shaivism (god Shiva), Vaishnavism (god Vishnu).
- The Upanishads present a vision of an interconnected universe with a single, unifying principle behind the apparent diversity in the cosmos, any articulation of which is called brahman.
- Within this context, the Upanishads teach that brahman resides in the atman, the unchanging core of the human individual.



### The Ramayana

- The Ramayana is an ancient Indian epic poem, composed in Sanskrit by the sage Valmiki .
- It belongs to the genre of Itihasa, narratives of past events (purāvṛtta), which includes the Mahabharata, the Puranas, and the Ramayana. The genre also includes teachings on the goals of human life .
- The Ramayana was composed probably not before 300 BCE and in its present form consists of some 24,000 couplets divided into seven books .
- It tells the story of the exile and then return of Rama, prince of Ayodhya .
- The Ramayana was an important influence on later Sanskrit poetry and the Hindu life and culture, and its main characters were fundamental to the cultural consciousness of a number of nations, both Hindu and Buddhist .




### The Mahabharata

- The Mahabharata is an exposition of dharma (codes of conduct), including the proper conduct of a king, of a warrior, of an individual living in times of calamity, and of a person seeking to attain moksha (freedom from samsara, or rebirth).
- It is an important source of information on the development of Hinduism between 400 BCE and 200 CE and is regarded by Hindus as both a text about dharma (Hindu moral law) and a history.



### Puranas

- The Puranas are ancient Hindu texts eulogizing various deities of the Hindu pantheon through divine stories .

### Buddhist Literature in Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit

### Sangama Literature in India

Sangama literature, also spelled as Cankam, Chankam, or Shangam, is the earliest known writings in the Tamil language. It is thought to have been produced in three literary academies, or Chankams, in Madurai, India, from the 1st to the 4th century CE .

### Six System Indian Philosophy

The Six Systems of Indian Philosophy are:

1. **Nyaya**: Founded by Akshapada Gautama, it is a school that deals with logic and epistemology.
2. **Vaisheshika**: Popularly understood as the atomistic school of Indian Philosophy.

### Various Philosophical Doctrines in India

Indian philosophy refers to the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. These systems include both orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) schools of philosophy. The orthodox systems are the Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva-Mimamsa (or Mimamsa), and Vedanta schools of philosophy. The unorthodox systems include Buddhism and Jainism .

Indian philosophy is characterized by its acceptance of the authority of ancient philosophers and its tendency to synthesize. Innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought. Indian schools generally embody the doctrines of karma and rebirth; man must be perfected before he can achieve salvation .

Indian thought has been concerned with various philosophical problems, significant among which are the nature of the world (cosmology), the nature of reality (metaphysics), logic, the nature of knowledge (epistemology), ethics, and the philosophy of religion .

Indian philosophies share many concepts such as dharma, karma, samsara, reincarnation, dukkha, renunciation, meditation, with almost all of them focusing on the ultimate goal of liberation of the individual through a diverse range of spiritual practices (moksha, nirvana)  .



### Heterodox Sects in India

Heterodox sects refer to the religious movements that arose in ancient India as a response to the ritualistic and orthodox practices of the Vedic period. These sects were founded by ascetic wanderers and teachers who attracted groups of followers and established various sects. Their philosophies encompassed antinomian, materialist, and fatalist elements.

Some of the major heterodox sects that arose in India include:

1. **Jainism**: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, in the 6th century BCE. Jainism is an ascetic religion that emphasizes non-violence and the attainment of liberation through the purification of the soul.

2. **Ajivika**: Founded by Makkhali Gosala in the 5th century BCE, Ajivika was a Śramaṇa movement and a major rival of Vedic religion, early Buddhism, and Jainism. The Ajivikas believed in the doctrine of niyati or destiny, which held that everything in the universe is predetermined and that human effort is futile.

3. **Cārvāka**: Also known as Lokāyata, Cārvāka was a materialist and atheist philosophical movement that rejected the authority of the Vedas and the existence of an afterlife. The Cārvākas believed that the pursuit of pleasure was the ultimate goal of life and that the material world was the only reality.

These heterodox sects challenged the orthodox practices of the time and offered alternative philosophical and religious beliefs to the people of ancient India.

### Socio-religious reform movement of 19th century in India

The 19th century was a period of transition and contestation in the socio-religious sphere of pre-independent India. Three streams of reform, revival, and rejection of ancient cultural values were woven around the socio-religious movement or social reform movement initiated by eminent intelligentsia of vision and foresight.

The main reason for the emergence of the reform movements was the spread of western education and liberal ideas. These reforms started in Bengal and soon spread to all parts of India.

The Indian society in the first half of the 19th century was caste-ridden, decadent, and rigid. It followed certain practices that were not in keeping with humanitarian feelings or values but were still being followed in the name of religion.

One of the social and religious reform movements in North India was spearheaded by Swami Dayanand Saraswati (1824-1883) who founded the Arya Samaj in 1875. This society strove against idolatry, polytheism, rituals, priesthood, animal sacrifice, child marriage, and the caste system. It also encouraged the dissemination of western scientific knowledge.

### Harappan Technologies in India and Ancient India

The Harappan Civilization, also known as the Indus River Valley Civilization, existed from 3300-1300 BCE and extended from modern-day northeast Afghanistan to Pakistan and northwest India . Some of the important innovations of this civilization include:

1. **Standardized weights and measures** .
2. **Seal carving** .
3. **Metallurgy with copper, bronze, lead, and tin** .
4. **Efficient system for conservation, harvesting, and storage of water** .
