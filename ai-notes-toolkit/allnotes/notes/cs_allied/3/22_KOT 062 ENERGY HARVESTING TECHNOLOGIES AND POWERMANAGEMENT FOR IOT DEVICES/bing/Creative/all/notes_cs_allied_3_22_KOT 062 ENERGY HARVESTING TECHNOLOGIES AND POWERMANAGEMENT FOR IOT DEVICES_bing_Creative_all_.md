- A power conditioning circuit is a circuit that regulates and optimizes the output of the transducer to match the requirements of the storage element and the load. For example, a power conditioning circuit can include a rectifier, a voltage converter, a maximum power point tracker, etc.
- A storage element is a device that stores the electrical energy generated by the transducer for later use. For example, a storage element can be a capacitor, a battery, a supercapacitor, etc.
- A load is a device that consumes the electrical energy stored in the storage element. For example, a load can be a sensor, a microcontroller, a wireless transmitter, etc.
- Energy harvesting systems can be classified into different types based on the source of ambient energy, such as vibration energy harvesting, thermal energy harvesting, solar energy harvesting, radio frequency energy harvesting, etc .

# Photovoltaic Cell Technologies

- Photovoltaic (PV) technologies are devices that absorb energy from sunlight and convert it into electrical energy through semiconducting materials.
- PV devices are also called solar cells, and they are connected together in chains to form larger units known as modules or panels .
- PV technologies are based on the photovoltaic effect, which is the process of converting light (photons) to electricity (voltage) in a material that exhibits this property .
- PV technologies can be classified into different types based on the materials, structures, and efficiencies of the solar cells. Some of the common types are:
  - Crystalline silicon (c-Si) solar cells, which are made of thin slices of pure silicon that are doped with impurities to create p-n junctions. These cells have high efficiencies and long lifetimes, but they are also expensive and require a lot of energy to produce.
  - Thin-film solar cells, which are made of thin layers of semiconducting materials such as amorphous silicon (a-Si), cadmium telluride (CdTe), copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS), or perovskite. These cells have lower efficiencies and shorter lifetimes, but they are also cheaper and more flexible than c-Si cells.
  - Multi-junction solar cells, which are made of multiple layers of different materials that can absorb different wavelengths of light. These cells have very high efficiencies, but they are also very complex and expensive to manufacture.
  - Organic solar cells, which are made of organic molecules or polymers that can generate electricity when exposed to light. These cells have low efficiencies and short lifetimes, but they are also very cheap and easy to produce.



# Generation of Electric Power in Semiconductor PV Cells

- PV cells, or photovoltaic cells, are devices that convert light energy into electrical energy using the photovoltaic effect .
- The photovoltaic effect is the phenomenon of generating electric voltage or current in a material when it is exposed to light .
- The material used in PV cells is usually a semiconductor, such as silicon, which has the property of changing its electrical conductivity when light is absorbed .
- A semiconductor is a material that can conduct electricity better than an insulator but not as well as a metal .
- A PV cell consists of two layers of semiconductor with different types of impurities, called dopants, that create a positive (p-type) and a negative (n-type) region .
- The junction between the p-type and n-type regions is called the p-n junction, which forms an electric field that prevents the free movement of electrons .
- When light photons with enough energy strike the p-n junction, they can excite electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, creating electron-hole pairs .
- An electron-hole pair is a pair of free electrons and positively charged holes that can move within the semiconductor .
- The electric field at the p-n junction separates the electron-hole pairs, pushing the electrons to the n-type region and the holes to the p-type region .
- This creates a potential difference, or voltage, between the two regions, which can be used to power an external circuit .
- The power generated by a single PV cell is typically only about two watts, so multiple cells are connected in series or parallel to form a PV module or panel .
- The PV modules or panels can be further connected to form a PV array or system, which can provide electricity for various applications, such as homes, buildings, or grids .
- Some of the factors that affect the performance and efficiency of PV cells are the type and quality of the semiconductor material, the intensity and spectrum of the light source, the temperature and orientation of the cell, and the presence of dust or shading  .

# Unit 2 - Piezoelectric Energy Harvesting and Electromechanical Modeling

- Piezoelectric energy harvesting is the process of converting mechanical vibrations into electrical energy using piezoelectric materials, which are materials that generate electric charge when subjected to mechanical stress or strain.
- Piezoelectric energy harvesters are devices that utilize piezoelectric materials to scavenge energy from ambient vibrations, such as those caused by wind, traffic, human motion, etc. They can be used to power low-power electronic devices, such as wireless sensors, microcontrollers, or LEDs, without the need for batteries or wires.
- Electromechanical modeling of piezoelectric energy harvesters is the mathematical description of the coupled dynamics of the mechanical and electrical domains of the harvesters, taking into account the geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, and excitation sources of the harvesters.
- Electromechanical modeling can help to understand the behavior, performance, and optimization of piezoelectric energy harvesters, as well as to design and test new configurations and applications of the harvesters.
- There are different approaches to electromechanical modeling of piezoelectric energy harvesters, such as lumped-parameter models, distributed-parameter models, finite element models, and analytical models. Each approach has its own advantages and limitations, depending on the complexity, accuracy, and computational cost required for the analysis.
- Lumped-parameter models are the simplest and most common models, which assume that the harvester can be represented by a single degree of freedom (DOF) mechanical system coupled to an equivalent electrical circuit. They are suitable for linear and symmetric harvesters with simple geometries and uniform material properties, but they cannot capture the effects of higher modes, shear deformation, or non-uniform stress distribution.
- Distributed-parameter models are more general and accurate models, which consider the harvester as a continuous system with infinite DOF. They are based on the partial differential equations (PDEs) that govern the electromechanical coupling of the piezoelectric material, such as the Euler-Bernoulli beam equation or the Timoshenko beam equation. They can account for the effects of higher modes, shear deformation, and non-uniform stress distribution, but they require more mathematical and numerical skills to solve and analyze.
- Finite element models are numerical models that discretize the harvester into a finite number of elements and nodes, and apply the finite element method (FEM) to solve the PDEs of the electromechanical coupling. They can handle complex geometries, material properties, boundary conditions, and excitation sources, but they are computationally expensive and require specialized software and tools.
- Analytical models are exact or approximate solutions of the PDEs of the electromechanical coupling, obtained by using mathematical techniques such as separation of variables, modal analysis, or perturbation methods. They can provide insight into the physical phenomena and parameters that affect the harvester performance, but they are often limited to idealized or simplified cases and assumptions.



# Piezoelectric materials

- Piezoelectric materials are materials that can convert mechanical stress into electrical charge, or vice versa. This phenomenon is called the **piezoelectric effect**.
- Piezoelectric materials can be classified into the following categories:
  - **Crystalline materials**: These are natural or synthetic materials that have a regular and ordered arrangement of atoms. Examples are quartz, tourmaline, and gallium nitride.
  - **Ceramic materials**: These are polycrystalline materials that are formed by sintering powders of different compounds. Examples are lead zirconate titanate (PZT), barium titanate, and zinc oxide.
  - **Polymer materials**: These are organic materials that consist of long chains of molecules. Examples are polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), polyurethane, and polyimide.
  - **Composite materials**: These are materials that combine two or more different types of materials to enhance their properties. Examples are PZT-polymer, PZT-ceramic, and PZT-epoxy composites.
  - **Glass-ceramic materials**: These are materials that are formed by controlled crystallization of glass. Examples are lithium silicate and barium titanate silicate glass-ceramics.
- Piezoelectric materials have many applications in various fields, such as :

# Strategies for enhancing the performance of energy harvesters

Energy harvesters are devices that can convert ambient energy sources, such as vibration, wind, light, heat, etc., into electrical energy. Energy harvesting is a promising technology for powering low-power consumption devices, such as sensors, wireless networks, wearable electronics, etc., without the need for batteries or external power supply. However, the performance of energy harvesters is often limited by the low and variable energy density of the ambient sources, the mismatch between the frequency and amplitude of the input and the output, the nonlinear and complex dynamics of the harvesting systems, and the losses in the conversion process. Therefore, various strategies have been proposed to enhance the performance of energy harvesters, such as increasing the power output, widening the frequency bandwidth, improving the efficiency, and optimizing the design and materials. Some of the common strategies are:

- **Adopting new materials**: The choice of materials for energy harvesters affects their output voltage, current, power, and durability. For example, piezoelectric materials, which can generate electric charge when subjected to mechanical stress, have different properties depending on their composition, crystal structure, polarization, and fabrication methods. Some of the new materials that have been explored for piezoelectric energy harvesting include lead-free ceramics, polymers, composites, nanomaterials, etc. These materials can offer advantages such as higher piezoelectric coefficients, lower density, higher flexibility, and environmental friendliness .

- **Optimizing the harvesting devices**: The design and configuration of the harvesting devices can also affect their performance. For example, the shape, size, mass, and stiffness of the cantilever beam, which is a common structure for piezoelectric energy harvesting, can influence its natural frequency, mode shape, and deflection. Some of the optimization methods that have been used for piezoelectric energy harvesting include geometric optimization, topology optimization, multi-objective optimization, etc. These methods can help to maximize the power output, match the resonance frequency to the ambient vibration frequency, and reduce the stress and strain in the beam.

- **Introducing nonlinearities**: Nonlinearities are phenomena that cause the response of a system to deviate from the linear relationship between the input and the output. Nonlinearities can be introduced in energy harvesters by using nonlinear elements, such as magnets, springs, bistable structures, etc. Nonlinearities can enhance the performance of energy harvesters by increasing the power output, widening the frequency bandwidth, inducing chaotic behavior, and enabling frequency tuning. For example, bistable energy harvesters, which have two stable equilibrium positions, can exhibit large-amplitude oscillations and snap-through transitions when subjected to broadband vibrations, resulting in higher power output and wider frequency response .

- **Applying power-amplifying strategies**: Power-amplifying strategies are methods that can increase the power output of energy harvesters by modifying the input or the output of the system. For example, one power-amplifying strategy for piezoelectric energy harvesting is to connect the additional lumped mass to a harmonically oscillating base through an elastic foundation. This can create a power-amplifying effect by increasing the relative displacement between the mass and the base, and by tuning the resonance frequency of the system. Another power-amplifying strategy is to use a synchronized switch harvesting on inductor (SSHI) circuit, which can increase the output voltage and power by switching the piezoelectric element to an inductor at optimal instants.

- **Hybridizing with other technologies**: Hybridization is the combination of two or more energy harvesting technologies or mechanisms to achieve a synergistic effect. Hybridization can enhance the performance of energy harvesters by increasing the power output, widening the frequency bandwidth, improving the reliability, and reducing the dependence on a single energy source. For example, triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs), which can generate electric charge by the contact and separation of two materials, can be hybridized with piezoelectric, electromagnetic, or electrostatic energy harvesters to harvest wind energy from different directions, speeds, and frequencies.



# Electromechanical modeling of Lumped parameter model and coupled distributed parameter models and closed-form solutions

- Electromechanical modeling is the process of describing the dynamic behavior of systems that involve both electrical and mechanical components, such as piezoelectric devices, motors, sensors, etc.
- Lumped parameter model (LPM) is a simplified approach that assumes that the system can be represented by a finite number of discrete elements (such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, masses, springs, dampers, etc.) that are connected at nodes with negligible spatial dimensions and interactions .
- Coupled distributed parameter model (CDPM) is a more accurate approach that takes into account the spatial variation of the physical properties and the interactions between different parts of the system, such as the transmission line, the fluid flow, the heat transfer, etc. CDPM usually involves partial differential equations (PDEs) that describe the field variables (such as voltage, current, pressure, temperature, etc.) as functions of space and time .
- Closed-form solution (CFS) is an analytical expression that gives the exact solution of a mathematical problem, such as an equation, a system of equations, or an optimization problem. CFS is usually preferred over numerical methods, as it can provide more insight into the nature and properties of the solution, such as existence, uniqueness, stability, etc. However, CFS is not always possible or easy to obtain, especially for nonlinear, complex, or high-dimensional problems.

- Non-linear techniques are methods that enhance the performance of electromagnetic energy harvesting systems by exploiting the non-linear behavior of some components or structures  .
  - Non-linear techniques can increase the bandwidth, output voltage, power density or efficiency of the harvesting systems, depending on the design and application  .
  - Non-linear techniques can be implemented using various mechanisms, such as magnetic levitation, bistability, snap-through, frequency up-conversion, synchronization or chaos  .

The heat sink is a metal or polymer structure that dissipates the heat from the hot side of the TEM to the environment. The heat sink can be designed to have a high thermal conductivity and a large surface area to enhance the heat transfer.
- The power output of a TEH depends on several factors, such as the thermoelectric material, the device geometry, the load resistance, and the temperature gradient

# Harvesting for RF Sensors and ID Tags

- RF sensors and ID tags are devices that use radio frequency (RF) signals to communicate data, such as identification, location, temperature, humidity, etc.
- RF sensors and ID tags can be classified into passive, semi-passive, and active, depending on their power source and functionality.
- Passive RF sensors and ID tags have no battery and rely on the RF energy from a reader or interrogator to power up their circuitry and transmit their data. They have a limited range and functionality, but are low-cost and maintenance-free.
- Semi-passive RF sensors and ID tags have a battery that powers their sensor and logic circuitry, but use the RF energy from a reader or interrogator to transmit their data. They have a longer range and functionality than passive tags, but are more expensive and require battery replacement.
- Active RF sensors and ID tags have a battery that powers their entire circuitry, including the RF transmitter. They have the longest range and functionality, but are the most expensive and require frequent battery replacement.
- Energy harvesting is a technique that enables RF sensors and ID tags to collect energy from the environment, such as solar, thermal, mechanical, or electromagnetic sources, and use it to power their circuitry or recharge their battery.
- Energy harvesting can extend the lifetime, range, and functionality of RF sensors and ID tags, as well as reduce the environmental impact and maintenance cost of batteries.
- Energy harvesting can be implemented in different ways, such as using the RF reader signal, the third harmonic signal generated by the RFID chip, or ambient RF sources, to deliver energy to the RF sensors and ID tags.
- Energy harvesting can also be combined with other techniques, such as duty cycling, power management, and adaptive modulation, to optimize the performance and efficiency of RF sensors and ID tags.



# Powering wireless SHM sensor nodes

- Structural health monitoring (SHM) is the process of regularly measuring factors such as strain, movement, vibration, temperature, etc. on infrastructure such as bridges, buildings, pipelines, etc. to assess their condition and detect any damage or deterioration  .
- Wireless sensor nodes (WSNs) are small devices that can sense, process, and communicate data wirelessly to a central node or a server. WSNs are widely used in SHM applications because they are low cost, easy to deploy, fast, and reliable .
- However, one of the main challenges of using WSNs in SHM is how to power them. WSNs typically rely on batteries, which have limited lifetime and require periodic replacement or recharging. This can be costly, inconvenient, and impractical, especially for large-scale or remote SHM systems.
- Therefore, energy harvesting (EH) is a promising solution to power WSNs in SHM. EH is the process of converting ambient energy sources such as solar, wind, vibration, thermal, etc. into electrical energy that can be used to power WSNs or charge their batteries.
- EH can provide continuous, autonomous, and maintenance-free power supply for WSNs in SHM, thus enhancing their performance, reliability, and sustainability. EH can also enable more frequent and accurate data collection and transmission, which can improve the quality and efficiency of SHM.
- However, EH also poses some challenges and limitations for WSNs in SHM, such as:
  - The availability and intensity of ambient energy sources can vary depending on the location, time, and weather conditions, which can affect the output and stability of EH devices.
  - The mismatch between the harvested energy and the power demand of WSNs can cause energy wastage or insufficient power supply, which can affect the functionality and lifetime of WSNs.
  - The integration of EH devices with WSNs can increase the size, weight, and cost of the sensor nodes, which can affect their deployment and scalability.
- Therefore, power management (PM) is a crucial component of EH systems for WSNs in SHM. PM is the process of regulating, storing, and distributing the harvested energy to the sensor nodes according to their power requirements and the availability of energy sources.
- PM can optimize the energy utilization and efficiency of WSNs in SHM, thus improving their performance, reliability, and sustainability. PM can also enable adaptive and intelligent control of WSNs in SHM, such as adjusting the sampling rate, data processing, and communication frequency according to the energy budget and the SHM objectives.
- However, PM also poses some challenges and limitations for WSNs in SHM, such as:
  - The design and implementation of PM circuits and algorithms can increase the complexity and cost of the sensor nodes, which can affect their deployment and scalability.
  - The trade-off between the performance and the power consumption of WSNs in SHM can affect the quality and efficiency of SHM, such as the accuracy, resolution, and timeliness of the data collection and transmission.
  - The coordination and synchronization of PM among multiple sensor nodes in a network can affect the functionality and lifetime of WSNs, such as the network connectivity, coverage, and robustness.
