# Photovoltaic Cell Technologies

Photovoltaic (PV) cells are devices that convert sunlight directly into electricity using semiconducting materials. PV cells are also known as solar cells. PV cells can be connected to form larger power-generating units known as modules or panels.

There are three main types of PV cell technologies that dominate the world market:

- **Monocrystalline silicon (c-Si)**: These cells are made of single-crystal silicon wafers that have high purity and efficiency. They have a uniform dark color and rounded edges. They are the most expensive and durable type of PV cells.
- **Polycrystalline silicon (p-Si)**: These cells are made of multiple-crystal silicon wafers that have lower purity and efficiency than c-Si cells. They have a speckled blue color and square edges. They are cheaper and less durable than c-Si cells.
- **Thin film (TF)**: These cells are made of thin layers of semiconducting materials deposited on a substrate such as glass, metal, or plastic. They have lower efficiency and durability than c-Si and p-Si cells, but they are flexible and lightweight. They can be made of various materials, such as amorphous silicon (a-Si), cadmium telluride (CdTe), copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS), or perovskite.

The performance of PV cells depends on several factors, such as the material properties, the cell design, the module configuration, the environmental conditions, and the power conversion system. Some of the key parameters that characterize PV cells are :

- **Short-circuit current (Isc)**: The maximum current that flows through the cell when it is short-circuited (i.e., when the voltage is zero).
- **Open-circuit voltage (Voc)**: The maximum voltage that the cell can produce when it is open-circuited (i.e., when the current is zero).
- **Maximum power point (MPP)**: The point on the current-voltage curve of the cell where the product of current and voltage is maximum. This is the optimal operating point of the cell.
- **Fill factor (FF)**: The ratio of the maximum power point to the product of Isc and Voc. It measures how close the cell is to the ideal current-voltage curve.
- **Efficiency (η)**: The ratio of the maximum power output to the incident solar power input. It measures how well the cell converts sunlight into electricity.

The efficiency of PV cells is limited by several factors, such as the band gap of the material, the recombination of charge carriers, the reflection and transmission losses, the series and shunt resistances, and the temperature effects. The theoretical maximum efficiency of a single-junction PV cell is given by the Shockley-Queisser limit, which is about 33.7% for a band gap of 1.34 eV. However, the practical efficiency of PV cells is much lower, ranging from about 5% to 25% depending on the technology and the conditions.

PV cells can be combined to form multi-junction cells, which use different materials with different band gaps to absorb a wider spectrum of sunlight. This can increase the efficiency beyond the Shockley-Queisser limit, but it also increases the complexity and cost of the cell. The highest efficiency achieved by a multi-junction cell is about 47.1%.

PV cells are the key components of solar photovoltaic systems, which are used to generate electricity from sunlight for various applications, such as residential, commercial, industrial, and utility-scale. PV systems can be classified into two types: grid-connected and off-grid. Grid-connected systems are connected to the utility grid and can either feed excess power to the grid or draw power from the grid when needed. Off-grid systems are not connected to the grid and rely on batteries or other energy storage devices to provide power when there is no sunlight. PV systems can also be integrated with other renewable energy sources, such as wind, hydro, or biomass, to form hybrid systems that can enhance the reliability and sustainability of power generation.



# Generation of electric power in semiconductor PV cells

- PV cells, or photovoltaic cells, are devices that convert light energy into electrical energy using the photovoltaic effect .
- The photovoltaic effect is the phenomenon of generating electric voltage or current in a material when it is exposed to light .
- The material used in PV cells is usually a semiconductor, such as silicon, which has the property of changing its electrical conductivity when light is absorbed .
- A semiconductor has two types of charge carriers: electrons and holes. Electrons are negatively charged particles that can move freely in the material, while holes are positively charged regions that can accept electrons .
- A PV cell consists of two layers of semiconductor with different doping levels. Doping is the process of adding impurities to a semiconductor to change its electrical properties .
- The layer with more electrons is called the n-type layer, while the layer with more holes is called the p-type layer. The junction between the two layers is called the p-n junction .
- When light strikes the p-n junction, it creates electron-hole pairs in both layers. The electrons in the p-type layer and the holes in the n-type layer are attracted to the junction by the electric field created by the doping .
- If an external circuit is connected to the two layers, the electrons and holes can flow through it, creating an electric current. The voltage generated by the PV cell depends on the intensity and wavelength of the light, as well as the characteristics of the semiconductor material .
- The power generated by a single PV cell is typically only about two watts. To increase the power output, multiple PV cells can be connected in series or parallel to form a PV module or panel .
- PV modules or panels can be further connected to form a PV array or system, which can provide electricity for various applications, such as lighting, heating, cooling, or charging batteries .
- PV systems can be classified into two types: grid-connected and off-grid. Grid-connected systems are connected to the public electricity network and can sell excess power to the grid or buy power from the grid when needed. Off-grid systems are independent of the grid and rely on batteries or other energy storage devices to provide power when there is no sunlight .

# Unit 2 - Piezo-Electric Energy Harvesting and Electromechanical Modeling

- Piezo-electric energy harvesting is the process of converting mechanical energy into electrical energy using piezoelectric materials, which are materials that generate electric charge when subjected to mechanical stress or strain .
- Piezo-electric energy harvesters are devices that utilize piezoelectric materials to scavenge energy from ambient vibrations, such as wind, traffic, human motion, etc., and convert it into usable electrical power for low-power applications, such as wireless sensors, microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), biomedical implants, etc. .
- Electromechanical modeling of piezo-electric energy harvesters is the process of developing mathematical models that describe the coupled mechanical and electrical behavior of the harvesters, taking into account the geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, excitation sources, and output circuits .
- Electromechanical modeling of piezo-electric energy harvesters is important for understanding the performance, optimization, and design of the harvesters, as well as for predicting the harvested power and voltage output under different operating conditions .
- Electromechanical modeling of piezo-electric energy harvesters can be classified into two categories: lumped-parameter models and distributed-parameter models .
  - Lumped-parameter models are models that assume the harvester as a single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) or a multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) system, and use equivalent circuit elements, such as capacitors, resistors, and inductors, to represent the mechanical and electrical components of the harvester .
  - Distributed-parameter models are models that consider the harvester as a continuous system, and use partial differential equations (PDEs) or integral equations to describe the spatial and temporal variations of the mechanical and electrical variables of the harvester .
- Lumped-parameter models are simpler and easier to implement than distributed-parameter models, but they have some limitations, such as neglecting the effects of higher modes, shear deformation, rotary inertia, and non-uniform stress distribution .
- Distributed-parameter models are more accurate and general than lumped-parameter models, but they are more complex and require numerical methods, such as finite element method (FEM), finite difference method (FDM), or Galerkin method, to obtain the solutions .
- Typically, piezo-electric energy harvesters are cantilevered structures with piezoceramic layers that generate alternating voltage output due to base excitation  . However, other forms of harvesters, such as beams, plates, shells, membranes, or metamaterials, have also been studied and developed  .
- The most common forms of excitation in piezo-electric energy harvesting are harmonic, random, and impulsive excitations, which correspond to different types of vibration sources, such as engines, machinery, human motion, etc. . However, other forms of excitation, such as airflow, acoustic waves, or moving loads, have also been investigated .
- The output circuit of a piezo-electric energy harvester is the electrical load that is connected to the terminals of the piezoelectric material, and it determines the amount of power that can be extracted from the harvester . The output circuit can be either resistive, capacitive, inductive, or a combination of them, and it can also include power conditioning and storage elements, such as rectifiers, regulators, batteries, etc. .
- The output power and voltage of a piezo-electric energy harvester depend on several factors, such as the geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, excitation frequency and amplitude, output circuit, and damping of the harvester . Therefore, it is important to analyze and optimize these factors to achieve the maximum performance of the harvester .

### Electromechanical Modeling

- Electromechanical modeling is the process of developing mathematical models that describe the coupled mechanical and electrical behavior of piezoelectric harvesters.
- Electromechanical modeling can be used to analyze the performance of piezoelectric harvesters, such as output voltage, power, or efficiency, under different operating conditions, such as frequency, amplitude, or load resistance.
- Electromechanical modeling can also be used to design and optimize piezoelectric harvesters, such as selecting the optimal geometry, material, or configuration, to maximize the energy conversion or adapt to the environment.
- Electromechanical modeling can be classified into two main approaches: lumped-parameter modeling and distributed-parameter modeling.
- Lumped-parameter modeling assumes that the piezoelectric harvester can be represented by a finite number of discrete elements, such as masses, springs, dampers, or capacitors, connected by ideal nodes. Lumped-parameter modeling is simple and intuitive, but may not capture the complex dynamics of the piezoelectric harvester, especially at higher frequencies or modes.

# Strategies for enhancing the performance of energy harvesters

Energy harvesters are devices that can convert ambient energy sources, such as vibration, wind, light, heat, etc., into electrical energy. Energy harvesting is a promising technology for powering low-power consumption devices, such as sensors, wireless networks, wearable electronics, etc., without the need for batteries or external power supply. However, the performance of energy harvesters is often limited by the low and variable energy density of the ambient sources, the mismatch between the frequency and amplitude of the input and the output, the nonlinear and complex dynamics of the harvesting systems, and the losses in the conversion process. Therefore, various strategies have been proposed to enhance the performance of energy harvesters, such as increasing the power output, widening the frequency bandwidth, improving the efficiency, and optimizing the design and materials.

Some of the common strategies for enhancing the performance of energy harvesters are:

- **Adopting new materials**: The choice of materials for energy harvesters affects their output voltage, current, power, and durability. For example, piezoelectric materials can convert mechanical strain into electric charge, and vice versa. The piezoelectric coefficient, which measures the amount of charge generated per unit strain, is an important parameter for piezoelectric energy harvesters. Therefore, researchers have explored various piezoelectric materials, such as ceramics, polymers, composites, nanomaterials, etc., to increase the piezoelectric coefficient and the output power . Similarly, other materials, such as electrostatic, electromagnetic, thermoelectric, triboelectric, etc., have been investigated for different types of energy harvesters.

- **Optimizing the design and structure**: The design and structure of energy harvesters affect their resonance frequency, mode shape, damping, coupling, and output impedance. For example, cantilevered beams are widely used for vibration energy harvesting, as they can resonate at a certain frequency and generate electric charge by piezoelectric, electrostatic, or electromagnetic effects. However, the resonance frequency of cantilevered beams is usually fixed and narrow, which limits their performance under variable and broadband vibrations. Therefore, researchers have proposed various design and structure modifications, such as adding mass, springs, magnets, nonlinear elements, etc., to tune the resonance frequency, widen the frequency bandwidth, induce bi-stable or multi-stable oscillations, and enhance the power output  .

- **Power management and hybridization**: The output power of energy harvesters is usually low and fluctuating, which makes it difficult to directly power the load devices. Therefore, power management circuits, such as rectifiers, regulators, converters, storage units, etc., are often used to condition the output power and match the load impedance. However, power management circuits also introduce losses and parasitic effects, which reduce the efficiency of energy harvesting. Therefore, researchers have developed various power management techniques, such as maximum power point tracking, impedance matching, adaptive control, etc., to optimize the power transfer and minimize the losses. Moreover, hybridization of different energy harvesting mechanisms, such as piezoelectric and electromagnetic, piezoelectric and triboelectric, etc., can also improve the performance of energy harvesters by exploiting multiple energy sources and increasing the output power.

These are some of the strategies for enhancing the performance of energy harvesters. However, there is no universal strategy that can suit all types of energy harvesters and ambient sources. Therefore, the performance enhancement of energy harvesters requires a comprehensive understanding of the energy harvesting principles, the characteristics of the ambient sources, the requirements of the load devices, and the trade-offs between different design parameters and optimization objectives.



# Electromechanical modeling of Lumped parameter model and coupled distributed parameter models and closed-form solutions

- Electromechanical modeling is the process of describing the dynamic behavior of systems that involve both electrical and mechanical components, such as piezoelectric devices, motors, sensors, etc.
- Lumped parameter model (LPM) is a simplified approach that assumes that the system can be represented by a finite number of discrete elements (such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, masses, springs, dampers, etc.) that are connected at nodes with negligible spatial dimensions and interactions .
- Coupled distributed parameter model (CDPM) is a more accurate approach that accounts for the spatial variation of the system properties and the interactions between different parts of the system, such as the transmission line model, the beam model, the network model, etc  .
- Closed-form solution (CFS) is an analytical expression that can be obtained for the system response in terms of the system parameters and the input variables, such as the voltage, current, displacement, force, etc .
- The advantages of LPM are that it is easier to implement, analyze, and control, and it can capture the main features of the system behavior with reasonable accuracy for some applications .
- The disadvantages of LPM are that it neglects the spatial effects, such as the wave propagation, the mode shapes, the boundary conditions, etc., and it may require a large number of elements to represent complex systems with high accuracy .
- The advantages of CDPM are that it can account for the spatial effects and the interactions between different parts of the system, and it can provide more accurate and detailed information about the system behavior for some applications  .
- The disadvantages of CDPM are that it is more difficult to implement, analyze, and control, and it may not have a closed-form solution or it may require numerical methods to obtain the system response  .
- The advantages of CFS are that it can provide an explicit and compact representation of the system response, and it can facilitate the understanding, optimization, and design of the system .
- The disadvantages of CFS are that it may not exist or it may be difficult to obtain for some systems, and it may not capture the nonlinearities, uncertainties, or disturbances that may affect the system behavior .
