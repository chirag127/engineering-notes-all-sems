### Energy sources for energy harvesting

Energy harvesting is the process of capturing and converting ambient energy from various sources into electrical energy for powering small, wireless autonomous devices. Energy harvesting can enable self-powered devices that do not require batteries or external power sources, which can reduce maintenance costs and environmental impact. Some of the common energy sources for energy harvesting are:

- **Thermal energy**: Thermal energy is the energy associated with heat and temperature differences. Thermal energy can be harvested from various sources, such as waste heat from industrial processes, human body heat, geothermal energy, and solar radiation. Thermal energy can be converted into electrical energy using thermoelectric generators (TEGs), which are devices that generate voltage based on the Seebeck effect. TEGs consist of two dissimilar materials connected in a loop, which create a temperature gradient across the junctions. The temperature difference induces an electric current in the loop, which can be used to power a load. TEGs can also work in reverse, using electrical energy to create a temperature difference, which can be used for cooling or heating applications.  

- **RF energy**: RF energy is the energy carried by electromagnetic waves in the radio frequency range. RF energy can be harvested from various sources, such as broadcast signals, cellular networks, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and RFID. RF energy can be converted into electrical energy using antennas, rectifiers, and capacitors, which form a rectenna. A rectenna is a device that captures RF waves and converts them into direct current (DC) voltage. The rectified voltage can be used to power a load or stored in a battery or capacitor. RF energy harvesting can enable wireless power transfer and wireless charging for devices that are within the range of the RF source.  

- **Solar energy**: Solar energy is the energy from the sun that reaches the earth in the form of visible light, infrared radiation, and ultraviolet radiation. Solar energy can be harvested using photovoltaic (PV) cells, which are devices that convert light into electrical energy. PV cells are made of materials that have the property of photoelectric effect, which is the emission of electrons when light hits a metal surface. The emitted electrons create a voltage difference between the two sides of the PV cell, which can be used to power a load or stored in a battery or capacitor. PV cells can be arranged in arrays or modules to increase the output power and voltage. Solar energy harvesting can enable renewable and clean energy generation for devices that are exposed to sunlight.  

- **Mechanical energy**: Mechanical energy is the energy associated with motion and force. Mechanical energy can be harvested from various sources, such as human or animal movements, vibrations, wind, water, and sound. Mechanical energy can be converted into electrical energy using different mechanisms, such as piezoelectric, electromagnetic, and electrostatic. Piezoelectric materials are materials that generate voltage when subjected to mechanical stress or strain, such as pressure, bending, or stretching. Piezoelectric materials can be used to make sensors, actuators, and generators that can harvest mechanical energy from vibrations, impacts, or deformations. Electromagnetic generators are devices that use magnets and coils to induce voltage based on Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. Electromagnetic generators can harvest mechanical energy from rotational or linear motion, such as spinning, shaking, or swinging. Electrostatic generators are devices that use variable capacitors to generate voltage based on the principle of electrostatic induction. Electrostatic generators can harvest mechanical energy from vibrations or fluctuations, such as tapping, flapping, or fluttering.

### Photovoltaic Cell Technologies

- Photovoltaic (PV) technologies are devices that absorb energy from sunlight and convert it into electrical energy through semiconducting materials.
- PV devices are also called solar cells, and they are connected together in chains to form larger power-generating units known as modules or panels .
- PV technologies can be classified into three main categories based on the type of semiconducting material used: crystalline silicon (c-Si), thin-film, and emerging technologies.
- Crystalline silicon (c-Si) is the most widely used and mature PV technology, accounting for about 90% of the global PV market. It has high efficiency, reliability, and durability, but also high manufacturing cost and energy consumption. c-Si can be further divided into monocrystalline and multicrystalline types.
- Thin-film PV technologies use thin layers of semiconducting materials deposited on a substrate, such as glass, metal, or plastic. They have lower efficiency, but also lower cost and weight, and better flexibility and adaptability to different surfaces and applications. Thin-film PV technologies include amorphous silicon (a-Si), cadmium telluride (CdTe), copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS), and perovskite.
- Emerging PV technologies are novel and innovative approaches that aim to overcome the limitations of existing PV technologies and achieve higher efficiency, lower cost, and better performance. They include organic PV, dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSC), quantum dot PV, and concentrator PV .



### Generation of electric power in semiconductor PV cells

- PV cells are devices that convert light energy into electrical energy using the photovoltaic effect .
- The photovoltaic effect is the phenomenon of generating an electric voltage or current in a material when it is exposed to light .
- The material used in PV cells is usually a semiconductor, such as silicon, which has the property of changing its electrical conductivity when light is absorbed.
- A semiconductor has two types of charge carriers: electrons and holes. Electrons are negatively charged particles that can move freely in the material, while holes are positively charged regions that can accept electrons.
- A PV cell consists of two layers of semiconductor with different doping levels. Doping is the process of adding impurities to a semiconductor to change its electrical properties.
- The layer with more electrons is called the n-type layer, while the layer with more holes is called the p-type layer. The junction between the two layers is called the p-n junction.
- When light strikes the p-n junction, it can excite electrons from the valence band (the lower energy level) to the conduction band (the higher energy level) in the semiconductor, creating electron-hole pairs .
- The electric field at the p-n junction separates the electron-hole pairs, pushing the electrons to the n-type layer and the holes to the p-type layer. This creates a potential difference or voltage across the cell .
- The voltage can be measured by connecting an external circuit to the cell. The current that flows in the circuit is proportional to the intensity of the light and the area of the cell .
- The power generated by a PV cell depends on the voltage, the current, and the efficiency of the cell. The efficiency is the ratio of the electrical power output to the light power input.
- The power generated by a single PV cell is typically only about two watts. To increase the power output, multiple cells can be connected in series or parallel to form a PV module or panel.
- PV modules can be further connected to form a PV array or system, which can be used to power various applications, such as homes, buildings, or grids.
- Some of the factors that affect the performance of a PV system are the solar irradiance, the temperature, the shading, the orientation, and the dust.
- PV systems can be classified into two types: grid-connected and off-grid. Grid-connected systems are connected to the utility grid and can sell excess power or buy power when needed. Off-grid systems are independent of the grid and rely on batteries or other energy storage devices to provide power when there is no sunlight .

## Unit 2 - Piezo-Electric Energy Harvesting and Electromechanical Modeling

- Piezo-electric energy harvesting is the process of converting ambient mechanical vibrations into electrical energy using piezoelectric materials .
- Piezoelectric materials are materials that exhibit the piezoelectric effect, which is the generation of electric charge or voltage in response to mechanical stress or strain .
- Piezoelectric energy harvesters are devices that utilize piezoelectric materials to scavenge energy from their surroundings and power low-power electronics, such as wireless sensors, microsystems, or biomedical implants .
- Electromechanical modeling of piezoelectric energy harvesters is the mathematical description of the coupled mechanical and electrical behavior of the harvesters under various forms of excitation, such as base vibration, airflow, or moving load .
- Electromechanical modeling can help design, optimize, and control the performance of piezoelectric energy harvesters, as well as understand the underlying physics and phenomena involved .
- Electromechanical modeling can be done using different approaches, such as lumped-parameter, distributed-parameter, or finite element methods, depending on the complexity and accuracy required .
- Lumped-parameter models assume that the harvester can be represented by a single degree of freedom system with equivalent mass, stiffness, damping, and piezoelectric coefficients .
- Distributed-parameter models account for the spatial variation of the mechanical and electrical properties of the harvester, and can capture higher-order modes and nonlinear effects .
- Finite element models are numerical methods that discretize the harvester into small elements and solve the governing equations using matrix operations .
- Electromechanical models can be derived using various methods, such as Hamilton's principle, Lagrange's equations, or energy balance equations, depending on the assumptions and boundary conditions .
- Electromechanical models can be validated using experimental data obtained from various measurement techniques, such as strain gauges, accelerometers, oscilloscopes, or impedance analyzers .
- Electromechanical models can be used to analyze the performance metrics of piezoelectric energy harvesters, such as output voltage, power, efficiency, bandwidth, or resonance frequency .
- Electromechanical models can also be used to explore the effects of various design parameters, such as geometry, material, load, or circuit, on the performance of piezoelectric energy harvesters .
- Electromechanical models can also be used to investigate the nonlinear phenomena and challenges in piezoelectric energy harvesting, such as hysteresis, saturation, bifurcation, or chaos .
- Electromechanical models can also be used to design and implement various techniques to enhance the performance of piezoelectric energy harvesters, such as frequency tuning, impedance matching, synchronization, or harvesting from multiple modes .

  - Single crystalline materials, such as quartz (SiO2), which is the best known natural piezoelectric material.
  - Piezoceramics, such as lead zirconate titanate (PZT), barium titanate, and lead titanate, which are the most commonly produced and widely used synthetic piezoelectric materials  .
  - Piezoelectric semiconductors, such as zinc oxide (ZnO2), which have a relatively wide band gap and can be used as piezoelectric sensors and actuators.
  - Piezopolymers, such as polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), which are flexible and can be fabricated into thin films and fibers.
  - Piezoelectric composites, which are combinations of piezoelectric materials and other materials, such as polymers, metals, or ceramics, to enhance the performance and functionality of the piezoelectric components.
  - Glass ceramics, such as lithium disilicate (Li2Si2O5) and barium titanate silicate (Ba2TiSiO6), which are transparent and have high piezoelectric coefficients.
- Piezoelectric materials have useful applications in many industries, such as  :
  - Energy harvesting, where piezoelectric materials can convert ambient vibrations, such as from human motion, wind, or traffic, into electrical energy for powering low-power devices, such as wireless sensors, microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), or wearable electronics.

  - Acoustics and sound, where piezoelectric materials can act as speakers, microphones, or headphones, by converting electrical signals into sound waves, or vice versa. They can also be used for noise cancellation, sonar, or musical instruments.

### Strategies for enhancing the performance of energy harvesters

Energy harvesters are devices that can convert ambient energy sources, such as vibration, wind, light, heat, etc., into electrical energy. Energy harvesting can provide a sustainable and autonomous power supply for low-power electronic devices, such as sensors, wireless communication modules, and wearable devices. However, the performance of energy harvesters is often limited by the low and variable energy density of the ambient sources, the mismatch between the source frequency and the harvester resonance frequency, and the inefficiency of the energy conversion and storage processes. Therefore, various strategies have been proposed to enhance the performance of energy harvesters, such as:

- Adopting new materials and optimizing the harvesting devices. For example, using piezoelectric materials with high electromechanical coupling coefficient, such as lead zirconate titanate (PZT), or using flexible and stretchable materials, such as polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), to improve the mechanical deformation and electrical output of the harvesters.
- Designing nonlinear or bistable harvesters to increase the frequency bandwidth and the power output. For example, using magnets, springs, or elastic supports to introduce nonlinear or bistable potential functions to the harvesters, which can induce large-amplitude oscillations and multiple stable states under broadband vibrations .
- Applying power-amplifying strategies to maximize the generated power and match the source frequency. For example, using frequency tuning techniques, such as mechanical or electrical tuning, to adjust the resonance frequency of the harvesters to match the source frequency, or using frequency up-conversion techniques, such as impact or snap-through mechanisms, to convert low-frequency vibrations into high-frequency oscillations.
- Hybridizing different energy harvesting technologies to utilize multiple energy sources and enhance the power density. For example, combining piezoelectric, triboelectric, and electrostatic effects to harvest wind energy, or integrating thermoelectric, photovoltaic, and piezoelectric effects to harvest solar and thermal energy.
- Developing power management and storage systems to optimize the energy conversion and utilization. For example, using rectifiers, voltage multipliers, converters, and regulators to convert the alternating current (AC) output of the harvesters into direct current (DC) output with suitable voltage and current levels, or using capacitors, batteries, or supercapacitors to store the harvested energy for later use.

These strategies can help to improve the performance of energy harvesters in terms of power output, frequency bandwidth, power density, and energy efficiency. However, there are still challenges and trade-offs in the design and optimization of energy harvesters, such as the complexity, cost, size, and reliability of the devices. Therefore, further research and development are needed to explore new materials, structures, and mechanisms for energy harvesting, as well as to integrate energy harvesters with low-power consumption devices and applications.



### Electromechanical modeling of Lumped parameter model and coupled distributed parameter models and closed-form solutions

- Electromechanical modeling is the process of describing the dynamic behavior of systems that involve both electrical and mechanical components, such as piezoelectric devices, using mathematical equations.
- Lumped parameter model is a type of electromechanical model that assumes that the system can be represented by a finite number of discrete elements, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, masses, springs, and dampers, that are connected at nodes with zero electrical length and zero capacitance to ground. Lumped parameter models are often simpler and easier to analyze than distributed parameter models, but they may not capture the spatial variations and wave phenomena that occur in the system.
- Distributed parameter model is a type of electromechanical model that assumes that the system can be represented by a continuous distribution of elements, such as transmission lines, beams, plates, or membranes, that have spatial dimensions and distributed properties, such as resistance, inductance, capacitance, mass, stiffness, and damping. Distributed parameter models are often more accurate and realistic than lumped parameter models, but they may require more complex mathematical tools, such as partial differential equations, to solve.
- Coupled distributed parameter model is a type of electromechanical model that combines two or more distributed parameter models to describe the interactions between different physical domains, such as electrical, mechanical, thermal, or fluidic. Coupled distributed parameter models can capture the multiphysics behavior of complex systems, such as piezoelectric energy harvesters, but they may also increase the computational cost and difficulty of the analysis.
- Closed-form solution is a type of solution that can be expressed by a finite number of elementary functions, such as polynomials, trigonometric functions, exponential functions, or logarithmic functions, without the need of numerical methods or approximations. Closed-form solutions are often desirable and elegant, but they may not exist or be easy to find for some electromechanical models, especially for nonlinear or coupled distributed parameter models.



## Unit 3 - ELECTROMAGNETIC ENERGY HARVESTING AND NON-LINEAR TECHNIQUES

Electromagnetic energy harvesting is a process of converting ambient mechanical vibrations into electrical energy using electromagnetic transducers. Electromagnetic energy harvesters can be used to power wireless sensor nodes, wearable devices, biomedical implants and other low-power electronics.

Non-linear techniques are methods of enhancing the performance of electromagnetic energy harvesters by introducing non-linearities in the mechanical or electrical domain. Non-linearities can increase the bandwidth, output power and efficiency of the harvesters, as well as enable new functionalities such as frequency tuning, synchronization and self-adaptation.

Some examples of non-linear techniques are:

- Magnetic levitation: using permanent magnets to create a repulsive force that balances the gravity and provides a contactless suspension of the moving mass. This reduces the friction and damping, and allows for large displacements and high output voltages .

### Harvesting microelectronic circuits

- Harvesting microelectronic circuits are circuits that can convert ambient energy sources, such as light, heat, vibration, or radio waves, into electrical energy for powering low-power devices, such as wireless sensors, implantable devices, or wearable electronics.
- Harvesting microelectronic circuits typically consist of four main components: a transducer, a rectifier, a voltage regulator, and a storage element.
- A transducer is a device that converts one form of energy into another. For example, a photovoltaic cell converts light into electricity, a thermoelectric generator converts heat into electricity, a piezoelectric element converts mechanical stress into electricity, or an antenna converts electromagnetic waves into electricity.
- A rectifier is a circuit that converts alternating current (AC) into direct current (DC). This is necessary because most ambient energy sources produce AC signals, while most electronic devices require DC power. A rectifier can be implemented using diodes, switches, or capacitors.
- A voltage regulator is a circuit that maintains a constant output voltage regardless of the input voltage variations or the load current variations. This is necessary because the output voltage of the transducer and the rectifier may fluctuate depending on the ambient energy conditions or the power consumption of the device. A voltage regulator can be implemented using linear regulators, switching regulators, or charge pumps.
- A storage element is a device that can store electrical energy for later use. This is necessary because the ambient energy sources may not be available or sufficient at all times, and the device may need to operate in bursts or intermittently. A storage element can be implemented using batteries, capacitors, or supercapacitors.
- Harvesting microelectronic circuits face several challenges, such as low power density, low voltage level, high output impedance, and high variability of the ambient energy sources. Therefore, they require careful design and optimization to achieve high efficiency, low power consumption, and long lifetime .



### Power conditioning and losses for energy harvesting wireless sensors

- Power conditioning is the process of converting the harvested energy from various sources (such as solar, thermal, vibration, etc.) into a suitable form for powering the wireless sensor nodes (WSNs)   .
- Power conditioning typically involves voltage regulation, rectification, filtering, and storage of the harvested energy  .
- Power conditioning is essential for ensuring the reliability, efficiency, and functionality of the WSNs, as the harvested energy is often intermittent, unstable, and incompatible with the WSNs' requirements  .
- Power losses can occur in various stages of power conditioning, such as in the transducers, converters, regulators, and storage elements  .
- Power losses can reduce the amount of usable energy for the WSNs, and thus affect their performance and lifetime  .
- Power losses can be minimized by using appropriate power conditioning techniques, such as impedance matching, maximum power point tracking, adaptive control, and low-power design   .

### Harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags

- RF sensors and ID tags are devices that use radio frequency (RF) signals to communicate data, such as identification, location, temperature, or motion, to a reader or a network.
- RF sensors and ID tags can be classified into passive, semi-passive, or active, depending on their power source and functionality.
- Passive RF sensors and ID tags have no battery and rely on the RF energy from the reader to power up their circuitry and transmit their data. They have a limited range and performance, but a long lifetime and low cost.
- Semi-passive RF sensors and ID tags have a battery that powers their circuitry, but not their RF communication. They use the RF energy from the reader to transmit their data. They have a longer range and better performance than passive tags, but a shorter lifetime and higher cost.
- Active RF sensors and ID tags have a battery that powers both their circuitry and their RF communication. They can initiate their own transmission and communicate with other tags or networks. They have the longest range and the best performance, but the shortest lifetime and the highest cost.
- Harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags is the process of collecting ambient energy from the environment, such as light, heat, vibration, or RF waves, and converting it into electrical energy to power the devices.
- Harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags can extend their lifetime, reduce their cost, and enhance their functionality, especially for active and semi-passive devices.
- Harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags can be done using various techniques, such as solar cells, thermoelectric generators, piezoelectric transducers, or RF rectifiers.
- Harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags can also be done using the RF signal from the reader or other sources, such as TV or cellular towers, as the energy source. This is called RF energy harvesting or RF power harvesting.
- RF energy harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags can be done using different methods, such as direct power transfer, backscatter modulation, or harmonic power harvesting.
- Direct power transfer is the method of using the RF signal from the reader to directly power the tag circuitry and communication. This is the conventional method used by passive tags.
- Backscatter modulation is the method of using the RF signal from the reader to power the tag circuitry, and then modulating the reflected signal to transmit the tag data. This is the method used by semi-passive tags and some passive tags.
- Harmonic power harvesting is the method of using the RF signal from the reader or other sources to power the tag circuitry, and then generating a harmonic signal to transmit the tag data. This is a novel method that can enable passive tags to have longer range and higher data rate.




### Powering Wireless SHM Sensor Nodes through Energy Harvesting

- Wireless sensor nodes and sensor networks are widely used for structural health monitoring (SHM) applications, such as detecting damage, cracks, corrosion, or deformation in buildings, bridges, pipelines, aircraft, etc.
- However, one of the main challenges is how to power the sensor nodes, which typically consist of sensors, microcontrollers, memory, and wireless transceivers, without requiring frequent battery replacement or maintenance.
- Energy harvesting is a promising technique to provide a sustainable and autonomous power source for wireless sensor nodes by converting ambient energy sources, such as solar, thermal, vibration, wind, or electromagnetic radiation, into electrical energy.
- Energy harvesting systems usually consist of four components: an energy transducer, a power conditioning circuit, an energy storage device, and a power management unit.
- The energy transducer converts the ambient energy into electrical energy, such as a photovoltaic cell, a thermoelectric generator, a piezoelectric element, or an antenna.
- The power conditioning circuit adjusts the output voltage and current of the energy transducer to match the requirements of the sensor node, such as a DC-DC converter, a rectifier, or a regulator.
- The energy storage device stores the excess energy harvested when the ambient energy is abundant and provides energy when the ambient energy is scarce, such as a battery, a capacitor, or a supercapacitor.
- The power management unit controls the charging and discharging of the energy storage device and optimizes the power consumption of the sensor node, such as a microcontroller, a switch, or a sensor.
- Different energy harvesting techniques have different advantages and disadvantages, depending on the availability, intensity, and variability of the ambient energy sources, as well as the size, weight, cost, and efficiency of the energy transducers and power conditioning circuits.
- Some examples of energy harvesting techniques for wireless SHM sensor nodes are:

  - Solar energy harvesting: using photovoltaic cells to convert sunlight into electrical energy. This technique is suitable for outdoor applications where sunlight is abundant and predictable, but it requires large surface area, high cost, and low efficiency of the photovoltaic cells, and it cannot work at night or in cloudy conditions.
  - Thermal energy harvesting: using thermoelectric generators to convert temperature gradients into electrical energy. This technique is suitable for applications where there is a large and stable temperature difference between the sensor node and the environment, such as in industrial processes, but it requires high thermal conductivity, low electrical conductivity, and high Seebeck coefficient of the thermoelectric materials, and it has low efficiency and power density.
  - Vibration energy harvesting: using piezoelectric elements to convert mechanical vibrations into electrical energy. This technique is suitable for applications where there is a high and periodic vibration source, such as in machinery, vehicles, or human motion, but it requires high mechanical stress, high resonant frequency, and high coupling coefficient of the piezoelectric materials, and it has low power density and bandwidth.
  - Wind energy harvesting: using micro wind turbines to convert air flow into electrical energy. This technique is suitable for applications where there is a sufficient and steady wind speed, such as in coastal or mountainous areas, but it requires large size, high cost, and high maintenance of the wind turbines, and it is affected by turbulence and noise.
  - Electromagnetic energy harvesting: using antennas to convert electromagnetic radiation into electrical energy. This technique is suitable for applications where there is a strong and constant electromagnetic source, such as in radio frequency (RF) or microwave wireless energy transmission systems, but it requires high gain, high efficiency, and high impedance matching of the antennas, and it is limited by the distance and direction of the electromagnetic source.
