- Reduced resolution time and costs
- Increased customer satisfaction and loyalty
- Enhanced agent productivity and morale
- Improved service quality and consistency
- Increased organizational learning and innovation

- Integrate: Knowledge creation and maintenance should be integrated with the service delivery process, not separated from it.

- Strategy: Define the vision, goals, and governance of KCS.

- Energy harvesting systems are devices or systems that are used to convert ambient energy, which is derived from external sources existing in the atmosphere, into electrical energy.

- Power management is the component that regulates and controls the flow of energy between the energy transducer, the energy storage, and the load. It can be based on different circuits, such as rectifiers, voltage converters, maximum power point trackers, etc.
- Load is the component that consumes the electrical energy provided by the energy harvesting system. It can be any electronic device, such as sensor, microcontroller, wireless transmitter, etc.
- Energy harvesting systems have various applications, such as wireless sensor networks, wearable devices, biomedical implants, environmental monitoring, structural health monitoring, etc  .
- Energy harvesting systems have various advantages, such as reducing the need for battery replacement, extending the lifetime of the system, enhancing the reliability and safety of the system, reducing the environmental impact of the system, etc .

# Energy sources for the notes of the Unit 1 - ENERGY HARVESTING SYSTEMS in the subject of KOT 062 ENERGY HARVESTING TECHNOLOGIES AND POWERMANAGEMENT FOR IOT DEVICES KCS

- Energy harvesting is the process of capturing and converting ambient energy from various sources into electrical energy for powering low-power devices.
- Energy harvesting systems consist of three main components: an energy source, an energy transducer, and an energy storage device.
- Energy sources are the environmental phenomena that provide energy for harvesting, such as light, heat, vibration, radio waves, etc.
- Energy transducers are the devices that convert the ambient energy into electrical energy, such as photovoltaic cells, thermoelectric generators, piezoelectric elements, etc.
- Energy storage devices are the components that store the electrical energy generated by the transducers, such as batteries, capacitors, supercapacitors, etc.
- The choice of the energy source and the transducer depends on the availability, intensity, and frequency of the ambient energy, as well as the power requirements and specifications of the device to be powered.
- Some of the most common energy sources for energy harvesting systems are:

  - Thermal energy: Thermal energy is the energy associated with temperature differences and heat flows. Thermal energy can be harvested from various sources, such as body heat, industrial waste heat, geothermal heat, solar radiation, etc. Thermal energy can be converted into electrical energy using thermoelectric generators, which are based on the Seebeck effect. The Seebeck effect is the phenomenon of generating a voltage difference across a circuit of two dissimilar conductors when they are subjected to a temperature gradient  .

  - Solar energy: Solar energy is the energy from the sun's radiation. Solar energy can be harvested using photovoltaic cells, which are devices that convert light into electricity using the photovoltaic effect. The photovoltaic effect is the phenomenon of generating an electric current in a material when it absorbs photons of light. Photovoltaic cells are made of semiconductors, such as silicon, that have a band gap that matches the energy of the photons  .

  - Vibration energy: Vibration energy is the energy from mechanical oscillations and movements. Vibration energy can be harvested from various sources, such as human motion, machinery, vehicles, wind, etc. Vibration energy can be converted into electrical energy using piezoelectric elements, which are materials that generate an electric charge when they are deformed by an applied force. Piezoelectric elements are made of crystals, such as quartz, that have a polar structure that aligns the electric dipoles when they are stressed  .

  - Radio frequency (RF) energy: RF energy is the energy from electromagnetic waves in the radio spectrum. RF energy can be harvested from various sources, such as wireless communication signals, broadcast signals, radar signals, etc. RF energy can be converted into electrical energy using RF energy harvesters, which are devices that capture and rectify the RF signals using antennas and diodes. RF energy harvesters can also modulate and transmit data back to the source using backscatter communication  .

  - Other energy sources: There are other energy sources that can be used for energy harvesting, such as wind energy, fluid flow, blood sugar, atmospheric pressure changes, etc. These energy sources can be converted into electrical energy using different transducers, such as wind turbines, microfluidic devices, biofuel cells, piezoelectric membranes, etc  .



# Energy Harvesting Based Sensor Networks

- Energy harvesting is the process of capturing and converting ambient energy sources (such as solar, wind, thermal, kinetic, etc.) into electrical energy that can be used to power wireless sensor nodes.
- Energy harvesting based sensor networks (EHBSNs) are wireless sensor networks (WSNs) that use energy harvesting as the main or supplementary power source for the sensor nodes.
- EHBSNs have several advantages over conventional battery-powered WSNs, such as:
  - Longer lifetime and reduced maintenance cost
  - Higher reliability and robustness
  - Enhanced scalability and flexibility
  - Reduced environmental impact and waste
- EHBSNs also face several challenges, such as:
  - Intermittent and unpredictable energy availability
  - Trade-off between energy consumption and quality of service
  - Complex and dynamic network topology
  - Resource constraints and heterogeneity
- EHBSNs require novel design and optimization techniques at different layers of the network protocol stack, such as:
  - Energy harvesting and storage unit: This unit is responsible for capturing, converting, and storing the harvested energy. It should be efficient, adaptive, and compatible with the sensor node and the energy source.
  - Task allocation: This is the process of assigning tasks (such as sensing, processing, and communication) to the sensor nodes based on their energy availability, priority, and deadline. It should balance the energy consumption and the performance of the network.
  - Medium access control (MAC): This is the mechanism that coordinates the access of the sensor nodes to the shared wireless channel. It should avoid collisions, reduce overhead, and adapt to the energy harvesting conditions.
  - Routing: This is the process of finding and maintaining paths for data transmission from the source nodes to the sink node or the base station. It should minimize the energy consumption, maximize the throughput, and cope with the network dynamics.
  - Application: This is the layer that defines the objectives, requirements, and constraints of the network. It should be aware of the energy harvesting characteristics and the network conditions, and provide feedback and adaptation mechanisms.



# Photovoltaic Cell Technologies

- Photovoltaic (PV) cells are devices that absorb energy from sunlight and convert it into electrical energy through semiconducting materials .
- PV cells are based on the photovoltaic effect, which is the process of generating voltage and electric current in a material upon exposure to light.
- PV cells are usually made of different semiconductor materials, such as silicon, cadmium telluride, copper indium gallium selenide, etc.
- PV cells are connected together in chains to form larger power-generating units known as modules or panels .
- PV modules or panels can be installed on rooftops, ground-mounted systems, or integrated into building materials or vehicles.
- PV technologies can be classified into three main categories: crystalline silicon (c-Si), thin-film, and emerging technologies.
- Crystalline silicon (c-Si) is the most widely used PV technology, accounting for about 90% of the global PV market. It has high efficiency, durability, and reliability, but also high manufacturing cost and energy consumption.
- Thin-film PV technologies use thin layers of semiconducting materials deposited on a substrate, such as glass, metal, or plastic. They have lower efficiency, but also lower cost, weight, and material usage than c-Si. They can also be flexible and adaptable to different surfaces and applications.
- Emerging PV technologies include perovskite, organic, quantum dot, and dye-sensitized solar cells, which are still in the research and development stage. They have the potential to achieve higher efficiency, lower cost, and novel functionalities, such as transparency, color, and tunability.



# Generation of electric power in semiconductor PV cells

- PV cells are devices that convert light energy into electrical energy using the photovoltaic effect .
- The photovoltaic effect is the phenomenon of generating an electric voltage or current in a material when it is exposed to light .
- The material used in PV cells is usually a semiconductor, such as silicon, which has the property of changing its electrical conductivity when light is absorbed.
- A semiconductor has two types of charge carriers: electrons and holes. Electrons are negatively charged particles that can move freely in the material, while holes are positively charged regions where electrons are missing.
- When light with sufficient energy (or photons) hits a semiconductor, it can excite some of the electrons from the lower energy state (or valence band) to the higher energy state (or conduction band), creating electron-hole pairs .
- The electron-hole pairs are separated by an electric field created by a junction between two layers of semiconductor with different doping levels. Doping is the process of adding impurities to a semiconductor to change its electrical properties.
- The junction can be either a p-n junction, where one layer is doped with a material that creates excess holes (p-type) and the other layer is doped with a material that creates excess electrons (n-type), or a heterojunction, where two different semiconductors are used.
- The electric field at the junction pushes the electrons to the n-type layer and the holes to the p-type layer, creating a potential difference or voltage across the cell.
- When the cell is connected to an external circuit, the electrons and holes can flow through it, generating an electric current and power.
- The power output of a PV cell depends on several factors, such as the intensity and wavelength of the light, the area and efficiency of the cell, the temperature and the load resistance .
- The power output of a single PV cell is typically only about two watts, so multiple cells are connected in series or parallel to form modules, panels or arrays to increase the voltage or current .
- PV cells can be used to generate electricity for various applications, such as residential, commercial, industrial, or remote areas. They can also be integrated into buildings, vehicles, or devices as a form of distributed generation .

# Unit 2 - Piezoelectric Energy Harvesting and Electromechanical Modeling

- Piezoelectric energy harvesting is the process of converting mechanical vibrations into electrical energy using piezoelectric materials, which exhibit a voltage response when subjected to mechanical stress or strain.
- Piezoelectric energy harvesters can be used to power low-power devices such as sensors, wireless transmitters, or microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) by scavenging energy from ambient sources such as wind, human motion, or machinery.
- Electromechanical modeling of piezoelectric energy harvesters is the mathematical description of the coupled dynamics of the mechanical structure and the electrical circuit, which can be used to predict the performance, optimize the design, and analyze the stability of the system.
- Electromechanical modeling can be done at different levels of complexity and accuracy, depending on the assumptions and simplifications made about the geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, and excitation of the system.
- Some common electromechanical modeling approaches for piezoelectric energy harvesters are:
  - Lumped-parameter models, which treat the system as a single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) or a multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) oscillator with equivalent mass, stiffness, damping, and piezoelectric coefficients. These models are simple and easy to implement, but they may not capture the distributed effects and higher modes of the system.
  - Distributed-parameter models, which account for the spatial variation of the mechanical and electrical variables along the system using partial differential equations (PDEs). These models are more accurate and general, but they may require numerical methods or analytical techniques such as the Rayleigh-Ritz method or the Galerkin method to obtain the solutions.
  - Finite element models, which discretize the system into a finite number of elements with nodal degrees of freedom and apply the principle of virtual work or the principle of minimum potential energy to obtain the equations of motion and the output voltage. These models can handle complex geometries and nonlinearities, but they may be computationally expensive and require specialized software tools.



# Piezoelectric materials

- Piezoelectric materials are materials that can convert mechanical stress into electrical charge, or vice versa. This phenomenon is called the piezoelectric effect .
- Piezoelectric materials can be broadly classified into the following categories:
  - Crystalline materials, such as quartz, SiO2, which is the best known natural piezoelectric material.
  - Ceramic materials, such as lead zirconate titanate (PZT), barium titanate, and lead titanate, which are the most commonly produced and widely used synthetic piezoelectric materials . They have high piezoelectric coefficients and can be easily shaped and polarized.
  - Semiconductor materials, such as zinc oxide (ZnO) and gallium nitride (GaN), which can also be regarded as ceramics due to their relatively wide band gaps . They have high piezoelectric coefficients and can be used for high-frequency applications.
  - Polymer materials, such as polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) and its copolymers, which are flexible, lightweight, and biocompatible piezoelectric materials. They have low piezoelectric coefficients and can be used for low-frequency applications.
  - Composite materials, which are combinations of different piezoelectric materials, such as PZT and PVDF, to achieve improved properties and performance. They can be tailored to specific applications and have various geometries and configurations.
  - Glass-ceramic materials, such as lithium disilicate (Li2Si2O5) and barium titanate silicate (Ba2TiSiO6), which are polycrystalline materials with a glassy phase and a crystalline phase. They have moderate piezoelectric coefficients and can be used for high-temperature applications.
- Piezoelectric materials have useful applications in many industries, such as  :
  - Sensors and actuators, such as pressure sensors, accelerometers, microphones, ultrasonic transducers, and piezoelectric motors.
  - Energy harvesting and storage, such as piezoelectric generators, batteries, and capacitors, which can convert ambient mechanical vibrations into electrical energy, or store electrical energy for later use.
  - Medical and biological devices, such as piezoelectric implants, drug delivery systems, biosensors, and tissue engineering scaffolds, which can interact with the human body and provide diagnostic or therapeutic functions.
  - Information and communication technologies, such as piezoelectric displays, speakers, printers, scanners, and optical modulators, which can process and transmit signals and data.
  - Industrial and environmental applications, such as piezoelectric igniters, cleaners, welders, mixers, and filters, which can provide mechanical or electrical functions for various processes and systems.

# Electromechanical Modeling

- Electromechanical modeling is the process of developing mathematical models that describe the coupled mechanical and electrical behavior of piezoelectric harvesters.
- Electromechanical modeling can help to understand, analyze, design, and optimize piezoelectric harvesters for different applications and operating conditions.
- Electromechanical modeling can be performed at different levels of complexity and accuracy, depending on the assumptions and simplifications made.
- Electromechanical modeling can be classified into two main categories: lumped-parameter modeling and distributed-parameter modeling.

## Lumped-Parameter Modeling

- Lumped-parameter modeling is a type of modeling that treats the piezoelectric harvester as a system of discrete masses, springs, dampers, and electrical components, connected by ideal joints or wires.
- Lumped-parameter modeling is based on the application of Newton's laws of motion, Kirchhoff's laws of electric circuits, and the constitutive equations of piezoelectricity.
- Lumped-parameter modeling is relatively simple and fast, but it can only capture the global dynamics of the piezoelectric harvester, and it may neglect some important effects, such as shear deformation, rotary inertia, or higher-order modes.

## Distributed-Parameter Modeling

- Distributed-parameter modeling is a type of modeling that treats the piezoelectric harvester as a continuous medium, with spatially varying mechanical and electrical properties.
- Distributed-parameter modeling is based on the application of the principles of conservation of mass, momentum, and energy, the Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism, and the constitutive equations of piezoelectricity.
- Distributed-parameter modeling is more complex and computationally intensive, but it can capture the local dynamics of the piezoelectric harvester, and it can account for more realistic effects, such as geometric nonlinearity, material anisotropy, or boundary conditions.

# Strategies for enhancing the performance of energy harvesters

Energy harvesters are devices that can convert ambient energy sources, such as vibration, wind, light, heat, etc., into electrical energy. Energy harvesting is a promising technology for powering low-power consumption devices, such as sensors, wireless networks, wearable electronics, etc. Energy harvesters can reduce the dependence on batteries and extend the lifetime of the devices.

However, energy harvesters also face some challenges, such as low power output, narrow frequency bandwidth, and mismatched impedance. Therefore, various strategies have been proposed to enhance the performance of energy harvesters, especially for vibration-based piezoelectric energy harvesters (PEHs), which are widely used due to their high energy density and easy miniaturization.

Some of the strategies for enhancing the performance of PEHs are:

- **Material optimization**: The choice of piezoelectric material can affect the output voltage and power of the PEH. Some factors to consider are the piezoelectric coefficient, the Young's modulus, the density, the Curie temperature, the mechanical quality factor, etc. Some examples of piezoelectric materials are lead zirconate titanate (PZT), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), zinc oxide (ZnO), etc.  

- **Structure optimization**: The design of the PEH structure can also influence the performance of the device. Some aspects to consider are the shape, size, thickness, configuration, and arrangement of the piezoelectric element, the additional mass, the elastic support, etc. Some examples of PEH structures are cantilever, bridge, beam, plate, etc.   

- **Nonlinear techniques**: The nonlinear techniques can help to increase the power output and broaden the frequency bandwidth of the PEH. Some examples of nonlinear techniques are bi-stability, snap-through, magnetic coupling, frequency tuning, etc. These techniques can induce large-amplitude oscillations, chaotic motions, or frequency up-conversion in the PEH, which can enhance the energy conversion efficiency.   

- **Power management**: The power management can help to match the impedance between the PEH and the load, and to store and regulate the harvested energy. Some examples of power management are rectification, voltage boosting, voltage regulation, energy storage, etc. These methods can improve the power quality and stability of the PEH, and optimize the power transfer efficiency.  

- **Hybridization**: The hybridization can help to integrate the PEH with other energy harvesting technologies, such as thermoelectric, electromagnetic, electrostatic, triboelectric, etc. These technologies can complement each other and harvest energy from different sources or modes. The hybridization can increase the power output and reliability of the PEH, and expand the application scenarios.



# Electromechanical modeling of Lumped parameter model and coupled distributed parameter models and closed-form solutions

- Electromechanical modeling is the process of describing the dynamic behavior of systems that involve both electrical and mechanical components, such as piezoelectric devices, motors, sensors, etc.
- Lumped parameter model is a simplified representation of a system that assumes that the physical properties of the system are constant or uniform throughout the system, and that the system can be divided into a finite number of discrete elements connected by nodes.
- Coupled distributed parameter model is a more realistic representation of a system that takes into account the spatial variation of the physical properties of the system, and that the system can be described by partial differential equations or integral equations.
- Closed-form solution is an analytical expression that can be obtained for the output or response of a system, without the need of numerical methods or approximations.

## Lumped parameter model of piezoelectric devices

- Piezoelectric devices are devices that can convert mechanical energy into electrical energy, or vice versa, by exploiting the piezoelectric effect, which is the property of certain materials to generate electric charges when subjected to mechanical stress, or to deform when subjected to electric fields.
- A lumped parameter model of a piezoelectric device can be derived by applying the Kirchhoff's laws of electric circuits and the Newton's laws of motion to the device, and by assuming that the device can be modeled as a mass-spring-damper system with a voltage source and a capacitor.
- The lumped parameter model can be represented by the following equations:

  - Mechanical equation: $$m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = F + V_cC_p\dot{x}$$
  - Electrical equation: $$V_c = V - R_iI - \frac{1}{C_p}\int I dt$$
  - Constitutive equation: $$I = C_p\dot{V_c} + xC_p\dot{V}$$

  where:

  - $m$ is the mass of the device
  - $c$ is the damping coefficient of the device
  - $k$ is the stiffness of the device
  - $x$ is the displacement of the device
  - $F$ is the external force applied to the device
  - $V_c$ is the voltage across the capacitor
  - $V$ is the external voltage applied to the device
  - $R_i$ is the internal resistance of the device
  - $I$ is the current flowing through the device
  - $C_p$ is the piezoelectric capacitance of the device

- The lumped parameter model can be used to analyze the performance of the piezoelectric device in different modes of operation, such as sensing, actuation, or energy harvesting, and to design the optimal parameters of the device for a given application.

## Coupled distributed parameter model of piezoelectric devices

- A coupled distributed parameter model of a piezoelectric device can be derived by applying the Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism and the Navier's equations of elasticity to the device, and by taking into account the spatial variation of the electric potential, electric displacement, stress, strain, and displacement of the device.
- A coupled distributed parameter model of a piezoelectric device can be represented by the following equations:

  - Electromechanical coupling equation: $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_e$$
  - Constitutive equation: $$\mathbf{D} = \epsilon \mathbf{E} + e \mathbf{S}$$
  - Constitutive equation: $$\mathbf{S} = s \mathbf{E} + d \mathbf{E}$$
  - Equilibrium equation: $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{T} + \mathbf{F} = \rho \ddot{\mathbf{u}}$$

  where:

  - $\mathbf{D}$ is the electric displacement vector
  - $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field vector
  - $\mathbf{S}$ is the mechanical strain tensor
  - $\mathbf{T}$ is the mechanical stress tensor
  - $\mathbf{u}$ is the mechanical displacement vector

- Electromagnetic energy harvesting (EMEH) is a process of converting ambient mechanical vibrations into electrical energy using electromagnetic transducers.
- EMEH devices consist of a coil of wire and a magnet that move relative to each other under external excitation, inducing a voltage across the coil terminals.
- EMEH devices can be classified into linear and nonlinear types, depending on the nature of the magnetic force between the coil and the magnet.
- Linear EMEH devices have a constant magnetic force and operate within a narrow frequency band around the natural frequency of the system. They require precise tuning and matching of the load resistance to achieve optimal power output.
- Nonlinear EMEH devices have a variable magnetic force that depends on the position and velocity of the magnet. They can operate over a wide frequency range and exhibit multiple stable states, bifurcations and chaos. They can also adapt to the load resistance and achieve higher power output than linear devices.
- Nonlinear EMEH devices can be implemented using various techniques, such as magnetic levitation, magnetic springs, magnetic stops, bistable magnets, and nonlinear interface circuits.
- Magnetic levitation is a technique that uses repulsive magnetic forces to suspend the magnet above the coil, eliminating the need for mechanical springs and reducing the damping and friction losses.
- Magnetic springs are nonlinear springs that use attractive or repulsive magnetic forces to provide a restoring force for the magnet. They can be designed to have negative, zero or positive stiffness, depending on the configuration and orientation of the magnets.
- Magnetic stops are mechanical or magnetic barriers that limit the motion of the magnet and induce a sudden change in the magnetic force. They can create hardening or softening nonlinearities and enhance the bandwidth and power output of the EMEH device.
- Bistable magnets are magnets that have two stable equilibrium positions and can switch between them under external excitation. They can generate large displacements and high voltages, as well as exhibit complex dynamical behaviors such as hysteresis, snap-through and chaos.
- Nonlinear interface circuits are electrical circuits that modify the voltage and current characteristics of the EMEH device and create nonlinear feedback loops. They can improve the power conversion efficiency, increase the output power, and extend the operating frequency range of the EMEH device.

- **Using a free-standing core or a multi-layer core.** The core is the part of the harvester that connects the magnet and the coil. The core material and shape affect the magnetic flux distribution and the coupling coefficient of the harvester. A free-standing core is a core that is not attached to the base or the frame of the harvester. A free-standing core can reduce the magnetic leakage and increase the magnetic flux linkage. # Power Conditioning and Losses for Energy Harvesting Wireless Sensors

- Energy harvesting wireless sensors (EHWS) are devices that can collect ambient energy from the environment and convert it into electrical energy to power themselves and perform sensing and communication tasks.

- Power conditioning is the process of regulating and optimizing the harvested energy to meet the requirements of the sensor node. It involves the following steps:
  - Rectification: converting the AC output of some energy harvesters (such as piezoelectric, electromagnetic, or thermoelectric) into DC.
  - Maximum power point tracking (MPPT): adjusting the input impedance of the power conditioning circuit to match the output impedance of the energy harvester, so that the maximum possible power can be extracted.
  - Voltage regulation: boosting or bucking the rectified voltage to a desired level that can be used by the sensor node or stored in a capacitor or battery.
  - Energy management: controlling the charging and discharging of the energy storage element, and switching between different power modes of the sensor node, such as active, idle, or sleep.
- Power conditioning circuits can be implemented using various components, such as diodes, capacitors, inductors, transistors, switches, and microcontrollers. The choice of components depends on the type, characteristics, and output power of the energy harvester, as well as the power consumption and functionality of the sensor node.
- Power conditioning circuits introduce losses in the energy harvesting system, which reduce the overall efficiency and performance of the EHWS. Some sources of losses are:
  - Conduction losses: caused by the resistance of the wires and the components in the circuit, which dissipate power as heat.
  - Switching losses: caused by the finite switching time and the parasitic capacitance and inductance of the switches, which result in transient currents and voltages that waste power.
  - Control losses: caused by the power consumption of the control circuit or the microcontroller that implements the MPPT and the energy management algorithms.
  - Leakage losses: caused by the self-discharge of the energy storage element, which reduces the stored energy over time.
- To minimize the losses and improve the efficiency of the power conditioning circuit, some design techniques can be applied, such as:
  - Choosing low-resistance and low-leakage components, such as Schottky diodes, low-ESR capacitors, and low-RDS(on) transistors.
  - Reducing the switching frequency and the duty cycle of the switches, which reduces the switching losses and the control losses.
  - Using adaptive MPPT and energy management algorithms, which can adjust to the variations of the harvested energy and the load demand, and avoid unnecessary switching and control actions.
  - Using low-power and low-voltage microcontrollers, which can reduce the control losses and the voltage regulation losses.

# Case studies for Implanted medical devices

- Implantable medical devices (IMDs) are electronic devices that are inserted into the human body to monitor, diagnose, or treat various medical conditions.
- IMDs require a reliable and long-lasting power source to function properly and avoid frequent surgical replacements or infections.
- Energy harvesting is a promising technique to provide power for IMDs by converting ambient energy sources such as mechanical, thermal, or chemical energy into electrical energy.
- Some examples of energy harvesting for IMDs are:

  - **Piezoelectric energy harvesters**: These devices use piezoelectric materials that generate electric voltage when subjected to mechanical stress or strain. The mechanical energy can be derived from the heartbeat, blood flow, respiration, or body movements. For instance, a piezoelectric energy harvester was implanted in a sheep's heart to power a pacemaker .
  - **Thermoelectric energy harvesters**: These devices use thermoelectric materials that generate electric voltage when exposed to a temperature gradient. The temperature difference can be created by the body heat or the blood flow. For example, a thermoelectric energy harvester was implanted in a rat's aorta to power a wireless blood pressure sensor.
  - **Biofuel cells**: These devices use enzymes or microorganisms that catalyze the oxidation of glucose or other biomolecules in the body fluids and produce electric current. The biofuel cells can harvest chemical energy from the blood, interstitial fluid, or cerebrospinal fluid. For instance, a biofuel cell was implanted in a rat's abdominal cavity to power a wireless transmitter .

# Harvesting for RF Sensors and ID Tags

- RF sensors and ID tags are devices that use radio frequency (RF) signals to communicate data, such as identification, location, temperature, etc.
- RF sensors and ID tags can be classified into passive, semi-passive, and active, depending on their power source and functionality.
- Passive RF sensors and ID tags have no battery and rely on the RF reader signal to power up and transmit data. They have low cost, long lifetime, and small size, but limited range and functionality.
- Semi-passive RF sensors and ID tags have a battery that powers the sensor or the logic circuit, but not the RF communication. They have longer range and higher functionality than passive tags, but higher cost and shorter lifetime.
- Active RF sensors and ID tags have a battery that powers both the sensor and the RF communication. They have the longest range and the highest functionality, but also the highest cost and the shortest lifetime.
- Energy harvesting is a technique that enables RF sensors and ID tags to collect energy from the environment, such as light, heat, vibration, or RF signals, and use it to power the device or extend the battery life.
- Energy harvesting can improve the performance, reliability, and sustainability of RF sensors and ID tags, especially for active and semi-passive ones, by reducing the dependency on batteries and enabling self-powered operation.
- Energy harvesting can also enable new applications of RF sensors and ID tags, such as wireless sensor networks, smart buildings, smart cities, and internet of things, by increasing the scalability, flexibility, and adaptability of the devices.
- Energy harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags can be achieved by using different types of energy harvesters, such as solar cells, thermoelectric generators, piezoelectric generators, electromagnetic generators, or RF harvesters, depending on the available energy sources and the power requirements of the device.
- Energy harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags also requires power management circuits, such as rectifiers, voltage converters, regulators, and storage elements, to efficiently convert, regulate, and store the harvested energy for the device operation.
- Energy harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags is a challenging and active research area, as it involves trade-offs between the power consumption, the power generation, the power storage, and the power transfer of the device, as well as the environmental factors, the device design, and the application requirements.



# Powering Wireless SHM Sensor Nodes

- Wireless sensor nodes (WSNs) are devices that can sense, process, and communicate data wirelessly.
- Structural health monitoring (SHM) is an application of WSNs that aims to detect and assess the damage or degradation of structures such as bridges, buildings, pipelines, etc.
- Powering WSNs for SHM is a challenge because they need to operate for long periods of time, often in harsh or inaccessible environments, and with limited or no access to external power sources.
- Energy harvesting (EH) is a technique that can provide power to WSNs by converting ambient energy sources such as solar, thermal, vibration, wind, etc. into electrical energy.
- EH can extend the lifetime, reliability, and autonomy of WSNs for SHM, as well as reduce the maintenance and replacement costs of batteries.
- EH can also enable new applications of WSNs for SHM that require high power consumption, such as active sensing, data fusion, or actuation.
- EH for WSNs for SHM can be classified into two categories: local EH and remote EH.
- Local EH refers to the case where the energy harvester is attached to or integrated with the sensor node, and the energy source is in the vicinity of the node.
- Remote EH refers to the case where the energy harvester is separated from the sensor node, and the energy source is at a distance from the node.
- Remote EH can be achieved by wireless energy transmission (WET), which uses electromagnetic waves to transfer energy from a transmitter to a receiver.
- WET can overcome the limitations of local EH, such as the variability, intermittency, or scarcity of ambient energy sources, or the mismatch between the energy source and the sensor location.
- WET can also provide a controlled and constant power supply to the sensor node, which can improve the performance and functionality of the node.
- WET for WSNs for SHM can be implemented using different frequency bands, such as radio frequency (RF), microwave, or laser.
- RF WET uses low-frequency waves (below 6 GHz) to transmit energy, and has the advantages of low cost, easy implementation, and high safety. However, RF WET also has the disadvantages of low efficiency, low power density, and high interference.
- Microwave WET uses high-frequency waves (above 6 GHz) to transmit energy, and has the advantages of high efficiency, high power density, and low interference. However, microwave WET also has the disadvantages of high cost, complex implementation, and low safety.
- Laser WET uses optical waves (visible or infrared) to transmit energy, and has the advantages of high efficiency, high power density, and low interference. However, laser WET also has the disadvantages of high cost, complex implementation, and low safety.
- The choice of the frequency band for WET depends on the application requirements, such as the distance, power, and data rate of the sensor node, as well as the environmental conditions, such as the weather, obstacles, or regulations.
- Some examples of WET for WSNs for SHM are:

  - A prototype system for delivering energy to SHM sensor nodes by microwave WET in the 10 GHz X-band . The system achieved a power transfer efficiency of 10% and a power density of 0.1 W/cm2 at a distance of 10 m.
  - A feasibility study of using RF WET to provide power for SHM sensor nodes embedded in the host structure. The study showed that RF WET can deliver up to 1 mW of power to the sensor node at a distance of 10 cm.
  - A concept of using laser WET to power SHM sensor nodes on rotating structures, such as wind turbines or helicopter blades. The concept proposed a laser transmitter on the ground and a photovoltaic receiver on the structure, and estimated a power transfer efficiency of 20% and a power density of 1 W/cm2 at a distance of 100 m.
