

# Business Intelligence and Analytics

- Business intelligence and analytics are related but distinct concepts that involve the use of data to support business decisions and actions.
- Business intelligence (BI) refers to the process of collecting, organizing, and presenting data in user-friendly views such as reports, dashboards, charts, and graphs. BI tools enable business users to access and explore different types of data, such as historical, current, third-party, and in-house data, as well as semi-structured and unstructured data like social media. BI tools also provide features such as data cleansing, data integration, data warehousing, data visualization, and data governance. BI aims to provide accurate, consistent, and timely information to support strategic, tactical, and operational decision-making.  
- Business analytics (BA) refers to the process of applying statistical, mathematical, and computational methods to data sets to discover patterns, trends, and insights. BA tools enable business users to perform various types of analysis, such as descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analysis. BA tools also provide features such as data mining, machine learning, artificial intelligence, natural language processing, and optimization. BA aims to provide actionable, relevant, and valuable information to support problem-solving, innovation, and performance improvement. 
- Business intelligence and analytics are often used together to provide a comprehensive and holistic view of the business situation and the possible courses of action. BI and BA can help businesses to gain a competitive advantage, increase customer satisfaction, reduce costs, enhance efficiency, and drive growth. BI and BA can also help businesses to comply with regulations, manage risks, and improve quality.



# KCS

KCS stands for Knowledge-Centered Service, which is a methodology for creating and maintaining documentation as part of the service delivery process. KCS aims to improve service quality, productivity, and customer satisfaction by capturing and reusing the knowledge of service teams. Some of the main features of KCS are:

- KCS is based on the principle of "capture in the workflow", which means that service agents create and update knowledge articles while solving customer issues, rather than as a separate task.
- KCS uses a "demand-driven" approach, which means that knowledge articles are created and refined based on the actual needs and feedback of customers, rather than based on assumptions or predictions.
- KCS follows a "structured problem-solving" process, which involves identifying the issue, finding and verifying the cause, resolving the issue, and documenting the solution in a knowledge article.
- KCS adopts a "self-service" model, which enables customers to access and use the knowledge articles without contacting the service desk, reducing the workload and cost of service delivery.
- KCS promotes a "collaborative" culture, which encourages service agents to share and reuse knowledge, learn from each other, and improve their skills and performance.

KCS is widely used by service organizations across various industries and domains, such as IT, customer support, health care, education, and government. KCS can help service organizations achieve the following benefits:

- Improve customer satisfaction and loyalty by providing faster, more accurate, and consistent service.
- Reduce service costs and increase efficiency by reducing the number of repeat issues, escalations, and transfers.
- Increase service quality and innovation by capturing and leveraging the collective knowledge and experience of service teams.
- Enhance employee engagement and retention by empowering service agents to contribute and access knowledge, and rewarding them for their performance.

# Introduction - History and Evolution

- Business intelligence (BI) is the process of collecting, analyzing, and presenting data to support decision making in various domains of business.
- The term BI was coined in 1989 by Howard Dresner, a former Gartner analyst, who defined it as "concepts and methods to improve business decision making by using fact-based support systems" .
- However, the origins of BI can be traced back to the 19th century, when Richard Millar Devens described how a banker used information about market trends to gain a competitive advantage in his book "Cyclopaedia of Commercial and Business Anecdotes" .
- The evolution of BI can be divided into four stages: pre-digital, early digital, modern, and future   .

## Pre-digital stage
- This stage covers the period from the 19th century to the 1950s, when data was collected and analyzed manually using paper records, charts, and graphs.
- The main challenges in this stage were the lack of data availability, accuracy, and timeliness, as well as the difficulty of aggregating and synthesizing data from different sources.
- Some examples of BI applications in this stage are the use of census data for demographic analysis, the use of accounting data for financial analysis, and the use of sales data for market analysis.

## Early digital stage
- This stage covers the period from the 1950s to the 1980s, when the emergence of computers and databases enabled the automation of data collection and storage, as well as the development of simple query and reporting tools.
- The main challenges in this stage were the high cost and complexity of hardware and software, the lack of data integration and standardization, and the limited analytical capabilities and user-friendliness of the tools.
- Some examples of BI applications in this stage are the use of decision support systems (DSS) for scenario analysis, the use of online analytical processing (OLAP) for multidimensional analysis, and the use of executive information systems (EIS) for dashboard reporting.

## Modern stage
- This stage covers the period from the 1980s to the present, when the advancement of internet and cloud technologies enabled the expansion of data sources and formats, as well as the development of more sophisticated and interactive BI tools.
- The main challenges in this stage are the increasing volume, velocity, and variety of data, the need for data quality and security, and the demand for self-service and real-time BI solutions.
- Some examples of BI applications in this stage are the use of data mining for pattern discovery, the use of data visualization for data exploration, and the use of business analytics for predictive and prescriptive analysis.

## Future stage
- This stage covers the period from the present to the future, when the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) technologies will enable the automation of data analysis and presentation, as well as the development of more intelligent and personalized BI tools.
- The main challenges in this stage are the ethical and social implications of AI and ML, the need for human oversight and collaboration, and the integration of BI with other emerging technologies such as blockchain and IoT.
- Some examples of BI applications in this stage are the use of natural language processing (NLP) for conversational BI, the use of computer vision for image and video analysis, and the use of cognitive computing for cognitive BI.

- Business Intelligence (BI) is a set of processes, architectures, and technologies that convert raw data into meaningful information that drives profitable business actions.
- A business intelligence architecture articulates the technology standards and data management and analytics practices that support an organization's BI efforts, as well as the specific platforms and tools that will be deployed.
- A typical BI architecture consists of the following components :
  - Data sources: These are the original sources of data, such as databases, files, web services, etc. that contain the raw data to be analyzed.
  - Data integration: This is the process of extracting, transforming, and loading (ETL) data from different sources into a centralized data warehouse or data lake, where it can be stored and organized for analysis.
  - Data warehouse or data lake: This is the central repository of data that has been integrated, cleaned, and structured for analysis. A data warehouse is a relational database that follows a dimensional model, while a data lake is a distributed file system that stores data in its original format.
  - Data marts: These are subsets of data from the data warehouse or data lake that are tailored for specific business domains or analytical purposes. Data marts can be organized by subject areas, such as sales, marketing, finance, etc. or by functions, such as reporting, dashboarding, etc.
  - Data analysis and reporting: This is the process of applying various analytical techniques and tools to the data in the data marts to generate insights and reports that can support business decision-making. Data analysis and reporting can include descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analytics, as well as data visualization and storytelling.
  - Data consumption: This is the process of delivering and presenting the data analysis and reporting results to the end-users, such as business managers, analysts, customers, etc. Data consumption can involve various channels and formats, such as web portals, mobile apps, email, PDF, etc.

# Role of Mathematical Models in Business Intelligence

- A mathematical model is an abstract representation of a real-world system or problem that uses mathematical language and symbols to describe its behavior and outcomes.
- A business intelligence system provides decision makers with information and knowledge extracted from data, through the application of mathematical models and algorithms .
- The role of mathematical models in business intelligence is to:
  - Analyze data and identify patterns, trends, correlations, and anomalies that can help in decision making.
  - Predict future outcomes and scenarios based on historical data and assumptions.
  - Optimize resources and processes to achieve the best possible results and minimize costs and risks.
  - Learn from data and feedback and improve the performance and accuracy of the models over time.
- Some examples of mathematical models used in business intelligence are:
  - Regression models: These models estimate the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. They can be used for forecasting, testing hypotheses, and evaluating the impact of different factors on the outcome.
  - Classification models: These models assign data points to predefined categories or classes based on their features. They can be used for segmentation, targeting, and recommendation systems.
  - Clustering models: These models group data points based on their similarity or proximity. They can be used for finding patterns, outliers, and associations in data.
  - Optimization models: These models find the optimal solution to a problem that involves multiple objectives, constraints, and variables. They can be used for scheduling, routing, inventory management, and resource allocation.
  - Machine learning models: These models learn from data and adapt to new information without explicit programming. They can be used for classification, regression, clustering, anomaly detection, natural language processing, computer vision, and more.



# Real Time Business Intelligent System

- Real Time Business Intelligent System (RTBI) is a concept that describes the process of delivering business intelligence (BI) or information about business operations as they occur .
- BI is the process of collecting, analyzing, and presenting data to support decision making and improve business performance.
- RTBI aims to provide near to zero latency and access to information whenever it is required .
- RTBI can help organizations to monitor and respond to changing business conditions, identify and seize opportunities, and optimize business processes.
- RTBI can be implemented using different architectures, such as event-based, data warehouse, or server-less.
  - Event-based architecture: RTBI systems are event driven, and may use Complex Event Processing, Event Stream Processing and Mashup techniques to enable events to be analyzed without being first transformed and stored in a database.
  - Data warehouse architecture: RTBI systems increase the refresh cycle of an existing data warehouse, and use techniques such as change data capture, data federation, or data virtualization to provide real-time access to data.
  - Server-less architecture: RTBI systems use cloud-based services and platforms, such as Amazon Web Services or Google Cloud Platform, to provide scalable, elastic, and cost-effective data processing and analytics.

# Architecture of Data Mining and How Data Mining Works (Process)

## Architecture of Data Mining

- Data mining is the process of discovering meaningful new correlations, patterns, and trends by shifting through large amounts of data stored in repositories, using pattern recognition technologies as well as statistical and mathematical techniques.
- The data mining process involves several components, and these components constitute a data mining system architecture.
- The significant components of data mining systems are  :
  - A data source, which can be a database, a data warehouse, a data stream, or any other data collection.
  - A data mining engine, which consists of a set of functional modules that perform data analysis and mining tasks, such as clustering, classification, association, and regression.
  - A data warehouse server, which is responsible for fetching the relevant data from the data source and delivering it to the data mining engine or the user interface as required.
  - A pattern evaluation module, which interacts with the data mining engine and the data warehouse server to evaluate the patterns or models generated by the data mining engine according to some interestingness measures or user feedback.
  - A graphical user interface, which allows the user to interact with the data mining system, specify the data mining task, browse the data and the mining results, and visualize the patterns or models.
  - A knowledge base, which stores the domain knowledge, the user preferences, the data characteristics, and the interestingness measures that are used to guide the data mining process and evaluate the results.

- There are different types of data mining architecture, depending on the degree of coupling between the data mining system and the data source. These are:
  - No coupling, where the data mining system retrieves data from a particular data source and does not use any database or data warehouse functionality. The data mining system is independent of the data source and can be applied to any data set.
  - Loose coupling, where the data mining system retrieves data from the database or the data warehouse and stores the data in an intermediate file or a data cube. The data mining system operates on the intermediate data and does not affect the database or the data warehouse performance.
  - Semi-tight coupling, where the data mining system uses some database or data warehouse functionality, such as indexing, sorting, aggregation, and summarization, to facilitate the data mining process. The data mining system is integrated with the database or the data warehouse, but does not modify the data structure or schema.
  - Tight coupling, where the data mining system is embedded in the database or the data warehouse and uses the database or the data warehouse query language to perform the data mining tasks. The data mining system is fully compatible with the database or the data warehouse and can exploit the data organization and optimization techniques.

## How Data Mining Works (Process)

- Data mining is a process that consists of four main steps :
  - Setting objectives, which involves defining the business problem, the data mining goals, and the expected outcomes. This can be the hardest part of the data mining process, and many organizations spend too little time on this important step.
  - Data gathering and preparation, which involves collecting the relevant data from the data source, cleaning the data, handling missing values and outliers, transforming the data, reducing the dimensionality, and selecting the appropriate features. This can be the most time-consuming part of the data mining process, and many organizations spend too much time on this step.
  - Applying data mining algorithms, which involves choosing the suitable data mining techniques, such as clustering, classification, association, and regression, and applying them to the prepared data. This can be the most complex part of the data mining process, and many organizations need expert guidance on this step.
  - Evaluating results, which involves assessing the quality and validity of the patterns or models generated by the data mining algorithms, interpreting the results, and communicating them to the stakeholders. This can be the most challenging part of the data mining process, and many organizations struggle with this step.

- Data mining is an iterative and interactive process, which means that the steps can be repeated and refined until the desired results are achieved. Data mining is also a dynamic and evolving process, which means that the objectives, the data, the algorithms, and the evaluation criteria can change over time. Data mining is not a one-time activity, but a continuous and ongoing process that requires constant monitoring and improvement.



# Functionalities and Classifications of Data Mining

Data mining is the process of discovering useful patterns and insights from large and complex data sets. Data mining can help organizations make better decisions, improve customer satisfaction, enhance product quality, and optimize business processes.

Data mining functionalities are used to represent the type of patterns that have to be discovered in data mining tasks. In general, data mining tasks can be classified into two types including descriptive and predictive.

- Descriptive mining tasks define the common features of the data in the database and summarize the characteristics of the data. Examples of descriptive mining tasks are:

  - Association analysis: finding frequent patterns, associations, or correlations among a set of items or attributes. For example, finding that customers who buy bread also tend to buy milk.
  - Clustering analysis: grouping a set of data objects into clusters such that objects within a cluster are similar to each other and dissimilar to objects in other clusters. For example, clustering customers based on their demographics and preferences.
  - Outlier analysis: identifying data objects that deviate significantly from the normal behavior or expectation. For example, detecting fraud or anomaly in credit card transactions.
  - Summarization: providing a concise and compact representation of the data set, such as charts, tables, or reports. For example, summarizing the sales performance of a company in a dashboard.

- Predictive mining tasks act inference on the current data in order to make predictions or estimations for future or unknown data. Examples of predictive mining tasks are:

  - Classification: assigning a data object to one of the predefined classes or categories. For example, classifying an email as spam or not spam.
  - Regression: estimating or predicting a numeric value for a data object based on a set of input variables. For example, predicting the house price based on its size, location, and features.
  - Time series analysis: analyzing and modeling temporal data that change over time. For example, forecasting the stock price or the weather based on historical data.

Data mining can also perform other types of tasks, such as:

- Data integration: combining data from multiple sources into a unified view. For example, integrating data from social media, web analytics, and customer relationship management systems.
- Data cleaning: removing noise, inconsistencies, or errors from the data. For example, correcting spelling mistakes, missing values, or duplicates in the data.
- Data transformation: converting the data into a suitable format for data mining. For example, normalizing, scaling, or discretizing the data.
- Data visualization: presenting the data or the results of data mining in a graphical or interactive way. For example, using charts, maps, or dashboards to display the data or the patterns.

Data mining is a key component of business intelligence, which is the process of transforming data into actionable insights for business decision making. Data mining tools are built into executive dashboards, harvesting insight from big data, including data from social media, internet of things, sensor feeds, location-aware devices, unstructured text, video, and more.



# Representation of Input Data

- Data mining is the process of discovering patterns and knowledge from large amounts of data.
- The input data for data mining can be of various types, such as structured, unstructured, or semi-structured data, and can have different formats, such as relational tables, text documents, images, audio, video, etc.
- The representation of input data is an important step in data mining, as it affects the quality and efficiency of the data mining process and the results.
- The representation of input data involves choosing the appropriate data types, formats, and structures to store and manipulate the data, as well as applying suitable transformations, preprocessing, and reduction techniques to improve the data quality and reduce the data size.
- Some of the common data representation techniques in data mining are:

  - **Data types**: Data types are the categories of values that can be assigned to the data attributes or variables. Data types can be numeric, categorical, ordinal, binary, or text, depending on the nature and domain of the data. For example, age is a numeric data type, gender is a categorical data type, and customer reviews are text data types.
  - **Data formats**: Data formats are the ways of organizing and storing the data in files or databases. Data formats can be flat files, comma-separated values (CSV), XML, JSON, etc., depending on the structure and complexity of the data. For example, a flat file can store a simple table of data, while XML can store a hierarchical data structure.
  - **Data structures**: Data structures are the ways of organizing and accessing the data in memory or disk. Data structures can be arrays, lists, stacks, queues, trees, graphs, etc., depending on the relationships and operations of the data. For example, an array can store a sequence of data values, while a graph can store a network of data nodes and edges.
  - **Data transformations**: Data transformations are the processes of changing the data values or attributes to make them more suitable for data mining. Data transformations can be scaling, normalization, standardization, discretization, binning, encoding, etc., depending on the data type and distribution. For example, scaling can change the range of numeric data values, while encoding can convert categorical data values to numeric codes.
  - **Data preprocessing**: Data preprocessing is the process of cleaning and preparing the data for data mining. Data preprocessing can involve handling missing values, outliers, noise, inconsistencies, duplicates, etc., depending on the data quality and completeness. For example, missing values can be replaced by mean, median, or mode values, while outliers can be detected and removed or replaced.
  - **Data reduction**: Data reduction is the process of reducing the size and complexity of the data for data mining. Data reduction can involve data cube aggregation, dimensionality reduction, feature selection, feature extraction, data compression, etc., depending on the data volume and dimensionality. For example, data cube aggregation can summarize the data by grouping and aggregating the data values, while dimensionality reduction can reduce the number of data attributes by applying techniques like principal component analysis (PCA) or singular value decomposition (SVD).

- The choice of data representation techniques depends on the characteristics and requirements of the data and the data mining task. Different data representation techniques can have different advantages and disadvantages, such as accuracy, efficiency, interpretability, scalability, etc. Therefore, it is important to evaluate and compare the data representation techniques and choose the best one for the data mining task.



# Analysis Methodologies for Data Mining

- Data mining is the process of searching large sets of data to look out for patterns and trends that can’t be found using simple analysis techniques.
- Data mining works in conjunction with predictive analysis, a branch of statistical science that uses complex algorithms designed to work with a special group of problems.
- Data analysis and data mining tools use quantitative analysis, cluster analysis, pattern recognition, correlation discovery, and associations to analyze data with little or no IT intervention.
- The resulting information is then presented to the user in an understandable form, processes collectively known as business intelligence (BI).
- Some of the common data mining methodologies are:
  - Association: It is used to find a correlation between two or more items by identifying the hidden pattern in the data. For example, finding the items that are frequently bought together in a supermarket.
  - Classification: This data mining method is used to distinguish the items in the data sets into classes or groups. It uses techniques such as decision trees, neural networks, and support vector machines. For example, classifying customers based on their income, age, and spending habits.
  - Clustering Analysis: This data mining method is used to group the items in the data sets that are similar to each other. It uses techniques such as k-means, hierarchical, and density-based clustering. For example, clustering customers based on their geographic location, preferences, and behavior.
  - Outlier Detection: This data mining method is used to identify the items in the data sets that are different from the normal or expected pattern. It uses techniques such as box plots, z-scores, and isolation forest. For example, detecting fraud, errors, or anomalies in the data.
  - Regression: This data mining method is used to find the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. It uses techniques such as linear, logistic, and polynomial regression. For example, predicting the sales of a product based on the price, advertising, and demand.

# Online Analytical Processing (OLAP) – Tools

- Online Analytical Processing (OLAP) is a technology that is used to organize large business databases and support business intelligence.
- OLAP tools enable to analyze multidimensional data interactively from multiple perspectives.
- OLAP involves relational database, report writing and data mining and consists of three basic analytical operations: consolidation, drill-down, and slicing and dicing.
- Consolidation is the aggregation of data along one or more dimensions, such as summing up sales by region or by product category.
- Drill-down is the process of breaking down a higher level of data into more detailed levels, such as going from yearly sales to monthly sales or from product categories to individual products.
- Slicing and dicing is the ability to view a subset of data by selecting specific values or ranges of values for one or more dimensions, such as filtering sales by a specific time period or by a specific customer segment.
- OLAP databases are divided into one or more cubes, and each cube is organized and designed by a cube administrator to fit the way that you retrieve and analyze data.
- OLAP tools can be classified into three types: MOLAP, ROLAP, and HOLAP .
- MOLAP or Multidimensional Online Analytical Processing uses a multidimensional cube that accesses stored data through various combinations of dimensions and measures .
- ROLAP or Relational Online Analytical Processing stores data in relational tables using columns and rows and uses SQL queries to access the data .
- HOLAP or Hybrid Online Analytical Processing combines the features of both MOLAP and ROLAP and allows the user to choose the best storage option for different levels of data .
- OLAP tools are the basis for many business applications for Business Performance Management, Planning, Analysis, Budgeting, Simulation Models, and Data Warehouse Reporting.
- OLAP tools provide fast and flexible access to multidimensional data for complex analytical and specific queries.
- OLAP tools can help the user to discover trends, patterns, and insights from the data and support decision making.

# Difference between OLAP and OLTP

- OLAP stands for Online Analytical Processing, while OLTP stands for Online Transaction Processing.
- OLAP is optimized for conducting complex data analysis for smarter decision-making, while OLTP is ideal for making simple updates, insertions and deletions in databases.
- OLAP systems allow you to extract data for complex analysis, often involving large numbers of records, while OLTP systems are focused on processing transactions quickly and accurately.
- OLAP databases have a multi-dimensional schema, so they can support complex queries of multiple data facts and dimensions, while OLTP databases have a relational schema, so they can handle simple queries of one or a few tables.
- OLAP systems are read-intensive, meaning they perform more queries than updates, while OLTP systems are write-intensive, meaning they perform more updates than queries.
- OLAP systems are usually updated periodically from OLTP systems, while OLTP systems are updated in real-time from user inputs or applications.
- OLAP systems are used for business intelligence, data mining, data warehousing, and reporting, while OLTP systems are used for e-commerce, banking, reservation, and inventory management.



# Schema – Star and Snowflake Schemas

- A schema is a logical representation of the structure and organization of data in a data warehouse or data mart.
- A schema defines the tables, columns, relationships, constraints, and other properties of the data.
- A schema can help to optimize the performance, usability, and scalability of the data warehouse or data mart.
- There are different types of schemas that can be used for data warehousing and business intelligence projects, such as star schema, snowflake schema, galaxy schema, etc.
- In this note, we will focus on the star schema and the snowflake schema, which are two of the most common and popular schemas.

## Star Schema

- A star schema is a type of schema that consists of a single fact table and one or more dimension tables.
- A fact table contains the measures or metrics of the business process, such as sales amount, order quantity, profit, etc.
- A dimension table contains the attributes or characteristics of the business process, such as product name, customer name, region, date, etc.
- A star schema is called so because its structure resembles a star, with the fact table at the center and the dimension tables radiating from it.
- A star schema has the following advantages:
  - It is simple and easy to understand and design.
  - It has a high query performance, as it involves fewer joins and less data redundancy.
  - It is flexible and adaptable to changes in business requirements and data sources.
- A star schema has the following disadvantages:
  - It may have data quality issues, such as inconsistency, incompleteness, or incorrectness, due to the denormalization of the data.
  - It may have data storage issues, such as increased disk space and memory usage, due to the duplication of the data.
  - It may have data maintenance issues, such as increased complexity and overhead, due to the update and delete operations on the data.

## Snowflake Schema

- A snowflake schema is a type of schema that is an extension of a star schema, where some or all of the dimension tables are further normalized into sub-dimension tables.
- A sub-dimension table contains the attributes or characteristics of a specific dimension, such as product category, product subcategory, product type, etc.
- A snowflake schema is called so because its structure resembles a snowflake, with the fact table at the center and the dimension tables and sub-dimension tables branching from it.
- A snowflake schema has the following advantages:
  - It has a high data quality, as it eliminates the data redundancy and inconsistency, and preserves the data integrity and accuracy.
  - It has a low data storage, as it reduces the disk space and memory usage, and optimizes the data compression and indexing.
  - It has a low data maintenance, as it simplifies the update and delete operations on the data, and minimizes the data anomalies and errors.
- A snowflake schema has the following disadvantages:
  - It is complex and difficult to understand and design.
  - It has a low query performance, as it involves more joins and more tables to access the data.
  - It is rigid and inflexible to changes in business requirements and data sources.



# ETL Process – Role of ETL for BI

ETL stands for Extract, Transform, and Load. ## Standardization
- Standardization is a type of scaling that transforms the data values to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one.
- Standardization is also known as z-score normalization or standard score.
- Standardization can help to reduce the effect of outliers and different scales or units of measurement on the data.
- Standardization can also improve the performance of some machine learning algorithms, such as linear regression, logistic regression, k-means clustering, and support vector machines, that assume or prefer the data to be normally distributed and have similar variance.
- Standardization can be applied to a single data attribute or to a whole data set.
- Standardization can be performed using the following formula:

  - z = (x - mu) / sigma

  - where x is the original data value, mu is the mean of the data, sigma is the standard deviation of the data, and z is the standardized data value.

## Feature Extraction
- Feature extraction is the process of creating new data attributes from the original data that capture the essential information or characteristics of the data.
- Feature extraction can help to reduce the dimensionality, complexity, and redundancy of the data, and enhance the interpretability and predictive power of the data.
- Feature extraction can involve various techniques, such as:
  - Principal component analysis (PCA): a statistical method that transforms the data into a set of orthogonal and uncorrelated components that explain the maximum variance of the data.
  - Linear discriminant analysis (LDA): a supervised method that transforms the data into a set of linear combinations that maximize the separation between different classes or categories of the data.
  - Factor analysis (FA): a statistical method that models the data as a linear combination of a set of latent or hidden factors that account for the common variance or correlation of the data.
  - Independent component analysis (ICA): a statistical method that transforms the data into a set of independent and non-Gaussian components that capture the higher-order statistics of the data.
  - Kernel methods: a class of methods that apply a nonlinear transformation or function to the data, called a kernel, that maps the data into a higher-dimensional or feature space, where the data becomes more linearly separable or easier to analyze.



# Data Reduction – Sampling

- Data sampling can be useful for reducing the size of a dataset while still preserving the overall trends and patterns in the data .
- There are four types of sampling data reduction methods:
  - Simple random sample without replacement: This method selects a subset of the data randomly without putting the selected tuples back into the dataset. The size of the sample is determined by the user.
  - Simple random sample with replacement: This method selects a subset of the data randomly with putting the selected tuples back into the dataset. The size of the sample is determined by the user.
  - Cluster sample: This method divides the data into clusters based on some criteria, such as geographic location, and then selects one or more clusters to form the sample. The size of the sample depends on the number and size of the clusters selected.
  - Stratified sample: This method divides the data into strata based on some attribute, such as gender, and then selects a proportional number of tuples from each stratum to form the sample. The size of the sample depends on the number and size of the strata and the proportion of tuples selected from each stratum.

# PCA for the notes of the Unit 3 - BI – DATA PREPARTTION in the subject of BUSINESS INTELLIGENCE AND ANALYTICS KCS

- PCA stands for Principal Component Analysis, a dimensionality-reduction method that is often used to reduce the dimensionality of large data sets, by transforming a large set of variables into a smaller one that still contains most of the information in the large set.
- PCA is based on finding the principal components of the data, which are the directions of maximum variance in the data, and projecting the data onto a lower-dimensional subspace spanned by these principal components.
- PCA can be useful for BI and analytics because it can help to:
  - Simplify the data by reducing the number of variables and removing multicollinearity
  - Enhance the data quality by removing noise and outliers
  - Visualize the data by creating biplots that show the relationships between the variables and the observations
  - Identify the most informative features that explain the variance in the data
  - Perform clustering, classification, regression, or other analytical tasks on the reduced data
- The steps of PCA are:
  - Standardize the data to have zero mean and unit variance
  - Compute the covariance matrix of the data
  - Compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the covariance matrix
  - Sort the eigenvalues in descending order and choose the top k eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors, where k is the desired dimensionality of the reduced data
  - Form a matrix P with the k eigenvectors as columns
  - Transform the data by multiplying it with P, resulting in a k-dimensional matrix T
- The loadings of PCA are the coefficients that relate the original variables to the principal components. They can be computed by multiplying the standardized data with the eigenvectors. The loadings can be used to interpret the meaning of the principal components and to select the most relevant variables for each component.
- A biplot is a graphical display that shows both the principal components and the loadings in a single plot. It can help to visualize the variance in the data, the correlation between the variables, the contribution of each variable to each component, and the clustering of the observations. A biplot can be created by plotting the scores (the transformed data) and the loadings (scaled by a factor) on the same axes.

# Data Discretization

- Data discretization is the process of converting continuous data into discrete buckets by grouping it.
- Data discretization can be used to reduce the number of values for a given continuous attribute by dividing the range of the attribute into intervals.
- Data discretization can help to improve the performance and accuracy of some machine learning algorithms that prefer or require categorical or ordinal input variables.
- Data discretization can also help to reduce the noise and outliers in the data, and to simplify the data analysis and visualization.

## Types of Data Discretization

- Data discretization can be classified into two types: supervised and unsupervised.
- Supervised discretization uses the class labels of the data to guide the discretization process, while unsupervised discretization does not.
- Supervised discretization can be further divided into two categories: global and local.
- Global supervised discretization applies the same discretization scheme to all the instances of the data, while local supervised discretization applies different discretization schemes to different subsets of the data.

## Methods of Data Discretization

- There are many methods of data discretization, each with its own advantages and disadvantages.
- Some of the common methods are:

  - Equal-width discretization: This method divides the range of the attribute into equal-sized intervals. This method is simple and fast, but it may not capture the underlying distribution of the data well.
  - Equal-frequency discretization: This method divides the range of the attribute into intervals that have equal number of instances. This method can balance the class distribution of the data, but it may create intervals with different widths and lose some information.
  - Entropy-based discretization: This method uses the concept of entropy to measure the information gain of each interval. This method can find the optimal number and boundaries of the intervals that maximize the class separation, but it may be computationally expensive and sensitive to noise.
  - Chi-square-based discretization: This method uses the chi-square statistic to test the independence of each interval and the class variable. This method can merge the intervals that have similar class distributions, but it may require a large sample size and a predefined significance level.
  - K-means discretization: This method uses the k-means clustering algorithm to group the values of the attribute into k clusters. This method can adapt to the data distribution and find the natural breakpoints, but it may be affected by the choice of k and the initial centroids.

# Iris Dataset

The Iris dataset is a multivariate dataset that contains measurements on four different attributes (in centimeters) for 150 flowers from three different species of iris plants. The attributes are:

- Sepal length
- Sepal width
- Petal length
- Petal width

The three species are:

- Iris setosa
- Iris versicolor
- Iris virginica

The dataset was introduced by the British statistician and biologist Ronald Fisher in his 1936 paper "The use of multiple measurements in taxonomic problems" as an example of linear discriminant analysis. It is sometimes called Anderson's Iris dataset because Edgar Anderson collected the data to quantify the morphologic variation of Iris flowers of three related species.

The Iris dataset is widely used as a beginner's dataset for machine learning purposes. The dataset is included in R base and Python in the machine learning library scikit-learn, so that users can access it without having to find a source for it.

Some of the applications of the Iris dataset are:

- Exploratory data analysis
- Data visualization
- Classification
- Clustering
- Dimensionality reduction
- Regression
- Neural networks

# Complex Event Processing

Complex event processing (CEP) is a technology for aggregating, processing, and analyzing massive streams of data in order to gain real-time insights from events as they occur . CEP can be used to identify meaningful events (such as opportunities or threats) in real-time situations and respond to them as quickly as possible . These events may be happening across the various layers of an organization as sales leads, orders, or customer service calls.

Some of the key concepts and features of CEP are:

- **Events**: Events are occurrences or changes of state that are of interest to an organization or a system. Events can be simple or complex, depending on the level of abstraction and the number of sources involved. For example, a simple event could be a sensor reading or a click on a website, while a complex event could be a fraud detection or a stock price change .
- **Event streams**: Event streams are continuous and unbounded sequences of events that are generated by various sources, such as sensors, applications, or databases. Event streams can have different formats, velocities, and volumes, depending on the nature and frequency of the events .
- **Event processing**: Event processing is a method of tracking and analyzing (processing) streams of information (data) about things that happen (events), and deriving a conclusion from them. Event processing can be done in different ways, such as filtering, transforming, aggregating, enriching, or correlating events .
- **Event patterns**: Event patterns are expressions or rules that specify the conditions or relationships among events that indicate a complex event or a situation of interest. Event patterns can be temporal, spatial, causal, or logical, depending on the type and context of the events .
- **Event processing agents**: Event processing agents are software components that perform event processing tasks, such as consuming, producing, or processing events. Event processing agents can be deployed in different architectures, such as centralized, distributed, or hybrid, depending on the scalability and reliability requirements .
- **Event processing network**: Event processing network is a graph of event processing agents that are connected by event streams. Event processing network defines the flow and logic of event processing in a system or an application .

Some of the benefits and applications of CEP are:

- **Real-time decision making**: CEP enables organizations to make timely and informed decisions based on the current state and trends of their business processes, markets, or environments. CEP can help to optimize performance, reduce costs, increase revenue, or mitigate risks .
- **Operational intelligence**: CEP provides organizations with a comprehensive and dynamic view of their operations, by monitoring and analyzing key performance indicators, metrics, and events. CEP can help to improve efficiency, quality, and customer satisfaction .
- **Business process management**: CEP supports the execution and improvement of business processes, by detecting and responding to events that affect the process flow, such as exceptions, delays, or errors. CEP can help to automate, orchestrate, or adapt business processes .
- **Fraud detection**: CEP can be used to identify and prevent fraudulent activities, such as credit card fraud, insurance fraud, or cyberattacks, by analyzing patterns and anomalies in event streams. CEP can help to protect assets, reputation, and compliance .
- **Algorithmic trading**: CEP can be used to execute trading strategies based on real-time market data and events, such as price changes, news, or orders. CEP can help to maximize profits, minimize losses, and optimize portfolio .

# Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis (RCA) is a method of problem solving used for identifying the root causes of faults or problems. A root cause is the core issue that sets in motion the entire cause-and-effect reaction that ultimately leads to the problem. RCA assumes that it is much more effective to systematically prevent and solve for underlying issues rather than just treating ad hoc symptoms and putting out fires.

RCA is a team approach methodology that works by understanding that systems and actions are related and that one action can trigger an event and that event can trigger another event and so on until you have a problem or multiple problems. There are four main steps in an RCA:

1. Identify the problem: Define the problem clearly and accurately, and gather data and evidence to support the problem statement.
2. Analyze the problem: Use tools and techniques such as brainstorming, fishbone diagrams, 5 whys, Pareto charts, etc. to identify all possible causes of the problem and narrow down the root cause(s).
3. Identify solutions: Generate and evaluate potential solutions that address the root cause(s) and prevent the problem from recurring.
4. Implement solutions: Plan and execute the chosen solution(s), monitor the results, and verify the effectiveness of the solution(s).

RCA is a useful technique for improving the quality and performance of business processes, products, and services. It can help to identify the root causes of errors, defects, failures, inefficiencies, and customer complaints, and to implement corrective and preventive actions that eliminate or reduce the occurrence of such problems. RCA can also help to foster a culture of continuous improvement and learning within an organization.
