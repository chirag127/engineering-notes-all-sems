1. Social network analysis (SNA) is the process of investigating social structures through the use of networks and graph theory. It characterizes networked structures in terms of nodes (individual actors, people, or things within the network) and the ties, edges, or links (relationships or interactions) that connect them.
2. Key concepts in SNA include network density, centrality, prestige, and power. Network density refers to the number of ties in a network relative to the total number possible. Centrality refers to the degree to which a node is near the center of a network. Measures of prestige indicate the extent of respect or influence a node has within the network. Power can be understood in terms of a node's control over resources and information within the network.
3. SNA has found applications in many disciplines, including sociology, anthropology, political science, history, public health, and information science. It is used to study complex phenomena in these fields, including the spread of diseases, diffusion of innovations, and the robustness of social movements. It has emerged as a key technique in modern sociology, organizational studies, and network science.
4. Historically, SNA grew out of graph theory and the study of human communication in the mid-20th century. It gained prominence among sociologists in the 1970s with work from notable researchers including Mark Granovetter, Harrison White, Linton Freeman, and Barry Wellman. Today, SNA is used in many academic disciplines and areas of practice as a means of modeling social structures, understanding social interactions and relationships, and explaining emergent network-level phenomena.

## Unit 1 - Social Network Analysis

1. Introduction to Social Networks
- A social network is a structure made up of individuals or organizations that are connected by one or more specific types of interdependency (friendship, common interest, financial exchange, etc.).
- Social network analysis focuses on the structure of the relationships between actors in a social network and how this structure influences individuals and groups.
- The key components of a social network are nodes (actors, people, or things) and ties (relationships or connections).

2. Measures and Metrics
- Degree centrality: Number of direct connections a node has to other nodes. Indicates activity or popularity.
- Betweenness centrality: Amount of control a node exerts over the flow of information. Indicates potential for information brokers or gatekeepers.
- Closeness centrality: How close a node is to all other nodes in a network. Indicates potential for efficiently diffusing information.
- Density: Proportion of possible ties between nodes that are actual ties. Indicates overall connectivity of a network.
- Centralization: Tendency of a network to be organized around centralized points or nodes. Indicates potential hierarchy or inequality.

3. Visualization and Analysis
- Social networks are often visualized using sociograms, graphs, and diagrams to help uncover patterns and insights.
- Analysis may involve identifying key players, communities or clusters, bridges, and other network properties to understand dynamics, influence, information flow, and more.

### Key concepts and measures in network analysis

1. Nodes: Nodes refer to the actors or individuals in a network. They are represented by points or circles.
2. Ties: Ties refer to the relationships or connections between the nodes. They are represented by lines or arrows connecting the nodes.

5. Centrality: Centrality refers to the relative importance of a node in a network. There are three main types of centrality:
 - Degree centrality: Based on the degree of a node. Nodes with higher degree are more central.
 - Betweenness centrality: Based on the number of shortest paths going through a node. Nodes through which more shortest paths pass are more central.
 - Closeness centrality: Based on the average distance of a node from all other nodes. Nodes that are closer to all other nodes are more central.

Centrality measures help identify the most influential nodes in a network.

### The global structure of networks for the notes of the Unit 1 - Social Network Analysis in the subject of SOCIAL NETWORK ANALYTICS

1. Degree distribution: The degree distribution refers to the probability distribution of the degrees over the whole network. It helps in understanding the connectivity pattern of the network. Some networks follow a power law degree distribution which indicates the presence of hubs (nodes with high degrees) and a scale-free network.

2. Average path length: The average path length refers to the average number of steps along the shortest paths for all possible pairs of network nodes. It measures the navigability or efficiency of communication in the network. A lower average path length implies that information can spread faster in the network.

3. Diameter: The diameter of a network refers to the longest of all the shortest paths in the network. It is a measure of how far two nodes in the network can be from each other. A lower diameter also implies a faster spread of information.

4. Clustering coefficient: The clustering coefficient measures the probability that two nodes connected to a common node are also connected to each other. It measures the tendency of nodes to cluster or form groups. A higher clustering coefficient implies a more clustered network.

5. Centrality: Centrality measures the relative importance of a node in a network. It identifies the most influential nodes in a network which can help in understanding the flow of information, spread of diseases or identifying key players. The three common centrality measures are - degree centrality, closeness centrality and betweenness centrality.

### Ontology languages for the Semantic Web

- RDF: Resource Description Framework is a framework for representing information in the Web. RDF uses statements to represent information in the form of subject-predicate-object expressions.
- RDF Schema: RDF Schema is a vocabulary description language. It provides basic elements to describe classes and properties used in RDF and enables simple reasoning about the RDF data.
- OWL: Web Ontology Language is a vocabulary extension of RDF Schema which provides additional vocabulary along with formal semantics. OWL enables more automated reasoning about Web data than RDF Schema.
- There are three increasingly expressive sublanguages of OWL:
	- OWL Lite: Used when simple ontological statements are needed.
	- OWL DL: Used when more expressive power is required along with computational completeness (all conclusions are guaranteed to be computable) and decidability (all statements have a yes/no answer).
	- OWL Full: Most powerful but reasoning (computability and decidability) is no longer guaranteed.

- Classification of classes and relations based on their properties and relations.
- Inference of new information based on the structure of the classes and properties and on instance data.

### Comparison to the Entity/Relationship (E/R) model and the relational model for the notes of the Unit 2 - Web Semantics in Social Network Applications in the subject of SOCIAL NETWORK ANALYTICS

1. Entity/Relationship (E/R) model:
- Entities are objects or concepts of interest.
- Relationships show how entities are connected.
- Cardinality constraints specify the number of entities to which another entity can be connected via a relationship.

2. Relational model:
- Data is stored in relations (tables) with rows and columns.
- Each column has a name and contains values of a particular type.
- A relational schema specifies the name of the relation, plus the name and type of each column.
- Relationships between data are expressed via common columns (foreign keys) rather than separate structures as in the E/R model.

3. Differences:
- E/R model uses entities and relationships while relational model uses tables and columns.
- Cardinality constraints are specified in E/R model but not in relational model. They can be enforced using database rules though.
- E/R model is a conceptual data modeling approach while relational model is a implementation of the conceptual model. Relational model is better suited for implementation in databases.

4. Conclusion:
- E/R model is useful for conceptual design but relational model provides a more practical implementation of the design in databases.
- Both models are similar in expressing relationships between data but differ in their approach - explicit relationships in E/R vs common columns in relational model.
- Choice between the models depends on the use case and requirements.

### Comparison to theExtensible Markup Language (XML) and XML Schema

1. XML is a markup language designed to store and transport data. It focuses on what data is.

5. XML can be transformed and processed easily using languages like XSLT. XML Schema can be used to validate and constrain the XML document to ensure data correctness but it cannot transform the XML documents.

### State-of-the-art in network data representation

### Aggregating and reasoning with social network data

1. Degree centrality - Counts the number of direct connections a node has to other nodes. Higher degree means the node is more central.
2. Betweenness centrality - Measures the number of shortest paths between all pairs of nodes that pass through a given node. Nodes with high betweenness centrality control the flow of information.
3. Closeness centrality - Measures the inverse of the sum of distances from a node to all other nodes. Nodes with high closeness centrality can reach other nodes more quickly.
4. Eigenvector centrality - Assigns relative scores to all nodes in the network based on the concept that connections to high-scoring nodes contribute more to the score of the node in question than equal connections to low-scoring nodes.
5. Clustering coefficient - Measures the extent to which nodes tend to cluster together in tightly knit groups. A higher clustering coefficient means more of a node's neighbors are also neighbors of each other.
6. Connected components - The largest subset of nodes in a network where there is a path between every pair of nodes. Networks can have multiple connected components if some nodes are not reachable from others.
7. Diameter - The longest shortest path between any two nodes in the network. A lower diameter means signals can spread more quickly through a network.

The nodes and connections in a social network can be analyzed using these metrics to identify influencers, bottlenecks, tightly-knit groups, and the overall efficiency of information spread. These metrics provide a macro-level understanding of network structure beyond the micro-level of individual nodes and ties. ### The features of Flink for the notes of the Unit 4 - Developing Social-Semantic Applications in the subject of SOCIAL NETWORK ANALYTICS.

1. **Stateful Computations**: Flink supports stateful computations that can maintain state (data) while processing unbounded data streams. This allows Flink to cope with out-of-order and late-arriving data.
2. **Exactly-once Semantics**: Flink provides exactly-once semantics for stateful computations, even in the case of failures. This means that state updates will be applied exactly once, even when there are failures involved.
3. **High Performance and Scalability**: Flink is designed to run with high throughput and low latency on large clusters with potentially thousands of nodes. It can process huge volumes of data at high speeds.
4. **Event-Time Processing**: Flink includes an event-time concept to perform time-based operations like windows on records with timestamp attributes. Windows can be either tumbling (fixed size) or sliding (overlapping). This is critical for out-of-order streams.
5. **Low Latency Streaming**: Flink features pipelined streaming which allows for low latency processing while maintaining high throughput. Latency/throughput trade-offs can be configured as needed.
6. **Fault Tolerance**: Flink is fault tolerant and can recover from failures. It can restart tasks and recover state and positions, providing end-to-end exactly-once guarantees.
7. **DataSet API**: In addition to DataStream API for streaming, Flink provides DataSet API for bounded data processing on clusters. DataSets can be converted to DataStreams and vice-versa.
8. **Table API**: Flink provides a SQL-like Table API to work with both DataStreams and DataSets in a relational manner. Table API programs can be converted to and from DataStream and DataSet programs.
9. **Timely State Consistency**: Flink's consistency model for its stateful computations offers timely consistency, providing stronger guarantees than eventual consistency.
