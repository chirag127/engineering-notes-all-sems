

# SOCIAL NETWORK ANALYTICS

- Social network analytics is the application of social network analysis (SNA) to various types of data, such as social media, communication, collaboration, and transaction data, to gain insights into the patterns and behaviors of social actors and their relationships .
- Social network analytics can be used for various purposes, such as:
  - Understanding the structure and dynamics of social networks, such as communities, cliques, bridges, brokers, influencers, and outliers .
  - Measuring the social capital, trust, reputation, and influence of social actors within and across networks .
  - Identifying the key drivers and barriers of information diffusion, innovation adoption, and behavior change in social networks .
  - Detecting and preventing fraud, abuse, and malicious activities in social networks .
  - Optimizing the design and performance of social media platforms, campaigns, and interventions based on network metrics and feedback .
- Social network analytics involves the following steps :
  - Data collection: Gathering and preprocessing the relevant data from various sources, such as social media platforms, surveys, web analytics, and databases.
  - Network construction: Defining the nodes (social actors) and edges (relationships) of the network, and assigning attributes and weights to them based on the data.
  - Network analysis: Applying network measures, models, and algorithms to analyze the network structure, properties, and processes, such as centrality, density, clustering, homophily, reciprocity, and diffusion.
  - Network visualization: Creating graphical representations of the network and its features, such as graphs, matrices, maps, and dashboards, to facilitate exploration and communication of the results.
  - Network interpretation: Drawing conclusions and implications from the network analysis and visualization, and validating them with domain knowledge and additional data sources.

# Development of Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is the study of patterns of relationships that connect social actors, such as individuals, groups, organizations, or communities.
- SNA has its theoretical roots in the work of early sociologists such as Georg Simmel and Émile Durkheim, who wrote about the importance of studying social structures and networks.
- SNA has also been influenced by graph theory, a branch of mathematics that deals with abstract representations of nodes and links, and by social psychology, which studies the effects of social interactions on human behavior and cognition.
- SNA has emerged as a key technique in modern sociology and has gained popularity in various disciplines, such as anthropology, biology, communication studies, economics, geography, history, information science, organizational studies, political science, public health, social psychology, and development studies.
- SNA can be used to address various research questions, such as how social networks affect the diffusion of information, innovation, disease, or behavior; how social networks shape the formation of social capital, trust, norms, or identity; how social networks influence the outcomes of collective action, social movements, or political participation; and how social networks evolve over time and across contexts.
- SNA can be applied to different levels of analysis, such as micro-level (individuals or dyads), meso-level (groups or organizations), or macro-level (communities or societies), and can use different types of data, such as surveys, interviews, observations, archival records, or digital traces.
- SNA can be conducted using various methods and tools, such as network graphs, matrices, measures, models, algorithms, and software, to visualize, describe, compare, explain, or predict the properties and dynamics of social networks.
- SNA has a rich and diverse history, which can be traced back to the early 20th century, when pioneers such as Jacob Moreno, Kurt Lewin, and J.L. Moreno introduced the concepts of sociometry, group dynamics, and sociograms to study the interactions and relationships among social actors .
- SNA has also been influenced by the works of scholars such as Harrison White, Mark Granovetter, Stanley Milgram, Ronald Burt, Barry Wellman, and others, who developed and refined the theories and methods of SNA in the second half of the 20th century and the beginning of the 21st century .
- SNA has faced various challenges and opportunities, such as the availability and quality of data, the validity and reliability of measures, the complexity and scalability of models, the ethical and legal issues of network research, and the integration and innovation of network approaches across disciplines and domains .



### Key concepts and measures in network analysis

- Network analysis is a method of studying the structure and behavior of complex systems composed of many interacting elements, such as social networks, computer networks, biological networks, etc.  
- Network analysis involves the following key concepts and measures:
  - **Network**: A network is a representation of a system as a set of nodes and links, where nodes are the elements of the system and links are the connections or interactions between them.
  - **Node**: A node is an individual unit or entity in a network, such as a person, a computer, a gene, etc. Nodes can have attributes or properties that describe their characteristics or features. 
  - **Link**: A link is a connection or relationship between two nodes in a network, such as a friendship, a communication, a co-occurrence, etc. Links can have attributes or properties that describe their strength, direction, type, etc. 
  - **Degree**: Degree is a measure of the number of links that a node has in a network. It indicates how connected or popular a node is. Degree can be divided into in-degree and out-degree, which count the number of incoming and outgoing links, respectively. 
  - **Path**: Path is a sequence of links that connects two nodes in a network. It represents a possible way of reaching one node from another. The length of a path is the number of links in the path. 
  - **Distance**: Distance is a measure of the shortest path between two nodes in a network. It indicates how close or far apart two nodes are. Distance can be used to calculate the diameter of a network, which is the longest distance between any pair of nodes. 
  - **Centrality**: Centrality is a measure of the importance or influence of a node in a network. It reflects how central or peripheral a node is. There are different types of centrality, such as degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, etc. 
  - **Clustering**: Clustering is a measure of the tendency of nodes to form groups or communities in a network. It reflects how dense or sparse a network is. Clustering can be calculated at the level of individual nodes, links, or the whole network. 
  - **Modularity**: Modularity is a measure of the quality of a partition of a network into clusters or communities. It reflects how well the network is divided into groups of nodes that are more connected within than between them. 
  - **Network analysis methods**: Network analysis methods are techniques or algorithms that are used to analyze, visualize, model, or manipulate networks. Some examples of network analysis methods are nodal analysis, mesh analysis, matrix methods, graph theory, network models, network visualization, network statistics, etc.

- The global structure of networks refers to the overall shape and configuration of the network, which can reveal the characteristics and functions of the network . Some of the aspects of the global structure of networks are:

  - The size of the network, which is the number of nodes (actors) and edges (relationships) in the network .
  - The density of the network, which is the proportion of actual edges to the maximum possible edges in the network. A dense network has many connections among the nodes, while a sparse network has few connections .
  - The diameter of the network, which is the longest shortest path between any pair of nodes in the network. A small diameter indicates that the network is compact and the nodes can reach each other quickly, while a large diameter indicates that the network is dispersed and the nodes are far apart .
  - The degree distribution of the network, which is the frequency of nodes with a given number of edges. The degree distribution can indicate whether the network is homogeneous or heterogeneous, and whether it follows a random, regular, or scale-free model .
  - The centrality of the network, which is the measure of the importance or influence of a node or a group of nodes in the network. There are different types of centrality, such as degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality .
  - The clustering of the network, which is the measure of the tendency of nodes to form groups or communities within the network. A high clustering coefficient indicates that the network has many triangles, which are the simplest form of cliques. A low clustering coefficient indicates that the network has few triangles and is more tree-like .
  - The modularity of the network, which is the measure of the quality of the partition of the network into communities or modules. A high modularity indicates that the network has a clear community structure, where the nodes within a community are more connected to each other than to the nodes outside the community .

- These aspects of the global structure of networks can help identify the patterns, roles, functions, and dynamics of the social network, and provide insights for network analysis and intervention   .



### The macro-structure of social networks

- Social networks are composed of **nodes** and **ties**. Nodes are the actors or entities that participate in the network, such as individuals, organizations, or groups. Ties are the connections or relationships between the nodes, such as friendship, collaboration, or communication.
- The macro-structure of social networks refers to the overall pattern of ties and nodes in a network, as well as the properties and processes that emerge from this pattern. The macro-structure can be analyzed using various methods and measures, such as density, centrality, clustering, modularity, and community detection.
- The macro-structure of social networks can have significant effects on various social phenomena and outcomes, such as civil uprising, state formation, policy diffusion, and economic development. For example, dense and cohesive networks can facilitate collective action and social movements, while sparse and heterogeneous networks can promote innovation and diffusion of information.
- The macro-structure of social networks can also be influenced by various factors, such as individual behavior, social norms, institutional arrangements, and environmental constraints. For example, individuals can choose their ties based on their preferences, interests, or identities, creating homophily or heterophily in the network. Social norms can regulate the formation and maintenance of ties, creating reciprocity or transitivity in the network. Institutional arrangements can shape the opportunities and constraints for tie formation, creating hierarchy or equality in the network. Environmental constraints can affect the availability and accessibility of ties, creating locality or globality in the network.
- The macro-structure of social networks can be studied at different levels of analysis, such as the meso-level, the macro-level, and the meta-level. The meso-level focuses on the subgroups or clusters within a network, such as cliques, factions, or communities. The macro-level focuses on the whole network or the system of networks, such as the world-system or the internet. The meta-level focuses on the network of networks or the higher-order structures, such as the interlocking directorates or the multilevel governance.



### Personal networks

Personal networks are subsets of social networks that focus on the relationships and interactions of a specific individual, group, or entity. Personal networks can be used to study and understand various aspects of social life, such as social support, social capital, social influence, and social identity. Personal networks can also reveal the diversity, density, centrality, and multiplexity of one's social ties.

Some key concepts and methods of personal network analysis are:

- **Ego network**: An ego network is a personal network that consists of a focal node (ego) and the nodes that are directly connected to it (alters), as well as the ties among the alters. Ego networks can be used to measure the size, composition, and structure of one's personal network, as well as the types and quality of the relationships with the alters.
- **Name generator**: A name generator is a survey question or technique that asks respondents to list the names of people they know or interact with in a specific context or domain. Name generators can be used to elicit ego networks or personal network samples, as well as to collect information about the attributes and roles of the alters.
- **Name interpreter**: A name interpreter is a survey question or technique that asks respondents to provide additional information about the names they generated, such as the frequency, duration, and nature of the contact, the strength and closeness of the relationship, the resources and support exchanged, and the perceived similarity and trustworthiness of the alter. Name interpreters can be used to assess the functional content and quality of one's personal network ties.
- **Personal network visualization**: A personal network visualization is a graphical representation of a personal network, usually in the form of a node-link diagram, where nodes represent actors and links represent relationships. Personal network visualizations can be used to illustrate the patterns and properties of one's personal network, such as the clusters, bridges, and holes in the network structure, and the attributes, roles, and labels of the network actors.

Personal network analysis can be applied to various domains and disciplines, such as sociology, psychology, anthropology, communication, education, health, and business. Some examples of personal network analysis applications are:

- Studying how personal networks provide social support, emotional well-being, and coping resources for individuals facing stress, illness, or life transitions.
- Examining how personal networks influence one's attitudes, beliefs, behaviors, and outcomes, such as political opinions, consumer choices, health practices, and career success.
- Exploring how personal networks reflect and shape one's social identity, such as gender, ethnicity, culture, and religion, and how they affect one's sense of belonging, trust, and solidarity.
- Comparing and contrasting personal networks across different contexts, groups, and cultures, and identifying the factors that affect the formation, maintenance, and dissolution of personal network ties.

### Electronic sources for network analysis

- Network analysis is the process of determining the electrical parameters of a circuit or a network of interconnected components, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, voltage sources, current sources, etc. 
- Electronic sources for network analysis are devices that provide electrical energy to the circuit or network, such as batteries, generators, solar cells, etc. 
- There are two types of electronic sources for network analysis: independent sources and dependent sources. 
- Independent sources are sources that have a fixed value of voltage or current, regardless of the rest of the circuit. For example, a battery is an independent voltage source that provides a constant voltage across its terminals. 
- Dependent sources are sources that have a value of voltage or current that depends on another voltage or current in the circuit. For example, a current-controlled voltage source is a dependent voltage source that provides a voltage proportional to a current elsewhere in the circuit. 
- Electronic sources for network analysis can be transformed from one type to another, such as from a voltage source to a current source, or vice versa, using source transformation techniques. This can simplify the analysis of complex circuits by reducing the number of elements or branches. 
- Electronic sources for network analysis can also be used to model real-world phenomena, such as social networks, search engines, discussion networks, etc. For example, a social network can be represented as a graph, where the nodes are the users and the edges are the connections or interactions between them. The electronic sources can then be used to measure the properties of the network, such as the degree, centrality, clustering, etc.

### Knowledge Representation on the Semantic Web

- Knowledge representation (KR) is the process of encoding information in a form that can be manipulated by machines, such as logic, rules, ontologies, etc.
- The Semantic Web is an extension of the current Web that aims to make the data on the Web more understandable and interoperable by machines, using standards such as RDF, RDFS, OWL, SPARQL, etc.

  - It enables the integration and reuse of heterogeneous and distributed data sources across the Web.
  - It facilitates the discovery and inference of new knowledge from existing data, using reasoning and query mechanisms.
  - It supports the annotation and enrichment of Web content with semantic metadata, enhancing its accessibility and usability.
  - It enables the development of intelligent and personalized Web services and applications, such as search engines, recommender systems, social networks, etc.
- The main challenges of using KR on the Semantic Web are:
  - It requires the development and maintenance of large and complex ontologies that capture the semantics of the data and the domain.
  - It faces the trade-off between expressivity and tractability of the KR languages and tools, affecting the scalability and performance of the system.
  - It deals with the issues of inconsistency, incompleteness, uncertainty, and ambiguity of the data and the knowledge on the Web.
  - It has to cope with the social and ethical aspects of the Web, such as privacy, trust, provenance, quality, etc.

### Ontology languages for the Semantic Web

- Ontology languages are formal languages that are used to represent the concepts, properties, and relations of a domain of interest in a machine-readable and interoperable way.
- Ontology languages can be used to define vocabularies, schemas, and axioms that can be shared and reused by different applications and agents on the web.
- Ontology languages can also support automated reasoning and inference, which can enhance the functionality and intelligence of web applications and services.
- Some of the main ontology languages for the semantic web are:

  - **XML (eXtensible Markup Language)**: A general-purpose markup language that can be used to define the syntax and structure of data and documents on the web. XML is not an ontology language by itself, but it provides the basis for other ontology languages that use XML syntax. 
  - **RDF (Resource Description Framework)**: A framework for describing and linking resources on the web using triples of the form (subject, predicate, object). RDF can be used to represent simple ontologies and metadata, as well as to encode complex ontologies using RDF Schema (RDFS) or other extensions. RDF can be serialized in different formats, such as XML, N-Triples, Turtle, or JSON-LD. 
  - **OWL (Web Ontology Language)**: A family of ontology languages that are based on description logic and that extend RDF and RDFS with more expressive features, such as classes, properties, restrictions, individuals, datatypes, and axioms. OWL can be used to define rich and complex ontologies that can support logical reasoning and consistency checking. OWL has different sublanguages with different levels of expressivity and computational complexity, such as OWL Lite, OWL DL, and OWL Full.  
  - **SKOS (Simple Knowledge Organization System)**: A standard for representing and sharing knowledge organization systems, such as thesauri, taxonomies, classification schemes, and glossaries. SKOS is based on RDF and provides a simple and intuitive model for defining concepts, labels, relations, and mappings. SKOS can be used to facilitate the integration and interoperability of different knowledge sources on the web. 
  - **RIF (Rule Interchange Format)**: A standard for exchanging rules among different rule systems on the web. RIF provides a common syntax and semantics for representing different types of rules, such as production rules, logic programming rules, and constraint rules. RIF can be used to complement ontologies with additional inferential capabilities and to enable rule-based reasoning and decision making on the web.

### The Web Ontology Language (OWL)

- The Web Ontology Language (OWL) is a family of knowledge representation languages for authoring ontologies.
- An ontology is a formal way to describe the classes and relations between them that are inherent in a domain of interest .
- OWL is a Semantic Web language that is designed to process and integrate information over the web, making sense of it in a manner similar to human reasoning.
- OWL is intended to facilitate interpretability among web content using vocabulary and formatting that allows automatic machine processing .
- OWL ontologies can be used to describe the properties of web resources, such as their categories, relationships, constraints, and semantics .
- OWL has three sublanguages with different levels of expressiveness and complexity: OWL Lite, OWL DL, and OWL Full .
- OWL Lite is the simplest and least expressive sublanguage, suitable for defining simple class hierarchies and constraints .
- OWL DL is based on description logic, a subset of first-order logic, and supports more expressive features such as cardinality, disjointness, and equality .

- One way to do this is to use statistical models that can capture the essential features of the network data, such as the distribution of ties, the presence of clusters or communities, the patterns of reciprocity or transitivity, and the effects of individual or group characteristics.
- These models are called exponential random graph models (ERGMs) or stochastic block models (SBMs), depending on the level of complexity and generality they can handle.
- ERGMs and SBMs are both probabilistic models that specify the likelihood of observing a given network configuration, based on a set of parameters that represent the network properties of interest.
- To fit these models to the network data, one needs to estimate the parameters using methods such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or Bayesian inference.
- However, fitting a model to the data is not enough to ensure that the model is appropriate and valid for the data. One also needs to assess the goodness of fit (GOF) of the model, which is the degree to which the model can reproduce the observed features of the data.
- GOF can be measured using various indices or criteria, such as the likelihood ratio test, the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the deviance information criterion (DIC), or the posterior predictive check (PPC).
- These indices or criteria can be used to compare different models for the same data, or to evaluate the fit of a single model to the data.
- The choice of the GOF index or criterion depends on the type of model, the type of data, and the research question or objective.
- Some GOF indices or criteria are more suitable for ERGMs, while others are more suitable for SBMs. Some GOF indices or criteria are more sensitive to the overall fit of the model, while others are more sensitive to the local fit of the model.
- The GOF indices or criteria can also be complemented by graphical or visual methods, such as plotting the observed and simulated network statistics, or comparing the observed and simulated network structures.
- These methods can help to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the model, and to diagnose the sources of misfit or discrepancy.
- Predicting the GOF of a model is a challenging task, as it depends on many factors, such as the size and complexity of the network, the number and type of parameters, the estimation method, and the sampling variability.
- However, some general guidelines can be followed to improve the prediction of the GOF, such as:

  - Choosing a model that is parsimonious, interpretable, and consistent with the research question or objective.
  - Using a large and representative sample of the network data, or applying appropriate weighting or correction methods for small or biased samples.
  - Using a reliable and efficient estimation method, such as MLE or Bayesian inference, and checking the convergence and stability of the estimates.
  - Using multiple GOF indices or criteria, and comparing them with the theoretical or empirical distributions or thresholds.

- Data acquisition for SNA can be challenging due to the complexity, diversity, and dynamism of social networks, as well as the ethical and legal issues involved in accessing and analyzing personal data.
- Three main techniques for obtaining data from online social networks are network traffic analysis, application programming interfaces (API), and web crawling.
  - Network traffic analysis involves capturing and analyzing the packets of data that are exchanged between the users and the servers of a social network. This technique can provide detailed and real-time information about the network structure and activity, but it requires access to the network infrastructure and may raise privacy and security concerns.
  - API is a set of protocols and tools that allow developers to access and manipulate data from a social network. This technique can provide standardized and convenient access to the network data, but it may be limited by the availability, functionality, and rate limits of the API, as well as the terms of service and policies of the social network provider.
  - Web crawling is a technique that involves downloading and parsing the web pages of a social network. This technique can provide comprehensive and flexible access to the network data, but it may be inefficient, unreliable, and inconsistent due to the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of the web, as well as the anti-crawling mechanisms and regulations of the social network provider.
