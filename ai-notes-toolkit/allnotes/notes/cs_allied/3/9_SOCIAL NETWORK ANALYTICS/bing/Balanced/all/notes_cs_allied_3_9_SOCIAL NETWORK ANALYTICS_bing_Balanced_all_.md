

# Social Network Analytics

- Social network analytics is the application of social network analysis (SNA) to various types of data, such as social media, communication, collaboration, and transaction data, to gain insights into the patterns and behaviors of individuals, groups, and organizations within a network .
- Social network analysis is the process of investigating social structures through the use of networks and graph theory. It characterizes networked structures in terms of nodes (individual actors, people, or things within the network) and the ties, edges, or links (relationships or interactions) that connect them.
- Social network analytics can be used for various purposes, such as:
  - Understanding the structure and dynamics of social networks, such as their size, density, centrality, clustering, and evolution over time .
  - Identifying the key actors, influencers, connectors, and brokers in a network, and their roles, positions, and functions .
  - Measuring the social capital, trust, reciprocity, and cohesion of individuals and groups in a network .
  - Detecting and analyzing communities, subgroups, and cliques within a network, and their characteristics, boundaries, and overlaps .
  - Exploring the diffusion of information, ideas, opinions, behaviors, and innovations through a network, and the factors that affect them .
  - Evaluating the impact and effectiveness of interventions, policies, and strategies on a network, and the outcomes and feedback loops .
- Social network analytics can be performed using various methods and tools, such as:
  - Data collection and preprocessing: This involves gathering and cleaning the data from various sources, such as social media platforms, surveys, interviews, emails, texts, phone calls, etc., and transforming it into a network format, such as a matrix or a graph .
  - Network visualization: This involves creating graphical representations of the network, such as node-link diagrams, sociograms, or heat maps, to display the nodes, ties, and attributes of the network, and to highlight the patterns and features of interest .
  - Network analysis: This involves applying mathematical and statistical techniques, such as graph theory, centrality measures, clustering algorithms, network models, and network metrics, to quantify and interpret the network structure and properties .
  - Network interpretation: This involves deriving insights and implications from the network analysis, and relating them to the research questions, hypotheses, and objectives of the study .

# Development of Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is the study of patterns of relationships that connect social actors, such as individuals, groups, organizations, or countries.
- SNA has its theoretical roots in the work of early sociologists such as Georg Simmel and Émile Durkheim, who wrote about the importance of studying social structures and networks.
- SNA has also been influenced by graph theory, a branch of mathematics that deals with abstract representations of nodes and links, and by social psychology, which studies the effects of social interactions on human behavior and cognition.
- SNA has emerged as a key technique in modern sociology and has gained popularity in various other disciplines, such as anthropology, biology, communication studies, economics, geography, history, information science, organizational studies, political science, public health, social psychology, and development studies.
- SNA can be used to address various research questions, such as how social networks affect the diffusion of information, innovation, disease, or behavior; how social networks shape the formation of social capital, trust, norms, or identity; how social networks influence the outcomes of collective action, cooperation, conflict, or power; and how social networks evolve over time and across contexts.
- SNA involves several steps, such as defining the social actors and the types of relationships among them, collecting data on the network structure and attributes, analyzing the network properties and patterns, and interpreting the results in relation to the research question and theory.
- SNA relies on various concepts and measures, such as nodes, links, degree, centrality, density, clustering, distance, path, diameter, connectedness, component, subgraph, clique, community, network level, ego level, homophily, heterophily, reciprocity, transitivity, triad, balance, structural hole, brokerage, embeddedness, social capital, and social influence .
- SNA can be applied to different types of networks, such as one-mode, two-mode, multiplex, multilevel, longitudinal, dynamic, and weighted networks .
- SNA can be performed using various methods and tools, such as qualitative, quantitative, mixed, or computational methods, and software packages such as UCINET, Pajek, Gephi, R, or Python .
- SNA faces some challenges and limitations, such as the difficulty of defining and measuring social relationships, the ethical issues of collecting and analyzing network data, the validity and reliability of network data and analysis, the generalizability and comparability of network results, and the causality and endogeneity of network effects  .



# Key concepts and measures in network analysis

- Network analysis is the process of measuring and evaluating the performance, structure, and behavior of a network, such as a computer network, a social network, or a biological network.
- Network analysis involves collecting data on the network components, such as nodes, links, attributes, and interactions, and applying various methods and techniques to analyze and visualize the network properties and patterns.
- Some of the key concepts and measures in network analysis are:

  - **Nodes** are the entities or actors that make up the network, such as computers, people, or genes. Nodes can have attributes, such as name, location, or type, that describe their characteristics or features.
  - **Links** are the connections or relationships between nodes, such as cables, friendships, or interactions. Links can have attributes, such as weight, direction, or type, that describe their strength, orientation, or nature.
  - **Network size** is the number of nodes in the network. It indicates the scale or scope of the network.
  - **Network density** is the ratio of the number of actual links to the number of possible links in the network. It indicates how connected or cohesive the network is.
  - **Network degree** is the number of links that a node has with other nodes in the network. It indicates how active or central the node is in the network.
  - **Network centrality** is a measure of the importance or influence of a node or a group of nodes in the network. There are different types of centrality, such as degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality, that capture different aspects of the node's position or role in the network.
  - **Network distance** is the number of links that separate two nodes in the network. It indicates how close or far the nodes are from each other in the network.
  - **Network path** is a sequence of links that connect two nodes in the network. It indicates how the nodes are related or reachable in the network.
  - **Network diameter** is the longest network distance among all pairs of nodes in the network. It indicates how spread out or compact the network is.
  - **Network clustering** is a measure of the tendency of nodes to form groups or clusters in the network. It indicates how homogeneous or heterogeneous the network is.
  - **Network modularity** is a measure of the quality of the network clustering, based on the number and size of the clusters and the number and weight of the links within and between the clusters. It indicates how well the network is partitioned or structured into meaningful subgroups.
  - **Network community** is a group of nodes that are more densely connected to each other than to the rest of the network. It indicates a subnetwork that has a common identity or interest.
  - **Network homophily** is a measure of the similarity or dissimilarity of the attributes of the nodes that are linked in the network. It indicates how the network reflects or shapes the social or biological characteristics of the nodes.
  - **Network reciprocity** is a measure of the symmetry or asymmetry of the links in the network. It indicates how the network reflects or shapes the social or biological behaviors of the nodes.
  - **Network transitivity** is a measure of the tendency of the links in the network to form triangles, that is, closed loops of three nodes. It indicates how the network reflects or shapes the social or biological norms of the nodes.

# Personal networks

Personal networks are subsets of social networks that focus on the relationships and interactions of a specific individual, group, or entity. Personal network analysis is the application of social network analysis methods and techniques to personal networks. Personal network analysis can help to study and understand various aspects of social life, such as social support, social capital, personal identity, and personal well-being.

Some of the main concepts and measures of personal network analysis are:

- **Ego**: The focal individual, group, or entity whose personal network is being analyzed.
- **Alter**: Any other individual, group, or entity that is connected to the ego by some relationship or interaction.
- **Tie**: The relationship or interaction that links the ego and an alter, or two alters within the personal network. Ties can be directed or undirected, binary or valued, and can have different types or attributes.
- **Egocentric network**: The set of all alters and ties that are directly connected to the ego. Egocentric network analysis is the simplest form of personal network analysis, and it can provide information about the size, diversity, density, and centrality of the ego's network.
- **Personal community**: The set of all alters and ties that are indirectly connected to the ego through one or more intermediaries. Personal community analysis is a more complex form of personal network analysis, and it can provide information about the structure, composition, and function of the ego's network.
- **Personal network sampling**: The process of identifying and selecting the alters and ties that belong to the ego's personal network. Personal network sampling can be done using various methods, such as name generators, name interpreters, snowball sampling, or network visualizations.
- **Personal network survey**: The process of collecting data on the characteristics and attributes of the ego, the alters, and the ties in the personal network. Personal network survey can be done using various instruments, such as questionnaires, interviews, diaries, or online platforms.
- **Personal network visualization**: The process of representing the personal network graphically, using nodes to depict the ego and the alters, and edges to depict the ties. Personal network visualization can help to explore and communicate the patterns and properties of the personal network.

# Knowledge Representation on the Semantic Web

- Knowledge representation (KR) is the process of encoding information in a form that can be understood and manipulated by machines.
- The Semantic Web is an extension of the current Web that aims to make the data on the Web more accessible and meaningful for humans and machines.
- The Semantic Web relies on KR languages and technologies to express and reason about the semantics (meaning) of the data on the Web.
- Some of the main KR languages and technologies for the Semantic Web are:

  - RDF (Resource Description Framework): The data model and syntax for representing structured and semi-structured data on the Web. RDF uses triples of the form (subject, predicate, object) to express statements about resources.
  - RDFS (RDF Schema): The vocabulary and schema language for defining classes and properties of RDF resources. RDFS enables the creation of taxonomies and hierarchies of concepts and relations.
  - OWL (Web Ontology Language): The schema language, or KR language, of the Semantic Web. OWL enables the definition of complex and expressive concepts and constraints using logic. OWL supports various levels of expressivity and reasoning capabilities, such as OWL Lite, OWL DL, and OWL Full .
  - SPARQL (SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language): The query language and protocol for accessing and manipulating RDF data on the Web. SPARQL allows the formulation of complex queries over multiple data sources using graph patterns and filters.

- The Semantic Web also leverages other KR technologies and standards, such as XML (Extensible Markup Language), XML Schema, URI (Uniform Resource Identifier), and SKOS (Simple Knowledge Organization System).
- The Semantic Web aims to enable the following benefits and applications:

  - Data integration and interoperability: The Semantic Web facilitates the sharing and reuse of data across different domains and applications by providing a common and standardized framework for describing and linking data.
  - Data discovery and exploration: The Semantic Web enables the discovery and exploration of relevant and related data on the Web by providing rich and structured metadata and vocabularies.
  - Data analysis and inference: The Semantic Web enables the analysis and inference of new and implicit knowledge from the data on the Web by using logic and reasoning techniques.
  - Data visualization and presentation: The Semantic Web enables the visualization and presentation of the data on the Web in various formats and modalities, such as graphs, tables, maps, charts, etc.
  - Data quality and provenance: The Semantic Web enables the assessment and improvement of the quality and provenance of the data on the Web by providing mechanisms for validating, annotating, and tracing the data sources and processes.
  - Data access and security: The Semantic Web enables the access and security of the data on the Web by providing protocols and mechanisms for authentication, authorization, encryption, and privacy  .

- The Semantic Web faces some challenges and limitations, such as:

  - Data heterogeneity and inconsistency: The Semantic Web has to deal with the diversity and inconsistency of the data on the Web, which may result from different formats, languages, schemas, ontologies, and quality levels.
  - Data scalability and performance: The Semantic Web has to cope with the large and dynamic volume and variety of the data on the Web, which may pose challenges for storing, indexing, querying, and reasoning over the data.
  - Data usability and adoption: The Semantic Web has to ensure the usability and adoption of the data on the Web by providing user-friendly and intuitive tools and interfaces for creating, accessing, and consuming the data  .

# Ontology languages for the Semantic Web

- Ontology languages are formal languages that are used to represent the concepts, properties, and relations of a domain of knowledge in a machine-readable way.
- Ontology languages can enable semantic interoperability, reasoning, and inference on the web, by providing a common vocabulary and a logical framework for describing and querying data.
- Ontology languages can also facilitate the integration, annotation, and discovery of web resources, by enabling the expression of rich and complex semantics.
- Some of the main ontology languages for the semantic web are:

  - **XML (eXtensible Markup Language)**: A general-purpose markup language that can define the syntax and structure of data in a hierarchical and human-readable way. XML can be used to create custom tags and attributes for describing data, but it does not provide any semantics or meaning to the data. XML can be used as a basis for other ontology languages, such as RDF and OWL.
  - **RDF (Resource Description Framework)**: A standard model and syntax for representing data as triples of subject, predicate, and object. RDF can describe the resources on the web and their properties and relations in a uniform and machine-readable way. RDF can also use URIs (Uniform Resource Identifiers) to identify and link resources across different data sources. RDF can be serialized in different formats, such as XML, N-Triples, Turtle, and JSON-LD.
  - **RDFS (RDF Schema)**: A vocabulary and schema language that extends RDF by providing basic concepts and constructs for defining classes, properties, domains, ranges, and hierarchies of resources. RDFS can be used to create simple ontologies and taxonomies for describing the structure and semantics of data. RDFS can also support basic inference and consistency checking on RDF data.
  - **OWL (Web Ontology Language)**: A family of ontology languages that are based on description logic and that extend RDF and RDFS by providing more expressive and complex constructs for defining classes, properties, individuals, axioms, and restrictions. OWL can be used to create rich and detailed ontologies and to support advanced reasoning and inference on the web. OWL has different sublanguages with different levels of expressivity and computational complexity, such as OWL Lite, OWL DL, and OWL Full.
  - **SKOS (Simple Knowledge Organization System)**: A vocabulary and schema language that is based on RDF and that provides simple and standardized constructs for representing and linking knowledge organization systems, such as thesauri, classification schemes, and glossaries. SKOS can be used to create and share controlled vocabularies and to enable semantic search and navigation on the web.
  - **RIF (Rule Interchange Format)**: A framework and syntax for representing and exchanging rules on the web. RIF can be used to define and apply rules for data manipulation, transformation, validation, and inference. RIF can also integrate with other ontology languages, such as RDF and OWL, to enable rule-based reasoning on the semantic web. RIF has different dialects with different features and semantics, such as RIF Core, RIF BLD, and RIF PRD.

# The Web Ontology Language (OWL)

- OWL is a **Semantic Web language** that is designed to **represent rich and complex knowledge** about things, groups of things, and relations between things.
- OWL is a **family of knowledge representation languages** for authoring **ontologies**. Ontologies are a formal way to describe **taxonomies and classification networks**, essentially defining the **structure of knowledge** for various domains.
- OWL is intended to **facilitate interpretability** among web content using **vocabulary and formatting** that allows **automatic machine processing**.
- OWL is based on the **Resource Description Framework (RDF)**, which is a standard for describing web resources using **triples** of subject, predicate, and object.
- OWL has three **sublanguages** with different levels of **expressiveness** and **computational complexity**:
  - OWL Lite: the simplest and least expressive sublanguage, suitable for **simple classification hierarchies** and **constraint specifications**.
  - OWL DL: the sublanguage that supports the **full expressiveness** of OWL while maintaining **computational decidability** and **completeness**. DL stands for **description logic**, which is a family of logic-based knowledge representation formalisms.
  - OWL Full: the most expressive and complex sublanguage, which allows **arbitrary RDF graphs** to be combined with OWL. OWL Full is **undecidable**, meaning that there is no guarantee that a reasoner can find an answer to a query.
- OWL has two primary uses:
  - **Expressive and flexible data modeling**: OWL allows users to define **classes, properties, individuals, and restrictions** on them using a **rich set of constructors**. OWL also supports **modularity, reusability, and extensibility** of ontologies by allowing **imports, namespaces, and annotations**.
  - **Efficient automated reasoning**: OWL enables **inference** over the data using **logical rules** and **axioms**. OWL reasoners can **check the consistency** of the data and the ontology, **classify the data** into the defined classes, and **answer queries** using the inferred knowledge.

# Comparison of E/R model and relational model

- E/R model and relational model are two types of data models present in DBMS.
- E/R model stands for Entity-Relationship model. It is used to model the logical view of the system from data perspective which consists of these components: Entity, Entity Type, Entity Set, Attribute, Relationship, Relationship Type, Relationship Set, and Constraint .
- Relational model stands for Relational Database model. It is used to model the data in the form of tables which consists of these components: Relation, Tuple, Attribute, Domain, Key, and Foreign Key .
- The main differences between E/R model and relational model are:

  - E/R model is an entity-specific data model, while relational model is a table-specific data model.
  - E/R model deals with entities and their relations, while relational model deals with data and relation between the data of those tables.
  - E/R model is a conceptual model, while relational model is a logical model.
  - E/R model is independent of the implementation, while relational model is dependent on the implementation.
  - E/R model can represent complex relationships and constraints, while relational model can only represent simple relationships and constraints.
  - E/R model can be easily converted into relational model, while relational model cannot be easily converted into E/R model.

- XML is a markup language that defines a set of rules for encoding documents in a format that is both human-readable and machine-readable.
- XML Schema Definition (XSD) is a language that specifies how to formally describe the elements and attributes in an XML document.
- XML and XSD are both based on the Standard Generalized Markup Language (SGML), which is a meta-language for defining markup languages.
- Some of the differences between XML and XSD are :
  - XML is used to describe data, while XSD is used to define the structure and format of the data.
  - XML does not have any predefined tags or attributes, while XSD defines a set of built-in data types and allows the creation of custom data types.
  - XML does not enforce any validation or constraints on the data, while XSD provides mechanisms for validating the data and enforcing rules such as cardinality, uniqueness, and dependencies.
  - XML can be used independently or with other languages such as HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, while XSD is dependent on XML and cannot be used without it.
  - XML can be processed by any XML parser, while XSD requires a special XML Schema processor to validate the XML document against the schema.
- Some of the advantages of using XML and XSD are :
  - XML simplifies data exchange and integration between different systems and platforms, as it is independent of software and hardware.
  - XML stores data in plain text format, which makes it easy to read and edit by humans and machines.
  - XML supports internationalization and localization, as it can handle different languages and character sets.
  - XSD ensures that the data is properly interpreted and formatted, as it defines the data types and constraints.
  - XSD improves data quality and reliability, as it catches errors and inconsistencies in the data.

## Unit 3 - Modelling and Aggregating Social Network Data

- This unit covers the basic concepts and methods for modelling and analysing social network data.
- A social network is a set of actors (nodes) and their relationships (edges).
- Social network data can be represented by graphs, matrices, or tables.
- Graphs are visual representations of nodes and edges, where nodes are usually drawn as circles or points, and edges are usually drawn as lines or curves.
- Matrices are numerical representations of nodes and edges, where rows and columns correspond to nodes, and entries indicate the presence or absence of an edge, or the strength or weight of an edge.
- Tables are textual representations of nodes and edges, where each row corresponds to an edge, and each column corresponds to an attribute of the edge, such as the source node, the target node, the type of relationship, or the time of occurrence.
- Social network data can be analysed using various measures and methods, such as:
  - Degree centrality: the number of edges incident to a node, indicating the popularity or activity of a node.
  - Betweenness centrality: the number of shortest paths between pairs of nodes that pass through a node, indicating the influence or brokerage of a node.
  - Closeness centrality: the inverse of the average distance from a node to all other nodes, indicating the accessibility or efficiency of a node.
  - Eigenvector centrality: the sum of the centrality values of the neighbours of a node, indicating the prestige or reputation of a node.
  - Clustering coefficient: the ratio of the number of triangles (closed triads) to the number of triples (open or closed triads) involving a node, indicating the tendency of a node to form cliques or groups.
  - Modularity: the extent to which a network can be partitioned into communities or clusters of densely connected nodes, indicating the structure or diversity of a network.
  - Homophily: the tendency of nodes to connect with similar nodes, indicating the similarity or diversity of a network.
  - Assortativity: the correlation between the attributes of nodes that are connected by edges, indicating the mixing or segregation of a network.
  - Network density: the ratio of the number of actual edges to the number of possible edges, indicating the completeness or sparseness of a network.
  - Network diameter: the maximum distance between any pair of nodes, indicating the size or complexity of a network.
  - Network reciprocity: the ratio of the number of mutual edges to the number of directed edges, indicating the symmetry or asymmetry of a network.
  - Network transitivity: the ratio of the number of triangles to the number of triples, indicating the cohesion or fragmentation of a network.



# State-of-the-art in network data representation for social network analysis

- Network data representation, also known as network embedding, is the process of encoding network data into low-dimensional vectors that can preserve the network structure and attributes .
- Network data representation is a fundamental step for social network analysis, which is the study of social structures and interactions through the use of networks and graph theory .
- Network data representation can facilitate various tasks in social network analysis, such as node classification, link prediction, community detection, recommendation, and anomaly detection .
- The state-of-the-art methods for network data representation can be categorized into three types: matrix factorization, random walk, and deep learning .
- Matrix factorization methods aim to decompose the network adjacency matrix or other similarity matrices into low-rank matrices, and use the rows or columns of these matrices as the node embeddings. Examples of matrix factorization methods include Laplacian eigenmaps, singular value decomposition, and non-negative matrix factorization .
- Random walk methods generate node sequences by performing random walks on the network, and then apply natural language processing techniques, such as skip-gram or continuous bag-of-words, to learn node embeddings from these sequences. Examples of random walk methods include DeepWalk, node2vec, and LINE .
- Deep learning methods leverage neural networks to learn node embeddings from network data, either in an unsupervised or supervised manner. Examples of deep learning methods include autoencoders, graph convolutional networks, graph attention networks, and graph neural networks .
- The state-of-the-art methods for network data representation face some challenges, such as scalability, heterogeneity, dynamics, and interpretability. Scalability refers to the ability to handle large-scale networks efficiently. Heterogeneity refers to the ability to handle networks with multiple types of nodes and links. Dynamics refers to the ability to handle networks that evolve over time. Interpretability refers to the ability to explain the meaning and significance of the node embeddings .
- The state-of-the-art methods for network data representation also have some opportunities, such as incorporating node attributes, link weights, and network contexts, as well as integrating multiple networks, modalities, and domains. These opportunities can enhance the quality and diversity of the node embeddings, and enable more applications in social network analysis .

# On the notion of equality for the notes of the Unit 3 - Modelling and Aggregating Social Network Data in the subject of SOCIAL NETWORK ANALYTICS

- Equality is a social and ethical value that implies that all individuals or groups have the same rights, opportunities, and status in a society.
- Social network analysis (SNA) is a method of studying social structures and interactions through the use of networks and graph theory .
- SNA can be used to measure and compare the level of equality or inequality in different social networks, based on various criteria such as centrality, diversity, homophily, segregation, and cohesion  .
- Centrality is a measure of how important or influential a node (individual or group) is in a network, based on the number and quality of its connections. Higher centrality implies higher power and access to resources, while lower centrality implies marginalization and exclusion.
- Diversity is a measure of how heterogeneous or diverse a network is, based on the attributes and characteristics of its nodes and links. Higher diversity implies more variety and richness of perspectives, while lower diversity implies more uniformity and conformity.
- Homophily is a tendency of nodes to form ties with similar others, based on shared attributes or preferences. Higher homophily implies more similarity and affinity, while lower homophily implies more difference and diversity .
- Segregation is a measure of how separated or isolated different groups are in a network, based on the density and distribution of their ties. Higher segregation implies more division and inequality, while lower segregation implies more integration and equality .
- Cohesion is a measure of how connected or unified a network is, based on the strength and frequency of its ties. Higher cohesion implies more solidarity and cooperation, while lower cohesion implies more fragmentation and conflict.

- Equality in social networks can be seen as a balance between the different criteria mentioned above, depending on the context and the goals of the analysis. For example, a network that is highly diverse, low in homophily, low in segregation, and high in cohesion can be considered more equal than a network that is low in diversity, high in homophily, high in segregation, and low in cohesion.
- However, equality in social networks is not a fixed or objective state, but a dynamic and subjective process that can change over time and across situations. Therefore, SNA can be used as a tool to monitor and evaluate the effects of social policies, interventions, and innovations on the level of equality or inequality in different social networks.

# Reasoning with instance equality

- Reasoning is the inference of new statements (facts) that necessarily follow from the set of known statements.
- Instance equality is the relation between two individuals that are identical in all their properties.
- Reasoning with instance equality can help us to simplify and unify our knowledge base by eliminating redundant or inconsistent individuals.
- For example, suppose we have a social network of people and their friends, and we know that Alice and Bob are friends, and Bob and Carol are friends. We also know that Alice and Carol have the same name, age, gender, and hobbies. Then, we can reason that Alice and Carol are the same person, and we can replace them with a single individual in our network. This reduces the complexity and ambiguity of our network.
- However, reasoning with instance equality can also introduce errors or uncertainties if we do not have enough information or evidence to support our inference.
- For example, suppose we have a social network of people and their tweets, and we know that Alice and Bob have tweeted the same content, and Bob and Carol have tweeted the same content. We also know that Alice and Carol have the same profile picture, location, and bio. Then, we might reason that Alice and Carol are the same person, and we can replace them with a single individual in our network. However, this might be incorrect if Alice and Carol are actually different people who are using the same account or copying each other's tweets. This could lead to false conclusions or missed opportunities in our network analysis.
- Therefore, reasoning with instance equality requires careful consideration of the context, the source, and the quality of the data, as well as the goals and assumptions of the analysis.

# Data collection for social network analysis

- Data collection is the process of obtaining information about the structure and dynamics of social networks, such as the actors, ties, and attributes involved.
- Data collection methods for social network analysis can be classified into two types: **socio-centric** and **ego-centric**.
  - Socio-centric methods focus on the whole network or a well-defined subset of actors, such as an organization, a community, or a group. Socio-centric methods aim to capture the complete network structure and the relationships among all actors.
  - Ego-centric methods focus on a single actor or a sample of actors, and their personal networks, such as their friends, family, or colleagues. Ego-centric methods aim to capture the network characteristics and outcomes of individual actors and their alters.
- Data collection methods for social network analysis can also be classified into two modes: **direct** and **indirect**.
  - Direct methods involve asking actors to report their own ties or attributes, or those of others, using surveys, interviews, or observations. Direct methods can provide rich and detailed information, but they may also suffer from biases, errors, or incompleteness due to memory, social desirability, or non-response.
  - Indirect methods involve inferring ties or attributes from other sources, such as archival records, digital traces, or experiments. Indirect methods can provide large and objective data, but they may also suffer from noise, ambiguity, or irrelevance due to measurement, interpretation, or validity issues.
- Data collection methods for social network analysis can also be classified into two types: **static** and **dynamic**.
  - Static methods involve collecting data at one point in time, or aggregating data over a period of time, to represent the network structure and attributes at a given moment. Static methods can provide a snapshot of the network, but they may not capture the changes, processes, or mechanisms that shape the network over time.
  - Dynamic methods involve collecting data at multiple points in time, or continuously, to represent the network structure and attributes as they evolve. Dynamic methods can provide a longitudinal view of the network, but they may also require more resources, complexity, and challenges in data collection, analysis, and interpretation.
- Data collection methods for social network analysis can also be classified into two types: **relational** and **attribute**.
  - Relational methods involve collecting data on the ties or interactions among actors, such as communication, collaboration, friendship, or influence. Relational methods can provide information on the network topology, density, centrality, cohesion, and subgroups.
  - Attribute methods involve collecting data on the characteristics or properties of actors, such as demographics, attitudes, behaviors, or outcomes. Attribute methods can provide information on the network composition, diversity, homophily, and correlation.
- Data collection methods for social network analysis can also be combined or integrated to provide a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the network. For example, one can collect both relational and attribute data, or both socio-centric and ego-centric data, or both direct and indirect data, or both static and dynamic data, depending on the research question, design, and purpose.
- Data collection for social network analysis requires careful planning, preparation, and execution, as well as ethical considerations and quality assurance. Some of the steps involved in data collection for social network analysis are :
  - Define the research question and the network boundary, scope, and level of analysis.
  - Choose the appropriate data collection method, mode, type, and source, based on the research question, design, and purpose, as well as the availability, feasibility, and validity of the data.
  - Design and pilot the data collection instrument, such as the survey questionnaire, the interview guide, the observation protocol, or the data extraction procedure, ensuring that the instrument is clear, consistent, reliable, and valid.
  - Implement and monitor the data collection process, such as the sampling, recruitment, administration, or extraction of the data, ensuring that the process is ethical, efficient, and complete.
  - Preprocess and clean the data, such as the transformation, reduction, or enrichment of the data, ensuring that the data is suitable, accurate, and ready for social network analysis.
