# INTRODUCTION TO INTERNET OF THINGS

1. Internet of Things or IoT refers to the interconnection of everyday physical objects via the internet. These physical objects are equipped with sensors, software, and network connectivity that enables them to send and receive data.
2. IoT devices are able to connect with each other and exchange data over a network without any human-to-human or human-to-computer interaction. For example, a smartwatch can monitor your health in real-time and send alerts to your doctor if any critical metrics are detected.
3. Some of the key components of IoT are:
- Sensors: To sense and collect data from the surrounding environment. For example, sensors can detect motion, light, temperature, pressure, etc.
- Connectivity: To connect the devices to the Internet, technologies like Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, NFC, etc. are used.
- Cloud: The collected data is stored and processed in the cloud. The IoT applications and analytics operate on the cloud.
- Software and Applications: The IoT devices run on embedded software and systems that control the device and applications. The data from IoT devices can be viewed via dashboards or mobile applications.
4. The advantages of IoT include increased efficiency, automation, remote monitoring, predictive analysis, and improved customer experience. However, IoT also brings security and privacy risks that must be addressed.
5. IoT has a wide range of applications in industries like transportation, healthcare, manufacturing, home automation, and more. As IoT technology continues to advance, it will enable more intelligent and connected ecosystems with significant transformational potential.

### Communication Technologies

- WiFi: WiFi is a local area wireless technology that enables connected devices to communicate over a wireless local area network (WLAN). WiFi is the most common communication technology used in IoT devices and systems.
- Bluetooth: Bluetooth is a wireless technology standard for exchanging data over short distances using radio waves in the frequency range of 2.4 to 2.485 gigahertz from fixed and mobile devices, and building personal area networks (PANs). Bluetooth is used to connect devices such as headphones, speakers, keyboards, and other peripherals to computing devices.
- Zigbee: Zigbee is a wireless communication protocol based on the IEEE 802.15.4 standard. It is designed for low-power and low-bandwidth applications requiring intermittent data transmissions from a large number of wireless devices. Zigbee enables wireless control and monitoring applications.
- RFID: Radio-frequency identification (RFID) uses electromagnetic fields to automatically identify and track tags attached to objects. The tags contain electronically stored information which can be read from up to several meters away. RFID technology is commonly used to track objects and physical items throughout a supply chain.
- NFC: Near field communication (NFC) is a set of communication protocols that enable two electronic devices, one of which is usually a portable device such as a smartphone, to establish radio data communication with each other by bringing them close together or touching them together. NFC is used in contactless payments, access control, and device pairing.
- Cellular: Cellular networks provide wide area wireless connectivity and enable IoT devices and systems to have a wide range. 4G LTE and emerging 5G cellular technologies are enabling new IoT applications that require higher bandwidth and lower latency. Cellular IoT technologies include NB-IoT and LTE-M.

### Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) Technology

- RFID is a wireless technology that uses radio waves to identify objects.
- RFID system consists of RFID tags and RFID readers.
- RFID tags are microchips that store data and are attached to objects. They contain antennas to enable them to receive and respond to radio-frequency queries from an RFID reader.
- RFID readers are devices that emit radio waves and receive responses from RFID tags. They convert the radio waves received from the tags into digital data that can be passed on to computers and networks.
- The RFID tags can be passive, semi-passive or active.
- Passive tags do not have an internal power source and get activated when they are in the electromagnetic field of the reader. Semi-passive and active tags have internal power sources. Active tags have longer range but are more expensive.
- RFID technology enables contactless identification of objects and can be used for applications such as access control, payment, supply chain management, etc.

### Wireless Sensor Networks

- Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs) consist of spatially distributed autonomous sensors to monitor physical or environmental conditions, such as temperature, sound, vibration, pressure, motion or pollutants and to cooperatively pass their data through the network to a main location.
- WSNs are used in many applications such as environmental monitoring, industrial process monitoring and control, home automation, traffic control, and medical applications.
- The key components of a wireless sensor network are:

1. Sensor Nodes: These are the tiny devices that contain sensors, processors, transceivers, and power sources. They sense the environment and transmit the data to be collected and analyzed.
2. Gateways: These collect the data from the sensor nodes and transmit it to the network. They have more powerful processors and memory and are responsible for data aggregation and routing.
3. Sink or Base Station: This is the destination of the data. The data collected from the sensor nodes is sent to the sink, which may be a computer that analyzes the data and displays the sensed information.

- The advantages of WSNs are:

1. Inexpensive: Sensor nodes are cheap and the network is easy to deploy.
2. Flexible: The network can be configured in any shape and the nodes can be placed anywhere.
3. Scalable: Large numbers of nodes can be added easily to the network.
4. Applications: They enable a huge variety of applications and the data can be accessed from anywhere.

- The challenges in WSNs are:

1. Limited Resources: Sensor nodes have limited power, memory, and processing capabilities. This can affect the lifetime and scalability of the network.
2. Data Aggregation: As huge amounts of data are generated, efficient in-network data aggregation and compression techniques are required to reduce the amount of data transmitted and to avoid congestion.
3. Routing: Energy-efficient routing protocols are required due to the limited resources and to maximize network lifetime.
4. Location Awareness: Knowledge of the location of the sensor nodes can enable advanced functionalities but localization is challenging and requires additional hardware and resources.
5. Security: The wireless nature and limited resources of the nodes make WSNs vulnerable to various attacks, and security mechanisms are required to protect the network and data.

### Wireless Medium access issues

- Wireless medium is shared among multiple devices. This can lead to collisions and interference which degrades performance.
- Hidden terminal problem: When two nodes are out of range of each other but can affect a third node in between them. The third node may not be able to resolve the collision.
- Exposed terminal problem: When a node avoids transmission due to an ongoing transmission on the channel but the transmission was not actually going to affect it. This leads to underutilization of the channel.
- Near-far effect: When a strong signal from a nearby transmitter overpowers a weaker signal from a farther node. The farther node gets masked leading to unfairness.
- Frequency interference from other systems or devices can corrupt the communication.

### Mitigation techniques

- Contention-based MAC protocols like CSMA/CA and its variants help resolve medium access issues.
- Using directional antennas and limiting the transmission power can reduce the near-far effect.
- Using frequency hopping and spread spectrum can avoid interference.
- Reserving time slots for critical transmissions can provide guaranteed delivery.
- Coordinating transmissions spatially or temporally can avoid hidden and exposed terminal problems.

1. CSMA: Carrier Sense Multiple Access
- Devices sense the medium and transmit when idle
- Collision possible if two devices sense idle medium and transmit simultaneously

- Devices sense medium and choose random backoff time before transmission
- Backoff time reduces probability of collision

- Time is divided into slots and allocated to devices
- Coordinated access eliminates collisions
- Inefficient if some slots unused
- Example: Bluetooth, cellular networks

4. FDMA: Frequency Division Multiple Access
- Frequency spectrum divided into bands allocated to devices
- Guard bands reduce interference
- Inefficient use of spectrum if bands unused
- Example: cellular networks

5. CDMA: Code Division Multiple Access
- Devices assigned unique codes to spread signal over frequency spectrum
- Collected signals separated using codes
- Example: cellular networks (3G, 4G), some satellite networks

### Survey routing protocols

- Protocols for finding routes in IoT networks:
-- Flooding: Each node broadcasts messages to neighbors, leading to redundancy and collisions.
-- Gossiping: Nodes exchange routing information with randomly selected neighbors. Scales better but may be slow to converge.
-- Hierarchical routing: Organize nodes into clusters with inter-cluster and intra-cluster routing. Reduces overhead compared to flooding but additional latency.
-- Geographic routing: Use position info to route closer to the destination. Need accurate position data and don't work well with obstacles.
-- Opportunistic routing: Take advantage of mobility and broadcast nature of wireless to improve delivery ratio. Need prediction of node encounters/mobility.

### Sensor deployment & Node discovery

- Sensor deployment refers to the placement of sensors in a network to achieve desired coverage and connectivity. It depends on the application and its requirements.
- For monitoring large areas, a random deployment may be sufficient while for monitoring specific spots or points of interest, deliberate deployment is needed.
- Node discovery refers to the process by which nodes in a network detect the presence of and learn about other nodes.
- Node discovery is critical for network formation, routing, data aggregation, etc.
- Methods for node discovery:
-- Flooding: Nodes broadcast discovery messages periodically. This leads to high overhead and collisions.
-- Trie-based: Nodes use a trie data structure to store information about other nodes. Scalability is limited.
-- Location-based: If nodes know their location, they can limit discovery to neighboring nodes leading to lower overhead. Location information may not always be available.
-- Code-based: Nodes allocate and share codes to identify and discover each other. Extra mechanisms are needed to handle code conflicts and exhaustion.
