## Internet of Things (IoT): Vision, Definition, Conceptual Framework, Architectural view, technology behind IoT, Sources of the IoT, M2M Communication, IoT Examples

- The **vision** of IoT is to create a network of interconnected devices that can sense, communicate, and act on the physical and virtual world, enabling new applications and services that can improve the quality of life, efficiency, and sustainability of various domains such as smart cities, smart homes, smart health, smart agriculture, smart industry, etc.
- The **definition** of IoT varies depending on the perspective and context, but a general one is: IoT is a system of interrelated computing devices, mechanical and digital machines, objects, animals or people that are provided with unique identifiers and the ability to transfer data over a network without requiring human-to-human or human-to-computer interaction.
- The **conceptual framework** of IoT consists of three main components: **things**, **networks**, and **applications**. Things are the physical or virtual entities that have identities, attributes, and capabilities. Networks are the communication infrastructures that enable data exchange and processing among things and applications. Applications are the software systems that provide value-added services to the end-users by exploiting the data and functionalities of things and networks.
- The **architectural view** of IoT can be divided into four layers: **perception layer**, **network layer**, **middleware layer**, and **application layer**. The perception layer is responsible for sensing, identifying, and collecting data from the physical world. The network layer is responsible for transmitting, routing, and processing data among things and other network nodes. The middleware layer is responsible for providing common services and functionalities such as data management, security, privacy, interoperability, etc. The application layer is responsible for delivering specific services and solutions to the end-users according to their needs and preferences.
- The **technology behind IoT** involves various hardware, software, and communication technologies that enable the realization of the IoT vision. Some of the key technologies are: **sensors and actuators**, **RFID and NFC**, **wireless sensor networks (WSNs)**, **cloud computing and fog computing**, **big data and data analytics**, **artificial intelligence and machine learning**, **blockchain and distributed ledger**, etc.
- The **sources of the IoT** are the different domains and sectors that generate and consume data and services through the IoT system. Some of the main sources are: **smart cities**, **smart homes**, **smart health**, **smart agriculture**, **smart industry**, **smart energy**, **smart transportation**, **smart environment**, etc.
- **M2M communication** is a type of communication that occurs between machines or devices without human intervention. It is an essential component of IoT that enables data exchange and coordination among things. M2M communication can be achieved through various protocols and standards such as **MQTT**, **CoAP**, **HTTP**, **AMQP**, **XMPP**, etc.
- **IoT examples** are the concrete instances of IoT applications and services that demonstrate the benefits and challenges of IoT in various domains and scenarios. Some of the common examples are: **smart lighting**, **smart metering**, **smart parking**, **smart waste management**, **smart irrigation**, **smart wearable devices**, **smart medical devices**, **smart manufacturing**, **smart logistics**, etc.

### Technology behind IoT

- IoT stands for Internet of Things, which is the concept of connecting any device with an on/off switch to the internet and to other connected devices.
- IoT devices can collect and share data about their usage and environment, enabling various applications such as smart homes, smart cities, smart factories, smart healthcare, etc.
- IoT works through a combination of wireless networking technology, physical devices, advanced data analytics and cloud computing.
- The basic process of how IoT works is as follows:
  - A group of physical devices is wired or wirelessly linked to each other and/or a central area.
  - The devices have sensors, actuators, or other components that can collect data or perform actions.
  - The data is transmitted to a cloud platform or a local server via a network connection.
  - The data is processed, stored, and analyzed by software applications or artificial intelligence algorithms.
  - The results or commands are sent back to the devices or to other systems or users.
- Some of the technologies used in IoT systems are :
  - Edge computing: It increases the computing power at the edges of an IoT network, reducing communication latency and improving response time. It allows devices to perform some data processing, filtering, or analysis locally, without relying on the cloud.
  - Cloud computing: It provides scalable, on-demand, and cost-effective resources for storing, processing, and analyzing large amounts of data from IoT devices. It also enables remote management, monitoring, and updating of IoT devices and applications.
  - Connectivity: It refers to the wireless or wired technologies that enable data transmission between IoT devices and the cloud or local servers. Some of the common connectivity technologies are Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Zigbee, cellular networks (e.g., GSM, 4G, 5G), Ethernet, and mesh networks.
  - Security: It refers to the hardware and software mechanisms that protect IoT devices, data, and networks from unauthorized access, tampering, or attacks. Some of the security technologies are encryption, authentication, authorization, firewalls, and security chips.
  - Sensors and actuators: They are the components that enable IoT devices to sense, measure, or control physical phenomena such as temperature, humidity, motion, light, sound, etc. Sensors convert physical signals into electrical signals, while actuators convert electrical signals into physical actions.
  - Microcontrollers and microprocessors: They are the chips that provide the computing power and memory for IoT devices. Microcontrollers are small, low-power, and embedded devices that can run simple programs, while microprocessors are more powerful and complex devices that can run operating systems and applications.
  - Other technologies: Depending on the specific use case and requirements, IoT systems may also use other technologies such as GPS, RFID, NFC, cameras, displays, speakers, etc.

### Design Principles for Connected Devices

- Connected devices are products that use the Internet of Things (IoT) or machine-to-machine (M2M) communication to interact with other devices, systems, or users.
- Designing connected devices requires considering various aspects such as functionality, value, security, scalability, flexibility, serviceability, connectivity, and hybridity.
- Some of the design principles for connected devices are  :

  - Do your research: Understand the purpose, context, and user needs of the connected device. Identify the problems, opportunities, and goals that the device can address or achieve.
  - Concentrate on value: Align the features and functions of the connected device with the value proposition for the users. Provide clear and meaningful benefits that justify the use of the device and the data it generates or consumes.
  - Don't forget about the bigger picture: Consider the ecosystem and the network that the connected device operates in. Ensure interoperability and compatibility with other devices, systems, and standards. Design for scalability and flexibility to accommodate future changes and growth.
  - Remember about the security: Protect the connected device and the data it handles from unauthorized access, manipulation, or theft. Implement appropriate security measures such as encryption, authentication, authorization, and auditing. Follow best practices and guidelines for IoT security.
  - Build with the context in mind: Design the connected device to suit the environment and the situation where it will be used. Consider the physical, social, and cultural factors that may affect the user experience and the device performance. Adapt the device to different settings and scenarios.
  - Make good use of prototypes: Test and validate the connected device design with prototypes and user feedback. Use iterative and agile methods to refine and improve the design based on the results and insights. Evaluate the device against the design criteria and the user expectations.



### IoT/M2M systems layers and design standardization

- IoT/M2M systems are composed of interconnected devices and applications that communicate and exchange data over the internet or other networks.
- IoT/M2M systems can be divided into three major domains: the application layer, the service layer, and the network layer .
- The application layer is the domain where the end-user interacts with the IoT/M2M system through various applications, such as smart home, smart city, smart health, etc. The application layer provides the user interface, the business logic, and the data analysis for the IoT/M2M system.
- The service layer is the domain where the IoT/M2M system provides common functionalities and services, such as device management, data management, security, discovery, orchestration, etc. The service layer enables interoperability and scalability of the IoT/M2M system across different devices, applications, and networks.

- **Bluetooth**: A short-range wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices. Bluetooth is widely used for personal area networks (PANs), such as connecting smartphones, headphones, speakers, keyboards, and mice. Bluetooth has low power consumption and can support multiple devices simultaneously. Bluetooth also supports mesh networking, which allows devices to relay data to each other without a central hub. Bluetooth is suitable for IoT applications that require low data rates and low latency, such as smart home, wearable, and health devices.

- **Zigbee**: A low-power wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices. Zigbee is similar to Bluetooth, but has a longer range and can support more devices in a network. Zigbee is based on the IEEE 802.15.4 standard and operates in the 2.4 GHz frequency band. Zigbee also supports mesh networking, which enables self-healing and scalability of the network. Zigbee is suitable for IoT applications that require low data rates and high reliability, such as smart lighting, smart metering, and industrial automation.

- **Z-Wave**: A low-power wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices. Z-Wave is designed for home automation and operates in the sub-GHz frequency band, which has less interference and better penetration than the 2.4 GHz band. Z-Wave also supports mesh networking, which allows devices to communicate with each other and the gateway. Z-Wave is suitable for IoT applications that require low data rates and low power consumption, such as smart locks, smart thermostats, and smart sensors.

- **Wi-Fi**: A wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices to the internet. Wi-Fi is based on the IEEE 802.11 standard and operates in the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz frequency bands. Wi-Fi has high data rates and can support multiple devices simultaneously. Wi-Fi also supports direct device-to-device communication, such as Wi-Fi Direct and Wi-Fi Aware. Wi-Fi is suitable for IoT applications that require high data rates and high bandwidth, such as video streaming, gaming, and cloud computing.

- **Cellular**: A wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices to the internet via cellular networks. Cellular is based on the 3G, 4G, and 5G standards and operates in various frequency bands, depending on the region and the network operator. Cellular has high data rates and can support long-range and wide-area communication. Cellular also supports low-power and low-bandwidth communication, such as Narrowband IoT (NB-IoT) and Long Term Evolution for Machines (LTE-M). Cellular is suitable for IoT applications that require high mobility, high security, and global coverage, such as smart vehicles, smart cities, and smart agriculture.

- **NFC**: A short-range wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices. NFC is based on the ISO/IEC 14443 standard and operates in the 13.56 MHz frequency band. NFC has low data rates and can support peer-to-peer, reader-writer, and card emulation modes. NFC is suitable for IoT applications that require low data rates and high security, such as contactless payment, access control, and identification.

- **LoRaWAN**: A low-power wide-area network (LPWAN) technology that uses radio waves to connect devices. LoRaWAN is based on the LoRa modulation technique and operates in the sub-GHz frequency band. LoRaWAN has low data rates and can support long-range and low-power communication. LoRaWAN also supports adaptive data rate, which allows devices to adjust their transmission parameters according to the network conditions. LoRaWAN is suitable for IoT applications that require low data rates and long battery life, such as smart parking, smart waste management, and smart agriculture.

- Unlike a general-purpose computer, such as a personal computer, an embedded system performs one or a few pre-defined tasks, usually with very specific requirements, and often includes task-specific hardware and mechanical parts not usually found in a general-purpose computer.
- Embedded systems are typically characterized by low cost, low power, small size, high reliability, and real-time performance.
- Embedded systems can be classified into four types: standalone, networked, mobile, and real-time.
- Standalone embedded systems operate independently and do not require a host system or network connection.
- Networked embedded systems rely on a network connection to communicate with other systems or devices, such as the Internet of Things (IoT) devices.
- Mobile embedded systems are portable and battery-powered, such as smartphones, tablets, and wearable devices.
- Real-time embedded systems have strict timing constraints and must respond to events or inputs within a specified deadline, such as industrial control systems, medical devices, and avionics systems.
- Embedded systems are widely used in various domains, such as consumer electronics, automotive, aerospace, telecommunications, healthcare, and industrial automation.
- Embedded systems are developed using a combination of hardware and software components, such as microcontrollers, microprocessors, sensors, actuators, memory, input/output devices, operating systems, and application software.
- Embedded systems software and development environments are specialized tools and techniques for designing, testing, debugging, and optimizing embedded systems.
- Some of the common embedded systems software and development environments are C/C++ programming languages, assembly language, embedded Linux, real-time operating systems, cross compilers, debuggers, simulators, and emulators.
- Some of the popular embedded systems hardware platforms for IoT are Arduino, Netduino, Raspberry Pi, BeagleBone, Intel Galileo, and ARM Cortex.
- Arduino is an open-source platform that consists of a microcontroller board and a software development environment that can be used to create interactive electronic projects.
- Netduino is a similar platform to Arduino, but it uses the .NET Micro Framework and C# programming language instead of C/C++.
- Raspberry Pi is a low-cost, credit-card sized computer that can run Linux and other operating systems, and can be used for various applications, such as web servers, media centers, robotics, and home automation.
- BeagleBone is another single-board computer that can run Linux and other operating systems, and has more processing power and memory than Raspberry Pi, but is also more expensive.

## Unit 3 - Network & Communication aspects in IoT

- IoT devices communicate with other devices, applications, and services over the internet using various protocols and technologies .
- IoT communication can be classified into three types: device-to-device, device-to-cloud, and device-to-gateway.
- Device-to-device communication enables direct data exchange between IoT devices without any intermediary. It can use protocols such as Bluetooth, Zigbee, Z-Wave, or Wi-Fi Direct.
- Device-to-cloud communication involves sending data from IoT devices to cloud servers for processing, storage, or analysis. It can use protocols such as MQTT, CoAP, HTTP, or AMQP.
- Device-to-gateway communication connects IoT devices to a local gateway device that acts as a bridge between the device network and the internet. It can use protocols such as 6LoWPAN, Thread, or LoRaWAN.
- Wireless medium access issues in IoT include interference, congestion, energy efficiency, scalability, and security.
- MAC protocols are used to coordinate the access of multiple devices to a shared wireless medium. They can be classified into contention-based and contention-free protocols.
- Contention-based protocols allow devices to compete for the medium access using random or deterministic methods. They can be further divided into slotted and unslotted protocols.
- Slotted protocols divide the time into slots and assign them to devices based on some criteria. Examples are TDMA, FDMA, and CDMA.
- Unslotted protocols do not use slots and allow devices to transmit whenever they have data. Examples are CSMA, CSMA/CA, and CSMA/CD.
- Contention-free protocols avoid collisions by using a central controller or a reservation mechanism to grant access to devices. Examples are polling, token passing, and reservation-based protocols.
- Routing protocols are used to find the optimal path for data delivery in a network of IoT devices. They can be classified into proactive, reactive, and hybrid protocols.
- Proactive protocols maintain routing tables for all nodes in the network and update them periodically. They have low latency but high overhead. Examples are DSDV, OLSR, and RIP.
- Reactive protocols discover routes on demand when a source node needs to send data to a destination node. They have low overhead but high latency. Examples are AODV, DSR, and TORA.
- Hybrid protocols combine the features of proactive and reactive protocols to achieve a balance between latency and overhead. Examples are ZRP, CEDAR, and EIGRP.
- Sensor deployment and node discovery are the processes of placing and identifying IoT devices in a network. They can be done manually or automatically.
- Manual deployment and discovery involve human intervention to install and configure the devices. They are suitable for small-scale and static networks.
- Automatic deployment and discovery involve self-organization and self-configuration of the devices. They are suitable for large-scale and dynamic networks.
- Data aggregation and dissemination are the processes of collecting and distributing data in a network of IoT devices. They can be done centrally or distributively.
- Centralized aggregation and dissemination involve a single node or a cluster head that collects data from other nodes and sends it to the sink or the cloud. They have low complexity but high energy consumption and latency.
- Distributed aggregation and dissemination involve multiple nodes that cooperate to aggregate and disseminate data in a hop-by-hop or a multi-hop manner. They have high complexity but low energy consumption and latency.

### Wireless Medium Access Issues for IoT

- Wireless medium access issues refer to the challenges and problems that arise when multiple IoT devices share the same wireless channel for data transmission and reception.
- Some of the common wireless medium access issues are:
  - Interference: Interference occurs when unwanted signals from other sources or devices interfere with the desired signal, causing degradation of performance, packet loss, and energy consumption. Interference can be caused by devices operating in the same or adjacent frequency bands, such as WLAN, Bluetooth, ZigBee, etc.
  - Hidden terminal problem: Hidden terminal problem occurs when two or more devices that are out of the range of each other try to communicate with a common device, resulting in collisions and retransmissions. For example, if device A and device B are both trying to send data to device C, but device A and device B cannot hear each other, they may transmit at the same time and collide at device C.
  - Exposed terminal problem: Exposed terminal problem occurs when a device is prevented from transmitting to another device because of a transmission by a third device, even though the transmission does not interfere with the intended receiver. For example, if device A is sending data to device B, and device C wants to send data to device D, device C may defer its transmission because it hears device A, even though device D is not affected by device A's transmission.
  - Near-far problem: Near-far problem occurs when a device with a stronger signal dominates the channel and prevents other devices with weaker signals from accessing the channel. For example, if device A is close to device B and device C is far from device B, device A may have a higher chance of transmitting successfully to device B than device C, even if device C has data to send.
  - Fading: Fading refers to the variation of signal strength over time and space due to multipath propagation, shadowing, and mobility. Fading can cause fluctuations in the received signal quality, leading to errors and retransmissions.

- To overcome these wireless medium access issues, various Medium Access Control (MAC) protocols have been proposed and designed for IoT applications. MAC protocols are responsible for coordinating and regulating the access of multiple IoT devices to the shared wireless channel.
- Some of the common objectives and features of MAC protocols for IoT are:
  - Energy efficiency: Energy efficiency is the ability to minimize the energy consumption of IoT devices, especially those that are battery-powered or energy-harvesting. Energy efficiency can be achieved by reducing the idle listening, collision, overhearing, and control overhead of the MAC protocol.
  - Scalability: Scalability is the ability to support a large number of IoT devices with diverse requirements and capabilities, without compromising the performance and reliability of the MAC protocol. Scalability can be achieved by using adaptive and distributed algorithms, avoiding synchronization and contention, and exploiting spatial reuse and diversity.
  - Reliability: Reliability is the ability to ensure the successful and timely delivery of data packets from the source to the destination, despite the wireless medium access issues and the dynamic network conditions. Reliability can be achieved by using error control, retransmission, acknowledgment, and feedback mechanisms, as well as prioritizing and scheduling the data packets according to their importance and urgency.
  - Throughput: Throughput is the amount of data that can be transmitted and received over the wireless channel per unit time, measured in bits per second (bps) or packets per second (pps). Throughput can be improved by increasing the channel utilization, reducing the collision and contention, and using multiple channels and antennas.



### MAC protocol survey for IoT

- MAC (Medium Access Control) protocols are responsible for coordinating the access of multiple devices to the shared wireless medium in IoT (Internet of Things) networks.
- MAC protocols can be classified into two main categories: contention-based and contention-free protocols.
- Contention-based protocols allow devices to compete for the channel access without reservation or scheduling. They are suitable for low traffic and dynamic networks, but they may suffer from collisions, overhead, and unfairness. Examples of contention-based protocols are CSMA/CA, ALOHA, and IEEE 802.11.
- Contention-free protocols allocate the channel access to devices based on reservation or scheduling mechanisms. They can provide guarantees on throughput, delay, and fairness, but they may incur high overhead and complexity. Examples of contention-free protocols are TDMA, FDMA, CDMA, and IEEE 802.15.4.
- MAC protocols for IoT need to address the challenges of scalability, energy efficiency, reliability, and heterogeneity of IoT devices and applications.
- IEEE 802.11ah, also known as WiFi HaLow, is a recent MAC protocol that aims to provide a scalable and energy-efficient solution for medium-range communication in IoT. It leverages various features such as restricted access window, target wake time, and group formation to support a large number of devices with low power consumption and high reliability.
- Other MAC protocols for IoT include LoRaWAN, Sigfox, NB-IoT, and LTE-M, which are designed for long-range and low-power communication in IoT. They use different modulation and coding schemes, frequency bands, and network architectures to achieve different trade-offs between coverage, data rate, and battery life.



### Survey routing protocols for IoT

- Routing protocols are responsible for finding and maintaining routes between nodes in a network, especially in dynamic and resource-constrained environments like IoT.
- Routing protocols for IoT can be classified into three categories based on the network structure: flat, hierarchical and location-based.
- Flat routing protocols treat all nodes equally and use flooding or gossiping techniques to disseminate data. Examples of flat routing protocols are SPIN, Directed Diffusion, Flooding and Gossiping.
- Hierarchical routing protocols divide the network into clusters and assign different roles to nodes based on their energy or capabilities. Examples of hierarchical routing protocols are LEACH, PEGASIS, HEED and TEEN.
- Location-based routing protocols use the geographic information of nodes to make routing decisions. Examples of location-based routing protocols are GEAR, GPSR, GAF and ZRP.
- Routing protocols for IoT can also be classified based on the network layer they operate on: network layer, transport layer or application layer.
- Network layer routing protocols are designed to provide end-to-end connectivity and address the challenges of low-power and lossy networks (LLNs). Examples of network layer routing protocols are RPL, LOADng, AODV and DYMO .
- Transport layer routing protocols are designed to provide reliable and congestion-controlled data delivery and address the challenges of heterogeneous and constrained devices. Examples of transport layer routing protocols are CoAP, MQTT, AMQP and XMPP.
- Application layer routing protocols are designed to provide semantic interoperability and address the challenges of data representation and discovery. Examples of application layer routing protocols are LWM2M, OMA-DM, IPSO and oneM2M.
- Routing protocols for IoT must also consider the security and privacy issues that arise from the open and distributed nature of the network. Examples of secure routing protocols for IoT are S-RPL, S-LOADng, S-AODV and S-DYMO .
- Secure routing protocols for IoT must address the vulnerabilities to attacks such as sinkhole, wormhole, blackhole, Sybil, replay and selective forwarding.
- Secure routing protocols for IoT must also provide mechanisms for authentication, encryption, integrity, confidentiality, availability and non-repudiation.

  - Reducing the data redundancy and overhead by aggregating similar or correlated data from multiple sources .
  - Enhancing the data accuracy and completeness by disseminating data to multiple destinations or aggregators .
  - Balancing the network load and energy consumption by selecting appropriate nodes or paths for data aggregation and dissemination .
  - Improving the data security and privacy by encrypting, authenticating, or anonymizing the data before aggregation and dissemination .

  - Routing: finding the optimal or suboptimal paths or routes for data transmission between source and destination nodes, considering the network topology, constraints, and objectives.

# Arduino Platform Boards Anatomy

Arduino boards are the microcontroller development platform that will be at the heart of your projects. When making something you will be building the circuits and interfaces for interaction, and telling the microcontroller how to interface with other components. Here are some of the main features of Arduino boards  :

- **Microcontroller**: This is the brain of the board. It is a small chip that can run programs and perform calculations. Arduino boards use different types of microcontrollers, such as the ATmega328P on the Arduino Uno.
- **Digital pins**: These are the pins that can be used to send or receive digital signals, such as HIGH or LOW voltage levels. They can be used to control LEDs, motors, switches, and other devices. Some of the digital pins can also be used as PWM outputs, which means they can vary the voltage level to create analog-like effects, such as dimming an LED or controlling the speed of a motor  .
- **Analog pins**: These are the pins that can be used to read analog signals, such as the voltage level from a potentiometer, a light sensor, or a temperature sensor. They can also be used to send analog signals using a technique called DAC (Digital-to-Analog Conversion), which means they can generate voltage levels between 0 and 5 volts  .
- **Power pins**: These are the pins that can be used to supply power to the board or to other components. They include the 5V pin, the 3.3V pin, the GND pin, and the VIN pin. The 5V and 3.3V pins provide regulated voltage levels that can be used to power sensors and other devices. The GND pin is the common ground for all the circuits. The VIN pin can be used to power the board from an external source, such as a battery or an adapter  .
- **USB connection**: This is the port that can be used to connect the board to a computer or a power source. It can be used to upload programs to the board, to communicate with the board using serial communication, or to power the board  .
- **Power jack**: This is the port that can be used to power the board from an external source, such as a battery or an adapter. It can accept voltages from 7 to 12 volts  .
- **ICSP header**: This is the header that can be used to program the board using an external programmer, such as an AVR ISP. It can also be used to communicate with other devices using SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) protocol  .
- **Reset button**: This is the button that can be used to reset the board, which means to restart the program that is running on the board  .
