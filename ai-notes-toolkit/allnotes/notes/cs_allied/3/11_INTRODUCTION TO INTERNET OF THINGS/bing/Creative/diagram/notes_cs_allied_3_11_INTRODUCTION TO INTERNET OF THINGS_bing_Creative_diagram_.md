The Internet of Things (IoT) is a network of physical objects that can communicate and exchange data with other devices and systems over the internet or other communications networks . These objects can include anything with a sensor and a unique identifier (UID), such as household appliances, wearable devices, industrial machines, vehicles, etc.

Some of the benefits of IoT are:

- It can enable new applications and services that improve the quality of life, efficiency, productivity, and safety of various domains, such as smart homes, smart cities, smart health, smart agriculture, etc .
- It can generate and analyze large amounts of data that can provide insights and intelligence for decision making, optimization, and innovation .
- It can reduce costs and energy consumption by enabling remote monitoring and control, automation, and predictive maintenance of IoT devices .

Some of the challenges of IoT are:

- It requires a complex and scalable architecture that can integrate different types of devices, communications, services, management, security, and applications .
- It poses significant security and privacy risks, as IoT devices can be vulnerable to cyberattacks, data breaches, unauthorized access, and misuse of personal information .
- It can raise ethical and social issues, such as the impact of IoT on human dignity, autonomy, rights, and responsibilities, as well as the potential for discrimination, bias, and harm .

# Technology behind IoT

The technology behind IoT refers to the hardware, software, and communication protocols that enable IoT devices to connect, collect, and exchange data over the internet. Some of the key technologies used in IoT systems are:

- **Sensors and actuators**: These are the devices that measure physical phenomena (such as temperature, humidity, motion, sound, etc.) or perform actions (such as turning on/off a light, opening/closing a valve, etc.) in the physical world. Sensors and actuators are the interface between the IoT devices and their environment .
- **Microcontrollers and processors**: These are the chips that provide the computing power and memory for the IoT devices. They run the software that controls the sensors and actuators, processes the data, and communicates with other devices or the cloud. Microcontrollers are typically low-power and low-cost chips that are suitable for simple IoT devices, while processors are more powerful and complex chips that can handle more advanced functions and applications .
- **Communication technologies**: These are the technologies that enable the IoT devices to transmit and receive data over the internet. They can be wired or wireless, depending on the requirements and constraints of the IoT system. Some of the common communication technologies used in IoT are: GSM cellular networks, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Zigbee, LoRaWAN, NB-IoT, 5G, and Ethernet  .
- **Cloud computing**: This is the technology that provides the storage, processing, and analysis of the data collected by the IoT devices. Cloud computing also enables the remote management and control of the IoT devices, as well as the integration of IoT data with other applications and services. Cloud computing offers scalability, reliability, and security for IoT systems  .
- **Edge computing**: This is the technology that enhances the capabilities of the IoT devices by adding more computing power and intelligence at the edge of the network, closer to the data sources. Edge computing reduces the communication latency and bandwidth consumption, improves the response time and performance, and enables more complex and autonomous functions for the IoT devices.

These are some of the main technologies behind IoT, but there are also other technologies that support and complement the IoT system, such as security, data analytics, artificial intelligence, machine learning, blockchain, etc.

### IoT/M2M systems layers and design standardization

- IoT/M2M systems are composed of devices, networks, and applications that communicate and exchange data with each other.
- To enable interoperability and scalability of IoT/M2M systems, standardization is needed at different layers of the system architecture.
- One of the prominent IoT/M2M standardization initiatives is oneM2M, which was launched in 2012 by ETSI and 13 other founding members.
- The oneM2M architecture divides IoT functions into three major domains: the application layer, the service layer, and the network layer  .
- The application layer is responsible for providing the end-user functionality and user interface of the IoT/M2M system. It can include various types of applications, such as smart home, smart city, smart health, smart agriculture, etc.
- The service layer is responsible for providing common services and functions that are shared by different applications and devices, such as data management, security, device management, discovery, subscription, notification, etc. The service layer exposes a set of standardized APIs that can be used by the application layer and the network layer to access the services  .
- The network layer is responsible for providing the connectivity and transport of data between devices and applications. It can include various types of networks, such as cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Zigbee, LoRa, etc. The network layer also supports different protocols, such as CoAP, MQTT, HTTP, etc   .
- The oneM2M standardization aims to achieve interoperability, reusability, and modularity of IoT/M2M systems, as well as to reduce complexity, cost, and time-to-market.
- Other IoT/M2M standardization initiatives include Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC), which focuses on sensors and geospatial information, and ETSI, which focuses on radio layer technologies, such as LTE-M, NB-IoT, and EC-GSM-IoT.



# Communication Technologies for IoT

Communication technologies are essential for enabling the exchange of data and information between IoT devices and the internet. Different communication technologies have different characteristics and requirements, such as range, bandwidth, power consumption, security, and cost. Therefore, choosing the right communication technology for a specific IoT application is a crucial design decision.

Some of the common communication technologies for IoT are:

- **Bluetooth**: A short-range wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices within a range of 10 meters. Bluetooth is widely used for personal area networks (PANs), such as connecting smartphones, headphones, speakers, keyboards, and mice. Bluetooth is also suitable for low-power IoT devices that need to communicate with nearby devices or gateways. Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) is a variant of Bluetooth that consumes less power and supports longer battery life for IoT devices. 

- **Zigbee**: A low-power wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices within a range of 100 meters. Zigbee is mainly used for industrial and home automation applications, such as smart lighting, smart metering, smart security, and smart appliances. Zigbee devices form a mesh network, where each device can relay data to other devices, extending the network coverage and reliability. Zigbee is based on the IEEE 802.15.4 standard and supports various security and encryption features. 

- **Z-Wave**: A low-power wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices within a range of 30 meters. Z-Wave is similar to Zigbee, but operates on a different frequency band and supports fewer devices per network. Z-Wave is mainly used for home automation applications, such as smart locks, smart thermostats, smart sensors, and smart cameras. Z-Wave devices also form a mesh network, where each device can relay data to other devices, extending the network coverage and reliability. Z-Wave is based on the Z-Wave Alliance standard and supports various security and encryption features. 

- **Wi-Fi**: A wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices within a range of 100 meters. Wi-Fi is widely used for local area networks (LANs), such as connecting laptops, tablets, printers, routers, and access points. Wi-Fi is also suitable for high-bandwidth IoT applications that need to communicate with the internet or cloud services, such as video streaming, voice control, and online gaming. Wi-Fi is based on the IEEE 802.11 standard and supports various security and encryption features. Wi-Fi Direct is a variant of Wi-Fi that allows devices to connect directly without a router or access point. 

- **Cellular**: A wireless technology that uses radio waves to connect devices over long distances. Cellular is widely used for wide area networks (WANs), such as connecting mobile phones, tablets, laptops, and cars. Cellular is also suitable for IoT applications that need to communicate with the internet or cloud services over large geographic areas, such as smart agriculture, smart transportation, smart cities, and smart health. ### Wireless Medium Access Issues

- Wireless medium access issues refer to the challenges and problems that arise when multiple wireless devices share the same communication channel and compete for the limited bandwidth and resources.
- Some of the common wireless medium access issues are:
  - Interference: Interference occurs when unwanted signals from other sources or devices interfere with the desired signal and degrade its quality or cause errors. Interference can be caused by various factors, such as noise, multipath fading, co-channel interference, adjacent channel interference, or hidden terminal problem  .
  - Collision: Collision occurs when two or more devices transmit data at the same time on the same channel and cause the data to be corrupted or lost. Collision can result in retransmissions, delays, or energy wastage .
  - Congestion: Congestion occurs when the demand for the channel exceeds its capacity and causes the network performance to deteriorate. Congestion can lead to increased packet loss, delay, jitter, or throughput reduction .
  - Energy consumption: Energy consumption is a critical issue for wireless devices, especially for those that are battery-powered or have limited energy sources, such as IoT devices. Energy consumption can be affected by various factors, such as transmission power, reception power, idle listening, overhearing, control overhead, or retransmissions  .

- To address these issues, various medium access control (MAC) protocols have been proposed and designed for wireless networks, especially for IoT networks. MAC protocols are responsible for coordinating and regulating the access of multiple devices to the shared wireless channel and ensuring efficient and fair data transmission  .
- Some of the common MAC protocols for IoT networks are:
  - Carrier sense multiple access (CSMA): CSMA is a contention-based protocol that allows a device to sense the channel before transmitting data and avoid collision with other devices. CSMA can be further classified into CSMA with collision avoidance (CSMA/CA), CSMA with collision detection (CSMA/CD), or CSMA with reservation (CSMA/R) .
  - Time division multiple access (TDMA): TDMA is a reservation-based protocol that divides the channel into fixed time slots and assigns them to different devices in a round-robin or pre-defined manner. TDMA can avoid collision and interference, but it may introduce synchronization and scheduling overhead .
  - Frequency division multiple access (FDMA): FDMA is a reservation-based protocol that divides the channel into fixed frequency bands and assigns them to different devices according to their needs or priorities. FDMA can avoid collision and interference, but it may introduce frequency allocation and coordination overhead .
  - Code division multiple access (CDMA): CDMA is a spread-spectrum protocol that allows multiple devices to transmit data simultaneously on the same channel using different codes or sequences. CDMA can avoid collision and interference, but it may introduce code assignment and correlation overhead .
  - Cognitive radio (CR): CR is a spectrum-aware protocol that allows devices to dynamically access the unused or underutilized spectrum bands of licensed or primary users without causing harmful interference. CR can improve spectrum efficiency and utilization, but it may introduce spectrum sensing and management overhead .
  - Cooperative communication (CC): CC is a relay-based protocol that allows devices to cooperate with each other and forward data for other devices to improve the network performance and reliability. CC can overcome the effects of fading and interference, but it may introduce cooperation and coordination overhead .



### MAC protocol survey

- MAC (Medium Access Control) protocols are responsible for coordinating the access of multiple devices to a shared wireless medium in IoT (Internet of Things) networks.
- MAC protocols can be classified into two main categories: contention-based and contention-free.
- Contention-based MAC protocols allow devices to compete for the channel access without reservation or scheduling. They are suitable for low traffic and dynamic networks, but they may suffer from collisions, overhead, and unfairness. Examples of contention-based MAC protocols are CSMA/CA, ALOHA, and IEEE 802.11.
- Contention-free MAC protocols allocate the channel access to devices in advance using reservation or scheduling mechanisms. - Short-range MAC protocols are designed for devices that communicate within a few meters, such as Bluetooth, ZigBee, and NFC. They are low-power, low-cost, and low-data-rate protocols that support various applications such as personal area networks, home automation, and smart cards.
- Long-range MAC protocols are designed for devices that communicate over tens or hundreds of meters, such as WiFi, WiMAX, and LoRa. ### Survey routing protocols for IoT

- Routing protocols are responsible for finding and maintaining routes between nodes in a network, especially in low-power and lossy networks (LLNs) that are common in IoT applications.
- Routing protocols for IoT must consider the challenges and requirements of IoT networks, such as heterogeneity, scalability, mobility, energy efficiency, security, and quality of service (QoS).
- There are different types of routing protocols for IoT, depending on the network topology, routing strategy, and objective function. Some of the common types are  :
  - **Flat routing protocols**: These protocols do not use any hierarchy or clustering among the nodes, and treat all nodes equally. They are suitable for small and homogeneous networks, but may suffer from scalability and overhead issues in large and heterogeneous networks. Examples of flat routing protocols are AODV, DSR, and OLSR.
  - **Hierarchical routing protocols**: These protocols divide the network into clusters or groups of nodes, and assign different roles and responsibilities to the nodes based on their cluster membership. They are suitable for large and heterogeneous networks, but may suffer from cluster formation and maintenance issues. Examples of hierarchical routing protocols are LEACH, PEGASIS, and HEED.
  - **Geographic routing protocols**: These protocols use the location information of the nodes to make routing decisions, and do not rely on any topology or network state information. They are suitable for mobile and dynamic networks, but may suffer from location accuracy and privacy issues. Examples of geographic routing protocols are GPSR, GEAR, and GAF.
  - **Data-centric routing protocols**: These protocols use the content or attributes of the data to make routing decisions, and do not rely on any address or identity information of the nodes. They are suitable for query-based and event-driven applications, but may suffer from data redundancy and aggregation issues. Examples of data-centric routing protocols are SPIN, Directed Diffusion, and COUGAR.

RPL is a distance vector protocol that uses a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) as the routing topology, and supports both point-to-point and point-to-multipoint communication .
- RPL has several advantages, such as support for IPv6, multiple objective functions, loop avoidance, and local repair. However, RPL also has several limitations, such as lack of mobility support, security vulnerabilities, and overhead issues .
- Several extensions and modifications have been proposed to improve RPL performance and security, such as MRPL, RPL-PSO, RPL-SEC, and RPL-DSR .
- Other routing protocols that have been proposed or adapted for IoT networks include 6LoWPAN, CoAP, MQTT, XMPP, and AMQP .
