

# CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- Cyber forensic analytics is the application of cybersecurity and analytics tools to identify and mitigate damage from cyberthreats, such as malware, hacking, phishing, ransomware, etc.
- Cyber forensic analytics involves the following key topics:
  - Cyber forensics: the practice of extracting information, analyzing the data and gaining intelligence into activities that involve the use of technology as a structured chain of evidence that can be presented in the court of law.
  - Network defense: the process of protecting a network from unauthorized access, misuse, modification, or denial of service.
  - Applied cyber data analytics: the use of data mining, machine learning, statistical analysis, and visualization techniques to discover patterns, anomalies, and insights from large and complex cyber data sets.
  - Security data visualization: the representation of cyber data in graphical or pictorial forms to enhance human understanding and decision making.
  - Auditing and intrusion detection: the methods of monitoring and verifying the security and integrity of a system or network, and detecting any unauthorized or malicious activities or attempts.
- Cyber forensic analytics requires the use of various tools and technologies, such as:
  - Computer forensic analysis tools: software applications that help detect unknown, malicious threats across devices and networks, and perform tasks such as data extraction, file recovery, malware analysis, encryption cracking, etc.
  - Cloud security tools: software applications that help protect data and applications hosted on cloud platforms from cyberattacks, and provide features such as encryption, authentication, access control, backup, etc.
  - Digital forensics and cyber investigation tools: software applications that help collect, preserve, analyze, and present digital evidence from various sources, such as computers, mobile devices, networks, social media, etc.
- Cyber forensic analytics has various applications and benefits, such as:
  - Enhancing the security and resilience of systems and networks against cyberthreats.
  - Supporting the investigation and prosecution of cybercrimes and cyber incidents.
  - Improving the efficiency and effectiveness of cybersecurity operations and management.
  - Providing valuable insights and intelligence for cybersecurity research and development.



## Unit 1 - Cyber Crime

Cyber crime is any criminal activity that involves a computer, a network, or a device connected to the internet. Cyber crime can affect individuals, organizations, or governments, and can cause various types of harm, such as financial loss, identity theft, privacy breach, data corruption, or physical damage.

Some examples of cyber crime are:

- Hacking: unauthorized access to a computer system or network, often with malicious intent.
- Phishing: sending fraudulent emails or messages that appear to be from legitimate sources, in order to trick recipients into revealing personal or financial information, or clicking on malicious links or attachments.
- Ransomware: a type of malware that encrypts the victim's data and demands a ransom for its decryption.
- Cyberstalking: using electronic means to harass, threaten, or intimidate another person, often with the intention of causing emotional distress or fear.
- Cyberterrorism: using cyber attacks to cause disruption, damage, or violence for political or ideological purposes.

Some of the challenges in combating cyber crime are:

- The global and anonymous nature of the internet, which makes it difficult to trace the origin and identity of cyber criminals, and to enforce laws across different jurisdictions.
- The rapid evolution and sophistication of cyber threats, which require constant vigilance and updating of security measures and technologies.
- The lack of awareness and education among users, who may not follow best practices or take precautions to protect themselves and their devices from cyber attacks.
- The lack of coordination and cooperation among different stakeholders, such as governments, law enforcement agencies, private sector, civil society, and international organizations, who may have different interests, priorities, and capabilities in addressing cyber crime.

Some of the possible solutions or strategies to prevent and respond to cyber crime are:

- Developing and implementing national and international laws, policies, and standards to regulate and deter cyber crime, and to facilitate cooperation and information sharing among relevant actors.
- Enhancing the capacity and capability of law enforcement agencies, prosecutors, judges, and other professionals to investigate, prosecute, and adjudicate cyber crime cases, and to provide assistance and support to victims.
- Promoting a culture of cyber security and resilience among users, by raising awareness, providing education and training, and encouraging the adoption of good practices and behaviors, such as using strong passwords, updating software, and backing up data.
- Investing in research and innovation to develop and deploy effective and affordable security technologies and solutions, and to anticipate and counter emerging and future cyber threats.
- Fostering collaboration and partnership among different stakeholders, such as governments, private sector, civil society, and international organizations, to exchange information, expertise, and resources, and to coordinate actions and responses to cyber crime.

### Jurisdictional Concerns

- Jurisdiction is the authority of a state or a court to apply its laws and regulations to a person, an entity, or an activity.
- Jurisdictional concerns arise in cybercrime cases because cybercrime is non-local in nature, meaning that it can cross geographical and legal boundaries easily and quickly.
- Jurisdictional concerns pose challenges for law enforcement agencies and prosecutors in investigating and prosecuting cybercrime, such as:
  - Determining which agency or court has the authority and responsibility to handle the case, especially when multiple jurisdictions are involved.
  - Obtaining evidence and cooperation from other jurisdictions, which may have different laws, procedures, and standards of proof.
  - Respecting the sovereignty and human rights of other states and individuals, while pursuing the cybercriminals and protecting the victims.
- Jurisdictional concerns can be addressed by applying various principles and criteria to establish jurisdiction, such as:
  - The principle of territoriality, which grants jurisdiction to the state where the cybercrime was committed or where its effects occurred.
  - The principle of nationality, which grants jurisdiction to the state of the nationality of the offender or the victim.
  - The principle of universality, which grants jurisdiction to any state when the cybercrime is considered a grave offense against the international community, such as terrorism or child pornography.
  - The principle of protection, which grants jurisdiction to the state whose interests or security are affected by the cybercrime.
  - The principle of subsidiarity, which grants jurisdiction to the state that is most capable and willing to prosecute the cybercrime effectively and fairly.
- Jurisdictional concerns can also be addressed by enhancing international cooperation and coordination among states and law enforcement agencies, such as:
  - Adopting and implementing international conventions and treaties on cybercrime, such as the Council of Europe Convention on Cybercrime (2001) and the African Union Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection (2014).
  - Establishing and maintaining channels of communication and information exchange, such as the 24/7 Network of Contact Points for Cybercrime, the INTERPOL Global Complex for Innovation, and the Global Action on Cybercrime Extended (GLACY+) project.
  - Developing and applying common standards and best practices for cybercrime investigation and prosecution, such as the UNODC Manual on International Cooperation in Cybercrime Matters (2017) and the UNODC E4J University Module Series on Cybercrime (2019).
  - Providing and requesting mutual legal assistance and extradition in cybercrime cases, in accordance with the applicable laws and agreements of the states involved.



### Jurisprudential Inconsistency

- Jurisprudential inconsistency refers to the lack of uniformity or coherence in the legal system or the legal principles that govern cybercrime and digital forensics investigations.
- Jurisprudential inconsistency can arise from various sources, such as:
  - The non-local and transnational nature of cybercrime, which challenges the traditional notions of territorial jurisdiction and sovereignty .
  - The intangibility and volatility of digital evidence, which complicates the processes of identification, collection, preservation, analysis, and presentation of evidence in court .
  - The diversity and complexity of cyber-attacks and cybercrimes, which require specialized knowledge and skills to investigate and prosecute effectively .
  - The lack of harmonization or standardization of laws, regulations, policies, procedures, and practices among different countries, regions, or jurisdictions that deal with cybercrime and digital forensics  .
- Jurisprudential inconsistency can have negative impacts on the effectiveness and efficiency of cybercrime and digital forensics investigations, such as:
  - Creating legal loopholes or gaps that can be exploited by cybercriminals to evade detection, prosecution, or punishment .
  - Reducing the reliability, validity, or admissibility of digital evidence in court, which can undermine the credibility of the investigation or the outcome of the case .
  - Increasing the costs, risks, or delays of the investigation or the legal proceedings, which can affect the resources, time, or reputation of the investigators, prosecutors, or judges .
  - Hindering the cooperation or coordination among different stakeholders, such as law enforcement agencies, judicial authorities, private sector, or international organizations, which can limit the sharing of information, expertise, or assistance  .
- Jurisprudential inconsistency can be addressed or reduced by various measures, such as:
  - Developing or updating the legal framework or the legal principles that can accommodate the specificities and challenges of cybercrime and digital forensics .
  - Establishing or enhancing the technical capabilities or the technical standards that can ensure the quality and integrity of digital evidence and digital forensics .
  - Promoting or facilitating the education or the training of the relevant personnel or the public on the issues and implications of cybercrime and digital forensics .
  - Strengthening or expanding the collaboration or the communication among different actors or entities that are involved or affected by cybercrime and digital forensics  .

### Prepaid Cards

- Prepaid cards are cards that are loaded with a certain amount of money and can be used for various purposes, such as gift cards, travel cards, payroll cards, etc.
- Prepaid cards are different from regular credit and debit cards issued by banks, as they do not require a bank account, a credit check, or a personal identification number (PIN) to use them.
- Prepaid cards are convenient and flexible, but they also pose a high risk of fraud and money laundering, as they are often anonymous, untraceable, and easily accessible.
- Some of the common types of cyber crimes involving prepaid cards are:

  - **Card cloning**: Hackers use skimming devices or malware to copy the card information and create duplicate cards that can be used to withdraw cash or make purchases.
  - **Card theft**: Hackers steal the physical cards or the card numbers and use them without the owner's knowledge or consent.
  - **Card fraud**: Hackers use stolen or fake payment information to buy prepaid cards and then use them to make illicit transactions or transfer funds to other accounts.
  - **Card laundering**: Hackers use prepaid cards to launder the proceeds of crime or fund terrorist activities, by moving money across borders, mixing funds from different sources, or converting cash into digital currency.

- Some of the ways to prevent or detect prepaid card fraud and money laundering are:

  - **Regulation**: Governments and regulators should impose stricter rules and standards on the issuance, use, and monitoring of prepaid cards, such as requiring customer identification, verification, and due diligence, limiting the amount and frequency of transactions, and reporting suspicious activities.
  - **Education**: Consumers and merchants should be aware of the risks and benefits of prepaid cards, and how to protect themselves from scams and frauds, such as checking the card balance, keeping the card secure, and reporting any loss or theft.
  - **Technology**: Card issuers and payment networks should use advanced technology and security features to prevent and detect prepaid card fraud and money laundering, such as encryption, authentication, biometrics, artificial intelligence, and blockchain.



### Stored Values Cards

- Stored value cards (SVCs) are cards with data encoded in either a magnetic strip or a computer chip that are preloaded with a fixed amount of electronic currency or value.
- SVCs can be classified into three types: closed system, semi-closed system, and open system.
- Closed system cards are issued by a specific merchant or service provider and can only be used within that merchant's network (e.g., gift cards, phone cards, transit cards).
- Semi-closed system cards are issued by a network of merchants or service providers that have a contractual agreement with the card issuer and can only be used within that network (e.g., university cards, loyalty cards, mall cards).
- Open system cards are issued by a financial institution or a payment processor and can be used at any merchant that accepts the card brand (e.g., Visa, MasterCard, American Express) as a form of payment (e.g., prepaid debit cards, travel cards, payroll cards).
- SVCs offer convenience, security, and flexibility to consumers, merchants, and service providers, but they also pose significant money laundering and cybercrime risks .
- The widespread availability of SVCs (particularly at non-financial outlets), the high loading and card balance value limits of open system cards, and the anonymity offered by closed and semi-closed system cards could be abused by organised criminals for illicit financial transactions, money laundering and bulk cash smuggling, particularly as value limits increase.
- Stored value cards have been identified in several reports as a potential tool for organised crime groups to launder their illicit crime proceeds.
- A study on cross-border electronic funds transfer systems raises similar concerns: "The use of stored value cards to move funds across borders is a relatively new phenomenon, but one that is likely to grow in the future. Stored value cards are attractive to money launderers because they are easy to obtain, easy to use, and can be loaded with large amounts of value. They also offer a high degree of anonymity, as they do not require identification or verification of the cardholder or the source of funds. Furthermore, they are difficult to trace, as they do not generate a paper trail or leave an audit trail in the financial system."
- Cybercriminals can also exploit SVCs to commit fraud, theft, and identity theft by compromising legitimate users' account credentials, counterfeiting cards, or hacking into card issuers' or payment processors' systems.
- Fraudulent monetary transfers and counterfeiting of stored value cards are the most common result of exploits against financial institutions, payment processors, and merchants.
- Cybersecurity measures are essential to protect SVCs from cyberattacks and to prevent their misuse for money laundering and other criminal activities.
- Electronic Crimes Task Forces (ECTFs) are operated by the U.S. Secret Service (USSS) out of their field offices to assist local law enforcement in investigating computer crimes, including those involving SVCs.
- Regulatory measures are also needed to address the money laundering risks of SVCs, such as imposing customer identification and verification requirements, transaction and balance limits, record-keeping and reporting obligations, and anti-money laundering compliance programs on card issuers and payment processors .

### Mobile Payments and Cyber Crime

Mobile payments offer convenience, speed, and flexibility for consumers and merchants, but they also pose significant security challenges and risks. Mobile devices are vulnerable to various types of cyber attacks, such as malware, spyware, data breaches, or SIM swapping. Cyber criminals can use these attacks to steal sensitive information, such as bank account details, credit card numbers, passwords, or personal identification numbers (PINs), and use them to conduct fraudulent transactions, access online accounts, or impersonate the victims.

Some of the factors that increase the risk of mobile payments and cyber crime are:

- The lack of standardization and regulation of mobile payment systems and providers, which may result in inconsistent or inadequate security measures, policies, or protocols.
- The low awareness and education of consumers and merchants about the potential threats and best practices of mobile payment security, such as using strong passwords, enabling biometric authentication, or updating software and apps regularly.
- The rapid growth and innovation of mobile payment technologies and services, which may outpace the development and implementation of security solutions and defenses.
- The high volume and value of mobile payment transactions, which may attract more cyber criminals and create more opportunities for fraud and theft.

### How to Protect Yourself from Mobile Payments and Cyber Crime

There are some steps that consumers and merchants can take to protect themselves from mobile payments and cyber crime, such as:

- Choosing reputable and trusted mobile payment providers and platforms, and verifying their security features and certifications.
- Using secure and encrypted connections and networks when making or accepting mobile payments, and avoiding public or unsecured Wi-Fi or Bluetooth.
- Reviewing and monitoring mobile payment transactions and statements regularly, and reporting any suspicious or unauthorized activity to the mobile payment provider and the bank or card issuer.
- Installing and updating anti-virus, anti-malware, and firewall software and apps on mobile devices, and scanning them for any potential threats or infections.
- Locking and securing mobile devices with passwords, PINs, or biometric authentication, and enabling remote wipe or lock features in case of loss or theft.
- Being cautious and vigilant of phishing emails, texts, or calls that ask for personal or financial information, or request to download or click on links or attachments.



### Internet Payment Services

- Internet payment services are methods of transferring money online using electronic devices such as computers, smartphones, or tablets.
- Internet payment services can be used for various purposes, such as online shopping, bill payment, peer-to-peer transfers, donations, subscriptions, etc.
- Internet payment services offer convenience, speed, security, and flexibility to users, as they can pay from anywhere, anytime, using different payment options and currencies.
- Internet payment services can be classified into different types, such as:

  - **Card-based services**: These services allow users to pay using credit, debit, or prepaid cards issued by banks or other financial institutions. Examples of card-based services are Visa, Mastercard, American Express, etc.
  - **Bank-based services**: These services allow users to pay directly from their bank accounts using online banking, electronic funds transfer (EFT), or automated clearing house (ACH) systems. Examples of bank-based services are NEFT, ECS/NECS, IMPS, etc.
  - **Wallet-based services**: These services allow users to store money in a digital wallet, which can be linked to a bank account, card, or other payment methods. Users can pay using their wallet balance or by scanning a QR code. Examples of wallet-based services are PayPal, Amazon Pay, Google Pay, etc.
  - **Platform-based services**: These services allow users to pay using a specific online platform, such as an e-commerce site, a social media network, or a messaging app. Users can pay using their platform account or by entering their payment details. Examples of platform-based services are Stripe, Braintree, Shopify, Facebook Pay, etc.

- Internet payment services are regulated by various laws and standards, such as the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), the Payment Services Directive (PSD2), the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the Consumer Protection Act, etc.
- Internet payment services are also vulnerable to various cyber threats, such as phishing, hacking, identity theft, fraud, malware, etc. Users and providers of internet payment services should take preventive measures, such as using strong passwords, encryption, authentication, firewalls, antivirus, etc.



### Cyber stalking

- Cyber stalking is the repeated use of electronic communications to harass or frighten someone, for example by sending threatening emails.
- Cyber stalking can take many forms, including: harassment, embarrassment, humiliation, defamation, slander, libel, monitoring, identity theft, threats, vandalism, solicitation for sex, doxing, or blackmail.
- Cyber stalking is a criminal offense under various state anti-stalking, slander and harassment laws. A conviction can result in a restraining order, probation, or criminal penalties against the assailant, including jail.
- Cyber stalking is different from cyberbullying, which is the use of electronic communication to harm or intimidate someone, usually among minors. Cyberbullying is more common and less severe than cyberstalking, but can still have negative effects on the victim's mental health and well-being.
- Cyber stalking can have serious consequences for the victim, such as fear, anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, isolation, loss of trust, and reduced quality of life.
- Cyber stalking can be prevented or stopped by taking some measures, such as: blocking or reporting the stalker, changing passwords and privacy settings, saving evidence of the harassment, informing friends and family, contacting the authorities, and seeking professional help if needed .



### Cyber extortion

Cyber extortion is a type of cyber crime that involves an attack or threat of an attack on a computer system, network, or data, coupled with a demand for money or some other benefit in return for stopping or remedying the attack. Cyber extortion can take various forms, such as:

- **Ransomware**: A malicious software that encrypts the victim's files or locks the victim's device, and then demands a ransom for the decryption key or the unlock code .
- **Distributed denial-of-service (DDoS)**: A large-scale attack that overwhelms the victim's website or server with a flood of traffic, and then demands a payment for stopping the attack .
- **Data breach**: A theft or exposure of the victim's sensitive or confidential data, such as personal information, financial records, or trade secrets, and then demands a payment for not disclosing, deleting, or destroying the data .
- **Doxxing**: A publication or threat of publication of the victim's personal or private information, such as identity, address, phone number, or email, and then demands a payment for not revealing or removing the information.

Cyber extortion is a serious threat to individuals, businesses, and organizations, as it can cause financial losses, reputational damage, operational disruption, legal liability, and emotional distress. Cyber extortionists may target any vulnerable system or data, and may use various methods to launch their attacks, such as phishing, malware, brute force, or social engineering. Cyber extortionists may also use anonymous or encrypted communication channels, such as email, instant messaging, or cryptocurrency, to avoid detection and prosecution .

Some of the best practices to prevent or mitigate cyber extortion are:

- Implementing strong security measures, such as firewalls, antivirus software, encryption, backup, and authentication, to protect the system and data from unauthorized access or modification .
- Educating and training the users and employees on how to recognize and avoid potential cyber threats, such as suspicious links, attachments, or requests, and how to report and respond to any incidents .
- Developing and testing a contingency plan, such as an incident response team, a recovery strategy, and a communication protocol, to minimize the impact and restore the normal operations in case of an attack .
- Cooperating and coordinating with the law enforcement authorities, such as the FBI, to report and investigate any cyber extortion attempts, and to seek legal advice and assistance .
- Refraining from paying the ransom or complying with the demand, as it may not guarantee the resolution of the problem, and may encourage further attacks or demands .



### Cyber terrorism

- Cyber terrorism means the **politically motivated use of computers and information technology to cause severe disruption or widespread fear in society**.
- Cyber terrorism can target **vital computer systems** that control infrastructure, communication, transportation, finance, or security.
- Cyber terrorism can result in, or threaten, the **loss of life or significant bodily harm**, as well as **political or ideological gains** through threat or intimidation.
- Cyber terrorism can be carried out by **subnational groups or clandestine agents** who have the skills, resources, and motives to conduct cyber attacks.
- Cyber terrorism can pose a serious threat to **national security, public safety, and social stability**.



### Cyber warfare

Cyber warfare is the use of computer technology to disrupt the activities of a state or organization, especially the deliberate attacking of information systems for strategic or military purposes  . Cyber warfare can cause comparable harm to actual warfare and/or disrupt vital computer systems. Some intended outcomes of cyber warfare could be espionage, sabotage, propaganda, manipulation or economic warfare.

Some key points to note about cyber warfare are:

- Cyber warfare is usually waged against government and military networks in order to disrupt, destroy, or deny their use.
- Cyber warfare can also target civilian infrastructure, such as power grids, water supply, transportation, or communication systems .
- Cyber warfare can involve a variety of actors, such as nation-states, international organizations, terrorist groups, hackers, or cybercriminals  .
- Cyber warfare can employ a range of techniques, such as computer viruses, denial-of-service attacks, phishing, ransomware, malware, botnets, or cyber espionage  .
- Cyber warfare can pose significant challenges for international law, ethics, and security, as it can blur the boundaries between war and peace, offense and defense, and state and non-state actors  .



### Cyber weapons

- Cyberwarfare is the use of cyberattacks to damage, disrupt, or destroy another nation's computers or information networks.

- Cyber weapons can be classified into two types: **kinetic** and **non-kinetic**.

- Cyber weapons can be delivered through various means, such as email attachments, phishing links, removable media, or network connections.
- Cyber weapons can have different objectives, such as espionage, sabotage, propaganda, or coercion.
- Cyber weapons can have different effects, such as denial of service, data breach, data manipulation, data destruction, or system hijacking.
- Cyber weapons can have different targets, such as individuals, organizations, governments, or critical infrastructures.
- Cyber weapons can have different sources, such as state actors, non-state actors, or cyber mercenaries.



### ATM frauds

- ATM frauds are cyber crimes that involve stealing or misusing the debit card number, personal identification number (PIN), or other data of the ATM users for illegal purposes .
- ATM frauds can be classified into two main types: skimming and card trapping.
- Skimming is the process of installing a counterfeit device on the ATM or the card reader that can capture the data on the card's magnetic strip and the user's PIN  . The device can be a hidden camera, a keypad overlay, or a wireless transmitter . The criminals can then use the stolen data to clone the card or make online transactions .
- Card trapping is the process of inserting a device into the card slot that can prevent the card from being ejected after the transaction. The device can be a thin metal strip, a spring-loaded trap, or a plastic sleeve. The criminals can then retrieve the card when the user leaves the ATM and use it with the PIN they have observed or recorded.
- ATM frauds can have serious consequences for the victims, such as financial losses, identity theft, or compromised accounts .
- ATM frauds can also target cryptocurrency transactions, such as Bitcoin ATMs, which are convenient and anonymous but also vulnerable to hacking, phishing, or money laundering schemes.
- ATM frauds can be prevented by taking some precautions, such as:
  - Checking the ATM for any signs of tampering or suspicious devices before using it  .
  - Covering the keypad when entering the PIN and avoiding using the ATM if someone is watching or offering help  .
  - Using ATMs that are located in well-lit and secure areas and avoiding using ATMs that are standalone or unfamiliar  .
  - Reviewing the bank statements and reporting any unauthorized or suspicious transactions to the bank or the authorities  .
  - Using secure and updated devices and software when making online transactions and avoiding clicking on links or attachments from unknown sources   .



### Phreaking

- Phreaking is a slang term coined to describe the activity of a culture of people who study, experiment with, or explore telecommunication systems, such as equipment and systems connected to public telephone networks.
- Phreaking involves hacking into secure telecommunication networks by mimicking dialing tones to trigger the automatic switches using whistles or custom devices called blue boxes .
- Phreaking originated in the 1960s and 1970s, when phone companies used in-band signaling, which transmitted routing information along the same path as the voice signal. Phreakers exploited this system by generating the same tones used by the phone companies to route long distance calls and thus make free calls around the world .

### Disk Characteristics

- A disk is a physical device that stores data on one or more platters, which are circular disks coated with a magnetic material.

  - The number and size of platters: more platters and larger platters can store more data, but may also increase the weight and power consumption of the disk.

  - Hybrid disk: a disk that combines the features of HDDs and SSDs, such as a HDD with a SSD cache or a SSD with a HDD backup. Hybrid disks can offer a balance between cost, capacity, and performance, but they may also have compatibility and reliability issues.

### Port scans

- A port scan is a technique used by cybercriminals to find out information about a system they are going to target .
- It involves scanning through a network system and determining which ports are open, which are sending data, and which are receiving data .
- A port is a logical endpoint of communication in a network, identified by a number from 0 to 65535.
- A port scan can reveal whether active security devices like firewalls are being used by an organization, and what services or applications are running on the system .
- Port scanning is a fundamental part of the pre-attack phase of a penetration test, as it can help locate vulnerabilities in a network that malicious hackers can exploit.
- There are several different port scanning techniques, including :
  - Ping scans: A ping is used to check whether a network data packet can reach an IP address without any issues. Ping scans can identify which hosts are alive on a network.
  - Half-open or SYN scans: Attackers can check the state of a port without creating a full connection, by sending a SYN packet and waiting for a response. If the response is a SYN-ACK packet, the port is open. If the response is a RST packet, the port is closed.
  - Full connection or TCP scans: Attackers can establish a full connection with a port by sending a SYN packet, receiving a SYN-ACK packet, and sending an ACK packet. This can confirm the port state and the service running on it, but it can also be detected by firewalls and intrusion detection systems.
  - Stealth or FIN scans: Attackers can avoid detection by sending a FIN packet to a port, which normally indicates the end of a connection. If the port is closed, it will send a RST packet. If the port is open, it will ignore the packet. This can bypass some firewalls and IDS.
  - UDP scans: Attackers can scan for UDP ports, which are used for connectionless protocols. They can send a UDP packet to a port and wait for a response. If the response is an ICMP port unreachable message, the port is closed. If there is no response, the port is open or filtered by a firewall.
  - Service or version scans: Attackers can scan for the specific service or application running on a port, by sending a probe packet and analyzing the response. This can reveal the name, version, and configuration of the service, which can help identify potential vulnerabilities.
- Port scanning can be detected and prevented by using various methods, such as :
  - Firewalls: Firewalls can block or filter incoming and outgoing traffic based on rules and policies. They can also log and alert on suspicious activity, such as multiple connection attempts from the same source.
  - Intrusion detection systems (IDS): IDS can monitor and analyze network traffic for signs of malicious activity, such as port scans. They can also alert or respond to potential attacks, such as blocking the source IP address or sending a fake response.
  - Honeypots: Honeypots are decoy systems that are designed to attract and trap attackers. They can simulate real services and ports, and record the attackers' actions and techniques. They can also divert the attackers' attention from the real systems and ports.
  - Port knocking: Port knocking is a technique that involves hiding a port until a specific sequence of packets is sent to other ports. This can prevent unauthorized access to the port, as only the legitimate users know the secret sequence.

The analyzer uses various techniques, such as static and dynamic analysis, reverse engineering, data mining, and machine learning, to extract useful information from the botnet evidence, such as botnet architecture, protocol, behavior, and origin .
- Botnet forensics is a challenging and complex task, as botnets are constantly evolving and adopting new techniques to evade detection and analysis. Some of the challenges faced by botnet forensics are  :
  - Encryption and obfuscation: Botnets use encryption and obfuscation techniques to hide their communication and code from network security tools and forensic analyzers. For example, some botnets use SSL/TLS, XOR, or custom encryption algorithms to encrypt their C&C traffic, while others use packing, polymorphism, or metamorphism to obfuscate their malware code .
  - Peer-to-peer (P2P) and decentralized architectures: Botnets use P2P and decentralized architectures to avoid relying on a single point of failure or detection, such as a central C&C server. In P2P and decentralized botnets, the bots communicate with each other directly or through a subset of bots (super nodes or proxies) to receive commands and updates from the botmaster. This makes it difficult for botnet forensics to identify and isolate the C&C servers and the botmaster  .
  - Fast flux and domain generation algorithms (DGAs): Botnets use fast flux and DGAs to dynamically change their C&C domains and IP addresses, making it hard for botnet forensics to track and block their C&C traffic. Fast flux is a technique that uses a network of compromised machines (flux agents) to act as proxies for the C&C servers, while DGAs are algorithms that generate a large number of pseudo-random domains for the C&C servers at regular intervals  .
  - Low-and-slow and stealthy attacks: Botnets use low-and-slow and stealthy attacks to avoid raising suspicion and triggering alarms from network security tools and forensic analyzers. For example, some botnets use low bandwidth and infrequent C&C communication, while others use legitimate protocols and services, such as HTTP, DNS, or social media, to disguise their C&C traffic as normal traffic  .
- Botnet forensics is an important and emerging field of cyber forensics, as botnets pose a serious threat to the security and privacy of the Internet and its users. Botnet forensics can help to identify, analyze, and mitigate botnet attacks, as well as to provide evidence for legal prosecution and attribution of the botnet crimes   .



### DoS for the notes of the Unit 2 - Cyber Forensics

- DoS stands for Denial-of-Service, which is a type of cyberattack that aims to prevent legitimate users from accessing a computer system or network by overwhelming it with malicious traffic or requests.
- DoS attacks can cause serious damage to the availability, integrity, and confidentiality of the targeted system or network, as well as financial losses, reputation damage, and legal consequences for the victims.
- DoS attacks can be classified into two main categories: volumetric and application-layer. Volumetric attacks flood the network bandwidth or resources of the target with a large amount of packets or connections, while application-layer attacks exploit vulnerabilities or weaknesses in the application protocols or software of the target.
- Some common types of DoS attacks are:
  - SYN flood: The attacker sends a large number of TCP SYN packets to the target, initiating a connection request, but never completing the handshake. This causes the target to allocate resources for each connection request, eventually exhausting its memory or processing capacity.
  - UDP flood: The attacker sends a large number of UDP packets to random ports on the target, causing the target to generate ICMP error messages for each packet, consuming its network bandwidth and CPU resources.
  - ICMP flood: The attacker sends a large number of ICMP echo request packets (also known as ping) to the target, causing the target to reply with ICMP echo reply packets, consuming its network bandwidth and CPU resources.
  - HTTP flood: The attacker sends a large number of HTTP requests to the target, either legitimate or malformed, causing the target to process each request, exhausting its web server resources or application logic.
  - Slowloris: The attacker sends a large number of HTTP requests to the target, but keeps the connection open by sending partial or incomplete headers, preventing the target from closing the connection or serving other requests.
- Cyber forensics is the process of collecting, analyzing, and presenting digital evidence related to cybercrimes or incidents. Cyber forensics can help to identify the source, method, and impact of a DoS attack, as well as to provide legal proof for prosecution or mitigation.
- Cyber forensics involves four main steps: preservation, acquisition, analysis, and presentation. Preservation is the process of securing the digital evidence from tampering or alteration. Acquisition is the process of extracting the digital evidence from the source device or network. Analysis is the process of examining the digital evidence using various tools and techniques to identify relevant information or patterns. Presentation is the process of reporting the findings and conclusions of the analysis in a clear and concise manner.
- Some of the challenges or issues in cyber forensics for DoS attacks are:
  - The large volume and variety of data involved in a DoS attack, which can make the acquisition and analysis of digital evidence difficult and time-consuming.
  - The dynamic and distributed nature of a DoS attack, which can involve multiple sources, intermediaries, or targets, as well as changing tactics or techniques, making the attribution and tracing of the attacker challenging and complex.
  - The legal and ethical aspects of cyber forensics, which can vary depending on the jurisdiction, legislation, or regulation of the involved parties, as well as the privacy and human rights of the affected individuals or organizations.



### DDoS Attacks

- A DDoS attack is a **distributed denial-of-service** attack that aims to disrupt the normal functioning of a target server, service, or network by overwhelming it with a large amount of traffic from multiple sources   .
- A DDoS attack can be motivated by various reasons, such as cybercrime, hacktivism, cyberwarfare, or personal vendetta .
- A DDoS attack can have different types and techniques, depending on the layer of the network protocol stack that is targeted, the type of traffic that is generated, and the source of the attack traffic   .
- Some common types of DDoS attacks are:
  - **SYN flood**: This attack exploits the TCP handshake process by sending a large number of SYN packets to the target server, without completing the connection. This causes the server to allocate resources for half-open connections, eventually exhausting its memory and bandwidth  .
  - **UDP flood**: This attack sends a large number of UDP packets to random ports on the target server, causing it to respond with ICMP packets indicating that the port is unreachable. This consumes the server's network resources and bandwidth  .
  - **HTTP flood**: This attack sends a large number of HTTP requests to the target web server, simulating legitimate users. This consumes the server's CPU and memory resources, as well as its application layer resources  .
  - **DNS amplification**: This attack exploits the DNS protocol by sending spoofed DNS queries to open DNS resolvers, with the source IP address set to the target server. The DNS resolvers then send large DNS responses to the target server, amplifying the attack traffic by a factor of 10 to 100  .
  - **NTP amplification**: This attack exploits the NTP protocol by sending spoofed NTP requests to open NTP servers, with the source IP address set to the target server. The NTP servers then send large NTP responses to the target server, amplifying the attack traffic by a factor of 200 to 1000  .
- Some common techniques to mitigate DDoS attacks are:
  - **Firewalls**: These devices can filter out malicious traffic based on predefined rules, such as blocking traffic from certain IP addresses, ports, or protocols  .
  - **Load balancers**: These devices can distribute the incoming traffic among multiple servers, reducing the load on each server and increasing the availability of the service  .
  - **Intrusion detection and prevention systems (IDS/IPS)**: These devices can monitor the network traffic and detect anomalous patterns, such as a sudden spike in traffic or a large number of packets from a single source. They can also take actions to block or redirect the malicious traffic  .
  - **DDoS protection services**: These services can provide cloud-based solutions to detect and mitigate DDoS attacks, by using techniques such as traffic scrubbing, traffic shaping, and content delivery networks (CDN)   .



### Honey Pots

- A **honeypot** is a **cybersecurity mechanism** that uses a **manufactured attack target** to lure cybercriminals away from legitimate targets, and also gathers intelligence about the identity, methods and motivations of adversaries .
- A honeypot can be modeled after any digital asset, including software applications, servers or the network itself .
- Honeypots are typically configured to look like attractive targets to attackers—such as servers running vulnerable versions of operating systems or databases with easy passwords.
- When attackers exploit the honeypot, their activities are monitored and recorded.
- Honeypots can be used to **attract, detect, and thereby deflect** cybercriminals from hacking into legitimate targets.
- Honeypots can also serve **forensic purposes**, quietly gathering data about the hackers who stole it.
- Honeypots can be classified into two types: **research honeypots** and **production honeypots**.
  - Research honeypots are used to study the behavior and techniques of cyber attackers, and to collect information that can help improve security measures.
  - Production honeypots are used to protect real systems and networks by diverting attackers' attention and resources away from them.
- Honeypots can also be categorized based on their level of interaction: **low-interaction honeypots**, **medium-interaction honeypots**, and **high-interaction honeypots**.
  - Low-interaction honeypots are simple and easy to deploy, but they only simulate a limited set of services and functionalities, and can be easily detected by sophisticated attackers.
  - Medium-interaction honeypots are more complex and realistic, but they still do not provide full access to the underlying system or network, and can be compromised by skilled attackers.
  - High-interaction honeypots are the most advanced and authentic, but they also require the most resources and maintenance, and can pose a high risk of damage or data leakage if breached by attackers.
- Honeypots should not serve as the centerpiece of a cybersecurity strategy, but they should be considered a crucial component of one.
- Honeypots can provide valuable benefits, such as:
  - Detecting and preventing cyberattacks before they cause harm to real systems and networks .
  - Collecting and analyzing data about the attackers' identity, methods, tools, and objectives  .
  - Improving the security awareness and preparedness of organizations and individuals .
  - Testing and evaluating new security solutions and policies.
- Honeypots can also pose some challenges, such as:
  - Requiring careful design, deployment, and management to avoid detection, compromise, or misuse by attackers .
  - Involving legal and ethical issues, such as privacy, consent, and liability.
  - Consuming significant resources and time, especially for high-interaction honeypots.
  - Creating a false sense of security or complacency, if not complemented by other security measures.



### Malware

Malware is a term that refers to any software or code that is intentionally harmful to a computer, network or server. Malware is developed by cybercriminals to steal data, damage or destroy devices and systems, or extort money from victims. Malware can take many forms, including:

- **Virus**: A program that infects other software and replicates itself, spreading from one computer to another. A virus needs a host file to execute its code and can cause various types of damage, such as deleting or modifying files, displaying unwanted messages, or corrupting the system .
- **Worm**: A program that replicates itself and spreads over a network, without the need for a host file. A worm can consume network bandwidth, create backdoors for hackers, or deliver other malware to infected devices .
- **Trojan**: A program that appears to be legitimate but contains hidden malicious functionality. A trojan can create a remote access for hackers, steal sensitive information, download additional malware, or perform other harmful actions .
- **Ransomware**: A program that encrypts the victim's files or locks the device and demands a ransom for the decryption key or the unlock code. Ransomware can cause data loss, financial losses, or operational disruptions .
- **Spyware**: A program that monitors the victim's online activities, keystrokes, or personal information and sends it to a third party. Spyware can compromise the victim's privacy, identity, or security .
- **Adware**: A program that displays unwanted or intrusive advertisements on the victim's device or browser. Adware can slow down the device, consume network resources, or redirect the victim to malicious websites .
- **Rootkit**: A program that hides itself or other malware from detection or removal by antivirus software or system tools. A rootkit can grant hackers full control over the infected device or system, or enable other malicious activities .

Malware can be delivered through various methods, such as email attachments, phishing links, removable media, software downloads, or network vulnerabilities. Malware can be prevented by using antivirus software, firewall, encryption, backup, and other security measures, as well as by following best practices such as avoiding suspicious links or attachments, updating the system and software regularly, and using strong passwords  .



### Virus and Worms

- Virus and worms are types of **malware**, which is a general term for software designed to do harm.
- Virus and worms can infect computers and networks by exploiting vulnerabilities in the security software, stealing sensitive information, installing backdoors, corrupting files, and doing other kinds of harm .
- Virus and worms differ in the way they replicate and spread. A virus needs a host program to attach itself to and execute. A worm can operate independently and propagate through the network without user intervention.
- Virus and worms can cause significant damage to the computer systems and networks, and can also disrupt the normal functioning of the users and organizations. Therefore, they are a major concern for cyber forensics .
- Cyber forensics is the process of collecting, preserving, analyzing, and presenting digital evidence related to cyber crimes or incidents .
- Cyber forensics can help in identifying the source, nature, and impact of virus and worms, and also provide legal evidence for prosecution or mitigation .
- Cyber forensics involves various sub-branches, such as network forensics, database forensics, and malware forensics. Malware forensics deals with the investigation and identification of malicious codes such as virus and worms .
- Malware forensics can use various tools and techniques, such as static and dynamic analysis, reverse engineering, signature-based and behavior-based detection, sandboxing, and memory forensics, to examine and understand the virus and worms.
- Malware forensics can also help in developing countermeasures and prevention strategies to protect the computer systems and networks from future attacks by virus and worms.

  - Remediate the web attack, by fixing the vulnerabilities, removing the malicious code or files, restoring the web application, and improving the web security.

- **Syntactic features**: These are features related to the structure and organization of sentences in a text, such as sentence length, sentence complexity, punctuation, part-of-speech tags, syntactic patterns, etc. Syntactic features can capture the grammatical and stylistic skills of an author and their level of formality and complexity.
- **Content-based features**: These are features related to the topic, theme, or genre of a text, such as keywords, named entities, semantic categories, sentiment, etc. Content-based features can capture the interests and opinions of an author and their level of expertise and familiarity with the subject matter.
- **Idiosyncratic features**: These are features related to the individual and unique characteristics of an author, such as spelling errors, typos, abbreviations, emoticons, slang, etc. Idiosyncratic features can capture the personality and attitude of an author and their level of care and attention to detail.

Stylometric techniques can be applied to texts using various methods, such as statistical analysis, machine learning, natural language processing, etc. Some of the methods are:

- **Parameter minimization**: This is a method that reduces the number of features to a minimum set that can distinguish between different authors. It can help to reduce the computational complexity and noise in the data and improve the accuracy and efficiency of the analysis.
- **Writeprints**: This is a method that creates a fingerprint of an author based on a large and diverse set of features. It can help to capture the subtle and fine-grained differences between authors and improve the robustness and reliability of the analysis.
- **RoBERTa**: This is a method that uses a pre-trained neural network model to encode the semantic and syntactic information of a text into a vector representation. It can help to leverage the state-of-the-art natural language processing techniques and improve the performance and scalability of the analysis.



### Warranted searches

- A warranted search is a search authorized by a court order based on probable cause that a computer or electronic device contains or is related to criminal activity .
- A warranted search may involve the seizure, examination, and analysis of digital evidence, also known as computer forensics.
- A warranted search must comply with the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure and the Fourth Amendment, which protect the privacy and security of individuals from unreasonable searches and seizures  .
- A warranted search may be challenged in court if it violates the constitutional rights of the owner or user of the device, or if it exceeds the scope or duration of the warrant .
- A warranted search may require different levels of suspicion depending on the type and extent of the search, such as manual or forensic, cursory or non-cursory, routine or non-routine.
- A warranted search may be subject to exceptions or limitations, such as the border search exception, the exigent circumstances exception, the consent exception, or the plain view doctrine .



### Warrantless searches for the notes of the Unit 3 - Cyber Investigation in the subject of CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- Warrantless searches are searches conducted by law enforcement without obtaining a warrant from a judge based on probable cause.
- Warrantless searches are generally prohibited by the Fourth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution, which protects the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures.
- However, there are some exceptions to the warrant requirement, such as consent, exigent circumstances, plain view, search incident to arrest, and border search.
- The border search exception allows the government to conduct routine inspections and searches of individuals entering at the U.S. border without a warrant or any individualized suspicion of criminal activity, based on the sovereign interest in protecting the nation's borders and regulating the flow of goods and people.
- The border search exception applies to searches of electronic devices such as computers and cell phones, which may contain digital evidence of cybercrimes or other offenses. However, courts have disagreed over whether more intrusive, forensic examinations of such devices require heightened suspicion of criminal activity, such as reasonable suspicion or probable cause.
- The Supreme Court has not addressed whether the routine border search exception extends to searches of electronic devices, but in 2014 the Court held that the police generally could not conduct a warrantless search of a cell phone seized during an arrest, even though the warrant requirement usually does not apply to searches incident to a lawful arrest. The Court noted that cell phones contain immense storage capacity and carry a broader range of private information than what would typically be found inside a traveler's briefcase or automobile, and thus implicate greater privacy concerns.
- Lower courts have considered whether the Supreme Court's reasoning in Riley v. California applies to searches of electronic devices at the border. Some courts have held that manually inspecting the contents of a device at the border is permissible given the government's broad authority at the border, but that forensic searches of a device require reasonable suspicion of a crime. Other courts have held that the Fourth Amendment requires no suspicion of criminal activity for intrusive border searches of electronic devices or any other type of personal property.
- In 2021, the First Circuit overturned a district court's ruling that any non-cursory border search of an electronic device requires reasonable suspicion that the device contains contraband. The First Circuit held that the border search exception applies to both manual and forensic searches of electronic devices, and that such searches are reasonable as long as they are not excessively intrusive or destructive. The court distinguished Riley from the border context, and noted that travelers have diminished privacy expectations at the border.
- The issue of warrantless searches of electronic devices at the border remains unsettled and may require further clarification from the Supreme Court or Congress. The Office of the Director of National Intelligence reported that the FBI conducted as many as 3.4 million warrantless searches of Americans' data in 2021, including 1.9 million searches related to a Russian cyberattack. Such searches may raise significant privacy and civil liberties concerns, as well as legal challenges.



### Undercover Techniques

Undercover techniques are methods of conducting cybercrime investigations by adopting fake online identities or personas to interact with suspects, gather evidence, or infiltrate criminal networks. Undercover techniques can be used to combat various types of cybercrime, such as online fraud, identity theft, cyberstalking, child exploitation, hacking, and terrorism. Some of the advantages of undercover techniques are:

- They can allow investigators to access information that is not publicly available or easily accessible through other means.
- They can help investigators to establish trust and rapport with suspects, and elicit confessions or admissions of guilt.
- They can help investigators to identify and locate other co-conspirators, victims, or witnesses of cybercrime.
- They can help investigators to prevent or disrupt ongoing or planned cybercrime activities.

Some of the challenges or risks of undercover techniques are:

- They can expose investigators to legal and ethical issues, such as entrapment, privacy, consent, and disclosure of identity.

- They should be conducted by trained and experienced investigators, who can maintain their cover, protect their identity, and document their activities.
- They should be supported by adequate technical and legal resources, such as encryption, anonymization, backup, and warrants.
- They should be evaluated and reviewed periodically, to assess their progress, effectiveness, and risks.

  - Direct evidence is the evidence that directly proves a fact or event, such as a video recording of a crime, a confession email, etc.

### Types of Evidence in Cyber Forensics

Cyber forensics is the process of collecting, preserving, analyzing and presenting digital evidence from electronic devices, networks and online platforms. Cyber forensics can be used to investigate various types of crimes, such as cyberattacks, fraud, identity theft, child pornography, terrorism and espionage.

There are different types of evidence that can be obtained from digital sources, depending on the nature and scope of the investigation. Some of the common types of evidence in cyber forensics are:

- **Logs**: These are records of events or activities that occur on a system, network or application. Logs can provide information about the actions, identities, locations and timestamps of the users, devices and processes involved in a digital incident. Examples of logs are operating system logs, web server logs, firewall logs, application logs, network logs and audit logs .
- **Video footage and images**: These are visual data that can capture the appearance, behavior and context of the persons, objects and scenes related to a digital incident. Video footage and images can be obtained from various sources, such as surveillance cameras, webcams, mobile phones, social media platforms and digital storage devices.
- **Archives**: These are collections of files or data that are compressed, encrypted or organized in a specific format. Archives can contain valuable information that is hidden, protected or preserved from unauthorized access or modification. Examples of archives are zip files, rar files, iso files, backup files and encrypted containers.
- **Active data**: These are files or data that are readily accessible and visible on a system, network or application. Active data can provide information about the current state, configuration and usage of the digital sources. Examples of active data are documents, emails, messages, photos, videos, databases, browser history, bookmarks and cookies .
- **Metadata**: These are data that describe or provide additional information about other data. Metadata can provide information about the origin, authorship, format, modification and properties of the files or data. Examples of metadata are file names, extensions, sizes, dates, hashes, EXIF data, geolocation data and digital signatures .
- **Residual data**: These are files or data that are deleted, overwritten or partially erased from a system, network or application. Residual data can provide information about the past state, history and usage of the digital sources. Examples of residual data are recycle bin, trash, temporary files, swap files, slack space, unallocated space and file fragments .
- **Volatile data**: These are files or data that are stored in the memory or cache of a system, network or application. Volatile data can provide information about the current activities, processes and connections of the digital sources. Examples of volatile data are RAM, CPU registers, network packets, clipboard, cache and DNS records .
- **Replicant data**: These are files or data that are copied, duplicated or replicated from one digital source to another. Replicant data can provide information about the transfer, distribution and dissemination of the files or data. Examples of replicant data are cloud storage, email attachments, file sharing, social media posts and backups.

These types of evidence can be classified into different categories based on their characteristics, such as:

- **Visible data**: These are data that can be seen or observed by the human eye or a standard software tool. Examples are logs, video footage, images and active data.
- **Latent data**: These are data that cannot be seen or observed by the human eye or a standard software tool, but can be revealed or extracted by a specialized software tool or technique. Examples are archives, metadata, residual data and replicant data.
- **Persistent data**: These are data that remain on a system, network or application until they are deleted, overwritten or erased. Examples are logs, archives, active data, metadata and residual data.
- **Transient data**: These are data that disappear or change on a system, network or application when they are accessed, modified or terminated. Examples are volatile data and replicant data.

The types of evidence in cyber forensics can vary in their availability, reliability, relevance and admissibility, depending on the circumstances and context of the investigation. ### Circumstantial evidence

- Circumstantial evidence is evidence that **indirectly** implies or infers a fact or a conclusion, but does not **directly** prove it.
- Circumstantial evidence is often used in cyber forensics to establish the **context**, **motive**, **opportunity**, or **identity** of a cybercriminal, but it usually requires **corroboration** from other sources to be conclusive.
- Examples of circumstantial evidence in cyber forensics are:
  - The **IP address** of a device that accessed a website, sent an email, or downloaded a file. This can indicate the **location** and **identity** of the device owner, but it can also be **spoofed** or **hacked** by someone else.
  - The **metadata** of a digital file, such as the creation date, modification date, author name, or file format. This can reveal the **origin**, **history**, or **purpose** of the file, but it can also be **altered** or **deleted** by someone else.
  - The **digital footprint** of a user, such as the browser history, cookies, cache, bookmarks, or passwords. This can show the **interests**, **preferences**, or **activities** of the user, but it can also be **cleared** or **forgotten** by someone else.
- Circumstantial evidence can be **strong** or **weak**, depending on the **reliability**, **consistency**, and **quantity** of the evidence. The more circumstantial evidence that points to the same fact or conclusion, the more likely it is to be true.
- Circumstantial evidence can be **challenged** or **refuted** by the **defense** or the **prosecution**, depending on the **relevance**, **admissibility**, or **interpretation** of the evidence. The **court** or the **jury** will ultimately decide the **weight** and **value** of the circumstantial evidence.



### Network Evidence

Network evidence is the data that is collected and analyzed from a computer network for the purposes of information gathering, legal evidence, or intrusion detection . Network evidence can provide clues about the source, nature, and impact of a cyber attack, as well as the vulnerabilities that were exploited .

Some of the sources of network evidence are :

- **Network traffic**: This is the data that flows across the network, such as packets, frames, segments, and datagrams. Network traffic can be captured and analyzed using tools such as Wireshark, tcpdump, or Nmap. Network traffic can reveal information such as IP addresses, ports, protocols, payloads, and timestamps of the communication between the attacker and the target.
- **Network devices**: These are the hardware components that facilitate the network communication, such as routers, switches, firewalls, and intrusion detection systems (IDS). Network devices can store logs, configurations, and alerts that can indicate the presence and activity of an attacker on the network. Network devices can also be compromised or tampered with by an attacker to hide their tracks or gain access to other devices or segments of the network.
- **Network services**: These are the software applications that provide functionality and resources on the network, such as web servers, email servers, file servers, and domain name servers (DNS). Network services can generate logs, records, and messages that can show the requests and responses between the attacker and the target. Network services can also be exploited or manipulated by an attacker to gain access, steal data, or disrupt the normal operation of the network.

Network evidence can be collected and analyzed using different methods, such as:

- **Catch it as you can**: This method involves capturing all network traffic and storing it for later analysis. This method guarantees that no important data is missed, but it also requires a lot of storage space and processing power to handle the large volume of data.
- **Stop, look and listen**: This method involves monitoring each data packet that flows across the network and capturing only the ones that are relevant or suspicious. This method reduces the amount of data to be stored and analyzed, but it also risks missing some important data that may not be detected by the filters or criteria used.

Network evidence can be challenging to collect and analyze due to the following factors :

- **Volatile and dynamic**: Network evidence can be easily lost, overwritten, or modified by the normal operation of the network or by the actions of the attacker. Network evidence can also change rapidly as the network traffic and activity fluctuates over time.
- **Distributed and heterogeneous**: Network evidence can be scattered across different devices, segments, and locations of the network, which may have different architectures, protocols, and standards. Network evidence can also involve multiple parties, such as the attacker, the target, the intermediary, and the witness, which may have different roles, interests, and perspectives.
- **Large and complex**: Network evidence can be composed of a huge amount of data that can be difficult to store, process, and analyze. Network evidence can also involve multiple layers, types, and formats of data that can be hard to correlate, interpret, and present.

Network evidence can be useful for the following purposes :

- **Information gathering**: Network evidence can provide valuable information about the network environment, such as the topology, configuration, devices, services, and traffic patterns. Network evidence can also provide information about the attacker, such as the identity, location, motive, and methods. Network evidence can also provide information about the target, such as the assets, vulnerabilities, and impact of the attack.
- **Legal evidence**: Network evidence can serve as proof or support for legal claims or actions, such as prosecution, defense, or litigation. Network evidence can also help to establish the facts, circumstances, and consequences of the attack, as well as the responsibility, liability, and damages of the parties involved.
- **Intrusion detection**: Network evidence can help to detect and prevent cyber attacks, by identifying and alerting the anomalies, threats, and incidents on the network. Network evidence can also help to respond and recover from cyber attacks, by containing and mitigating the effects, tracing and tracking the source, and restoring and improving the security of the network.

### Evidence Analysis

Evidence analysis is the process of examining and interpreting the digital evidence collected from a computer or a device involved in a crime or an incident.   - Collection: The device needs to be handled carefully and securely to avoid any damage, alteration, or loss of digital evidence. Some best practices for collection are:
    - Documenting the device and its surroundings with photographs, notes, and sketches
    - Isolating the device from any network or power source to prevent remote access or data wiping
    - Applying appropriate anti-static and anti-tamper measures to protect the device
    - Labeling and packaging the device with proper identification and chain of custody information
    - Transporting and storing the device in a safe and controlled environment
  - Acquisition: The device needs to be duplicated or imaged to create an exact copy of the data stored on it. The original device should be kept as intact as possible and only accessed when necessary. Some methods for acquisition are:
    - Physical acquisition: Copying the entire physical storage media, including unallocated and slack space, hidden partitions, and deleted files
    - Logical acquisition: Copying only the logical files and folders that are visible to the operating system
    - Live acquisition: Copying the data from a running device, including volatile memory, open files, and network connections
    - Remote acquisition: Copying the data from a device that is located in a different physical location, such as a cloud server or a mobile device
  - Preservation: The acquired data needs to be verified and protected from any unauthorized modification or corruption. Some techniques for preservation are:
    - Hashing: Calculating a unique value for the data that can be used to check its integrity and authenticity
    - Encryption: Scrambling the data with a secret key that can be used to decrypt it later
    - Signing: Attaching a digital signature to the data that can be used to prove its origin and validity
    - Locking: Applying access control mechanisms to the data, such as passwords, biometrics, or tokens
  - Analysis: The preserved data needs to be examined and interpreted to extract relevant information and evidence for the investigation. Some tools and methods for analysis are:
    - Forensic software: Using specialized software applications that can perform various tasks, such as data recovery, file carving, keyword searching, timeline analysis, malware analysis, network analysis, and data visualization
    - Forensic standards: Following established standards and guidelines that can provide a framework and a reference for conducting forensic analysis, such as the Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence (SWGDE), the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO)
    - Forensic reports: Producing clear, concise, and accurate reports that can summarize the findings and conclusions of the forensic analysis, as well as the methods and procedures used, the evidence sources and locations, the limitations and uncertainties, and the recommendations and implications



### Evidence Management

Evidence management is the process of ensuring that digital evidence is properly collected, preserved, analyzed and presented in a cyber forensic investigation. Evidence management involves the following steps:

- **Identification**: The first step is to identify the potential sources and types of digital evidence that are relevant to the case. This may include computers, mobile devices, network devices, cloud services, social media platforms, etc. The investigator should also determine the scope and objectives of the investigation, and the legal and ethical implications of accessing the evidence.
- **Collection**: The second step is to collect the digital evidence using appropriate tools and techniques that do not alter or damage the original data. The investigator should follow the best practices and standards for acquiring, copying, imaging and hashing the evidence. The investigator should also document the details of the collection process, such as the date, time, location, device, tool, method, etc.
- **Preservation**: The third step is to preserve the digital evidence in a secure and tamper-proof manner. The investigator should store the evidence in a locked and controlled environment, and use encryption, authentication and backup methods to protect the data from unauthorized access, modification or deletion. The investigator should also maintain a chain of custody record that tracks the movement and handling of the evidence.
- **Analysis**: The fourth step is to analyze the digital evidence using various tools and techniques that can extract, recover, interpret and correlate the data. The investigator should use a combination of manual and automated methods to identify, filter, search, compare and visualize the evidence. The investigator should also verify the accuracy and validity of the analysis results, and document the findings and conclusions.
- **Presentation**: The fifth step is to present the digital evidence in a clear and concise manner that can support the case and withstand the scrutiny of the court. The investigator should prepare a written report that summarizes the evidence, the analysis methods, the findings and the opinions. The investigator should also be prepared to testify as an expert witness and explain the evidence to the judge, jury and lawyers.

### Digital signature

A digital signature is a mathematical scheme to verify the authenticity of digital documents or messages. A digital signature uses a digital certificate from a trust service provider (TSP), such as a certificate authority (CA), to authenticate a signer's identity. The digital certificate demonstrates proof of signing by binding the digital certificate associated with each signature to the document using encryption .

A digital signature has the following properties:

- **Authentication**: It confirms the identity of the sender or signer of the document or message.
- **Integrity**: It ensures that the document or message has not been altered or tampered with after signing.
- **Non-repudiation**: It prevents the sender or signer from denying or disputing the validity of the document or message.

A digital signature is created by applying a hash function to the document or message, which produces a unique value called a hash or digest. The hash is then encrypted with the private key of the signer, which is known only to the signer. The encrypted hash is the digital signature, which is attached to the document or message. The recipient of the document or message can verify the digital signature by decrypting it with the public key of the signer, which is available from the TSP. The decrypted hash is then compared with the hash of the document or message, which is computed by applying the same hash function. If the hashes match, the digital signature is valid and the document or message is authentic and intact .

A digital signature is different from an electronic signature, which is a broader term that refers to any electronic data that carries the intent of a signature. An electronic signature can be a scanned image of a handwritten signature, a typed name, a click of a button, or a biometric identifier. An electronic signature does not necessarily provide the same level of security and legal validity as a digital signature.

A digital signature is governed by various laws and regulations, depending on the jurisdiction and the context of use. In the United States, the Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce (ESIGN) Act in 2000 legislated that electronic signatures, including digital signatures, are legal in every state and U.S. territory where federal law applies. Where federal law does not apply, most U.S. states have adopted the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA), which Congress passed in 1999. Both ESIGN and UETA establish the legal equivalence of electronic signatures and handwritten signatures, and provide guidelines for their use and acceptance .

In India, the Information Technology Act, 2000, governs the use of cyberspace and the protection of digital information. The Act defines a digital signature as "authentication of any electronic record by a subscriber by means of an electronic method or procedure in accordance with the provisions of section 3". Section 3 specifies the use of asymmetric cryptosystem and hash function for creating and verifying digital signatures. The Act also provides legal recognition to digital signatures and electronic records, and prescribes the duties and responsibilities of the TSPs and the signers .

A digital signature is an important tool for ensuring the security and legality of digital transactions and communications. It can be used for various purposes, such as:

- Signing contracts, agreements, invoices, receipts, and other documents electronically.
- Securing online banking, e-commerce, and e-government services.
- Protecting email messages and attachments from unauthorized access and modification.
- Verifying the authenticity and integrity of software downloads and updates.
- Certifying the identity and authority of online users and entities.

### Future Trends and Emerging Concerns for Cyber Laws

Cyber laws are the legal frameworks that govern the use, protection, and regulation of digital technologies, such as the internet, computers, mobile devices, and cloud services. Cyber laws aim to prevent and punish cybercrimes, such as hacking, identity theft, fraud, cyberattacks, and data breaches, as well as to protect the rights and interests of users, providers, and authorities in the cyberspace.

For example, cyber laws will need to define the legal status, liability, and accountability of artificial intelligence agents, as well as the ethical and human rights implications of their use. Cyber laws will also need to address the challenges and risks posed by quantum computing, which could potentially break the existing encryption and security systems.

Cyber laws will need to enhance the prevention, detection, and response mechanisms to combat cyber threats, as well as to harmonize the international cooperation and coordination among different jurisdictions and agencies. Cyber laws will also need to balance the trade-off between security and privacy, as well as to protect the rights and interests of the victims and the accused.

Cyber laws will also need to address the cross-border data flows and transfers, as well as the interoperability and compatibility of different data protection and privacy regimes, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) in the United States.

- **Increase in internet governance and regulation**: As the internet becomes more ubiquitous and influential, cyber laws will need to govern and regulate the various aspects and actors of the internet, such as the domain names, internet service providers, platforms, content, and users. Cyber laws will need to ensure the stability, security, and resilience of the internet infrastructure, as well as the openness, diversity, and inclusiveness of the internet content and access. Cyber laws will also need to address the challenges and conflicts that arise from the different interests, values, and norms of the internet stakeholders, such as the governments, private sector, civil society, and technical community.

- **Increase in digital rights and responsibilities**: As the digital technology affects and shapes various aspects of human life and society, cyber laws will need to protect and promote the digital rights and responsibilities of the users, providers, and authorities in the cyberspace. Cyber laws will need to ensure that the digital technology is used in a way that respects and enhances the human dignity, autonomy, and welfare, as well as the public interest, social justice, and environmental sustainability. Cyber laws will also need to ensure that the digital technology is used in a way that complies with and upholds the existing laws and regulations, as well as the ethical and moral principles and values.
