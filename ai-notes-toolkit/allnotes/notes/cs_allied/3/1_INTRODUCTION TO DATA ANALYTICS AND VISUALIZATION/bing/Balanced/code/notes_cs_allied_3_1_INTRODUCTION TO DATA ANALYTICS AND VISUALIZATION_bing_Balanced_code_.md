### Sources and nature of data

- Data is any type of information that can be measured, accessed or used in any way in a given study.
- Data sources are the physical or digital locations where data is stored in a data table, data object, or some other storage format.
- Data sources can be classified into two types: statistical and non-statistical.
  - Statistical sources refer to data that is gathered for some official purposes, such as censuses, surveys, and administrative records.
  - Non-statistical sources refer to data that is collected for other administrative purposes or for the private sector, such as business transactions, social media, web analytics, and sensors .
- Data sources can also be classified into two types based on the origin of data: primary and secondary.
  - Primary sources refer to data that is collected directly by the researcher or analyst for the specific purpose of the study .
  - Secondary sources refer to data that is collected by someone else for a different purpose and is reused by the researcher or analyst for the study .
- The nature of data refers to the characteristics or attributes of data, such as the type, format, quality, and validity of data.
  - The type of data refers to the classification of data based on the level of measurement, such as nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio.
  - The format of data refers to the representation of data in a specific structure, such as text, numeric, binary, or multimedia.
  - The quality of data refers to the degree of accuracy, completeness, consistency, and timeliness of data.
  - The validity of data refers to the extent to which data measures what it is intended to measure.
- The sources and nature of data are important to consider in data analytics and visualization, as they affect the selection, processing, analysis, and presentation of data.



### Classification of Data

Classification of data is the process of organizing data into categories or groups based on some criteria, such as characteristics, attributes, or quality. Classification of data helps to make data easier to locate, retrieve, analyze, and protect. Classification of data also supports risk management, security, and regulatory compliance.

There are different ways to classify data, depending on the purpose and context of the data analysis. Some of the common types of data classification are:

- **Qualitative classification**: This type of classification is based on the nature or quality of the data, such as gender, religion, hair color, intelligence, etc. Qualitative data are usually nominal or ordinal, meaning they have no inherent order or numerical value. For example, classifying students by their favorite subject is a qualitative classification.
- **Quantitative classification**: This type of classification is based on the quantity or measure of the data, such as height, weight, income, age, etc. Quantitative data are usually interval or ratio, meaning they have a fixed order and numerical value. For example, classifying students by their test scores is a quantitative classification.
- **Geographical classification**: This type of classification is based on the location or area of the data, such as country, state, city, region, etc. Geographical data are useful for spatial analysis, mapping, and visualization. For example, classifying customers by their zip code is a geographical classification.
- **Chronological classification**: This type of classification is based on the time or date of the data, such as year, month, day, hour, etc. Chronological data are useful for temporal analysis, trend detection, and forecasting. For example, classifying sales by the month of purchase is a chronological classification.
- **Security classification**: This type of classification is based on the level of sensitivity or confidentiality of the data, such as public, private, internal, restricted, secret, top secret, etc. Security data are useful for data protection, access control, and compliance. For example, classifying documents by their clearance level is a security classification.



### Characteristics of Data

Data is any form of information that has been gathered and organized in a meaningful format that can be processed further. Data can be used for various purposes, such as analysis, decision making, communication, and visualization. Data can be classified into different types, such as numerical, categorical, ordinal, and nominal. Data can also have different levels of measurement, such as nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio.

To understand and evaluate data, it is important to consider its characteristics, such as:

- **Accuracy**: This refers to how pure and clean the data is, and how free it is from errors, mistakes, and biases. Accurate data should reflect the true state of reality and be consistent with the source of information .
- **Relevance**: This refers to how well the data matches the needs and requirements of the user. Relevant data should be useful, meaningful, and appropriate for the intended purpose and context .
- **Consistency**: This refers to how reliable and stable the data is across different sources, systems, and time periods. Consistent data should have the same format, structure, and values, and should not contradict or conflict with other data .
- **Completeness**: This refers to how much of the data is available and how well it covers the scope and range of the phenomenon of interest. Complete data should have no missing, incomplete, or outdated values, and should capture all the relevant aspects and dimensions of the data.
- **Uniqueness**: This refers to how distinct and different the data is from other data. Unique data should have no duplicates, redundancies, or repetitions, and should represent the data in the most efficient and concise way .

Data can also be described by its two statistical characteristics, namely:

- **Central tendency**: This refers to how the data is centered around a point, such as the mean, median, or mode. Central tendency measures the average or typical value of the data, and indicates the location of the data distribution .
- **Dispersion**: This refers to how the data is spread out from the center, such as the range, variance, or standard deviation. Dispersion measures the variability or diversity of the data, and indicates the shape and size of the data distribution .

These characteristics of data can help us understand, summarize, compare, and visualize data in a meaningful and effective way.

### Evolution of Analytic Scalability

- Analytic scalability refers to the ability to handle large and complex data sets for analysis purposes.
- In the past, analytic scalability was limited by the constraints of the data environment, such as storage capacity, processing speed, and data integration.
- Analysts had to pull the data together in a separate analytics environment and then start performing analysis. This involved merging, cleansing, transforming, and aggregating data sets, which was time-consuming and error-prone.
- As the volume, variety, and velocity of data increased, the traditional methods of analytic scalability became inadequate and inefficient.
- To cope with the challenges of big data, new paradigms of analytic scalability emerged, such as:
  - The convergence of the analytic and data environments: This means that the analytics are performed in the same environment where the data resides, eliminating the need for data movement and duplication. Examples of this paradigm are in-database analytics, in-memory analytics, and streaming analytics.
  - Massively parallel processing (MPP) architectures: These are systems that use multiple processors or nodes to perform parallel computations on distributed data sets. MPP architectures enable faster and more scalable analytics by dividing the workload among multiple nodes and reducing the data transfer among them. Examples of this paradigm are data warehouse appliances, columnar databases, and NoSQL databases.
  - The cloud: This is a model of computing that provides on-demand access to shared resources, such as storage, processing, and software, over the internet. The cloud enables greater scalability and flexibility for analytics by allowing users to pay for what they use, scale up or down as needed, and access a variety of tools and services. Examples of this paradigm are cloud-based data platforms, analytics platforms, and applications.
  - Grid computing: This is a form of distributed computing that uses a network of computers to perform coordinated tasks on large data sets. Grid computing enables more scalable and cost-effective analytics by leveraging the idle resources of multiple computers and sharing the computational load among them. Examples of this paradigm are grid-based data platforms, analytics platforms, and applications.
  - MapReduce: This is a programming model and framework that enables parallel processing of large data sets on clusters of commodity hardware. MapReduce enables more scalable and fault-tolerant analytics by breaking down the data and the analysis into smaller and simpler tasks, and then combining the results. Examples of this paradigm are Hadoop, Spark, and Hive.
- These paradigms of analytic scalability have enabled analysts to handle big data more effectively and efficiently, and to perform more advanced and sophisticated analytics, such as machine learning, text mining, and sentiment analysis.

### Analysis vs Reporting

- Reporting is the process of presenting data in a structured and summarized way, such as tables, charts, or dashboards, to communicate information to a specific audience.
- Analysis is the process of exploring, interpreting, and finding insights from data, such as patterns, trends, correlations, or anomalies, to answer a specific question or solve a problem.

- Reporting helps to monitor the performance of a business, process, or system, by providing descriptive statistics and historical data. Reporting answers questions such as what happened, when, where, and how much.
- Analysis helps to understand the causes and effects of a situation, by providing inferential statistics and predictive models. Analysis answers questions such as why something happened, what will happen next, and what actions should be taken.
- Reporting and analysis can be done using various tools and techniques, such as spreadsheets, databases, business intelligence software, data visualization tools, statistical methods, machine learning algorithms, etc.
- Reporting and analysis can be done at different levels of complexity and detail, depending on the needs and goals of the audience and the data analyst. ## Unit 2 - Data Analytics Lifecycle

The data analytics lifecycle is a framework for planning and executing data analysis projects. It consists of six phases:

- **Discovery**: This phase involves identifying the business problem, defining the objectives and scope of the analysis, and gathering the relevant data sources and stakeholders.
- **Data preparation**: This phase involves cleaning, transforming, and integrating the data into a suitable format for analysis. It also involves exploring the data to understand its characteristics, quality, and distribution.
- **Model planning**: This phase involves selecting the appropriate analytical techniques and tools for the data and the problem. It also involves creating and validating hypotheses, and designing experiments or tests to evaluate them.
- **Model building**: This phase involves applying the selected techniques and tools to the data, and generating insights, patterns, or predictions. It also involves assessing the accuracy, validity, and reliability of the results, and refining the models as needed.
- **Communicating results**: This phase involves presenting and visualizing the results to the stakeholders, and explaining the methods, assumptions, and limitations of the analysis. It also involves providing recommendations, actions, or solutions based on the results.
- **Operationalizing**: This phase involves implementing the recommendations, actions, or solutions into the business processes or systems, and monitoring and evaluating their impact and performance. It also involves documenting the analysis project and its outcomes, and identifying the lessons learned and the best practices.

### Various phases of data analytics lifecycle

The data analytics lifecycle is a series of steps that guide the process of transforming data into insights and actions that can help achieve business or project goals. Data preparation is often the most time-consuming and challenging phase in the data analytics lifecycle, as it involves dealing with various data issues, such as missing values, outliers, inconsistencies, duplicates, errors, and noise. Data preparation also requires selecting the relevant variables, features, and samples for the analysis, as well as creating new variables or features from the existing data. Data preparation can be done using various tools and methods, such as spreadsheets, databases, programming languages, and software applications.

Some of the common tasks and steps involved in data preparation are  :

- **Data access**: Connect to the data sources and extract the data needed for the project.
- **Data loading**: Load the data into a suitable platform or environment for analysis, such as an analytical sandbox, which is a scalable and flexible space for data processing and exploration.
- **Data structuring**: Organize the data into a consistent and logical format, such as tables, matrices, or arrays, and define the data types, labels, and units.

### Inference and Bayesian Networks

- Bayesian networks are a type of probabilistic graphical model that uses Bayesian inference for probability computations .
- Bayesian networks aim to model conditional dependence, and therefore causation, by representing conditional dependence by edges in a directed graph .
- Through these relationships, one can efficiently conduct inference on the random variables in the graph through the use of factors.
- Factors are functions that map a set of variables to a real number, representing the strength of their association.
- Inference is one key objective in a Bayesian network, and it aims to estimate the posterior distributions of state variables based on evidence (observations).
- Inference over a Bayesian network can come in two forms: exact inference and approximate inference.
- Exact inference is the process of computing the exact posterior distributions of the variables of interest, given the evidence.
- Exact inference can be done by enumeration, which involves summing out all the irrelevant variables from the joint distribution, or by variable elimination, which involves applying the distributive law to factorize the joint distribution and eliminate variables one by one .
- Exact inference is computationally expensive and intractable for large and complex networks, especially if they are multiply connected (have more than one undirected path between any two nodes) .
- Approximate inference is the process of computing an approximation of the posterior distributions of the variables of interest, given the evidence.
- Approximate inference can be done by stochastic simulation, which involves sampling from the joint distribution and counting the frequency of the samples that match the evidence, or by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), which involves constructing a Markov chain that converges to the desired posterior distribution and sampling from it  .
- Approximate inference is computationally efficient and scalable for large and complex networks, but it introduces some error and uncertainty in the results.

# Competitive Learning

### Fuzzy Decision Trees

Fuzzy decision trees are a type of decision tree that use fuzzy logic to handle uncertainty and imprecision in data. ### Counting Distinct Elements in a Stream

- A stream is a sequence of data items that arrives one by one and cannot be stored in memory.
- Counting the number of distinct elements in a stream is a fundamental problem in data stream mining, with applications in network monitoring, web analytics, database systems, etc.
- The challenge is to design algorithms that use limited memory and processing time, and can handle stream updates and queries efficiently.
- There are two main approaches to counting distinct elements in a stream: exact and approximate.
- Exact algorithms guarantee to return the exact number of distinct elements, but they require memory proportional to the number of distinct elements, which can be impractical for large or unbounded streams.
- Approximate algorithms trade off accuracy for memory efficiency, and return an estimate of the number of distinct elements with some error bound or confidence interval.
- Some examples of approximate algorithms are:

  - Flajolet-Martin algorithm: uses a hash function and a bit array to estimate the number of distinct elements based on the longest run of zeros in the hashed values .
  - HyperLogLog algorithm: improves on the Flajolet-Martin algorithm by using multiple hash functions and bit arrays, and applying a harmonic mean to reduce the variance of the estimate.
  - KMV algorithm: maintains a sorted set of the k smallest hashed values seen so far, and estimates the number of distinct elements based on the inverse of the k-th smallest value.
  - Count-Min sketch: uses a two-dimensional array and multiple hash functions to count the frequency of each element, and estimates the number of distinct elements by taking the minimum of the counts.

- Some variations of the problem are:

  - Counting distinct elements in a sliding window: the stream is divided into fixed-length or time-based windows, and the number of distinct elements in the current window is computed.
  - Counting distinct elements in a multi-set: the stream contains elements with multiplicities, and the number of distinct elements is weighted by their multiplicities.
  - Counting distinct elements in a distributed setting: the stream is partitioned among multiple nodes, and the number of distinct elements in the whole stream is computed by combining the estimates from each node.



### Estimating Moments

- Estimating moments is a generalization of the problem of counting distinct elements in a stream. The problem, called computing "moments," involves the distribution of frequencies of different elements in the stream.
- Suppose a stream consists of elements chosen from a universal set. Let $m_i$ be the number of occurrences of the $i$-th element for any $i$. The $p$-th frequency moment of the stream is defined as $F_p = \sum_i m_i^p$ for any $p > 0$.
- Estimating frequency moments is useful for various applications, such as estimating all-pairs distances in a large data matrix, machine learning, and data stream computation .
- The main challenge of estimating frequency moments is to design space-efficient algorithms that can handle large and dynamic streams of data. The algorithms should also have low error probability and small approximation error .
- Two simple sketches for estimating frequency moments are the Alon-Matias-Szegedy (AMS) sketch and the Flajolet-Martin (FM) sketch  .
- The AMS sketch is based on sampling a random position in the stream and storing the frequency of the element at that position. The sketch can estimate the second frequency moment ($F_2$) with high probability and low relative error  .
- The FM sketch is based on hashing the elements of the stream to binary strings and storing the minimum number of leading zeros in the hashed values. The sketch can estimate the zeroth frequency moment ($F_0$), which is the number of distinct elements in the stream, with high probability and low relative error  .
- Both sketches can be extended to handle multiple streams, weighted streams, and sliding windows  .



### Counting oneness in a window

- Counting oneness in a window is a problem of estimating the number of 1's in the last k bits of a data stream, where k is a large number that cannot be stored in memory.
- One possible solution is to use the DGIM algorithm, which uses O(log2 N) bits to represent a window of N bits, and allows us to estimate the number of 1's in the window with an error of no more than 50% .
- The DGIM algorithm works as follows:
  - Each bit of the stream has a timestamp, the position in which it arrives. The first bit has timestamp 1, the second has timestamp 2, and so on.
  - The algorithm divides the window into buckets, each consisting of:
    - The timestamp of its right (most recent) end.
    - The number of 1's in the bucket.
  - The algorithm maintains the following invariants for the buckets:
    - There are at most two buckets with the same number of 1's.
    - The buckets are ordered by their timestamps, from left (oldest) to right (most recent).
    - The number of 1's in a bucket is a power of 2.
  - When a new bit arrives, the algorithm updates the buckets as follows:
    - If the bit is 0, nothing changes.
    - If the bit is 1, a new bucket with timestamp equal to the current position and number of 1's equal to 1 is created at the right end of the window.
    - If there are now three buckets with the same number of 1's, the two oldest buckets are merged into one bucket with timestamp equal to the newer of the two and number of 1's equal to the sum of the two.
    - If the oldest bucket falls outside the window, it is discarded.
  - To estimate the number of 1's in the last k bits, the algorithm does the following:
    - Find the rightmost bucket that is completely within the last k bits.
    - Sum the number of 1's in all the buckets to the right of this bucket, and add half of the number of 1's in this bucket.
    - This sum is an upper bound on the number of 1's in the last k bits, with an error of at most 50%.



### Decaying Window

- A decaying window is a technique to handle data streams that are too large or fast to store or process in their entirety.
- A decaying window assigns a weight or score to each element of the data stream, based on how recent or relevant it is.
- A decaying window can be used to find frequent itemsets, which are sets of elements that appear together often in the data stream.
- A decaying window can also be used to compute aggregates, such as sums, averages, or counts, over the data stream, by applying the weights to the values of the elements.
- A decaying window can be implemented in different ways, such as:
  - Exponentially decaying window: The weight of an element is proportional to an exponential function of its age, such as e^-c(t-i), where c is a constant, t is the current time, and i is the arrival time of the element.
  - Time-fading window: The weight of an element is proportional to a function of its timestamp, such as 1/(1+t-i), where t is the current time, and i is the timestamp of the element.
  - Landmark window: The weight of an element is 1 if it arrived after a certain landmark time, and 0 otherwise.
  - Sliding window: The weight of an element is 1 if it arrived within a fixed time interval from the current time, and 0 otherwise.
- A decaying window can help reduce the memory and computational requirements of processing data streams, by focusing on the most recent or relevant data and discarding the rest.
- A decaying window can also help deal with noise or outliers in the data stream, by giving them lower weights or ignoring them altogether.

### Mining frequent itemsets

- There are different algorithms for mining frequent itemsets, such as Apriori, Eclat, and FP-growth. These algorithms differ in how they generate and prune candidate itemsets, how they count the support of itemsets, and how they store and traverse the data structure.
- Apriori is the most commonly used algorithm for mining frequent itemsets. It works by applying the Apriori principle, which states that any subset of a frequent itemset must also be frequent. Apriori generates candidate itemsets of increasing size and scans the dataset to count their support. It prunes any candidate itemset whose support is below a minimum threshold. The remaining itemsets are the frequent itemsets.
- Eclat is a variation of Apriori that uses a vertical data format, where each item is associated with a set of transactions that contain it. Eclat generates candidate itemsets by intersecting the transaction sets of frequent items. It counts the support of itemsets by the size of the intersection sets. Eclat avoids scanning the dataset multiple times and reduces the number of candidate itemsets.
- FP-growth is another variation of Apriori that uses a compressed data structure called an FP-tree, which stores the frequent items and their frequencies in a prefix tree. FP-growth generates frequent itemsets by recursively constructing conditional FP-trees and extracting frequent itemsets from them. FP-growth avoids generating candidate itemsets and scans the dataset only twice.

### Apriori algorithm

- The Apriori algorithm is an algorithm for **frequent itemset mining** and **association rule learning** over relational databases  .
- Frequent itemsets are sets of items that appear together in a database with a frequency above a given threshold  .
- Association rules are rules that imply the presence of some items given the presence of other items in a transaction  .
- The Apriori algorithm was proposed by **Agrawal and Srikant** in 1994 .
- The Apriori algorithm is designed to operate on databases containing **transactions** (for example, collections of items bought by customers, or details of a website frequentation or IP addresses)  .
- The Apriori algorithm uses **prior knowledge** of frequent itemset properties to reduce the search space and improve efficiency  .
- The Apriori algorithm applies an **iterative** approach or **level-wise** search where k-frequent itemsets are used to find k+1 itemsets  .
- The Apriori algorithm consists of two main steps: **join** and **prune**  .
  - The join step generates candidate itemsets of length k from frequent itemsets of length k-1  .
  - The prune step eliminates candidate itemsets that have infrequent subsets using the **Apriori property**  .
- The Apriori property states that **all non-empty subsets of a frequent itemset must also be frequent**  .
- The Apriori algorithm stops when no more frequent itemsets can be generated  .
- The Apriori algorithm can be used to generate association rules by computing the **confidence** and **support** of each rule  .
  - The confidence of a rule is the ratio of the number of transactions that contain both the antecedent and the consequent of the rule to the number of transactions that contain the antecedent of the rule  .
  - The support of a rule is the ratio of the number of transactions that contain both the antecedent and the consequent of the rule to the total number of transactions  .
  - A rule is considered **strong** if its confidence and support are above given thresholds  .
- The Apriori algorithm has some advantages and disadvantages  .
  - Advantages:
    - It is easy to implement and understand  .
    - It can handle large datasets efficiently  .
    - It can be parallelized and distributed  .
  - Disadvantages:
    - It may generate a large number of candidate itemsets, especially when the minimum support threshold is low or the number of items is high  .
    - It may require multiple scans of the database, which can be costly in terms of time and memory  .
    - It may not be suitable for finding rare itemsets or itemsets with variable lengths  .

### Handling large data sets in main memory

- Large data sets are those that exceed the available memory of a single machine or a cluster of machines.
- Handling large data sets is a common challenge in data analytics and visualization, especially for tasks such as frequent itemset mining and clustering.
- Some possible solutions for handling large data sets in main memory are:

  - **Data compression**: Reducing the size of the data by applying techniques such as encoding, quantization, dimensionality reduction, or feature selection . This can help to fit more data into memory and speed up the processing time.
  - **Data chunking**: Splitting the data into smaller chunks that can be loaded and processed individually in memory . This can help to avoid memory overflow and enable parallel or distributed computing. Each chunk can be processed by a separate classifier or clusterer, and the results can be aggregated or compared later.
  - **Data streaming**: Processing the data as it arrives in a continuous stream, rather than loading it all at once. This can help to handle dynamic or real-time data that may not fit into memory. Streaming algorithms can update the model or the summary statistics incrementally as new data arrives, without storing the entire data.
  - **Data sampling**: Selecting a representative subset of the data that preserves the essential characteristics and patterns of the whole data . This can help to reduce the complexity and noise of the data and improve the accuracy and efficiency of the analysis. Sampling techniques can be random, stratified, or based on some criteria or heuristic.
  - **Data partitioning**: Dividing the data into disjoint or overlapping subsets that can be processed separately or jointly in memory . This can help to exploit the structure or the distribution of the data and improve the scalability and performance of the analysis. Partitioning techniques can be based on hashing, sorting, or grouping the data by some attribute or value.
  - **Data indexing**: Creating a data structure that allows fast and efficient access to the data based on some key or query. This can help to reduce the search space and the computational cost of the analysis. Indexing techniques can be based on trees, graphs, arrays, or hashes.
  - **Data approximation**: Replacing the data with a simpler or more compact representation that approximates the original data with some error or loss. This can help to reduce the storage and processing requirements of the data and speed up the analysis. Approximation techniques can be based on sketches, histograms, or summaries.



### Limited Pass Algorithm for Frequent Itemsets

- The limited pass algorithm is an approximate algorithm that aims to find most of the frequent itemsets in a large data set, using fewer passes than the exact algorithms like A-Priori or PCY .
- The basic idea of the limited pass algorithm is to use a random sample of the data set as the first pass, and then use a lower support threshold for the sample than for the whole data set.
- The sample size should be large enough to capture most of the frequent itemsets, but small enough to fit in the main memory and reduce the I/O cost.
- The second pass of the algorithm scans the whole data set and counts only the candidate itemsets that are frequent in the sample.
- The advantage of the limited pass algorithm is that it can find most of the frequent itemsets with high probability, using only two passes over the data set.
- The disadvantage of the limited pass algorithm is that it may miss some of the frequent itemsets that are not frequent in the sample, or include some of the infrequent itemsets that are frequent in the sample by chance.
- The accuracy of the limited pass algorithm depends on the choice of the sample size and the support threshold for the sample.
- The limited pass algorithm can be extended to find frequent itemsets of any size, by using a recursive approach similar to the A-Priori algorithm.
- The limited pass algorithm can also be combined with other techniques like hashing, bitmap compression, or partitioning to improve its performance and scalability .

It then iteratively assigns each data point to the nearest cluster center and updates the cluster centers based on the average of the assigned points. The algorithm stops when there is no change in the cluster assignments or when a maximum number of iterations is reached. K-Means is simple, fast, and scalable, but it can be sensitive to outliers, noise, and initial cluster centers. It also assumes that the clusters are spherical and have similar sizes and densities.

- **DBSCAN**: This is a density-based method that uses density-based spatial clustering to find clusters of arbitrary shapes and sizes. It does not require specifying the number of clusters in advance, but it requires two parameters: epsilon and minPts. Epsilon is the maximum distance between two points to be considered as neighbors, and minPts is the minimum number of points required to form a dense region. The algorithm starts by randomly selecting a point and finding its epsilon-neighborhood. If the neighborhood contains at least minPts points, it forms a cluster and expands it by recursively finding the neighbors of the neighbors. If the neighborhood contains less than minPts points, the point is marked as noise. The algorithm repeats this process until all points are either assigned to a cluster or marked as noise. DBSCAN can handle outliers, noise, and clusters of different shapes and sizes, but it can be sensitive to the choice of epsilon and minPts. It also assumes that the clusters have different densities and are well-separated.

- **Spectral Clustering**: This is a similarity graph-based method that models the nearest-neighbor relationships between data points as an undirected graph. The nodes of the graph are the data points, and the edges are weighted by the similarity between the points. The algorithm then applies a graph partitioning technique, such as normalized cuts or modularity maximization, to find the clusters that minimize the cut or maximize the modularity of the graph. The algorithm requires specifying the number of clusters in advance and the similarity measure to use. Spectral clustering can handle clusters of arbitrary shapes and sizes, but it can be computationally expensive and sensitive to the choice of similarity measure and graph partitioning technique.

- **Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering**: This is a bottom-up method that starts by treating each data point as a single cluster and then iteratively merges the closest pair of clusters until a single cluster or a desired number of clusters is reached. The algorithm requires specifying a distance measure to compute the similarity between clusters and a linkage criterion to determine how to merge the clusters. The distance measure can be Euclidean, Manhattan, Cosine, or any other metric that satisfies the properties of a distance function. The linkage criterion can be single, complete, average, or any other method that defines how to aggregate the distances between clusters. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering can produce a dendrogram that shows the hierarchical structure of the data and the order of the cluster merges. It can also handle clusters of different shapes and sizes, but it can be computationally expensive and sensitive to the choice of distance measure and linkage criterion.

### Hierarchical Clustering

### Clustering High-Dimensional Data

Clustering is the process of grouping data objects into clusters based on their similarity or dissimilarity. Clustering high-dimensional data is challenging because of the following problems :

- The curse of dimensionality: As the number of dimensions increases, the data becomes sparse and noisy, and the distance between any two points becomes less meaningful.
- The local feature relevance: Different clusters may exist in different subspaces of the data, and the relevance of each dimension may vary across clusters and subspaces.
- The interpretability of clusters: It is hard to visualize and understand the clusters and their characteristics in high-dimensional data.

To overcome these problems, various approaches have been proposed for clustering high-dimensional data , such as:

- Subspace clustering: This approach aims to find clusters that exist in multiple and possibly overlapping subspaces of the data, by localizing the search for relevant dimensions.
- Projection clustering: This approach projects the data onto a lower-dimensional space and then applies a clustering algorithm on the projected data, by reducing the dimensionality of the data.
- Correlation clustering: This approach identifies clusters that have high correlation among their dimensions, by measuring the correlation between dimensions instead of the distance between points.
- Ensemble clustering: This approach combines multiple clustering results from different subspaces or projections of the data, by aggregating the cluster memberships or similarities of the data objects.

#### CLIQUE

- CLIQUE is a **subspace clustering algorithm** that finds clusters in high-dimensional data by dividing each dimension into equal-width intervals and saving those intervals where the density is greater than a threshold as clusters .
- CLIQUE uses a **density and grid-based technique** to identify dense regions in subspaces of different dimensions.
- CLIQUE has four main steps :
  - Partitioning: Divide each dimension into a number of grids and count the number of points in each grid cell.
  - Identification: Identify the dense grid cells that have more points than the density threshold and form one-dimensional clusters.
  - Merging: Merge the adjacent one-dimensional clusters to form higher-dimensional clusters in a bottom-up manner.
  - Pruning: Eliminate the redundant clusters that are contained in other clusters.
- CLIQUE can find clusters of **any shape** and is able to find **any number of clusters** in **any number of dimensions**, where the number is not predetermined by a parameter.
- CLIQUE has been criticized for its **high sensitivity** to the input parameters (the number of bins and the minimal density) which can lead to very different results.

#### ProCLUS

- ProCLUS is a **projected clustering algorithm** that finds clusters in high-dimensional data by selecting a subset of dimensions (called a projection) for each cluster and assigning points to the closest cluster in the projection .
- ProCLUS uses a **k-medoids and dimensionality reduction technique** to identify the most relevant dimensions for each cluster and to reduce the noise and outliers .
- ProCLUS has four main steps :
  - Initialization: Select k medoids randomly from the data points and assign each point to the closest medoid.
  - Iterative Phase: For each medoid, find the best set of dimensions that have the lowest average distance to the medoid and assign points to the closest medoid in the projection.
  - Refinement Phase: Remove the bad medoids that have too few points or too many dimensions and reassign the points to the remaining medoids.
  - Final Phase: Fine-tune the clusters by reassigning the points to the closest medoid in the projection and updating the dimensions accordingly.
- ProCLUS can find clusters of **arbitrary shape** and is able to find **k clusters** in **a subset of dimensions**, where k is given by the user .
- ProCLUS has been praised for its **high accuracy and efficiency** compared to other clustering algorithms, such as k-means, DBSCAN, and CLIQUE .

### Issues for the notes of the Unit 6 - Introduction to Visualization in the subject of INTRODUCTION TO DATA ANALYTICS AND VISUALIZATION

- Data visualization is the process of transforming data into graphical or pictorial representations that can communicate information effectively and efficiently to the audience.
- Data visualization can have many benefits, such as revealing patterns, trends, outliers, correlations, and causations in the data, enhancing the understanding and retention of the data, and facilitating the decision-making process based on the data.
- However, data visualization can also have many issues, such as misleading, confusing, or deceiving the audience, violating the ethical or aesthetic principles of design, or failing to address the needs and expectations of the audience    .
- Some of the common issues for data visualization are:

  - **Correlation is not causation**: Correlations make us see false relationships between variables that may not have any causal link. For example, a graph that shows a correlation between ice cream sales and shark attacks may imply that eating ice cream causes shark attacks, which is obviously not true.
  - **Similarities now do not mean similarities forever**: A relationship happening within our observational set does not imply a relationship always. For example, a graph that shows a similarity between the GDP of two countries in a certain year may not reflect the similarity in the future years, as the GDP may change due to various factors.
  - **Axes make all the difference**: The size and number of axes can change the way we interpret data. For example, a graph that uses a truncated or distorted axis may exaggerate or minimize the differences between the data points, or a graph that uses multiple axes may confuse the audience or create false comparisons .
  - **Abusing the Law of Large Numbers (LLN)**: The LLN states that as the sample size increases, the sample mean approaches the population mean. However, some data visualizations may abuse this law by using large numbers to imply precision or significance that may not exist. For example, a graph that shows the population of a city as 8,175,133 may imply a high level of accuracy, but it may not reflect the uncertainty or variability of the data.
  - **Misleading color contrast**: Color is among the most persuasive design elements. Even subtle shade variations elicit strong emotional responses. In data visualization, high degrees of color contrast may cause viewers to believe that value disparities are greater than they really are. For example, a map that uses a red-green color scheme to show the political preferences of different regions may create a false impression of polarization or homogeneity.
  - **Using an unsuitable format**: The nature of the data usually dictates the format of the visualization. The most important characteristic is whether the data is qualitative (it describes or categorizes) or quantitative (meaning, it’s measurable). For example, a pie chart is suitable for showing the proportions of qualitative data, but not for showing the trends of quantitative data over time. Using an unsuitable format may misrepresent the data or obscure the message.
  - **Lack of better data visualization literacy**: A lack of proper knowledge of data visualization can do more harm than good. First, it confuses the audience, or worse, it misleads them. Additionally, a poorly structured data visualization denotes a loss of time and effort, which delays the decision-making process in deadline-driven projects. ## Unit 7 - Human Vision

Human vision is the ability to perceive and interpret visual information from the environment. Human vision involves the interaction of the eyes, the brain, and the nervous system. Human vision has the following features and limits:

- **Binocular vision**: Humans have two eyes that are located on the left and right sides of the face. Each eye forms a different image on the retina, which is the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. The brain combines these two images to create a single perception of vision that allows humans to see objects in three dimensions and estimate their depth and distance .
- **Eye structure**: The human eye is a complex organ that consists of several parts, such as the cornea, the pupil, the iris, the lens, the vitreous, the retina, the optic nerve, and the extraocular muscles. Each part has a specific function in the process of vision. For example, the cornea is the clear front layer of the eye that bends light to help the eye focus. The pupil is the opening in the center of the iris that controls the amount of light that enters the eye. The lens is the transparent structure behind the pupil that changes shape to focus light onto the retina. The retina is the layer of nerve cells that converts light into electrical signals that are sent to the brain via the optic nerve. The extraocular muscles are the six muscles that control the movements of the eye .
- **Color perception**: Humans can perceive different colors because of the presence of three types of cone cells in the retina. Cone cells are photoreceptors that respond to different wavelengths of light. The three types of cone cells are sensitive to red, green, and blue light, respectively. The brain interprets the signals from these cone cells to create the sensation of color. Humans can see about 10 million colors, but some people have color vision deficiencies that affect their ability to distinguish certain colors.
- **Visual acuity**: Visual acuity is the measure of how well a person can see the details of an object at a certain distance. Visual acuity is usually expressed as a fraction, such as 20/20, which means that a person can see an object that is 20 feet away as clearly as a normal person can. Visual acuity can be affected by various factors, such as the clarity of the eye's optical system, the size and contrast of the object, the lighting conditions, and the presence of refractive errors or eye diseases. Normal visual acuity is about 20/20, but some people have higher or lower visual acuity.
- **Field of view**: Field of view is the extent of the visible area that a person can see at any given moment. Field of view depends on the position and orientation of the eyes, the shape and size of the eye sockets, and the presence of any obstacles or blind spots. The human field of view is about 180 degrees horizontally and 135 degrees vertically, but it varies from person to person. The field of view can be divided into central and peripheral vision. Central vision is the area of the field of view that is directly in front of the eyes and is used for detailed and focused vision. Peripheral vision is the area of the field of view that is around the edges of the central vision and is used for detecting motion and changes in the environment.

### Space Limitation

- Space limitation refers to the constraint of the available space on a display device or a visualization medium to present data effectively and efficiently.
- Space limitation can affect the quality, readability, and usability of data visualizations, especially when dealing with large or complex data sets.
- Some of the challenges and solutions related to space limitation are:

  - **Overplotting**: This occurs when too many data points or graphical elements are plotted in the same area, causing occlusion and clutter. Overplotting can reduce the visibility and distinguishability of the data, and make it difficult to identify patterns, trends, outliers, or correlations. Some possible solutions for overplotting are:
    - Using transparency or color blending to show the density or distribution of the data points.
    - Applying filtering, aggregation, or sampling techniques to reduce the number of data points or graphical elements.
    - Using interactive techniques such as zooming, panning, brushing, or linking to explore the data at different levels of detail or focus on regions of interest.
    - Using alternative visual encodings such as glyphs, icons, or symbols to represent the data points or graphical elements more compactly or expressively.
  - **Labeling**: This refers to the process of adding text annotations to the data visualizations to provide additional information or context, such as the names, values, or categories of the data points or graphical elements. Labeling can enhance the interpretability and communication of the data visualizations, but it can also introduce challenges such as:
    - Finding enough space to place the labels without overlapping or obscuring the data or other labels.
    - Choosing the appropriate font size, style, color, and orientation for the labels to make them readable and consistent with the data visualizations.
    - Balancing the trade-off between the amount and the relevance of the information provided by the labels. Too few or too many labels can affect the clarity and comprehensibility of the data visualizations. Some possible solutions for labeling are:
      - Using layout algorithms or heuristics to optimize the placement and alignment of the labels.
      - Using interactive techniques such as tooltips, pop-ups, or mouse-over effects to show the labels on demand or in response to user actions.
      - Using abbreviations, acronyms, or symbols to shorten the labels or convey more information with less text.
      - Using visual cues such as lines, arrows, or connectors to link the labels to the data points or graphical elements.
  - **Multivariate data**: This refers to the data that has more than two variables or dimensions. Multivariate data can provide rich and comprehensive information, but it can also pose challenges for data visualization, such as:
    - Finding suitable visual encodings or mappings to represent the multiple variables or dimensions of the data. Some common visual encodings are position, size, shape, color, orientation, texture, or motion. However, not all visual encodings are equally effective or perceptually accurate for different types of data or tasks. For example, position and size are more effective for quantitative data than color or shape, and color and shape are more effective for categorical data than size or orientation.
    - Finding enough space to display the multiple variables or dimensions of the data without compromising the legibility or aesthetics of the data visualizations. Some possible solutions for multivariate data are:
      - Using dimensionality reduction or feature selection techniques to reduce the number of variables or dimensions of the data or focus on the most relevant or informative ones.
      - Using multiple coordinated views or small multiples to show different variables or dimensions of the data in separate but linked visualizations.
      - Using composite or derived visual encodings or mappings to combine or integrate multiple variables or dimensions of the data into a single visualization. For example, using color hue and saturation to represent two variables, or using glyphs or icons to represent multiple variables.
