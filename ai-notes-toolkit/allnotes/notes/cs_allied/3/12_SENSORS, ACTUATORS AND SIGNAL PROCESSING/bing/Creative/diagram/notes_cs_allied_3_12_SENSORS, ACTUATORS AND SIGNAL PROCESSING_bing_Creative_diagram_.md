

# SENSORS, ACTUATORS AND SIGNAL PROCESSING

## Sensors
- A sensor is a device that converts a physical event into an electrical signal.
- Sensors are used to monitor physical activity and alert the control center to changes in asset performance or its environment.
- Sensors can be classified into different types based on the physical quantity they measure, such as temperature, pressure, light, sound, etc.
- Sensors can also be classified based on the principle of operation, such as resistive, capacitive, inductive, piezoelectric, etc.
- Sensors can have different characteristics, such as sensitivity, accuracy, resolution, range, linearity, etc.

## Actuators
- An actuator is a device that converts an electrical signal into a physical event.
- Actuators are used to perform physical actions, such as manipulating a robotic arm, opening a valve, or moving a motor.
- Actuators can be classified into different types based on the physical output they produce, such as linear, rotary, hydraulic, pneumatic, etc.
- Actuators can also be classified based on the principle of operation, such as electromagnetic, electrostatic, piezoelectric, etc.
- Actuators can have different characteristics, such as force, torque, speed, displacement, etc.

## Signal Processing
- Signal processing is the analysis, manipulation, and transformation of signals, such as sensor data, audio, images, etc.
- Signal processing can be performed in different domains, such as time, frequency, space, etc.
- Signal processing can have different objectives, such as filtering, compression, enhancement, detection, classification, etc.
- Signal processing can use different techniques, such as Fourier transform, wavelet transform, convolution, correlation, etc.
- Signal processing can be implemented in different platforms, such as hardware, software, or embedded systems.

## Unit 1 - Sensors / Transducers: Principles Classification, Parameters, Characteristics, Environmental Parameters (EP), Characterization

- A sensor is an element that senses a variation in input energy to produce a variation in another or same form of energy.
- A transducer is an element that converts one form of energy to another form. The process of conversion of energy from one form to another is called transduction.
- Sensors and transducers can be classified based on different criteria, such as:
  - The principle of transduction form used, such as resistive, inductive, capacitive, piezoelectric, etc. depending on their conversion into resistance, inductance, capacitance, voltage, etc. respectively.
  - The type of input energy, such as mechanical, thermal, optical, chemical, biological, etc. depending on the physical phenomenon they sense or detect.
  - The type of output signal, such as analog or digital, depending on the format of the electrical signal they produce.
  - The mode of operation, such as active or passive, depending on whether they require an external source of power (excitation voltage) or not.
- Sensors and transducers have various parameters that describe their performance, such as:
  - Characteristics, such as sensitivity, range, resolution, accuracy, precision, repeatability, linearity, hysteresis, drift, response time, etc. that define the relationship between the input and output signals.
  - Environmental parameters (EP), such as temperature, humidity, pressure, vibration, radiation, etc. that affect the operation and stability of the sensors and transducers.
- Characterization is the process of measuring and evaluating the parameters and characteristics of the sensors and transducers under different conditions and environments.
- Characterization can be done in different ways depending on the types of sensors and transducers, such as:
  - Electrical characterization, such as measuring the resistance, capacitance, voltage, current, power, etc. of the sensors and transducers.
  - Mechanical and thermal characterization, such as measuring the force, displacement, stress, strain, pressure, temperature, etc. of the sensors and transducers.
  - Optical characterization, such as measuring the intensity, wavelength, polarization, phase, etc. of the light emitted or received by the sensors and transducers.
  - Chemical and biological characterization, such as measuring the concentration, pH, conductivity, affinity, etc. of the chemical or biological substances sensed or detected by the sensors and transducers.

## Unit 2 - Thermal Sensors

Thermal sensors are devices that measure temperature or heat flux by converting thermal signals into electrical signals. Thermal sensors have various types, principles, and applications depending on the physical phenomenon they exploit and the environment they operate in. Some of the common types of thermal sensors are:

- **Gas thermometric sensors**: These sensors use the change in pressure, volume, or density of a gas due to temperature change. For example, a gas thermometer consists of a sealed bulb filled with a gas (such as helium or nitrogen) and connected to a manometer. The gas expands or contracts as the temperature changes, causing the pressure and the height of the liquid column in the manometer to vary. Gas thermometric sensors are suitable for measuring very low or very high temperatures, but they are slow, bulky, and sensitive to external pressure changes.

- **Thermal expansion type thermometric sensors**: These sensors use the change in length, area, or volume of a solid or liquid due to temperature change. For example, a bimetallic strip consists of two thin strips of different metals (such as brass and steel) bonded together. The metals have different coefficients of thermal expansion, so they bend or curl as the temperature changes. Bimetallic strips are used in thermostats, thermometers, and fire alarms. Thermal expansion type thermometric sensors are simple, cheap, and reliable, but they have low accuracy, resolution, and sensitivity.

- **Acoustic temperature sensor**: This sensor uses the change in speed of sound in a medium due to temperature change. For example, a quartz crystal resonator consists of a thin slice of quartz crystal sandwiched between two electrodes. The crystal vibrates at a natural frequency when an alternating voltage is applied. The frequency depends on the temperature, as the speed of sound in the crystal changes with temperature. Acoustic temperature sensors are fast, stable, and accurate, but they require complex electronics and calibration.

- **Dielectric constant and refractive index thermo-sensors**: These sensors use the change in dielectric constant or refractive index of a material due to temperature change. For example, a capacitance thermometer consists of a capacitor with a dielectric material (such as air, oil, or plastic) between the plates. The capacitance depends on the temperature, as the dielectric constant of the material changes with temperature. Dielectric constant and refractive index thermo-sensors are sensitive, linear, and easy to integrate, but they are affected by humidity, pressure, and electromagnetic interference.

- **Helium low temperature thermometer**: This sensor uses the change in specific heat of helium gas due to temperature change. For example, a helium gas thermometer consists of a sealed bulb filled with helium gas and connected to a heater and a thermometer. The heater supplies a constant amount of heat to the gas, and the thermometer measures the temperature of the gas. The specific heat of helium gas depends on the temperature, and it has a sharp transition at 2.17 K, the lambda point. Helium low temperature thermometers are used to measure temperatures below 4 K, but they are expensive, complex, and require liquid helium.

- **Nuclear thermometer**: This sensor uses the change in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) or nuclear quadrupole resonance (NQR) of a material due to temperature change. For example, a NMR thermometer consists of a sample of a material (such as copper or silver) placed in a magnetic field and exposed to a radio frequency pulse. The NMR signal depends on the temperature, as the spin-lattice relaxation time of the material changes with temperature. Nuclear thermometers are very accurate, precise, and stable, but they require high magnetic fields, sophisticated electronics, and calibration.

- **Magnetic thermometer**: This sensor uses the change in magnetic properties of a material due to temperature change. For example, a Curie point thermometer consists of a sample of a ferromagnetic material (such as iron or nickel) placed in a magnetic field and heated or cooled. The magnetic susceptibility of the material depends on the temperature, and it drops to zero at the Curie point, the temperature at which the material loses its magnetization. Magnetic thermometers are used to measure temperatures above 1000 K, but they are nonlinear, hysteresis, and sensitive to external magnetic fields.

# Magnetic Sensors

## Introduction
Magnetic sensors are the sensors that convert the change or magnitude of a magnetic field into an electrical signal. Magnetic fields, as exemplified by the magnetic field of the earth (earth magnetism) or magnets are familiar yet invisible phenomena. Magnetic sensors convert invisible magnetic fields into electrical signals and help to have visible effects.

Magnetic sensors are the solid state devices used in detecting and sensing the distance, speed, rotation, angle, and position by converting magnetic information into electrical signals. The converted signals are processed by electrical circuits.

Magnetic sensors have various applications in automotive, industrial, consumer, medical, and security sectors. Some examples of magnetic sensors are Hall effect sensors, magnetoresistive sensors, inductance sensors, eddy current sensors, and SQUID sensors.

## Sensors and the Principles Behind
There are different types of magnetic sensors based on different principles of operation. Some of the common principles are:

- **Hall effect**: The Hall effect is the phenomenon of generating a voltage across a conductor when a magnetic field is applied perpendicular to the current flow. The Hall effect sensors measure the magnetic field strength by detecting the Hall voltage. Hall effect sensors can be used for detecting the presence or absence of a magnet, measuring the speed of a rotating object, or sensing the position of a linear or angular displacement.

- **Magnetoresistance**: Magnetoresistance is the property of some materials to change their electrical resistance when exposed to a magnetic field. The magnetoresistive sensors measure the magnetic field strength by detecting the change in resistance. Magnetoresistive sensors can be used for measuring the direction and angle of a magnetic field, detecting the polarity of a magnet, or sensing the position of a linear or angular displacement.

- **Inductance**: Inductance is the property of an electrical circuit or a coil to generate an electromotive force when the current or the magnetic flux changes. The inductance sensors measure the magnetic field strength by detecting the change in the induced voltage or current. Inductance sensors can be used for measuring the distance or speed of a moving object, detecting the presence or absence of a metal, or sensing the position of a linear or angular displacement.

- **Eddy current**: Eddy current is the phenomenon of generating circular currents in a conductor when a magnetic field changes. The eddy current sensors measure the magnetic field strength by detecting the change in the impedance of a coil due to the eddy currents. Eddy current sensors can be used for measuring the distance or speed of a moving object, detecting the presence or absence of a metal, or sensing the position of a linear or angular displacement.

- **SQUID**: SQUID stands for superconducting quantum interference device. It is a device that uses the quantum mechanical properties of superconductors to detect extremely weak magnetic fields. The SQUID sensors measure the magnetic field strength by detecting the change in the current or voltage across a superconducting loop. SQUID sensors can be used for measuring the magnetic fields of the brain, heart, or earth, detecting the presence or absence of a magnetic object, or sensing the position of a linear or angular displacement.

## Unit 3 - Radiation Sensors

- Introduction
  - Radiation sensors or radiation detectors are devices that can sense and measure radiation emissions or levels of radiation produced by a source  .
  - Radiation sensors or detectors transform a portion of the energy lost by incident radiation into an electrical signal that can be measured.
  - Radiation sensors or detectors are used for various applications, such as radiation protection, medical imaging, nuclear power, industrial process control, environmental monitoring, and scientific research .
- Basic Characteristics
  - The basic characteristics of radiation sensors or detectors are:
    - Sensitivity: the ability to detect low levels of radiation or small changes in radiation intensity.
    - Efficiency: the ratio of the number of radiation events detected to the number of radiation events incident on the detector.
    - Resolution: the ability to distinguish between different types or energies of radiation or to measure the energy of each radiation event.
    - Linearity: the proportionality between the output signal and the input radiation intensity.
    - Stability: the ability to maintain consistent performance over time and under varying environmental conditions.
    - Response time: the time required for the detector to produce an output signal after the radiation event occurs.
- Types of Photosensistors/Photo detectors
  - Photosensistors or photo detectors are devices that convert light into electrical current or voltage.
  - The main types of photosensistors or photo detectors are:
    - Photodiodes: semiconductor devices that generate a current proportional to the light intensity when reverse biased.
    - Phototransistors: semiconductor devices that amplify the current generated by a photodiode when forward biased.
    - Photovoltaic cells: semiconductor devices that generate a voltage proportional to the light intensity when connected to a load.
    - Photoresistors: resistive devices that change their resistance according to the light intensity.
    - Photomultiplier tubes: vacuum tubes that multiply the current generated by a photocathode by using a series of dynodes.
- X-ray and Nuclear Radiation Sensors
  - X-ray and nuclear radiation sensors are devices that can detect and measure X-rays and nuclear radiation, such as alpha, beta, gamma, and neutron radiation .
  - The main types of X-ray and nuclear radiation sensors are:
    - Gas-filled detectors: devices that use the ionization effect that occurs when radiation passes through a gas-filled chamber.
    - Scintillation detectors: devices that use the level of light energy produced when radiation interacts with a scintillating material.
    - Solid-state detectors: devices that use the charge carriers generated when radiation interacts with a semiconductor material.
- Fiber Optic Sensors
  - Fiber optic sensors are devices that use optical fibers to transmit, modulate, or reflect light according to the physical or chemical parameters to be measured.
  - The main types of fiber optic sensors are:
    - Intensity-based sensors: devices that measure the intensity of light at the output of the fiber, which depends on the attenuation or modulation of light in the fiber.
    - Phase-based sensors: devices that measure the phase difference between the light waves at the output of the fiber, which depends on the interference or modulation of light in the fiber.
    - Polarization-based sensors: devices that measure the polarization state of light at the output of the fiber, which depends on the birefringence or modulation of light in the fiber.
    - Wavelength-based sensors: devices that measure the wavelength of light at the output of the fiber, which depends on the dispersion or modulation of light in the fiber.



### Electro Analytical Sensors

- Electro analytical sensors are devices that measure the concentration or activity of an analyte in a solution by using an electrochemical reaction as the sensing principle .
- Electro analytical sensors can be classified into three main types based on the measurement technique: amperometric, potentiometric, and conductometric.
- Amperometric sensors measure the current generated by the oxidation or reduction of the analyte at an electrode surface. The current is proportional to the analyte concentration and can be detected by a potentiostat or a galvanostat circuit.
- Potentiometric sensors measure the potential difference between two electrodes in contact with the solution. The potential is related to the activity of the analyte by the Nernst equation and can be detected by a high-impedance voltmeter or a potentiometer circuit.
- Conductometric sensors measure the electrical conductivity or resistance of the solution, which depends on the concentration and mobility of the ions present. The conductivity or resistance can be detected by an alternating current (AC) bridge or a Wheatstone bridge circuit.

### The Electrochemical Cell

- An electrochemical cell is a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy or vice versa by using redox reactions.
- An electrochemical cell consists of two electrodes (anode and cathode) immersed in an electrolyte solution and connected by an external circuit. The electrodes are usually made of metals or other conductive materials.
- An electrochemical cell can be classified into two main types based on the direction of the current flow: galvanic or voltaic cells and electrolytic cells.
- Galvanic or voltaic cells produce electrical energy from spontaneous redox reactions. The anode is the electrode where oxidation occurs and the cathode is the electrode where reduction occurs. The electrons flow from the anode to the cathode through the external circuit, generating a positive cell potential.
- Electrolytic cells consume electrical energy to drive non-spontaneous redox reactions. The anode is the electrode where reduction occurs and the cathode is the electrode where oxidation occurs. The electrons flow from the cathode to the anode through the external circuit, generating a negative cell potential.

### The Cell Potential

- The cell potential or electromotive force (EMF) of an electrochemical cell is the difference in electrical potential between the two electrodes. It is a measure of the driving force for the redox reaction and the amount of electrical energy that can be obtained or consumed by the cell.
- The cell potential can be calculated by using the standard electrode potentials of the two electrodes, which are the potentials of the electrodes when they are in equilibrium with their respective ions in a 1 M solution at 25°C and 1 atm.
- The standard cell potential (E°cell) is the cell potential when the cell operates under standard conditions. It can be calculated by using the following equation:

E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode

- The actual cell potential (Ecell) is the cell potential when the cell operates under non-standard conditions. It can be calculated by using the following equation, which is derived from the Nernst equation:

Ecell = E°cell - (RT/nF) ln Q

where R is the universal gas constant, T is the absolute temperature, n is the number of electrons transferred in the redox reaction, F is the Faraday constant, and Q is the reaction quotient.

### Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

- The standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) is a reference electrode that is used to measure the standard electrode potentials of other electrodes. It is defined as a platinum electrode in contact with a 1 M H+ solution and a hydrogen gas at 1 atm.
- The standard hydrogen electrode has a standard electrode potential of 0 V by convention. It is the basis for the electrochemical series, which is a list of electrodes arranged in order of their standard electrode potentials.
- The standard hydrogen electrode is rarely used in practice because it is difficult to maintain and operate. Instead, other reference electrodes, such as the saturated calomel electrode (SCE) or the silver-silver chloride electrode (Ag/AgCl), are used to measure the electrode potentials of other electrodes.

### Smart Sensors: Introduction, Primary Sensors, Excitation, Amplification, Filters, Converters, Compensation, Information Coding/Processing, Data Communication, Standards for Smart Sensor Interface, the Automation.

- A smart sensor is a device that uses a transducer to gather particular data from a physical environment to perform a predefined & programmed function on the particular type of gathered data then it transmits the data through a networked connection.
- A smart sensor has three components: a sensor that captures data, a microprocessor that computes on the output of the sensor via programming and communications capabilities. A smart sensor might also include several other components besides the primary sensor.
- The primary sensor is the component that converts the physical phenomenon into an electrical signal. The primary sensor can be based on different principles, such as resistive, capacitive, inductive, optical, magnetic, etc.
- The excitation is the component that provides the necessary energy or stimulus to the primary sensor to generate the output signal. The excitation can be constant or variable, depending on the type of sensor.
- The amplification is the component that increases the magnitude of the output signal from the primary sensor to a suitable level for further processing. The amplification can be analog or digital, depending on the type of signal.
- The filters are the components that remove the unwanted noise or interference from the output signal of the primary sensor or the amplifier. The filters can be analog or digital, depending on the type of signal and the desired frequency range.
- The converters are the components that change the format of the output signal from the primary sensor or the amplifier to a compatible one for the microprocessor. The converters can be analog-to-digital (ADC) or digital-to-analog (DAC), depending on the direction of the conversion.
- The compensation is the component that corrects the output signal from the primary sensor or the amplifier for the effects of environmental factors, such as temperature, humidity, pressure, etc. The compensation can be done by using additional sensors, calibration data, or mathematical algorithms.
- The information coding/processing is the component that performs the predefined and programmed function on the output signal from the primary sensor or the amplifier. The information coding/processing can be done by using logic circuits, microcontrollers, or microprocessors, depending on the complexity of the function.
- The data communication is the component that transmits the output signal from the information coding/processing to a networked connection. The data communication can be done by using wired or wireless methods, such as Ethernet, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, etc.
- The standards for smart sensor interface are the protocols or rules that define how the smart sensor communicates with other devices or systems. The standards for smart sensor interface can be based on different criteria, such as data format, data rate, data security, etc.
- The automation is the component that controls the operation of the smart sensor or the system that uses the smart sensor. The automation can be done by using feedback loops, timers, triggers, or commands, depending on the desired outcome.

- Pneumatic systems are suitable for applications that require low to medium force, high speed, and frequent cycling.
- Pneumatic systems are energy inefficient, noisy, and sensitive to air quality and moisture.

- Hydraulic actuation systems use pressurized liquid, usually oil, as the working fluid to generate force and movement.
- Hydraulic systems are powerful, precise, smooth, and adaptable to various loads and conditions.

## Unit 5 - Introduction of Signal Processing

Signal processing is the science of analyzing, modifying, and synthesizing signals, such as sound, images, and biological measurements. Signal processing can be performed in analog or digital domains, using different techniques and tools.

### Classification of systems

A system is a mathematical model that describes how an input signal is transformed into an output signal. Systems can be classified according to different properties, such as:

- **Continuous** or **discrete**: A continuous system operates on continuous-time signals, while a discrete system operates on discrete-time signals.
- **Linear** or **nonlinear**: A linear system satisfies the superposition principle, which means that the output of the system for a linear combination of inputs is the same linear combination of the outputs for each input. A nonlinear system does not satisfy this property.
- **Causal** or **noncausal**: A causal system depends only on the present and past values of the input signal, while a noncausal system can also depend on the future values of the input signal.
- **Stable** or **unstable**: A stable system produces bounded outputs for bounded inputs, while an unstable system can produce unbounded outputs for bounded inputs.
- **Dynamic** or **static**: A dynamic system has memory, which means that the output of the system depends not only on the current input, but also on the previous inputs. A static system has no memory, which means that the output of the system depends only on the current input.
- **Recursive** or **nonrecursive**: A recursive system uses feedback, which means that the output of the system is fed back to the input through a delay element. A nonrecursive system does not use feedback.
- **Time-invariant** or **time-variant**: A time-invariant system does not change its behavior over time, which means that the output of the system for a given input is the same regardless of when the input is applied. A time-variant system changes its behavior over time, which means that the output of the system for a given input depends on when the input is applied.

### Classification of signals

A signal is a function that conveys information about a phenomenon. Signals can be classified according to different properties, such as:

- **Continuous** or **discrete**: A continuous signal is defined for all values of time, while a discrete signal is defined only for discrete values of time.
- **Energy** or **power**: An energy signal has finite energy, which means that the integral of the square of the signal over all time is finite. A power signal has finite power, which means that the average of the square of the signal over a finite interval of time is finite.
- **Mathematical representation of signals**: Signals can be represented in different ways, such as:

  - **Time domain**: The signal is expressed as a function of time, such as $x(t)$ for a continuous signal or $x[n]$ for a discrete signal.
  - **Frequency domain**: The signal is expressed as a function of frequency, such as $X(f)$ for a continuous signal or $X(e^{j\omega})$ for a discrete signal. The frequency domain representation can be obtained by applying a transform, such as the Fourier transform, to the time domain representation.
  - **Spectral density**: The spectral density is a measure of how the energy or power of a signal is distributed over different frequencies. The spectral density can be obtained by taking the magnitude squared of the frequency domain representation of the signal.

### Sampling techniques

Sampling is the process of converting a continuous signal into a discrete signal by taking samples of the signal at regular intervals of time. Sampling can be performed in different ways, such as:

- **Ideal sampling**: Ideal sampling is a theoretical model that assumes that the samples are taken instantaneously and without any distortion. ### Digital Signal Processors: Introduction – Architecture – Features – Addressing Formats – Functional modes – Introduction to Commercial Processors

- Introduction
  - Digital Signal Processing is the process of representing signals in a discrete mathematical sequence of numbers and analyzing, modifying, and extracting the information contained in the signal by carrying out algorithmic operations and processing on the signal.
  - Digital Signal Processors (DSP) are specialized microprocessors that take real-world signals like voice, audio, video, temperature, pressure, or position that have been digitized and then mathematically manipulate them.
  - DSPs are mainly used for applications that require high-speed and high-accuracy computations, such as audio and video processing, communications, biomedical engineering, radar, sonar, etc.
- Architecture
  - DSPs have a unique architecture that is optimized for performing repetitive and numerically intensive tasks.
  - Most DSPs include a powerful data path and also the capacity to move large amounts of data to memory quickly.
  - The data path consists of multiple functional units, such as arithmetic logic units (ALUs), multipliers, accumulators, shifters, etc., that can operate in parallel and independently.
  - The memory system consists of multiple levels of memory, such as registers, cache, internal and external RAM, ROM, etc., that can be accessed by the data path with low latency and high bandwidth.
  - The control unit consists of an instruction decoder, a program counter, a pipeline controller, etc., that can fetch and execute multiple instructions per cycle.
  - Some DSPs also have specialized hardware units, such as direct memory access (DMA) controllers, serial and parallel ports, timers, interrupt controllers, etc., that can handle peripheral and external devices without involving the CPU.

    - Fixed-point and floating-point arithmetic support: DSPs can perform arithmetic operations on different formats of numbers, such as integers, fractions, and exponents, depending on the accuracy and dynamic range required by the application.
    - Single-cycle instruction execution: DSPs can execute most instructions in one clock cycle, which reduces the instruction overhead and increases the throughput.
