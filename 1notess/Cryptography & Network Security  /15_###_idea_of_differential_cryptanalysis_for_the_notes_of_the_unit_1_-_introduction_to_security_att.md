Differential cryptanalysis is a technique used to analyze the security of cryptographic algorithms by finding pairs of plaintext/ciphertext pairs that differ by a specific difference. The idea behind differential cryptanalysis is to study how differences in the plaintext affect the ciphertext, and to use this information to determine the encryption key.

In block ciphers, differential cryptanalysis is used to study the behavior of the cipher when pairs of plaintext blocks are XORed together and the resulting ciphertext blocks are XORed together. This allows the analyst to determine how the cipher behaves when the input changes, and to use this information to determine the encryption key.

One of the most famous applications of differential cryptanalysis was the attack on the Data Encryption Standard (DES), which was widely used in the 1970s and 1980s. The attack showed that DES was vulnerable to differential cryptanalysis and that the encryption key could be determined with a relatively small number of ciphertext/plaintext pairs.

Differential cryptanalysis is an important concept in modern block ciphers, as it helps to determine the security of the cipher and to identify potential weaknesses that can be exploited by attackers. Other modern block ciphers, such as Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), have been designed to resist differential cryptanalysis, and are widely used to protect sensitive information in modern systems.
