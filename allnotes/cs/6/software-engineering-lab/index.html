Software Project Management

Software project management is the process of planning and leading software projects. It is a sub-discipline of project management in which software projects are planned, implemented, monitored and controlled.

Some of the main aspects of software project management are:

  • Gathering client requirements: This involves understanding the needs and expectations of the client, as well as the scope and constraints of the project.
  • Estimating cost and time: This involves estimating the resources, budget and schedule needed to complete the project, as well as the risks and uncertainties involved.
  • Planning and designing: This involves defining the tasks, activities, deliverables and milestones of the project, as well as the roles and responsibilities of the project team.
  • Executing and monitoring: This involves implementing the project plan, tracking the progress and performance of the project, and managing any changes or issues that arise.
  • Closing and delivering: This involves completing the project, ensuring the quality and satisfaction of the client, and handing over the final product or service.

Software project management can be done using different methods and tools, depending on the nature and complexity of the project. Some of the common methods and tools are:

  • Waterfall model: This is a linear and sequential approach, where each phase of the project is completed before moving to the next one. It is suitable for well-defined and stable projects, but not for dynamic and uncertain ones.
  • Agile model: This is an iterative and incremental approach, where the project is divided into small and manageable units called sprints. Each sprint delivers a working product or feature, and the feedback from the client is used to improve the next sprint. It is suitable for complex and changing projects, but not for simple and predictable ones.

  • ClickUp: This is a cloud-based project management tool that helps teams collaborate and deliver software projects. It allows creating and managing goals, tasks, subtasks, checklists, dependencies, time tracking, documents, chat, and more.
  • Gantt chart: This is a graphical representation of the project schedule, showing the start and end dates, durations, dependencies and progress of each task or activity. It helps visualize and monitor the project timeline and milestones.

Methodologies in Software Project Management

  • A project management methodology is a way of organizing the software development process using a set of principles, guidelines, tools, communication channels, etc.

  • A project management methodology can impact the planning, controlling and implementation of a project.

  • There are many different project management methodologies, each with its own advantages, disadvantages, and suitability for different types of projects.

  • Some of the most common project management methodologies are:

    • Agile: An iterative and incremental approach that emphasizes fast responses to customer feedback and changes, focus on effective communication, and collaborative efforts.
    • Waterfall: A linear and sequential approach that follows a predefined set of stages, such as requirements, design, implementation, testing, and deployment.
    • Scrum: A subset of agile that uses short cycles called sprints, where a cross-functional team works on a prioritized list of tasks and delivers a potentially shippable product increment at the end of each sprint.
    • PRINCE2: A structured and process-based approach that divides the project into manageable stages, each with clear roles and responsibilities, deliverables, and quality criteria.
    • Lean: A customer-centric and waste-minimizing approach that aims to deliver value to the customer by eliminating any activities that do not add value to the product or service.
    • Six Sigma: A data-driven and quality-focused approach that uses statistical tools and techniques to measure and improve the performance of the processes and products.
    • Extreme Programming: A subset of agile that emphasizes frequent releases, continuous integration, test-driven development, pair programming, and code refactoring.
    • Critical Path Method: A network-based approach that identifies the longest sequence of tasks that must be completed on time to finish the project on schedule, and allocates resources accordingly.
    • Gantt Chart Method: A graphical approach that shows the start and end dates, dependencies, and progress of each task in a project, using horizontal bars on a timeline.
    • Kanban: A subset of agile that uses a visual board to limit the work in progress and optimize the flow of tasks from one stage to another.
    • Crystal: A subset of agile that adapts to the size, complexity, and criticality of the project, and emphasizes frequent delivery, close communication, and reflective improvement.
    • Spiral: A risk-driven and iterative approach that combines the elements of waterfall and prototyping, and evaluates the risks and alternatives at each cycle.

  • The choice of the project management methodology depends on various factors, such as the project scope, budget, timeline, quality, customer expectations, team size and skills, and organizational culture.

  • The project manager should evaluate the pros and cons of each methodology and select the one that best suits the project needs and goals.

Risk Evaluation in SPM

Risk evaluation is an important part of the Software Project Management (SPM) process. It is used to identify potential risks and to assess their impact on the project. The risk evaluation process consists of four steps:

  • Analyzing the project environment: This step involves understanding the project scope, objectives, deliverables, stakeholders, resources, constraints, and assumptions. It also involves identifying the external and internal factors that may affect the project, such as market conditions, customer expectations, legal regulations, technical challenges, organizational culture, etc.
  • Identifying risks: This step involves brainstorming, researching, and listing all the possible risks that may affect the project. Risks can be categorized into different types, such as technical, operational, financial, legal, environmental, etc. Risks can also be classified into positive or negative, depending on whether they represent opportunities or threats for the project.
  • Assigning risk ratings: This step involves estimating the probability and impact of each risk on the project. Probability is the likelihood of a risk occurring, and impact is the severity of the consequences if the risk occurs. Risk ratings can be expressed in qualitative or quantitative terms, such as low, medium, high, or numeric values. A common tool for assigning risk ratings is the risk matrix, which plots the probability and impact of each risk on a grid, and assigns a color code to indicate the level of risk.   • Creating a risk management plan: This step involves developing strategies and actions to prevent, reduce, transfer, or accept the risks. The risk management plan should include the following elements:

    • Risk owner: The person or group responsible for managing the risk.
    • Risk response: The action or decision to deal with the risk, such as avoiding, mitigating, transferring, or accepting the risk.
    • Risk trigger: The event or condition that indicates the risk is about to occur or has occurred.
    • Risk contingency: The backup plan or alternative action to deal with the risk if the risk response fails or is not sufficient.
    • Risk monitoring: The process of tracking and reviewing the risk status and the effectiveness of the risk response.
    • Risk communication: The process of informing and updating the relevant stakeholders about the risk and the risk management plan.

  • Step 2: Identify project infrastructure. This involves identifying the resources, roles, responsibilities, and communication channels that are needed for the project execution.
  • Step 3: Analyze project characteristics. This involves identifying the key features, functions, and requirements of the project, as well as the risks and assumptions.
  • Step 4: Identify project products and activities. This involves listing the main products and activities that are required to complete the project, and their dependencies and relationships.

  • Step 6: Identify activity risks. This involves identifying the potential threats and opportunities that may affect the project activities, and their likelihood and impact.
  • Step 7: Allocate resources. This involves assigning the available resources, such as people, equipment, and materials, to the project activities, based on their availability, suitability, and priority.
  • Step 8: Review and adjust plan. This involves reviewing the project plan for completeness, consistency, and feasibility, and making any necessary adjustments or revisions.
  • Step 9: Execute and control plan. This involves implementing the project plan, monitoring and controlling the project progress, and managing any changes or issues that may arise.

  • Closure phase: This is the fourth and final phase of the project life cycle, where the project is formally completed and closed. The project manager and the project team verify that all the project deliverables are delivered and accepted by the customer or the sponsor. The disadvantage is that it can be time-consuming and costly and may lead to unrealistic expectations.

The disadvantage is that it can require more planning and coordination and may result in inconsistent interfaces and architectures.
    • Spiral model: This is a type of software process model that combines the iterative and risk-driven aspects of prototyping and incremental models. It follows a circular process, where each cycle consists of four phases: planning, risk analysis, engineering, and evaluation. The advantage of this model is that it can handle complex and large-scale projects and address changing requirements and risks. The disadvantage is that it can be expensive and difficult to manage and may require a lot of documentation and expertise.

The disadvantage is that it can be challenging to scale and integrate and may require a high level of commitment and discipline from the team and the customer.

The advantage of this model is that it can ensure high quality and reliability and detect errors early. The disadvantage is that it can be rigid and inflexible and may not handle changes well.

  • Waterfall model: This is a linear and sequential process model that follows a fixed set of phases, such as requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, and maintenance.   • Prototyping model: This is an iterative and incremental process model that involves building and refining a prototype of the software until it meets the requirements and expectations of the users. This model is suitable for projects that have evolving and dynamic requirements, high user feedback, and incremental improvement.

  • Effort and cost estimation are important activities in software project management (SPM) that help to plan, monitor and control a software project.

Expert Judgment

  • Expert judgment is a technique that relies on the experience and intuition of one or more experts who have knowledge of similar projects or domains.
  • The experts can use various methods to provide their estimates, such as Delphi method, work breakdown structure (WBS), planning poker, etc.

Algorithmic Models

  • Algorithmic models are mathematical formulas that relate the effort or cost of a software project to one or more measurable attributes, such as size, complexity, functionality, quality, etc.
  • The most common algorithmic models are parametric models, such as COCOMO, SLIM, SEER-SEM, etc., which use regression analysis or other statistical methods to derive the parameters and coefficients of the formulas from historical data of similar projects.
  • The advantages of algorithmic models are that they are objective, consistent and based on empirical evidence.
  • The disadvantages of algorithmic models are that they are rigid, require accurate and reliable data, and may not capture all the factors that affect the effort or cost of a software project.

  • The advantages of analogy-based methods are that they are adaptable, intuitive and can handle uncertainty and variability.

### COCOMO II in SPM

COCOMO II is a software cost estimation model that was developed by Barry Boehm and his colleagues at the University of Southern California in the 1990s and 2000s. It is an extension and refinement of the original COCOMO model that was proposed by Boehm in 1981. COCOMO stands for Constructive Cost Model, and it is based on the empirical analysis of historical data from many software projects.

COCOMO II consists of three sub-models that can be applied at different stages of the software development life cycle:

- **Application Composition Model**: This model is used for estimating the effort and schedule of rapid application development (RAD) projects that use application generators, code reuse, or other productivity tools. It is based on the number of object points, which are a measure of the functionality and complexity of the software components.
- **Early Design Model**: This model is used for estimating the effort and schedule of projects that are in the initial stages of design, before the architecture is defined. It is based on the size of the software in terms of source lines of code (SLOC) or function points (FP), and it uses a set of 15 cost drivers that capture the characteristics of the product, the platform, the personnel, and the project.
- **Post-Architecture Model**: This model is used for estimating the effort and schedule of projects that have a defined architecture and a detailed design. It is also based on the size of the software in terms of SLOC or FP, and it uses a set of 17 cost drivers that capture the same characteristics as the Early Design Model, plus two additional ones that reflect the quality and risk of the software.

`Effort = A * Size^E * M`

### Activity Planning

Activity planning is the process of defining and sequencing the tasks, milestones, and deliverables of a software project. It involves:

- Identifying the activities that need to be performed to produce the software product.
- Estimating the effort, duration, and resources required for each activity.
- Determining the dependencies and constraints among the activities.
- Creating a schedule that shows the start and end dates of each activity and the allocation of resources.
- Monitoring and controlling the progress and performance of the activities.

Some of the tools and techniques used for activity planning are:

- Work breakdown structure (WBS): A hierarchical decomposition of the project scope into smaller and manageable units of work.
- Network diagram: A graphical representation of the logical relationships and dependencies among the project activities.
- Gantt chart: A bar chart that shows the planned and actual start and end dates of the project activities along the horizontal axis and the activities along the vertical axis.
- Critical path method (CPM): A technique that identifies the longest sequence of activities that determines the minimum duration of the project.
- Program evaluation and review technique (PERT): A technique that incorporates uncertainty and variability in the activity duration estimates by using three values: optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic.
- Resource leveling: A technique that adjusts the start and end dates of the activities to balance the demand and availability of resources.

### Risk Management

Risk management is the process of identifying, analyzing, and responding to the uncertainties and threats that may affect the software project. It involves:

- Planning risk management: Defining how to perform risk management activities for a project.
- Identifying risks: Determining the sources and causes of potential risks and their characteristics.
- Analyzing risks: Assessing the probability and impact of each risk and prioritizing them according to their severity and exposure.
- Evaluating risks: Deciding how to deal with each risk based on the risk appetite and tolerance of the stakeholders.
- Treating risks: Implementing strategies to avoid, reduce, transfer, or accept the risks and their consequences.
- Monitoring risks: Tracking and reviewing the status and effectiveness of the risk management plan and the risk responses.

Some of the tools and techniques used for risk management are:

- Risk register: A document that records and organizes the identified risks, their analysis, and their responses.
- Risk matrix: A table that shows the probability and impact of each risk and their corresponding risk level (low, medium, high, etc.).
- SWOT analysis: A technique that identifies the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats of the project or the organization.
- Brainstorming: A technique that generates a list of possible risks by using the collective knowledge and creativity of a group of people.
- Delphi method: A technique that collects and consolidates the opinions of experts on the potential risks and their estimates.
- Monte Carlo simulation: A technique that uses random values and statistical models to simulate the outcomes of the project under different scenarios and uncertainties.

Change Control in SPM

Change control is a process that ensures that any proposed changes to a software product or system are evaluated, approved, and implemented in a systematic and consistent manner. Change control is part of the broader discipline of software configuration management (SCM), which aims to manage the evolution and integrity of software artifacts, such as code, documentation, and requirements.

The main objectives of change control are:

  • To ensure that changes are aligned with the project goals and stakeholder expectations
  • To minimize the risks and costs associated with changes
  • To maintain the quality and functionality of the software product or system
  • To document and track the history and status of changes
  • To facilitate communication and collaboration among the project team and stakeholders

The change control process typically involves the following steps :

  1. Propose change: A change request (CR) is submitted by anyone who identifies a need for a change, such as a project team member, a stakeholder, or a customer. The CR should describe the change, the reason for the change, the expected benefits and impacts of the change, and the priority and urgency of the change.
  2. Impact summary: The CR is evaluated by a change control board (CCB), which is a group of authorized people who review and approve changes. The CCB assesses the technical merit, potential side effects, overall impact on other configuration objects and system functions, and the projected cost and schedule of the change. The CCB may also consult with other experts or stakeholders to gather more information or feedback on the change.
  3. Making a decision: The CCB decides whether to approve, defer, or reject the change, based on the impact summary and the project constraints. The CCB may also suggest modifications or alternatives to the change, or request more information or clarification from the change requester. The decision and the rationale behind it are documented and communicated to the change requester and other relevant parties.
  4. Make the change: If the change is approved, the CCB assigns the change to a responsible person or team, who implements the change according to the agreed specifications and standards. The change implementation may involve modifying the code, updating the documentation, testing the change, and deploying the change to the target environment. The change implementation should also follow the established change management procedures and tools, such as version control, change tracking, and change notification.
  5. Closure: After the change is implemented, the CCB verifies that the change meets the requirements and expectations, and that no new issues or defects are introduced by the change. The CCB also updates the change status and records the change in the change log. The change closure marks the end of the change control process for that change.

Some of the benefits of change control in SPM are :

  • It improves the visibility and traceability of changes, which helps to avoid confusion, duplication, or inconsistency among the project team and stakeholders
  • It enhances the quality and reliability of the software product or system, by ensuring that changes are tested and validated before they are deployed
  • It reduces the risks and costs associated with changes, by preventing unnecessary, unauthorized, or incompatible changes, and by minimizing the impact of changes on the project scope, schedule, and budget
  • It increases the customer satisfaction and trust, by delivering changes that meet their needs and expectations, and by involving them in the change decision process
  • It fosters a culture of continuous improvement and innovation, by encouraging the project team and stakeholders to identify and propose changes that can add value or solve problems

Some of the challenges of change control in SPM are:

  • It can be time-consuming and complex, especially for large or complex projects that involve many changes, dependencies, and stakeholders
  • It can be difficult to balance the competing demands and interests of different parties, such as the project team, the customer, the management, and the regulators
  • It can be affected by human factors, such as resistance to change, lack of communication, or poor judgment
  • It can be influenced by external factors, such as market changes, technological changes, or legal changes

To overcome these challenges, some of the best practices for change control in SPM are:

  • Establish a clear and consistent change control process and policy, and communicate them to the project team and stakeholders
  • Define the roles and responsibilities of the CCB and the change requesters, and ensure that they have the necessary authority, skills, and resources to perform their tasks
  • Use appropriate change management tools and techniques, such as change request forms, change logs, change tracking systems, and change notification systems

  • Audit: This is the process of verifying that the CIs and the CRs are consistent, complete, and correct. A configuration audit is a formal inspection that checks the compliance of the CIs and the CRs with the specifications, standards, and regulations.
  • Review: This is the process of evaluating the quality and performance of the software product and the SCM process. A configuration review is a formal meeting that involves the stakeholders, such as the developers, the testers, the customers, the managers, etc. The purpose is to identify the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats of the software product and the SCM process.

SCM is a part of the larger cross-disciplinary field of configuration management (CM), which also covers the management of hardware, network, and other resources. Assigning can be done by the project manager, the team leader, or the developers themselves, depending on the project methodology and the level of autonomy.     • Autonomy: the degree to which a job provides freedom, independence and discretion to the individual in scheduling the work and determining the procedures to be used .

Organizational Structures in SPM

Organizational structures in SPM (Strategic Performance Management) are the ways in which an organization arranges its resources, roles, and responsibilities to achieve its strategic objectives. Organizational structures can influence the efficiency, effectiveness, and adaptability of the organization and its projects.

There are different types of organizational structures that can be used in SPM, depending on the nature, size, and complexity of the organization and its projects. Some of the common types are:

  • Functional/Hierarchical: This is the traditional structure where the organization is divided into functional units or departments, such as finance, marketing, production, etc. Each unit has its own manager and staff, and they report to a higher-level manager or executive. Projects are usually managed within the functional units, and the project manager has limited authority and resources. This structure is suitable for stable and routine projects, but it can create silos, conflicts, and communication barriers among different units.

  • Matrix: This is a hybrid structure where the organization combines the functional and project-based structures. There are two types of matrix structures: weak, balanced, and strong. In a weak matrix, the functional managers have more authority and control over the projects than the project managers. In a balanced matrix, the functional and project managers share the authority and responsibility for the projects. In a strong matrix, the project managers have more authority and control over the projects than the functional managers. This structure is suitable for complex and dynamic projects, but it can create role ambiguity, power struggles, and dual reporting.

  • RACI: This is a tool that defines the roles and responsibilities of the project stakeholders using four categories: Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. Responsible means the person who performs the task or activity. Accountable means the person who approves or signs off the task or activity. Consulted means the person who provides input or feedback for the task or activity. Informed means the person who is notified or updated about the task or activity. This tool is useful for clarifying the expectations, communication, and accountability of the project stakeholders, but it can also create overlaps, gaps, or conflicts in the roles and responsibilities.

  • Agile: This is a flexible and adaptive structure where the organization is organized around cross-functional and self-organizing teams that deliver value to the customers in short iterations or sprints. The teams have a high degree of autonomy and collaboration, and they use agile principles and practices to manage the projects. The project manager role is replaced by a product owner, who represents the customer’s needs and priorities, and a scrum master, who facilitates the team’s work and removes impediments. This structure is suitable for innovative and uncertain projects, but it can also create challenges in scaling, coordination, and governance.

Dispersed and Virtual Teams in SPM

  • Dispersed and virtual teams are groups of people who work together on common projects across different locations, time zones, cultures, and organizational boundaries.
  • They use information and communication technologies (ICT) to coordinate their tasks and collaborate with each other.
  • Dispersed and virtual teams are becoming more common in software project management (SPM) due to the globalization of markets, the availability of skilled workers, the flexibility of work arrangements, and the cost savings of reducing travel and office expenses.
  • Dispersed and virtual teams face some challenges and risks that are different from those of co-located teams, such as:
    • Communication difficulties due to language barriers, cultural differences, and lack of non-verbal cues.
    • Coordination problems due to time differences, task interdependencies, and unclear roles and responsibilities.
    • Trust issues due to limited social interaction, low visibility, and potential conflicts of interest.
    • Motivation and performance issues due to isolation, lack of feedback, and reduced accountability.
  • Dispersed and virtual teams can overcome these challenges and achieve success by adopting some best practices, such as:
    • Establishing clear and shared goals, expectations, and norms for the team.
    • Selecting appropriate ICT tools and platforms that suit the team’s needs and preferences.
    • Communicating frequently, openly, and respectfully with the team members and stakeholders.
    • Building trust and rapport among the team members through social and informal interactions.
    • Providing feedback, recognition, and support to the team members and monitoring their progress and performance.
    • Managing conflicts and resolving issues promptly and constructively.

Communication Genres in SPM

  • Communication genres are the methods of communication that are selected and developed to deal with a particular need for project coordination.
  • Communication genres can be classified into four categories based on the place and time of communication :

Different Time Noticeboards, Pigeon-holes, Documents, etc. E-mail, Voicemail, Fax, etc.
  • Communication genres are influenced by the following factors :
    • The project life cycle stage: Different stages require different levels and modes of communication. For example, the early stages of a project require more face-to-face communication to build trust and confidence among team members, while the later stages may rely more on written documents and reports to document the progress and outcomes of the project.
    • The project size and complexity: Larger and more complex projects involve more stakeholders and more information to be communicated. Therefore, they may need more formal and structured communication genres, such as project plans, schedules, budgets, risk registers, etc.
    • The project culture and environment: The communication genres should be aligned with the organizational culture and the project environment. For example, some organizations may prefer more informal and flexible communication genres, such as e-mail and instant messaging, while others may prefer more formal and rigid communication genres, such as memos and meetings.
    • The project communication plan: The communication plan is a document that specifies the communication genres, frequency, purpose, audience, and responsibility for each communication activity in the project. The communication plan helps to ensure that the communication is effective, efficient, and consistent throughout the project.
  • Communication genres are important for the success of software project management because they :
    • Facilitate the exchange of information and feedback among project stakeholders, such as project managers, team members, clients, users, sponsors, etc.
    • Support the coordination and collaboration of project activities, such as planning, design, development, testing, deployment, etc.
    • Enhance the quality and performance of the project deliverables, such as software products, services, documents, etc.
    • Promote the trust and commitment of project stakeholders, which can improve the motivation and satisfaction of the project team and the satisfaction and loyalty of the project clients and users.

Communication Plans in SPM

  • Communication plans are essential for effective project management, as they define how important information will be communicated to stakeholders throughout the project.
  • Communication plans also specify who will receive the information, how they will receive it, when they will receive it, and how often they will receive it.
  • Communication plans help to ensure that all stakeholders are informed, engaged, and aligned with the project goals, scope, schedule, budget, risks, and issues.
  • Communication plans can be created using a simple template that includes the following elements:
    • Communication objectives: what are the main purposes and outcomes of the communication?
    • Communication methods: what are the best ways to deliver the information, such as email, phone, meeting, report, presentation, etc.?
    • Communication frequency: how often will the information be communicated, such as daily, weekly, monthly, etc.?
    • Communication roles and responsibilities: who will be responsible for sending, receiving, and responding to the information, and what are their expectations and accountabilities?
    • Communication stakeholders: who are the target audiences for the information, and what are their needs, interests, and preferences?
    • Communication feedback: how will the effectiveness and impact of the communication be measured and improved, such as surveys, polls, metrics, etc.?
  • Communication plans should be aligned with the project management plan and the strategic performance management (SPM) framework, which is a multistep process that guides an organization’s leadership in designing and revising a system of strategic performance management.
  • Communication is key to the success of any change initiative, and SPM is no exception. A communication lens is applied to SPM to strengthen its implementation and success.
  • A communication lens involves the following steps:
    • Assess the current communication environment and identify the gaps and opportunities for improvement.
    • Develop a communication strategy that defines the goals, objectives, messages, audiences, channels, and resources for SPM communication.
    • Implement the communication strategy and monitor its progress and impact.
    • Evaluate the communication strategy and make adjustments as needed.

Leadership in SPM

  • Leadership is the ability to influence, motivate, and guide others to achieve a common goal or vision.
  • Leadership is essential for effective project management, as it helps to align the project objectives with the organizational strategy, communicate the project vision and expectations, resolve conflicts and issues, and foster a positive and collaborative team culture.
  • Leadership is not a fixed trait or position, but a dynamic and situational process that can be learned and developed by anyone.
  • Leadership can be demonstrated by various attributes and actions, such as:
    • Setting a clear and compelling direction for the project
    • Inspiring and empowering the team members to perform their best
    • Providing feedback and recognition to the team members
    • Adapting to changing circumstances and challenges
    • Making sound and timely decisions based on data and analysis
    • Communicating effectively with different stakeholders and audiences
    • Building trust and rapport with the team members and other parties
    • Developing oneself and others through continuous learning and improvement
  • Leadership can be influenced by various factors, such as:
    • The personality, values, and style of the leader
    • The characteristics, needs, and expectations of the team members
    • The nature, scope, and complexity of the project
    • The culture, norms, and policies of the organization
    • The external environment, opportunities, and threats
  • Leadership can be applied to different levels and contexts of project management, such as:
    • Individual leadership: the ability to lead oneself and manage one’s own tasks, time, and resources
    • Team leadership: the ability to lead a group of people and coordinate their efforts, roles, and responsibilities
    • Organizational leadership: the ability to lead a larger entity and align its vision, mission, and values with the project goals
    • Strategic leadership: the ability to lead a change initiative and influence the direction, performance, and sustainability of the organization
  • Leadership can be evaluated and improved by various methods, such as:
    • Self-assessment: the process of reflecting on one’s own strengths and weaknesses as a leader and identifying areas for improvement
    • Feedback: the process of receiving and giving constructive comments and suggestions from others on one’s leadership performance and behavior
    • Coaching: the process of receiving and providing guidance and support from a mentor or a coach on how to enhance one’s leadership skills and competencies
    • Training: the process of attending and participating in formal or informal learning activities and programs that aim to develop one’s leadership knowledge and abilities
    • Practice: the process of applying and experimenting with different leadership techniques and strategies in real or simulated situations and scenarios
