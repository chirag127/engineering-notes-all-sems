1. Intellectual property (IP) refers to creations of the mind, such as inventions; literary and artistic works; designs; and symbols, names and images used in commerce.

2. IP is protected in law by, for example, patents, copyright and trademarks, which enable people to earn recognition or financial benefit from what they invent or create.

3. IP is a driver of economic growth, creativity and innovation. It encourages investment in research and development, and stimulates the creation and commercialization of new products and services.

4. IP also promotes competition and enhances the value of products and services.

5. IP laws vary from country to country. In general, they aim to strike a balance between the interests of creators and the wider public interest.

6. The main international treaties on IP are the Paris Convention for the Protection of Industrial Property and the Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works.

7. The World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), a specialized agency of the United Nations, is responsible for administering these treaties and promoting the development of a balanced and effective international IP system.

8. WIPO also administers a number of other treaties, including the WIPO Copyright Treaty and the WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty, which establish international standards for copyright and related rights.

9. WIPO provides a range of services to member states, including technical assistance, capacity building and the promotion of effective national IP systems.

10. WIPO also cooperates with a number of international organizations, including the World Trade Organization (WTO), in order to ensure that IP rights are respected and enforced globally.
