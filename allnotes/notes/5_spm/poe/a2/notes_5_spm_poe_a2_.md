# Software Project Management

1. Project Initiation: This is the first phase of a software project. The project manager, stakeholders and team are identified. The scope, requirements, constraints, risks and priorities of the project are determined. A project charter is prepared.

2. Project Planning: A detailed project plan is created which includes the schedule, budget, and resource allocation. The work is divided into tasks and activities. Dependencies and timelines are established. Risks are evaluated and mitigation plans are prepared.

3. Project Execution: The actual development of the software is done in this phase. The tasks are executed as per the project schedule. Team members work on their allocated activities. Issues and risks are identified and resolved. Progress is monitored and tracked against the plan.

4. Project Monitoring and Control: The project manager constantly monitors the progress, budget, quality, resources, and risks. Corrective actions are taken whenever required to ensure the project meets its goals. Formal control processes are used to track schedule and budget.

5. Project Closure: Once the product is developed as per requirements, the project enters the closure phase. The final product is evaluated. Resources are released. The team is disbanded. The project manager obtains sign-off from stakeholders and prepares a post-mortem report.

The project management processes ensure the software is delivered on time, within budget and as per specifications. Proper management of schedules, resources, changes, quality, risks, and issues is critical for project success.

### Setting Objectives in SPM

1. Clearly define the objectives: The objectives should be clearly defined in specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound terms. Vague objectives like 'increase sales' or 'improve processes' are not useful. Instead, define objectives such as 'increase sales of product X by 25% in 6 months' or 'reduce production defects by 50% in 3 months'.

2. Align objectives with organizational goals: The objectives must align with the broader goals and strategies of the organization. There should be a clear linkage between team or individual objectives and organizational goals. Objectives that are misaligned lead to the effort being spent in ineffective directions.

3. Focus on outcomes, not activities: The objectives must focus on outcomes and results, not activities or tasks. The outcomes are the end results that the activities and tasks are expected to achieve. For example, the objective should be 'launch new product' and not 'conduct market research for new product'. The activities and tasks can be planned once the outcomes are clear.

4. Keep the number of objectives limited: It is better to have a limited set of clear and focused objectives rather than a long list of vague and disparate objectives. As a general rule, a team or individual should not have more than 3-5 objectives at a time. This makes it easier to focus efforts and track progress.

5. Set deadlines and metrics: Each objective must have clearly defined deadlines and metrics to track progress. The deadlines specify when the objective needs to be achieved. The metrics are parameters that can be measured to monitor how close the objective is to being achieved. Measuring metrics periodically helps to take any corrective actions required and meet the objectives within deadlines.

### Risk Evaluation in spm

1. Identify risks: First step is to identify the potential risks in the project. Some common risks are:
- Delay in delivery of components or raw materials.
- Key resource leaving the project midway.
- Sudden changes in requirements by the customer.
- Government regulations impacting the project.
- Natural calamities impacting the project schedule or budget.

2. Assess impact: Once the risks are identified, assess the impact of each risk on project objectives - scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, etc. The impact can be high, medium or low.

3. Assign probability: Next step is to assign a probability of occurrence to each risk. The likelihood of a risk occurring can be high, medium or low.

4. Evaluate risks: Combining impact and probability, evaluate each risk. Risks with high impact and high probability are top priority.

5. Plan risk responses: Finally, plan appropriate risk responses - avoid, mitigate, transfer or accept the risk. The responses should be able to bring down impact or likelihood to an acceptable threshold.

6. Monitor and control: Continuous monitoring of risks and implementation of risk responses is required to ensure risks are managed effectively throughout the project lifecycle.

The above points cover the key steps involved in risk evaluation process as part of risk management in a project. A systematic approach to identify, assess, evaluate and respond to risks can help minimize threats and leverage opportunities to meet project goals.

### Choice of Process Models in Software Project Management

1. Waterfall Model: In this model, the software development process is divided into separate phases with the output of each phase becoming the input for the next phase. The order of the phases is strictly sequential i.e. starting from requirements gathering to testing and deployment. This model ensures that the requirements are clearly understood and documented before the development begins. However, it is not suitable for projects where requirements are not clear and may change frequently.

2. Iterative and Incremental Model: This process model involves repeating a set of steps over and over in a cycle. Each repetition called an iteration, builds on the work done in the previous iteration and improves it. With every iteration, a progressively more concrete and finished version of the software is produced. This model is suitable for large and complex projects where requirements are not precisely defined at the beginning.

3. Agile Model: The Agile process model follows an incremental, iterative approach. The various frameworks under Agile methodologies include Scrum, Extreme Programming, etc. The focus is on delivering a minimal but working product early and then iteratively adding features to it. This model is suitable for projects with changing or unclear requirements and embraces changes. However, managing scope creep and ensuring quality are challenges in this model.

4. Hybrid Model: This model combines the advantages of multiple models. For e.g. starting with the waterfall model to understand requirements and then shifting to iterative or agile models can be a possible combination. The specific strengths of different models can be leveraged in this way to develop software in a more efficient manner for a given project scenario.

The choice of a suitable software development process model depends on various factors such as requirements volatility, team size and experience, project size and criticality, technology constraints etc. A thorough analysis of the project conditions and requirements is essential to identify the most appropriate model for the given scenario.

### Rapid Application Development in Software Project Management

- RAD is an agile software development approach that focuses on rapid prototyping and quick feedback over long planning and testing phases.
- The key goals of RAD are speed, low cost, and active user involvement. It allows for fast development and delivery of a software product.
- The RAD life cycle consists of four phases:

1. Requirements planning: The project team conducts brainstorming sessions with the client to determine requirements.
2. Rapid design: The team uses object-oriented or component-based techniques to develop prototypes of the key modules.
3. Construction: The team builds the prototypes and integrates the modules into a whole. Functionality is prioritized over scalability and structure.
4. Testing and turnover: The product is tested, fixed, and implemented. User feedback is incorporated, and the cycle repeats as needed.

- Benefits of RAD include reduced development time and costs, strong focus on business requirements, and high user involvement and satisfaction. However, the process can be inefficient for large projects and the rapid pace may result in decreased software quality.
- RAD is best suited for projects with clear objectives and understandable user requirements. It works well for web and business applications that need to be delivered on tight schedules.

### Basics of Software Estimation in spm

### Effort and Cost Estimation Techniques in spm

1. Expert Judgment: In this technique, experts who have experience in similar projects provide estimates of effort and cost. Their estimates are based on their intuition and experience.

2. Analogous Estimation: In this technique, estimates are based on actual estimates of similar past projects. The estimated project is compared with previous, similar projects and their actual efforts/costs are used as estimates for the new project.

3. Bottom-Up Estimation: In this technique, estimates are derived by estimating individual work packages or tasks and then aggregating them to get the total effort/cost. The work breakdown structure is analyzed in detail and estimates are provided for each task. These are then added up to get the overall project estimate.

4. Three-Point Estimation: In this technique, an estimate is provided as a range having three values - most likely, optimistic, and pessimistic estimate. A weighted average of these three values is calculated to get the final estimate. The technique accommodates the inherent uncertainty in estimates.

5. Parametric Estimation: In this technique, a statistical relationship is established between project effort/cost and various parameters like lines of code, number of interfaces, etc. Such a relationship is established by analyzing historical data of similar projects. The relationship is then used to estimate effort/cost for the new project based on its known parameters.

### COSMIC Full Function Points in spm

1. Calculate Unadjusted Function Point (UFP):
- Count Inputs: Count each distinguishable input including those from interfaces to external systems.
- Count Outputs: Count each distinguishable output including those to interfaces to external systems.
- Count Queries: Count each distinct logical internal logical file or data base accessed for information.
- Count External Interfaces: Count each external input, output, query, or interface.

2. Determine Value Adjustment Factor (VAF):
- Consider 14 General System Characteristics and assign value to each from 0-5.
- Calculate VAF = Total of individual values/14.

3. Calculate Adjusted Function Point (AFP):
AFP = UFP x VAF

4. Calculate FP Count for Enhancement/Modification:
AFP of Enhancement or Modification / AFP of Base

The COSMIC Full Function Point method in Software Performance and Metrics (spm) helps obtain a quantitative measure of the functionality of software. The steps involve counting various components like inputs, outputs, queries, external interfaces, determining value adjustment factor and calculating adjusted function points. The adjusted function points can be used to measure enhancement or modification of software.

### COCOMO II in spm

- COCOMO II is a software cost estimation model. It stands for Constructive Cost Model II.
- It is an algorithmic model based on lines of code. It estimates effort, schedule and cost for software development.
- It comes in three versions - Basic, Intermediate and Detailed. Basic version estimates effort and schedule. Intermediate version estimates effort, schedule and cost. Detailed version additionally considers team capability and other risk factors.
- COCOMO II uses scale factors to adjust the estimates based on project and team attributes. The scale factors are - pre-architecture, architecture/risk resolution, team cohesion, analyst capability, programmer capability, multisite, etc.
- COCOMO II gives more precise estimates than COCOMO 81 as it has separate models for organic and semi-detached development. It also considers software reuse and offers calibration capability to improve estimates.
- The disadvantages of COCOMO II are that it only estimates at coarse-grained level and it requires extensive data to determine parameters and calibrate the model which may not be available for all projects and organizations.

### A Parametric Productivity Model in spm

1. Define productivity as output/input ratio: Productivity (P) = Output (O) / Input (I)
2. Identify major outputs and inputs:
- Outputs: Products, services, satisfied customers, profit, growth, etc.
- Inputs: Time, money, resources, people, etc.
3. Select key outputs and inputs to monitor: Focus on 3-5 major outputs and 3-5 major inputs that substantially impact productivity.
4. Set target levels for outputs and inputs: Establish realistic targets, goals or objectives for the key outputs and inputs.
5. Measure actual levels of outputs and inputs: Regularly measure actual performance and calculate the output/input ratios.

### Network Planning Models in spm

1. Fixed Channel Allocation (FCA) Model:
- In FCA model, fixed number of channels is allocated to each cell in a network.
- The channel allocation does not change with change in traffic load.
- This model is simple to implement but leads to under-utilization of channels in low traffic cells and call blocking in high traffic cells.

2. Dynamic Channel Allocation (DCA) Model:

- This model is a combination of FCA and DCA models.
- Some channels are allocated fixed to each cell (FCA) and remaining channels are dynamically allocated (DCA) based on load.
- The hybrid model provides trade-off between performance and complexity.

### PERT Technique in spm

1. PERT stands for Program Evaluation and Review Technique. It is a project management technique to analyze and represent the tasks involved in a project.
2. In PERT, the following three time estimates are taken for each activity:
- Optimistic time (O): Minimum time required to complete the activity.
- Most likely time (M): Time duration in which the activity is most likely to be completed.
- Pessimistic time (P): Maximum time that can be taken to complete the activity.
3. Expected time (TE) is calculated as:
TE = (O + 4M + P) / 6
4. Variance (V) is calculated as:
V = (P - O) / 6
5. Standard deviation (σ) is calculated as:
σ = √V
6. Using the expected time and standard deviation, we can find the probability of completing the project in a given time. For example, if TE = 15 weeks and σ = 3 weeks, then there is approximately 68% chance that the project will be completed in 15 ± 3 = 12 to 18 weeks.

- Actual Cost (AC): The actual cost incurred for the work completed by a specific point in time.
4. Using these metrics, we can calculate additional metrics to analyze performance:
- Cost Variance (CV) = Earned Value (EV) - Actual Cost (AC)
- Schedule Variance (SV) = Earned Value (EV) - Planned Value (PV)
- Cost Performance Index (CPI) = Earned Value (EV) / Actual Cost (AC)
- Schedule Performance Index (SPI) = Earned Value (EV) / Planned Value (PV)
5. The CPI and SPI indicate how the project is performing compared to the plan. A value over 1 is good, under 1 is bad. Corrective action may be needed if these fall outside an acceptable range.
6. EVA is a useful way to identify and address performance issues early before they lead to missing objectives or exceeding budgets. Regular EVA reviews help keep the project on track towards successful completion.

### The Oldham-Hackman Job Characteristic Model

1. The Oldham-Hackman Job Characteristic Model proposes that five core job dimensions lead to three critical psychological states which in turn lead to work-related outcomes.
2. The five core job dimensions are:
- Skill variety: The degree to which a job requires a variety of different activities and skills.
- Task identity: The degree to which the job involves completing a whole and identifiable piece of work.
- Task significance: The degree to which the job has a significant impact on the lives or work of other people.
- Autonomy: The degree to which the job provides freedom, independence, and discretion to the individual in scheduling the work and in determining the procedures to be used in carrying it out.
- Feedback: The degree to which carrying out the work activities required by the job results in the individual obtaining direct and clear information about the effectiveness of his or her performance.
3. The three critical psychological states are:
- Experienced meaningfulness: The degree to which the individual experiences the job as one which is meaningful, valuable, and worthwhile.
- Experienced responsibility: The degree to which the individual feels personally accountable and responsible for the outcomes of the work he or she does.
- Knowledge of results: The degree to which the individual knows and understands, on a continuous basis, how effectively he or she is performing the job.
4. The work-related outcomes are:
- Work satisfaction: The extent to which an individual feels positively or negatively about his or her job.
- Internal work motivation: The degree to which an individual is self-motivated to perform and work hard on a job.
- Growth satisfaction: The degree to which an individual achieves and experiences personal development and growth in his or her work.
- Work quality: The degree to which an individual's performance meets internal and external standards of excellence.
