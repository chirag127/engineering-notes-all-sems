### Photovoltaic Cell Technologies

-   **Silicon Wafer Based PV Cells:** Uses high purity silicon wafers. The wafer is exposed to light which excites the electrons causing it to flow through the material to produce electricity. Most common and efficient but more expensive to produce.
-   **Thin Film PV Cells:** Made by depositing one or more thin layers of photovoltaic material onto a substrate. More affordable to produce but less efficient than silicon wafer cells. Made of materials like cadmium telluride, copper indium gallium selenide, etc.
-   **Organic PV Cells:** Made of organic polymers or small organic molecules. Inexpensive to produce but less efficient and durable than inorganic cells. Still in early research stages.
-   **Perovskite PV Cells:** Uses a class of materials called perovskites that have high absorption of light and high charge carrier mobility. Shows high potential for efficiency and low cost but still prone to stability issues. Under active research.
-   **Dye-Sensitized PV Cells:** Uses a photosensitive dye to absorb light and inject electrons into a semiconductor material like titanium dioxide. Inexpensive but suffer from low efficiency and durability.

The key factors in evaluating different PV cell technologies are efficiency, cost of production and durability. More research is focused on improving efficiency and stability of thin film and emerging PV cell technologies to make them commercially viable compared to traditional silicon wafer cells.

### Generation of electric power in semiconductor PV cells

1. Photovoltaic effect: When light is incident on a semiconductor-semiconductor junction or semiconductor-metal junction, it generates electron-hole pairs in the semiconductor. This results in the flow of electric current through the material and is known as the photovoltaic effect.
2. Working of a semiconductor PV cell: A semiconductor PV cell consists of a large-area p-n junction. When light is incident on the cell, electron-hole pairs are generated in both p and n-regions. The electric field at the junction separates the electron-hole pairs. Electrons move towards the n-region and holes move towards the p-region, resulting in a flow of electric current through the external circuit.
3. Main components of a semiconductor PV cell: The key components of a semiconductor PV cell are:
- Semiconductor material: Silicon is most commonly used. It has a suitable bandgap for absorbing visible light.
- P-N junction: The interaction between p and n-type semiconductors results in the formation of a depletion region which generates an electric field to separate the electron-hole pairs.
- Metal contacts: The metal contacts allow the flow of electricity through the external load.
- Antireflection coating: The antireflection coating reduces the reflection of incident light and increases the amount of light absorbed by the semiconductor material.
4. Efficiency of a semiconductor PV cell: The efficiency of a semiconductor PV cell depends on the following key factors:
- Bandgap of the semiconductor material: It determines the fraction of the incident light that can be absorbed. A lower bandgap material can absorb more light but may have higher recombination losses.
- Thickness of the semiconductor: An optimum thickness is required to absorb most of the incident light. A lower thickness may result in lesser light absorption and a higher thickness can increase recombination losses.
- Minority carrier lifetime: It depends on the quality and purity of the semiconductor material. A higher lifetime results in lower recombination losses and improves the efficiency.
- Surface passivation: It is required to reduce the surface recombination of electron-hole pairs. Effective surface passivation improves the efficiency of the PV cell.

### Transducers

- Piezoelectric transducers convert mechanical energy into electrical energy and vice versa.
- They contain piezoelectric materials that produce an electric charge in response to applied mechanical stress.
- The most common piezoelectric materials are crystals like quartz, tourmaline, and ceramics like lead zirconate titanate (PZT).
- When mechanical stress is applied, the atoms in the crystal become polarized and produce electrical charge. This is known as the direct piezoelectric effect.
- When an electric field is applied, the crystal becomes strained. This is known as the inverse piezoelectric effect.
- Piezoelectric transducers are used in devices like buzzers, sensors, actuators, and for energy harvesting.

- Electromagnetic transducers convert mechanical energy into electrical energy and vice versa using magnetic fields.
- They contain a coil of wire wrapped around a movable magnetic core.
- When the magnetic core moves relative to the coil, it induces a voltage. This is known as electromagnetic induction and is the basis for operation.
- The moving core can be the input with the coil sensing the motion, or a voltage can be applied to the coil to produce motion of the core.
- Common applications of electromagnetic transducers include microphones, speakers, inductive sensors, and energy harvesting devices.

- Triboelectric transducers convert mechanical energy into electrical energy by the contact and separation of two materials.
- When two materials are brought into contact and then separated, electrons flow between them which produces a voltage. This is known as the triboelectric effect.
- Common triboelectric material pairs include polymers like polytetrafluoroethylene and polyethylene with metals, rubber/silicon, and some ceramics/polymers.
- The amount of charge transferred depends on the materials, surface area, speed of contact/separation, temperature, and other factors.
- Triboelectric transducers are used for energy harvesting and sensing applications. They can generate high voltage but low current output.

### Power Conditioning and Losses

- Power conditioning is required to convert the input power from the harvester to the required voltage and current levels needed by the load circuit. This is done using DC-DC converters.

- Other losses that affect the overall efficiency of the energy harvesting system include:
-- Transduction losses: The energy conversion from ambient source to electrical energy is not perfect and results in some losses. Eg. The solar cell does not convert all incident light into electricity.
-- Storage losses: If energy storage is used, the charging and discharging processes are not 100% efficient and result in some losses. The loss depends on the type and capacity of the storage device.
-- Leakage losses: There are small leakage currents in the system even when the system is not in use. These contribute to energy losses. Proper system design can minimize leakage losses.
