# KCS

KCS stands for Knowledge-Centered Service, a best practice methodology for service organizations to work more effectively with knowledge. 

## Benefits of KCS

- It improves service delivery by capturing, structuring, and reusing knowledge as part of the support process. 
- It reduces costs by eliminating redundant work and increasing efficiency. 
- It increases customer satisfaction by providing faster and more accurate solutions. 
- It enhances employee engagement by empowering them to create and share knowledge. 
- It fosters continuous improvement by measuring and improving knowledge quality and usage. 

## Principles of KCS

- Knowledge is a by-product of solving problems. 
- Knowledge should be captured and structured in the context of the customer. 
- Knowledge should be created as a collaborative effort. 
- Knowledge should be reused and improved over time. 
- Knowledge should be accessible to anyone who needs it. 

## Practices of KCS

- KCS has four main practices: Capture, Structure, Reuse, and Improve. 
- Capture: This practice involves capturing knowledge in the workflow, as close to the source as possible, using the customer's context and words. 
- Structure: This practice involves structuring knowledge in a consistent and logical way, using templates, metadata, and keywords. 
- Reuse: This practice involves reusing existing knowledge to solve problems, and updating or flagging it as needed. 
- Improve: This practice involves measuring and improving the quality, relevance, and effectiveness of knowledge, using feedback, analytics, and governance.

## Unit 1 - ENERGY HARVESTING SYSTEMS

- Energy harvesting systems are devices or systems that are used to convert ambient energy, which is derived from external sources existing in the atmosphere, into electrical energy.
- Energy harvesting systems are attractive alternatives to battery-operated systems, especially for long-term, low-power, and self-sustaining electronic systems.
- Energy harvesting systems can utilize various sources of ambient energy, such as mechanical load, vibrations, temperature gradients, light, radio frequency, etc. to generate power in the nW-mW range .
- Energy harvesting systems consist of four main components: energy transducer, energy storage, power management, and load.
- Energy transducer is the component that converts the ambient energy into electrical energy. The type of transducer depends on the type of energy source. For example, piezoelectric, electromagnetic, and electrostatic transducers can be used for vibration energy harvesting; thermoelectric and pyroelectric transducers can be used for thermal energy harvesting; photovoltaic and luminescent transducers can be used for light energy harvesting; and antenna and rectifier can be used for radio frequency energy harvesting.
- Energy storage is the component that stores the electrical energy generated by the transducer for later use. The type of storage depends on the power and energy requirements of the load. For example, capacitors, batteries, and supercapacitors can be used as energy storage devices.
- Power management is the component that controls and regulates the flow of energy between the transducer, the storage, and the load. The power management circuit can perform functions such as voltage conversion, impedance matching, maximum power point tracking, power conditioning, and power monitoring.
- Load is the component that consumes the electrical energy provided by the energy harvesting system. The load can be any electronic device that requires low and intermittent power, such as sensors, microcontrollers, wireless transmitters, etc.
- Energy harvesting systems can be classified into different categories based on the type of energy source, the type of transducer, the type of application, the type of architecture, etc.
- Energy harvesting systems have various applications in different domains, such as biomedical, environmental, industrial, military, consumer, etc . Some examples of energy harvesting systems are:

  - Body sensor networks that harvest energy from body heat, motion, or blood pressure to monitor health parameters.
  - Oceanographic monitoring sensors that harvest energy from ocean waves, tides, or currents to measure water quality, temperature, salinity, etc.
  - Wireless sensor networks that harvest energy from solar, wind, or vibration to monitor physical or environmental conditions, such as temperature, humidity, pressure, etc.
  - Smart buildings that harvest energy from light, heat, or motion to control lighting, heating, ventilation, air conditioning, etc.
  - Radio frequency identification (RFID) tags that harvest energy from radio waves to transmit information about the identity, location, or status of an object.
  - Levitation based energy harvesters that harvest energy from vibrations using a levitating magnet in a closed container.

### Energy sources for energy harvesting

Energy harvesting is the process of capturing and converting ambient energy from various sources into electrical energy for powering small, wireless autonomous devices. Some of the common energy sources for energy harvesting are:

- **Thermal energy**: Thermal energy is the energy associated with temperature variations and heat flows. It can be harvested from sources such as body heat, industrial waste heat, geothermal heat, solar radiation, etc. Thermal energy can be converted into electrical energy using thermoelectric generators (TEGs), which are devices that generate voltage based on the Seebeck effect .
- **RF energy**: RF energy is the electromagnetic energy that propagates through space as radio waves. It can be harvested from sources such as TV and radio broadcasts, cellular networks, Wi-Fi routers, etc. RF energy can be converted into electrical energy using RF energy harvesters, which are devices that capture and rectify RF signals into DC voltage .
- **Solar energy**: Solar energy is the radiant energy emitted by the sun. It can be harvested from sources such as natural or artificial light. Solar energy can be converted into electrical energy using photovoltaic (PV) cells, which are devices that use the photovoltaic effect to generate current from light .
- **Mechanical energy**: Mechanical energy is the energy associated with motion and deformation. It can be harvested from sources such as human and animal movements, vibrations, wind, water, etc. Mechanical energy can be converted into electrical energy using various mechanisms, such as piezoelectric, electromagnetic, electrostatic, etc. Piezoelectric devices generate voltage based on the piezoelectric effect, which is the property of some materials to produce electric charge when subjected to mechanical stress. Electromagnetic devices generate voltage based on the Faraday's law of induction, which is the phenomenon of inducing electric current in a conductor by changing magnetic flux. Electrostatic devices generate voltage based on the variable capacitance effect, which is the change in capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor due to the variation of the distance between the plates  .

### Photovoltaic Cell Technologies

- Photovoltaic (PV) cells are devices that absorb energy from sunlight and convert it into electrical energy through semiconducting materials.
- PV cells are based on the photovoltaic effect, which is the process of generating voltage and electric current in a material upon exposure to light.
- PV cells are connected to form larger power-generating units known as modules or panels, which can be installed on rooftops, ground-mounted systems, or floating structures.
- There are three main types of PV cell technologies that dominate the world market: monocrystalline silicon, polycrystalline silicon, and thin film.
  - Monocrystalline silicon cells are made from a single crystal of high-purity silicon, which is cut into thin wafers. They have a uniform dark color and a high efficiency of about 15-20%.
  - Polycrystalline silicon cells are made from multiple crystals of silicon, which are melted and cast into blocks, then sliced into wafers. They have a varied blue color and a lower efficiency of about 13-16%.
  - Thin film cells are made from thin layers of various materials, such as amorphous silicon, cadmium telluride, or copper indium gallium selenide, which are deposited on a substrate. They have a flexible and lightweight structure and a low efficiency of about 7-13%.
- The performance of PV cells depends on several factors, such as the material properties, the cell design, the module configuration, the environmental conditions, and the system integration.
- The open-circuit voltage (VOC) of a PV cell is a measure of the maximum voltage that can be obtained from the cell under illumination. It is related to the band gap of the absorber material, the temperature, and the light intensity.
- The short-circuit current (ISC) of a PV cell is a measure of the maximum current that can be obtained from the cell under illumination. It is related to the light intensity, the cell area, and the quantum efficiency.
- The fill factor (FF) of a PV cell is a measure of the quality of the cell output. It is the ratio of the maximum power point (MPP) to the product of VOC and ISC. It is affected by the series and shunt resistances, the diode ideality factor, and the recombination losses.
- The efficiency (η) of a PV cell is a measure of the fraction of the incident light power that is converted into electrical power. It is the ratio of the MPP to the incident light power. It is determined by the VOC, ISC, and FF.
- The main challenges and opportunities for PV cell technologies are improving the efficiency, reducing the cost, enhancing the reliability, increasing the scalability, and addressing the environmental and social impacts.



### Generation of electric power in semiconductor PV cells

- PV cells or photovoltaic cells are devices that convert light energy into electrical energy using the photovoltaic effect .
- The photovoltaic effect is the phenomenon of generating electric voltage or current in a material when it is exposed to light .
- The PV cell is composed of semiconductor material, such as silicon, which can conduct electricity better than an insulator but not as well as a metal .
- The semiconductor material is doped with impurities to create two layers: the n-type layer, which has excess electrons, and the p-type layer, which has excess holes .
- The junction between the n-type and p-type layers is called the p-n junction, which forms an electric field that prevents the electrons and holes from recombining .
- When light photons with enough energy strike the p-n junction, they can excite electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, creating electron-hole pairs .
- The electric field at the p-n junction separates the electrons and holes, pushing the electrons to the n-type layer and the holes to the p-type layer .
- The electrons and holes can then flow through external circuits as electric current, producing power .
- The power generated by a single PV cell is typically only about two watts, so multiple cells are connected in series or parallel to form PV modules or panels that can produce more power .
- The PV modules or panels can be further connected to form PV arrays or systems that can supply power to various applications, such as homes, buildings, or grids .

## Unit 2 - Piezoelectric Energy Harvesting and Electromechanical Modeling

- Piezoelectric energy harvesting is the process of converting mechanical vibrations into electrical energy using piezoelectric materials, which are materials that generate electric charge when subjected to mechanical stress or strain.
- Piezoelectric energy harvesters are devices that utilize piezoelectric materials to scavenge energy from ambient vibrations, such as those caused by wind, traffic, human motion, etc. They can be used to power low-power electronic devices, such as wireless sensors, microcontrollers, or LEDs, without the need for batteries or wires.
- Electromechanical modeling of piezoelectric energy harvesters is the mathematical description of the coupled dynamics of the mechanical and electrical domains of the system, taking into account the geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, and excitation sources of the harvester.
- Electromechanical modeling can help to understand the behavior, performance, and optimization of piezoelectric energy harvesters, as well as to design and test new configurations and applications of the technology.
- There are different approaches to electromechanical modeling of piezoelectric energy harvesters, depending on the level of detail, complexity, and accuracy required. Some of the common methods are:
  - Lumped-parameter modeling: This method simplifies the harvester as a mass-spring-damper system with a piezoelectric element, and uses ordinary differential equations to describe the motion and voltage of the system. This method is easy to implement and analyze, but it may not capture the effects of higher modes, distributed parameters, or complex geometries of the harvester.
  - Distributed-parameter modeling: This method considers the harvester as a continuous system with spatially varying parameters, and uses partial differential equations to describe the deformation and electric potential of the system. This method can account for the effects of higher modes, distributed parameters, and complex geometries of the harvester, but it may be more difficult to solve and require more computational resources.
  - Finite element modeling: This method discretizes the harvester into a finite number of elements, and uses numerical methods to solve the governing equations of each element. This method can handle arbitrary geometries, material properties, and boundary conditions of the harvester, but it may introduce numerical errors and require more computational resources.
- Electromechanical modeling of piezoelectric energy harvesters can also incorporate different forms of excitation, such as base excitation, tip excitation, airflow excitation, or moving load excitation, depending on the source and nature of the ambient vibrations. Each form of excitation may have different effects on the response and output of the harvester, and may require different modeling techniques and assumptions.



### Piezoelectric materials

- Piezoelectric materials are materials that can convert mechanical stress or strain into electrical charge or voltage, and vice versa. This phenomenon is called the piezoelectric effect.
- Piezoelectric materials can be broadly classified into the following categories :
  - Crystalline materials, such as quartz (SiO2), which are natural and have a fixed and ordered structure of atoms or molecules.
  - Ceramic materials, such as lead zirconate titanate (PZT), which are synthetic and have a polycrystalline structure of grains with random orientations.
  - Polymeric materials, such as polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), which are synthetic and have a flexible and chain-like structure of molecules.
  - Piezoelectric semiconductors, such as zinc oxide (ZnO), which are synthetic and have a narrow band gap and high electrical conductivity.
  - Piezoelectric composites, which are combinations of two or more piezoelectric materials with different properties and shapes.
  - Glass ceramics, such as lithium disilicate (Li2Si2O5) and barium titanate silicate (Ba2TiSiO6), which are synthetic and have a glassy and crystalline structure of atoms or molecules.
- Piezoelectric materials have useful applications in many industries, such as :
  - Sensors and actuators, which can measure or generate force, pressure, acceleration, vibration, sound, etc.
  - Energy harvesting, which can convert ambient mechanical energy into electrical energy for powering low-power devices.
  - Medical imaging and treatment, which can produce or detect ultrasound waves for diagnosis or therapy.
  - Sonochemistry and industrial processing, which can enhance chemical reactions or material properties by using ultrasound waves.
  - Tilt sensing, which can detect the orientation or inclination of an object or a surface.
  - Power monitoring, which can measure the voltage or current of high power applications.
  - Microbalances, which can weigh very small amounts of chemical or biological substances.
  - Strain gauges, which can measure the deformation or strain of a material or a structure.

### Strategies for enhancing the performance of energy harvesters

- Energy harvesters are devices that can convert ambient energy sources, such as vibration, wind, light, heat, etc., into electrical energy for powering low-power electronic devices, such as sensors, wireless communication modules, etc.
- Energy harvesting is a promising technology for the Internet of Things (IoT) devices, as it can provide a sustainable and maintenance-free power supply, reducing the dependence on batteries and wires.
- Piezoelectric energy harvesting (PEH) is one of the most widely studied techniques for harvesting vibration energy, as piezoelectric materials can generate electric charges when subjected to mechanical stress or strain.
- However, PEH has some limitations, such as low power output, narrow frequency bandwidth, and sensitivity to the resonant frequency of the ambient vibrations, which are often random and broadband.
- Therefore, various strategies have been proposed to enhance the performance of PEH, such as:

  - Adopting new materials with higher piezoelectric coefficients, such as lead-free ceramics, polymers, nanocomposites, etc. 
  - Optimizing the geometry and configuration of the harvesting devices, such as cantilevers, beams, plates, etc., to increase the strain and stress distribution. 
  - Introducing nonlinearities, such as bi-stability, snap-through, magnetic force, etc., to induce large-amplitude oscillations and widen the frequency bandwidth.  
  - Applying power-amplifying strategies, such as elastic foundations, synchronized switching, impedance matching, etc., to maximize the generated power and improve the efficiency. 
  - Hybridizing PEH with other energy harvesting techniques, such as electromagnetic, electrostatic, triboelectric, etc., to harvest multiple energy sources and enhance the output performance.  
  - Exploiting asymmetric potentials, such as Duffing, Leng, etc., to induce chaotic motions and increase the sensitivity to ambient vibrations. 

- These strategies can help to improve the performance of PEH and make it more suitable for various applications in the IoT devices.



### Electromechanical modeling of Lumped parameter model and coupled distributed parameter models and closed-form solutions

- Electromechanical modeling is the process of describing the dynamic behavior of systems that involve both electrical and mechanical components, such as piezoelectric devices, using mathematical equations and parameters.
- Lumped parameter model is a type of electromechanical model that assumes that the system can be represented by a finite number of discrete elements, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, masses, springs, and dampers, that are connected at nodes with zero electrical length and capacitance to ground. Lumped parameter models are often simpler and easier to analyze than distributed parameter models, but they may not capture the effects of spatial variations and wave propagation in the system .
- Distributed parameter model is a type of electromechanical model that considers the system as a continuous medium, such as a beam or a plate, that is characterized by spatially varying physical properties, such as density, stiffness, and piezoelectric coefficients. Distributed parameter models are often more accurate and realistic than lumped parameter models, but they require solving partial differential equations and boundary conditions, which can be challenging and computationally intensive .
- Coupled distributed parameter models are distributed parameter models that account for the interactions between different physical domains, such as mechanical, electrical, thermal, and fluidic, in the system. Coupled distributed parameter models can capture the complex phenomena and nonlinearities that arise from the coupling effects, such as hysteresis, creep, and feedback, but they also increase the complexity and dimensionality of the model .
- Closed-form solutions are analytical solutions that can be expressed in terms of a finite number of known functions, such as trigonometric, exponential, or polynomial functions, without the need for numerical methods or approximations. Closed-form solutions are desirable because they can provide exact and explicit expressions for the system variables, such as displacement, voltage, and power, as functions of the system parameters, such as frequency, load, and geometry. However, closed-form solutions are not always possible or easy to obtain, especially for nonlinear and coupled distributed parameter models .



## Unit 3 - ELECTROMAGNETIC ENERGY HARVESTING AND NON-LINEAR TECHNIQUES

- Electromagnetic energy harvesting is the process of converting ambient mechanical vibrations into electrical energy using electromagnetic transducers.
- Electromagnetic transducers consist of a coil and a magnet that move relative to each other and induce a voltage across the coil terminals.
- Non-linear techniques are methods that enhance the performance of electromagnetic energy harvesters by exploiting the non-linear behavior of the system, such as bistability, magnetic levitation, snap-through, etc.
- Non-linear techniques can increase the bandwidth, power output, and robustness of the energy harvesters, as well as enable self-powered operation and adaptive tuning.
- Some examples of non-linear techniques are:

  - Bistable energy harvesting: A system that has two stable equilibrium positions and can switch between them under external excitation. This can result in large-amplitude oscillations and high power output .
  - Magnetic levitation energy harvesting: A system that uses magnetic forces to suspend a magnet above a coil, creating a negative stiffness effect. This can reduce the damping and increase the frequency range of the system .
  - Snap-through energy harvesting: A system that uses a buckled beam or a curved membrane that can snap from one configuration to another under external force. This can generate high power pulses and widen the frequency bandwidth.

- Electromagnetic energy harvesting is the process of converting ambient electromagnetic radiation into electrical energy for powering wireless devices or charging batteries .
- Electromagnetic energy harvesting can be classified into two types: near-field and far-field. Near-field harvesting involves capturing the electromagnetic fields generated by nearby sources such as power lines, transformers, or wireless chargers. Far-field harvesting involves capturing the electromagnetic waves emitted by distant sources such as radio, TV, or cellular towers .
- Electromagnetic energy harvesting devices typically consist of three main components: an antenna, a rectifier, and a power management circuit. The antenna captures the electromagnetic waves and converts them into alternating current (AC). The rectifier converts the AC into direct current (DC). The power management circuit regulates the DC voltage and current and delivers it to the load or the battery .
- Non-linear techniques are methods that exploit the non-linear behavior of certain components or systems to enhance the performance of electromagnetic energy harvesting devices. Non-linear techniques can be applied to the antenna, the rectifier, or the power management circuit  .
- Non-linear techniques can improve the bandwidth, efficiency, power density, and adaptability of electromagnetic energy harvesting devices. For example, non-linear antennas can harvest energy from multiple frequency bands or tune to the optimal frequency. Non-linear rectifiers can increase the rectification efficiency or output voltage. Non-linear power management circuits can optimize the impedance matching or voltage conversion  .
- Some examples of non-linear techniques are:
  - Magnetic levitation: using magnets to suspend a coil or a magnet in mid-air and induce relative motion between them by external vibrations or rotations. This creates a variable magnetic flux that generates AC voltage in the coil .
  - Frequency up-conversion: using a switch or a diode to modulate the input AC signal at a higher frequency and then rectify it. This increases the output voltage and reduces the losses due to parasitic capacitance.
  - Synchronized switching: using a switch or a comparator to control the charging and discharging of a capacitor in parallel with the load. This maximizes the power transfer from the source to the load by synchronizing the switching with the input signal.



### Micro fabricated coils and magnetic materials

- Micro fabricated coils are miniature coils that are made using micro fabrication techniques such as lithography, electroplating, etching, etc. 
- Micro fabricated coils can be used for various applications such as magnetooptical data storage, electromagnetic energy harvesting, magnetic neurostimulation, etc.  
- Micro fabricated coils can be classified into two types: planar and three-dimensional.
- Planar micro coils have a flat structure with a transparent center that allows a laser beam to pass through. They consist of upper and lower magnetic films, a metal wire coil layer in the middle, and an insulating layer to isolate the magnetic core material and the wire.
- Three-dimensional micro coils have a cylindrical or helical structure that can generate a stronger magnetic field than planar micro coils. They can be fabricated by winding a metal wire around a cylindrical or helical mold and then removing the mold.
- The performance of micro fabricated coils depends on several factors such as the coil geometry, the coil resistance, the magnetic material, the excitation frequency, the excitation amplitude, the coil spacing, the coil thickness, etc.

- RF sensors and ID tags are devices that use radio frequency (RF) signals to communicate data, such as identification, location, temperature, humidity, etc.
- RF sensors and ID tags can be classified into passive, semi-passive, and active, depending on their power source and transmission mode.
- Passive RF sensors and ID tags do not have a battery and rely on the RF reader signal to deliver energy and power up their circuitry . They can only transmit data when they are in the reader's field.
- Semi-passive RF sensors and ID tags have a battery that powers the sensor and the logic circuit, but not the RF transmission. They use the RF reader signal to modulate and reflect back their data . They can transmit data even when they are not in the reader's field, but they have a limited battery life.
- Active RF sensors and ID tags have a battery that powers the sensor, the logic circuit, and the RF transmission. They can transmit data continuously and over longer distances, but they have a higher power consumption and a shorter battery life .
- Energy harvesting is a technique that enables RF sensors and ID tags to collect energy from the environment, such as RF, motion, heat, light, etc., and store it in a capacitor or a battery for later use .
- Energy harvesting can extend the battery life of semi-passive and active RF sensors and ID tags, or even eliminate the need for a battery in passive RF sensors and ID tags .
- Energy harvesting can also enable new applications and functionalities for RF sensors and ID tags, such as sensing, computation, encryption, authentication, etc .
- Energy harvesting can be performed using different methods and devices, such as antennas, rectifiers, piezoelectric materials, thermoelectric materials, solar cells, etc .
- Energy harvesting for RF sensors and ID tags faces several challenges, such as low power density, low conversion efficiency, mismatched impedance, variable load, etc .



### Powering Wireless SHM Sensor Nodes through Energy Harvesting

- Wireless sensor nodes and sensor networks are widely used for structural health monitoring (SHM) applications, such as detecting damage, cracks, corrosion, or deformation in buildings, bridges, pipelines, aircraft, etc.
- However, one of the main challenges in wireless SHM systems is how to power the sensor nodes, which typically consist of sensors, microcontrollers, memory, and wireless transceivers, and have limited battery life.
- Energy harvesting is a promising technique to provide continuous and sustainable power to wireless SHM sensor nodes by converting ambient energy sources, such as solar, thermal, vibration, wind, or electromagnetic radiation, into electrical energy.
- Energy harvesting can extend the lifetime of wireless SHM sensor nodes, reduce the maintenance cost and environmental impact of battery replacement, and enable the deployment of wireless SHM systems in remote or inaccessible locations.
- Some of the energy harvesting methods for wireless SHM sensor nodes are:

  - Solar energy harvesting: using photovoltaic cells to convert sunlight into electricity. Solar energy harvesting is suitable for outdoor SHM applications where there is sufficient solar irradiance. However, solar energy harvesting is dependent on the weather and diurnal cycles, and may require energy storage devices to provide power during the night or cloudy days.
  - Thermal energy harvesting: using thermoelectric generators to convert temperature gradients or heat fluxes into electricity. Thermal energy harvesting can exploit the waste heat from industrial processes, machinery, or human activities, or the natural temperature differences between the environment and the structure. Thermal energy harvesting is independent of the time of day, but may require thermal insulation or heat sinks to maintain the temperature gradient.
  - Vibration energy harvesting: using piezoelectric, electromagnetic, or electrostatic transducers to convert mechanical vibrations or strain into electricity. Vibration energy harvesting can utilize the ambient vibrations from traffic, wind, machinery, or human activities, or the structural vibrations induced by the SHM sensors themselves. Vibration energy harvesting is dependent on the frequency and amplitude of the vibrations, and may require tuning or frequency conversion mechanisms to match the resonance frequency of the transducer.
  - Wind energy harvesting: using micro wind turbines to convert wind flow into electricity. Wind energy harvesting can be applied to SHM applications where there is sufficient wind speed and direction, such as bridges, towers, or offshore structures. However, wind energy harvesting is dependent on the weather and seasonal variations, and may require energy storage devices to provide power during calm periods.
  - Electromagnetic energy harvesting: using antennas or rectennas to convert electromagnetic radiation, such as radio waves, microwaves, or light, into electricity. Electromagnetic energy harvesting can exploit the ambient electromagnetic fields from wireless communication, broadcasting, or power transmission systems, or the dedicated electromagnetic fields generated by wireless energy transmission systems. Electromagnetic energy harvesting is independent of the time of day, but may require impedance matching or rectification circuits to optimize the power conversion efficiency.
