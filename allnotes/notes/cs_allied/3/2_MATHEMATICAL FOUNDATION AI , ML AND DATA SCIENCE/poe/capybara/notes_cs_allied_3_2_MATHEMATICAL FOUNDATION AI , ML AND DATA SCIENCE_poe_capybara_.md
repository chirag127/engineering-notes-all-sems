## Unit 1 - Descriptive Statistics

- **Data visualization**: This involves creating graphs or charts to visually represent the data. Common types of graphs used in descriptive statistics include bar graphs, pie charts, and histograms. Data visualization can help to highlight patterns or relationships in the data that may not be immediately apparent from looking at the raw numbers.

- **Sampling and bias**: Descriptive statistics can only be accurate if the dataset is representative of the population it is meant to represent. Sampling bias can occur if the sample is not representative of the population, which can lead to inaccurate or misleading conclusions.

Overall, descriptive statistics provides a useful framework for understanding and summarizing data. By using measures of central tendency, variability, frequency distributions, and data visualization, analysts can gain insights into the key features of a dataset and communicate these findings effectively to others.

### Measures of Central Tendency

Measures of central tendency are statistical measures that help describe a set of data by identifying the central position of the data points. They help us understand the distribution of the data and provide a summary of the data in a single value. In this section, we will discuss some of the commonly used measures of central tendency.

#### Mean

The mean is the most commonly used measure of central tendency. It is calculated by adding up all the data points and dividing by the total number of data points. It is denoted by the symbol "x̄". The formula for calculating the mean is:

```
x̄ = (x1 + x2 + ... + xn) / n
```

Where x1, x2, ..., xn are the data points and n is the total number of data points.

#### Median

The median is the middle value of a set of data arranged in order of magnitude. It is denoted by the symbol "M". The formula for calculating the median depends on whether the number of data points is odd or even. If the number of data points is odd, the median is the middle value. If the number of data points is even, the median is the average of the two middle values.

#### Mode

The mode is the most frequent value in a set of data. It is denoted by the symbol "Mo". A set of data can have one mode, more than one mode, or no mode at all.

#### Comparison of Measures of Central Tendency

The mean, median, and mode provide different information about the data. In general, the mean is the most sensitive to outliers, while the median is less sensitive to outliers. The mode is not affected by outliers at all. Therefore, it is important to consider all three measures of central tendency when analyzing a set of data.

#### Conclusion

In conclusion, measures of central tendency provide important information about a set of data. They help us understand the distribution of the data and provide a summary of the data in a single value. The mean, median, and mode are the most commonly used measures of central tendency, and each has its own strengths and weaknesses.

### Inference Procedure for Correlation Coefficient

In descriptive statistics, correlation coefficient is used to measure the degree of association between two variables. Once the correlation coefficient has been calculated, it is necessary to make inferences about the population correlation coefficient from the sample data. The following are the steps involved in the inference procedure for correlation coefficient:

1. State the null and alternative hypotheses: The null hypothesis is that the population correlation coefficient is zero, while the alternative hypothesis is that the population correlation coefficient is not zero.

2. Determine the level of significance: The level of significance is the probability of making a Type I error, which is rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true. The commonly used levels of significance are 0.05 and 0.01.

3. Calculate the test statistic: The test statistic is calculated using the sample correlation coefficient and the sample size. The test statistic follows a t-distribution with n-2 degrees of freedom, where n is the sample size.

4. Determine the critical value: The critical value is obtained from the t-distribution table with n-2 degrees of freedom and the chosen level of significance.

5. Compare the test statistic with the critical value: If the absolute value of the test statistic is greater than the critical value, then the null hypothesis is rejected. Otherwise, the null hypothesis is not rejected.

6. State the conclusion: If the null hypothesis is rejected, it can be concluded that there is a significant correlation between the two variables. If the null hypothesis is not rejected, it can be concluded that there is not enough evidence to suggest a significant correlation between the two variables.

In conclusion, the inference procedure for correlation coefficient involves stating the null and alternative hypotheses, determining the level of significance, calculating the test statistic, determining the critical value, comparing the test statistic with the critical value, and stating the conclusion. This procedure is used to make inferences about the population correlation coefficient from the sample data.

### Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring given that another event has occurred. It is a fundamental concept in probability theory and is widely used in various fields such as artificial intelligence, machine learning, and data science.

#### Definition

Conditional probability is formally defined as the probability of event A given that event B has occurred. It is denoted by P(A|B) and is calculated as:

```
P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
```

where P(A and B) is the probability of both events occurring together, and P(B) is the probability of event B occurring.

#### Properties

Conditional probability has the following properties:

- If A and B are independent events, then P(A|B) = P(A)
- If A and B are mutually exclusive events, then P(A|B) = 0
- If A is a subset of B, then P(A|B) = P(A)/P(B)
- If A and B are not necessarily independent or mutually exclusive, then P(A and B) = P(A|B) * P(B)

#### Applications

Conditional probability has many applications in various fields. Some of the common applications are:

- In machine learning, conditional probability is used to calculate the probability of a data point belonging to a particular class given its features.
- In artificial intelligence, conditional probability is used in Bayesian networks to model the relationships between variables.
- In data science, conditional probability is used to analyze the relationships between variables and to make predictions based on observed data.

#### Conclusion

Conditional probability is a fundamental concept in probability theory and has many applications in various fields. A good understanding of conditional probability is essential for anyone working in the fields of artificial intelligence, machine learning, and data science.

### Bayes’ Theorem

- Bayes’ theorem is used to calculate conditional probabilities, which are probabilities of an event given that another event has occurred.
- The theorem is based on the concept of prior probability, which is the probability of an event before any additional evidence is taken into account.
- Bayes’ theorem combines prior probability with new evidence to update the probability of an event.
- The updated probability is called the posterior probability.
- Bayes’ theorem is often used in machine learning and artificial intelligence applications, particularly in Bayesian networks and decision trees.
- The theorem is also used in medical diagnosis, spam filtering, and other real-world applications.

Bayes’ theorem can be expressed mathematically as follows:

P(A|B) = P(B|A) * P(A) / P(B)

where:
- P(A|B) is the posterior probability of event A given event B
- P(B|A) is the conditional probability of event B given event A
- P(A) is the prior probability of event A
- P(B) is the prior probability of event B

In conclusion, Bayes’ theorem is a powerful tool for calculating conditional probabilities and updating prior probabilities based on new evidence. It is an important concept to understand in the field of mathematical foundations of AI, ML, and data science.

### Discrete and Continuous Probability Distributions

Probability distribution is a fundamental concept in statistics and probability theory. In this section, we will explore two types of probability distributions: discrete and continuous probability distributions.

#### Discrete Probability Distributions

A discrete probability distribution is a probability distribution that assigns probabilities to a finite or countable set of outcomes. In other words, the distribution only takes on a finite or countably infinite number of values. 

Some common examples of discrete probability distributions include the binomial distribution, the Poisson distribution, and the geometric distribution.

##### Binomial Distribution

The binomial distribution is a discrete probability distribution that describes the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials. It is characterized by two parameters: n, the number of trials, and p, the probability of success in each trial. The probability mass function (PMF) of the binomial distribution is given by:

P(X=k) = (n choose k) * p^k * (1-p)^(n-k)

where X is the number of successes, k is the number of successes, n is the number of trials, p is the probability of success in each trial, and (n choose k) is the binomial coefficient.

##### Poisson Distribution

The Poisson distribution is a discrete probability distribution that describes the number of events in a fixed interval of time or space. It is characterized by one parameter, lambda, which is the expected number of events in the interval. The probability mass function (PMF) of the Poisson distribution is given by:

P(X=k) = (e^(-lambda) * lambda^k) / k!

where X is the number of events, k is the number of events, lambda is the expected number of events, e is the mathematical constant e, and k! is the factorial of k.

##### Geometric Distribution

2. **Properties of the Kernel:** The kernel of a linear transformation has the following properties:

    - It is closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

4. **Properties of the Range:** The range of a linear transformation has the following properties:
    - It contains the zero vector.
    - It is closed under addition and scalar multiplication.
    - It is a span of the columns of the transformation matrix.

    - The range is the set of all vectors that the transformation can produce.
    - If the transformation is one-to-one, then the kernel only contains the zero vector.

6. **Dimension of the Kernel and Range:** The dimension of the kernel and range of a linear transformation is an essential factor in determining the properties of the transformation. The dimension of the kernel is the nullity of the transformation, and the dimension of the range is the rank of the transformation.

### Symmetric Matrices and Orthogonal Diagonalization

In this topic, we will discuss symmetric matrices and their properties. We will also learn how to perform orthogonal diagonalization of a symmetric matrix.

#### Symmetric Matrices

A matrix A is said to be symmetric if it is equal to its transpose, i.e., A = A^T. The following are some properties of symmetric matrices:

- All eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are real.
- Eigenvectors corresponding to different eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal.
- A symmetric matrix is diagonalizable, i.e., it can be expressed as A = PDP^T, where P is an orthogonal matrix and D is a diagonal matrix.

#### Orthogonal Diagonalization of Symmetric Matrices

To perform orthogonal diagonalization of a symmetric matrix A, we follow these steps:

1. Find the eigenvalues of A by solving the characteristic equation |A - λI| = 0, where λ is an eigenvalue of A and I is the identity matrix.
2. Find the eigenvectors corresponding to each eigenvalue of A.
3. Normalize the eigenvectors to obtain an orthogonal set of eigenvectors.
4. Form an orthogonal matrix P by using the normalized eigenvectors as its columns.
5. Form a diagonal matrix D by placing the eigenvalues on its diagonal.
6. Verify that A = PDP^T.

```

     [1, 2, 3],
     [1, 3, 6]]
```

1. The eigenvalues of A are λ1 = 7, λ2 = 3, and λ3 = 2.
2. The eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues are:

```
v1 = [1, 1, 1],
v2 = [-1, 1, 0],
v3 = [-1, -2, 1]
```

3. Normalize the eigenvectors:

```

v2' = [-1/√2, 1/√2, 0],
```

```
P = [[1/√3, -1/√2, -1/√6],

```

5. Form the diagonal matrix D:

```
D = [[7, 0, 0],
     [0, 3, 0],

```

6. Verify that A = PDP^T.

Therefore, we have successfully performed orthogonal diagonalization of the symmetric matrix A.
