The measures of dispersion summarize the spread or variability in a data set. They give us an idea about the clustering of the data around the central tendency. ### Measures of Skewness and Kurtosis

- Skewness refers to asymmetry of the data around the mean. It measures the extent to which the distribution varies from symmetry.
- Positive skewness indicates that the tail on the right side of the distribution is longer or fatter than the left side.
- Negative skewness indicates that the tail on the left side of the distribution is longer or fatter than the right side.
- Zero skewness indicates a symmetric distribution.
- Kurtosis refers to the peakedness of a distribution. It measures the extent to which the data is concentrated around the mean.
- A high kurtosis indicates a steep peak and heavy tails.
- A low kurtosis indicates a flat peak and light tails.
- The standard measure of kurtosis is called excess kurtosis. For a normal distribution, excess kurtosis equals 0. Distributions with excess kurtosis > 0 are said to be leptokurtic and those with excess kurtosis < 0 are said to be platykurtic.

### Correlation

### Inference Procedure for Correlation Coefficient

- Calculate the sample correlation coefficient, r, between the two variables.
- Determine the degrees of freedom, df = n - 2, where n is the sample size.
- Determine the critical values for the given significance level, α, from the correlation coefficient distribution table. The critical values are denoted by rcv.
- If the calculated r value lies in between -rcv and +rcv, then we fail to reject the null hypothesis (H0: ρ = 0). This means the variables are uncorrelated.
- If the calculated r value is less than -rcv or greater than +rcv, then we reject the null hypothesis. This means the variables are correlated either positively or negatively respectively.

### Linear Regression and its Inference Procedure

Linear regression is a statistical technique for modeling the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables using a straight line.

Inference procedure for linear regression:

1. Check the assumptions:
- Linear relationship between dependent and independent variables
- No multicollinearity between independent variables
- Residuals are normally distributed with zero mean and constant variance
- Independence of errors

2. Fit the linear regression model using least squares method
3. Evaluate the fit of the model using R-squared and p-values
- R-squared: Proportion of variance in dependent variable explained by the model
- p-values: Check significance of each regressor in the model

4. Diagnose the model and check for violations of assumptions
- Check residual plots for non-normality or heteroscedasticity
- Check variance inflation factor (VIF) for multicollinearity

5. Interpret the regression coefficients and make inferences
- Check significance of individual coefficients using t-test
- Interpret coefficients as change in predicted value of dependent variable due to one unit change in the regressor keeping others fixed

6. Evaluate performance and predict on new data
- Calculate mean absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE) on training data for evaluation
- Predict dependent variable for new data using the fitted regression equation

### Multiple Regression

- Multiple regression is a statistical technique that uses several independent variables to predict the outcome of a dependent variable.
- It is an extension of simple linear regression which uses only one independent variable to predict the dependent variable.
- The goal of multiple regression is to model the linear relationship between the independent variables (predictors) and the dependent variable (outcome).
- The regression equation is:

Y = b0 + b1X1 + b2X2 + ... + bnXn

Where Y is the dependent variable, X1...Xn are the independent variables, and b0...bn are the regression coefficients.
- The regression coefficients are estimated from the data using the least squares method. The coefficients determine the strength and direction of the relationship between the independent and dependent variables.
- Assumptions of multiple regression:

1. Linear relationship between the dependent and independent variables.
2. Multicollinearity: The independent variables are not highly correlated with each other.
3. Independence of errors: The errors are uncorrelated with each other and the independent variables.
4. Homoscedasticity: The error terms have equal variance.
5. Normality: The error terms are normally distributed.

- Advantages: It can handle multiple independent variables, more predictive power, interaction effects can be studied.
- Disadvantages: more assumptions, multicollinearity can be a problem, large sample sizes required.

- Multiple regression is a useful technique for modeling and understanding relationships between variables. It is widely used in many fields for analysis and prediction.

### Conditional Probability

- Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring given that another event has already occurred. It is denoted by P(A|B) which means the probability of event A given that event B has occurred.
- To calculate conditional probability, we use the formula: P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B)
- Conditional probability depends on the probability of the conditioned event (event B in this case). If the probability of event B is 0, then the conditional probability P(A|B) is 0 regardless of the probability of event A. Similarly, if the probability of event B is 1, then the conditional probability P(A|B) equals the probability of event A.
- Conditional probability is useful in calculating the probability of an event based on some given information or context about the event. It allows us to compute the probability of an event A after we already know that event B has occurred.
- Some examples of conditional probability:
    - Probability of getting heads when flipping a coin twice given that the first flip was heads.
    - Probability of rain given that the humidity is high.
    - Probability of a student getting a high SAT score given that they studied for over 10 hours.

### Bayes' Theorem

- Bayes' theorem is a mathematical formula that describes the probability of an event, based on prior knowledge of conditions that might be related to the event.
- It is stated as:
P(A|B) = P(B|A) x P(A) / P(B)
Where:
- P(A|B) is the posterior probability of A given B
- P(B|A) is the likelihood which is the probability of B given A
- P(A) is the prior probability of A
- P(B) is the prior probability of B
- Bayes' theorem is useful in calculating updated probabilities when new evidence is introduced. It allows you to update probabilities as you get more information.
- For example, if there is a 80% chance of rain (prior probability), and a weather forecast says there is a 70% chance of rain (likelihood), Bayes' theorem can be used to calculate the updated probability of rain (posterior probability).
- Bayes' theorem is widely used in machine learning and artificial intelligence for classification tasks. It allows algorithms to learn and improve their accuracy as they get exposed to more data.

### Markov Inequality

- Markov inequality states that for any random variable X and any constant k > 0,
P(|X| ≥ k) ≤ E(|X|)/k
- It gives an upper bound on the probability that the random variable X deviates from its mean by more than a certain amount k.
- The inequality provides a very crude bound but it holds for any random variable (independent of its distribution).
- For example, if X has mean 0 and E(|X|) = 1, then for any k > 0, P(|X| ≥ k) ≤ 1/k. So the probability that X deviates from its mean by more than 1 is at most 1/2 and the probability that it deviates by more than 2 is at most 1/4, and so on.
- The inequality is useful to get quick bounds on probabilities when exact computation or tighter bounds are difficult to obtain. It can be applied in many situations as an initial sanity check on probabilities.

### Chebyshev's Inequality

Chebyshev's inequality is a result which gives an upper bound on the probability that a random variable deviates from its mean by more than a certain amount.

- Let X be a random variable with mean μ and variance σ2. Then for any real number k > 0,

P(|X − μ| > kσ) ≤ 1/k2

- In other words, the probability that X deviates from its mean by more than k standard deviations is at most 1/k2.
- As k increases, the upper bound decreases. So the inequality gives a worst-case bound on the probability of a large deviation from the mean.
- The inequality is weak but the bound is easy to calculate since it only depends on k and not on the underlying distribution of X.
- The inequality is named after Pafnuty Chebyshev who proved a more general version of it in 1867. It is a fundamental result in probability with many applications in statistics and other fields.

### Central Limit Theorem

- The Central Limit Theorem states that the distribution of sample means approaches a normal distribution as the sample size increases, regardless of the shape of the underlying distribution.
- As the sample size (n) increases, the sample means distribution becomes more concentrated around the true mean (μ) of the population.
- The central limit theorem holds for any distribution of the population, not just for symmetric distributions. It applies to any distribution that has a finite mean and variance.
- The central limit theorem is the foundation for many statistical techniques, including confidence intervals and hypothesis testing. It allows us to assume that many distributions are approximately normal, which makes calculations and inferences much easier.
- The central limit theorem requires a sufficiently large sample size (n) for the approximation to be accurate. A rule of thumb is that n > 30 is sufficient, but for distributions that are very non-normal a larger sample size may be needed.

### Inference & Significance

- Inferential statistics is used to infer properties of a population from a sample. It allows us to draw conclusions about a large group based on a subset of the data.
- A significance test is used to assess whether an observed effect is "real" or just due to chance. It calculates a p-value, which is the probability of observing the given effect (or an even more extreme effect) if the null hypothesis is true.
- If the p-value is very small (typically less than 0.05), we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the observed effect is statistically significant. This means it is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone.

### Permutations and Randomization Test

### t-test/z-test (one sample, independent, paired)

One sample t-test:
- Used to determine whether a sample mean is significantly different from a known population mean.

- Used to compare means of two independent samples.

- Alternative hypothesis (H1): Mean of sample 1 ≠ Mean of sample 2
- Test statistic: t = (Difference between sample means) / (Standard error of difference between sample means)

Paired samples t-test:
- Used to compare two means where samples are dependent (paired observations).
- Null hypothesis (H0): Mean difference = 0
- Alternative hypothesis (H1): Mean difference ≠ 0
- Test statistic: t = (Mean of differences) / (Standard error of differences)
- P-value: Calculated using t-distribution and compared with significance level (α)
- If p-value ≤ α, reject H0, else fail to reject H0

### Acceptance-Rejection Method

- The acceptance-rejection method is a technique to generate random samples from a target distribution.
- It works by accepting or rejecting samples from an auxiliary distribution based on whether they fall within the target distribution.
- The key steps are:
- Choose an auxiliary distribution that envelops the target distribution. This means the auxiliary distribution has a higher density than the target distribution everywhere.
- Sample from the auxiliary distribution.
- Accept the sample with probability equal to the ratio of the target density to the auxiliary density at that point.
- Repeating this process generates samples from the target distribution.
- The efficiency of this method depends on how well the auxiliary distribution approximates the target distribution. A tighter fit leads to a higher acceptance rate and fewer rejected samples.
- The acceptance-rejection method is general but can be computationally intensive. It is useful when analytically sampling from a distribution is difficult or impossible.

Stratified sampling is a sampling technique wherein the population is divided into homogeneous subgroups called strata, and samples are selected from each stratum.

-   The strata should be mutually exclusive and collective exhaustive. This means the entire population should be divided into strata, and an element should belong to only one stratum.
-   Samples are selected from each stratum in a proportional allocation. This means that the ratio of sample size to population size in each stratum should be the same.
-   Stratified sampling reduces sampling error as it results in a sample that is more representative of the population.
-   It is useful when the population contains distinct subgroups and we want to ensure that all subgroups are adequately represented in the sample.
-   Examples: Students can be stratified based on gender and then samples selected. Voters can be stratified based on age, income, geography, etc. and then samples selected from each stratum for a survey.

The key benefits of stratified sampling are:

-   It results in increased precision as compared to simple random sampling.
-   It ensures that the sample represents all subgroups of the population.
-   It can lead to cost reduction if the cost of sampling each stratum is different. We can allocate a greater portion of the sample to strata that are less expensive to sample.

The drawbacks of stratified sampling are:

-   It requires identification of strata and homogeneous subgroups in the population, which may not always be straightforward.
-   It can be more complex to implement as compared to simple random sampling.

- The state of the Markov chain after a large number of steps is then used as a sample of the desired distribution.
- The key advantage of McMC methods is that they can sample from complex distributions that are not straightforward to sample from directly.
- The samples obtained are dependent due to the Markov property of the chain. However, under fairly general conditions the variance of functions of the samples will be reasonable as the number of samples increases.

-- Variance reduction techniques such as antithetic variables and control variates.
-- Adaptive MCMC methods where the proposal distribution is tuned during the sampling process.

- Examples of McMC methods include the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and Gibbs sampling. McMC is widely used in statistics, machine learning, computational physics, and computational biology.

### Metropolis-Hastings algorithm

Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is a Markov chain Monte Carlo method for obtaining a sequence of random samples from a probability distribution.

**Steps:**

1. Choose an initial state x0 from the state space. This is the starting point of the Markov chain.
2. For t = 1, 2, ...,

- Propose a new state xt' from a proposal distribution q(xt'|xt).
- Compute the acceptance ratio α = min(1, π(xt')/π(xt)) where π(x) is the target distribution.
- Generate a uniform random number u ~ Unif(0, 1).
- If u < α, accept the proposal and set xt = xt'. Otherwise, reject the proposal and set xt = xt.

3. The sequence of accepted states converges to samples from the target distribution π.

The key advantage of Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is that it can be used even when only an unnormalized density is known since the acceptance ratio does not depend on the normalizing constant. It is a very general MCMC technique with a wide range of applications.

The main drawbacks are:

- It can be difficult to choose an efficient proposal distribution.
- The Markov chain can be highly autocorrelated leading to slow convergence.
- The acceptance rate can be very low leading to inefficient exploration of the state space.

Various improvements and variants of the basic Metropolis-Hastings algorithm have been proposed to address these issues.

- A set of vectors v1, v2, ..., vn in a vector space V is called an **orthonormal basis** if the vectors are pairwise orthogonal (vi•vj = 0 for i≠j) and each has length 1 (‖vi‖ = 1 for all i).
- Every vector v in V has a unique representation as a linear combination of the basis vectors: v = a1v1 + a2v2 + ... + anvn. The scalars ai are called the **coordinates** of v with respect to the given basis.
- The standard basis for Rn consists of the vectors e1 = (1,0,0,...,0), e2 = (0,1,0,...,0), ..., en = (0,0,...,1). The standard basis is always an orthonormal basis.
- Given any basis B = {v1, v2, ..., vn} for a vector space V, there exists an orthonormal basis O = {u1, u2, ..., un} for V such that, for each i, vi can be written as a unique linear combination of the ui. Such an O is called a **Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization** of B.
- A vector space equipped with an orthonormal basis has a naturally defined inner product: The inner product of two vectors is the sum of the products of corresponding coordinates. With this inner product, the norm of a vector is the square root of the sum of the squares of its coordinates.

### Gram-Schmidt Process

- The Gram-Schmidt process is a method for orthonormalizing a set of vectors in an inner product space.
- It takes a basis as input and produces an orthonormal basis as output.
- The process proceeds iteratively:
- Start with the first vector $v_1$ in the basis. This vector is clearly already unit-length, so we have $u_1 = \frac{v_1}{||v_1||} = v_1$.
- For $k > 1$, assume we have constructed $u_1, ..., u_{k-1}$.
- Project $v_k$ onto the subspace spanned by $u_1, ..., u_{k-1}$, and subtract the projection. This gives us $w_k = v_k - (v_k, u_1)u_1 - ... - (v_k, u_{k-1})u_{k-1}$.
- Compute $u_k = w_k / ||w_k||$.
- In the end, we have constructed an orthonormal set of vectors $u_1, ..., u_n$ from the original input basis $v_1, ..., v_n$.

The key properties of the Gram-Schmidt process are:

- It produces an orthonormal basis.
- It is numerically stable.
- It works with any starting basis.

The Gram-Schmidt process is an important technique for orthogonalization and finds applications in many areas, including least squares approximation and QR factorization.

## Unit 5 - Linear Transformations

1. Introduction to Linear Transformations
- A linear transformation is a function that transforms vectors into vectors while preserving vector addition and scalar multiplication.
- In other words, a linear transformation must satisfy the following two properties:
-- Additivity: T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) for all vectors u and v.
-- Homogeneity: T(ku) = kT(u) for all scalars k and vectors u.
- Examples: Rotation, reflection, stretching, and shrinking of vectors are all linear transformations.

2. Matrix Representation of Linear Transformations
- Every linear transformation can be represented by a matrix, called its standard matrix.
- The standard matrix is obtained by evaluating the transformation at the standard basis vectors. For example, in 2D, the standard matrix would have two columns corresponding to T([1,0]) and T([0,1]).
- Two linear transformations are equal if and only if they have the same standard matrix. The matrix representation provides an efficient way to work with linear transformations.

3. Transformation Composition and Matrix Multiplication
- The composition of two linear transformations is another linear transformation.
- The matrix representing the composition can be found by multiplying the matrices representing the individual transformations.
- Matrix multiplication therefore provides an algebraic way to represent the composition of linear transformations.

2. The standard form of a linear transformation between two vector spaces V and W is:
T : V → W
3. The matrix of a linear transformation is a matrix A such that T(v) = Av for all v in V. The number of columns of A equals the dimension of V and the number of rows of A equals the dimension of W.
4. If V and W have the same dimension, then the matrix of a linear transformation is a square matrix. In this case, the matrix completely characterizes the linear transformation.
5. Two linear transformations T : V → W and S : V → W are equal if and only if they have the same matrix with respect to the standard bases of V and W.
6. The composition of two linear transformations is linear and the matrix of the composition is the product of the matrices of the individual transformations.
7. The inverse of a linear transformation is linear and its matrix is the inverse of the matrix of the original transformation. The inverse exists if and only if the matrix is invertible.

### Kernel and Range of a Linear Transformations

- The kernel of a linear transformation T: V->W is the set of all vectors v in V such that T(v) = 0. It is denoted by ker(T).
- The range of a linear transformation T: V->W is the set of all vectors w in W such that w = T(v) for some v in V. It is denoted by range(T).
- A linear transformation is injective if its kernel is {0}. It is surjective if its range is W. It is bijective if it is both injective and surjective.
- The dimension of the kernel plus the dimension of the range equals the dimension of the domain. That is, dim(ker(T)) + dim(range(T)) = dim(V).
- The rank-nullity theorem: For any linear transformation T: V->W, dim(range(T)) + dim(ker(T)) = dim(V). The rank of T is the dimension of its range.

### Change of Basis

- A change of basis is a linear transformation that converts vectors from one coordinate system to another.
- Given a vector space V and bases B1 and B2 for V, there exists a unique linear transformation T: V->V such that T(b1)=b'1, T(b2)=b'2, ..., T(bn)=b'n, where b1, b2, ..., bn and b'1, b'2, ..., b'n are the basis vectors in B1 and B2 respectively.
- The matrix of T with respect to B1 is the change of basis matrix CB: B2->B1. The elements of CB are the coordinate vectors of the b'i with respect to the bj.
- The vector expression of a vector v in terms of the two bases is related by: v = CBv' (or v' = CBv)
- A change of basis is useful for:
-- Simplifying vector expressions
-- Diagonalizing a linear transformation
-- Generating invariant subspaces
-- Connecting vector spaces with bases of interest (ex. comparing theoretical and empirical bases)

### Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

- Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are important concepts in linear algebra with applications in various fields like machine learning, computer graphics, etc.
- An eigenvector of a matrix is a vector that, when multiplied by the matrix, produces the same vector scaled by a factor called eigenvalue. In other words, a vector v is an eigenvector of a matrix A if Av = λv for some scalar λ. Here, λ is the eigenvalue corresponding to the eigenvector v.
- Not every matrix has eigenvectors. A matrix has eigenvectors only if it has real eigenvalues. The eigenvectors corresponding to different eigenvalues are linearly independent.
- Eigenvalues and eigenvectors can be computed using the characteristic polynomial. The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial and the eigenvectors can be computed from the eigenspace corresponding to an eigenvalue.
- Diagonalization is a process of converting a matrix into a diagonal matrix with the eigenvalues on the diagonal. A square matrix can be diagonalized if it has n linearly independent eigenvectors. Diagonalization simplifies computations and analysis.
- Eigenvalues and eigenvectors have applications in various fields like:
    - Data analysis and pattern recognition - Eigenfaces are used to identify faces
    - Computer graphics - Used to determine deformation and transformation of objects
    - Machine learning - Eigenvalues of a covariance matrix determine its shape and are used in PCA

### Symmetric Matrices and Orthogonal Diagonalization

- A square matrix A is symmetric if A = A^T. The elements on the main diagonal of a symmetric matrix are real.
- A symmetric matrix can be diagonalized by an orthogonal transformation. That is, there exists an orthogonal matrix Q and a diagonal matrix D such that A = QDQ^T.
- The diagonal entries of D are the eigenvalues of A. The columns of Q are the corresponding eigenvectors.
- The eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal. The eigenvectors form an orthogonal basis for the vector space.
- The eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are real. The spectral theorem for symmetric matrices states that a symmetric matrix can be decomposed into a basis of eigenvectors with real eigenvalues on the diagonal.
- The decomposition A = QDQ^T is unique up to the ordering of the diagonal entries of D and the corresponding columns of Q. The eigenvalues are unique but the eigenvectors have arbitrary signs.
- The trace of a symmetric matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues. The determinant of a symmetric matrix is the product of its eigenvalues.
