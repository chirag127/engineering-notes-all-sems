### Development of Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is the study of patterns of relationships that connect social actors, such as individuals, groups, organizations, or communities.
- SNA has its theoretical roots in the work of early sociologists such as Georg Simmel and Émile Durkheim, who wrote about the importance of studying social structures and networks.
- SNA emerged as a distinct field of research in the 1930s, when Jacob Levy Moreno introduced the concepts of sociometry and sociograms, which are graphical representations of social networks .
- SNA developed further in the 1950s and 1960s, with the contributions of researchers such as Alex Bavelas, Anatol Rapoport, Harrison White, and James Coleman, who applied mathematical and statistical methods to analyze social networks .
- SNA gained popularity in the 1970s and 1980s, with the publication of influential books and articles by researchers such as Mark Granovetter, Stanley Milgram, Linton Freeman, Ronald Burt, and Barry Wellman, who introduced new concepts and measures of network properties, such as centrality, density, cohesion, embeddedness, weak ties, and structural holes .
- SNA has become a key technique in modern sociology and has also been adopted by other disciplines, such as anthropology, biology, communication studies, economics, geography, history, information science, organizational studies, political science, public health, social psychology, and development studies  .
- SNA has also benefited from the advances in computer technology, software, and data availability, which have enabled the analysis of large-scale and dynamic networks, such as online social networks, citation networks, and co-authorship networks .
- SNA has also been applied to various practical and policy domains, such as education, health, crime, terrorism, social movements, innovation, and governance .



### Key concepts and measures in network analysis

- Network analysis is a method of studying the structure and behavior of complex systems composed of many interacting elements, such as social networks, computer networks, biological networks, etc.
- Network analysis involves collecting data on the elements (nodes) and their relationships (edges) in a network, and applying mathematical and computational techniques to analyze and visualize the network properties and patterns.
- Some of the key concepts and measures in network analysis are:

  - **Nodes and edges**: Nodes are the basic units of a network, such as individuals, organizations, devices, genes, etc. Edges are the connections or interactions between nodes, such as friendships, collaborations, communications, influences, etc. Nodes and edges can have attributes, such as name, age, gender, weight, type, etc.
  - **Degree**: Degree is the number of edges connected to a node. It measures the local popularity or activity of a node. Nodes with high degree are called hubs or central nodes. Degree can be divided into in-degree (number of incoming edges) and out-degree (number of outgoing edges) for directed networks, where edges have a direction or orientation.
  - **Path and distance**: A path is a sequence of edges that connects two nodes in a network. The distance between two nodes is the length of the shortest path between them. It measures how close or far two nodes are in a network. The average distance of all pairs of nodes in a network is called the characteristic path length. It measures the global efficiency or navigability of a network.
  - **Clustering coefficient**: Clustering coefficient is the fraction of possible triangles that exist in a network. A triangle is a set of three nodes that are all connected to each other. Clustering coefficient measures the local density or cohesion of a network. It indicates how likely two nodes are to be connected if they share a common neighbor. The average clustering coefficient of all nodes in a network is called the global clustering coefficient. It measures the overall tendency of a network to form clusters or communities.
  - **Centrality**: Centrality is a measure of the importance or influence of a node or an edge in a network. There are different types of centrality, such as degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, etc. Each type of centrality captures a different aspect of the node's or edge's role or position in a network. For example, degree centrality measures the local connectivity, closeness centrality measures the global reachability, betweenness centrality measures the control over information flow, and eigenvector centrality measures the influence of neighbors.
  - **Modularity**: Modularity is a measure of the quality of a partition of a network into subgroups or communities. A community is a group of nodes that are more densely connected to each other than to the rest of the network. Modularity compares the actual number of edges within communities to the expected number of edges if the network were random. Modularity ranges from -1 to 1, where higher values indicate better partitions. Modularity can be used to detect and evaluate communities in a network.

- The macro-structure of social networks can be applied to different levels of analysis, such as the meso level (between the macro and the micro levels) or the micro level (the behavior of the individual actors) .

- Electronic sources for network analysis are devices that provide electrical energy to the circuit or network, such as batteries, generators, solar cells, etc. 
- There are two types of electronic sources for network analysis: independent sources and dependent sources. 
- Independent sources are sources that have a fixed value of voltage or current, regardless of the other elements in the circuit or network. For example, a battery is an independent voltage source that provides a constant voltage across its terminals. 
- Dependent sources are sources that have a value of voltage or current that depends on another voltage or current in the circuit or network. For example, a current-controlled voltage source is a dependent voltage source that provides a voltage proportional to a current elsewhere in the circuit or network. 
- Electronic sources for network analysis can be transformed from one type to another, such as from a voltage source to a current source, or vice versa, using source transformation techniques. This can simplify the analysis of complex circuits or networks.

### Knowledge Representation on the Semantic Web

- Knowledge representation (KR) is the field of artificial intelligence that studies how to encode and manipulate information about the world in a form that a computer system can use.
- The Semantic Web is an extension of the current Web that aims to make the data on the Web more accessible and understandable by machines, using standards and technologies that support the representation and integration of semantics (meaning) on the Web.
- The main components of the Semantic Web are:
  - RDF (Resource Description Framework): The data model, or syntax, of the Semantic Web. RDF represents data as a graph of triples, where each triple consists of a subject, a predicate, and an object. RDF can express any kind of data, from simple facts to complex relationships, and can link data across different sources and domains.
  - RDFS (RDF Schema): The vocabulary, or ontology, language of the Semantic Web. RDFS defines the basic concepts and properties that can be used to describe RDF data, such as classes, subclasses, properties, domains, and ranges. RDFS also provides mechanisms for defining custom vocabularies and schemas for specific domains and applications.
  - OWL (Web Ontology Language): The schema language, or knowledge representation (KR) language, of the Semantic Web. OWL enables you to define concepts composably so that these concepts can be reused as much and as often as possible . OWL also supports more expressive and complex forms of reasoning and inference, such as class hierarchies, property restrictions, cardinality constraints, and logical equivalence.
  - SPARQL (SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language): The query language, or interface, of the Semantic Web. SPARQL allows you to retrieve and manipulate data stored in RDF format, using graph patterns and filters. SPARQL can also perform federated queries, which combine data from multiple sources and endpoints.
- The benefits of using KR on the Semantic Web are:
  - It enables the integration and interoperability of heterogeneous and distributed data sources, by providing a common and standardized framework for describing and linking data.
  - It enhances the discovery and reuse of data, by allowing users and applications to query and access data based on its meaning and context, rather than its location or format.
  - It facilitates the automation and intelligence of Web applications, by enabling machines to process and reason about data, and to perform tasks such as classification, validation, and recommendation.
- The challenges of using KR on the Semantic Web are:
  - It requires the development and maintenance of ontologies and schemas that capture the domain knowledge and the semantics of the data, which can be costly and time-consuming.
  - It depends on the availability and quality of the data sources, which may be incomplete, inconsistent, or unreliable.
  - It faces the trade-off between expressivity and scalability, as more complex and expressive forms of KR may require more computational resources and may not be feasible for large-scale and dynamic data.



### Ontologies and their role in the Semantic Web

- Ontologies are formal descriptions of the concepts and relationships in a domain of interest, such as social networks, e-commerce, or education.
- Ontologies provide a shared vocabulary and a common understanding of the domain, which is necessary to overcome the differences in terminology and interpretation among different data sources and applications.
- Ontologies enable the annotation, integration, and analysis of data in a machine-readable and interoperable way, which is the main goal of the Semantic Web.
- Ontologies can be expressed in different languages, such as RDF Schema, OWL, or SKOS, which have different levels of expressiveness and reasoning capabilities.
- Ontologies can be reused, extended, and aligned with other ontologies to create a network of linked data and knowledge on the Web.
- Ontologies can support various tasks and applications on the Semantic Web, such as:

  - Data organization and classification: Ontologies can provide a hierarchical and faceted structure for organizing and browsing data, such as products, services, or documents.
  - Data integration and mapping: Ontologies can help to harmonize and link data from different sources and formats, such as databases, XML, or JSON, by defining common concepts and relationships.
  - Data querying and retrieval: Ontologies can enable the formulation and execution of complex queries over heterogeneous data, such as SPARQL or natural language queries.
  - Data analysis and mining: Ontologies can facilitate the extraction of patterns, trends, and insights from data, such as clustering, association rules, or sentiment analysis.
  - Data visualization and exploration: Ontologies can provide a semantic layer for presenting and interacting with data, such as graphs, charts, or maps.
  - Data validation and quality: Ontologies can define constraints and rules for checking and improving the consistency and accuracy of data, such as integrity constraints, cardinality restrictions, or domain and range axioms.
  - Data inference and reasoning: Ontologies can enable the derivation of new and implicit knowledge from data, such as classification, subsumption, or entailment.



### Ontology languages for the Semantic Web

- Ontology languages are formal languages that are used to represent the concepts, properties, and relations of a domain of knowledge in a machine-readable way.
- Ontology languages enable the semantic web, which is an extension of the current web that aims to make the web content more meaningful and interoperable for humans and machines.
- Ontology languages differ from markup languages such as HTML, which only describe the structure and presentation of web pages, but not their meaning or semantics.
- Some of the main features of ontology languages are:
  - They allow the definition of vocabularies (or terms) that can be used to describe web resources and data.
  - They provide the means to formally specify the meaning and constraints of the vocabularies using logic-based syntax and semantics.
  - They support automated reasoning and inference over the web data and knowledge using logical rules and axioms.
  - They enable the integration and alignment of heterogeneous and distributed web data and knowledge sources using common ontologies or mappings.
- Some of the most widely used ontology languages for the semantic web are:
  - The Resource Description Framework (RDF), which is a standard model for data interchange on the web. RDF uses triples of the form (subject, predicate, object) to represent web resources and their properties and relations.
  - The RDF Schema (RDFS), which is a simple language for defining and describing RDF vocabularies. RDFS allows the specification of classes, properties, domains, ranges, and hierarchies of RDF terms.
  - The Web Ontology Language (OWL), which is a semantic web language based on description logic that extends RDFS with more expressive features. OWL allows the definition of complex classes, properties, restrictions, cardinalities, equivalence, disjointness, and other logical constructs .
  - The Simple Knowledge Organization System (SKOS), which is a common data model for sharing and linking knowledge organization systems such as thesauri, taxonomies, and classifications. SKOS provides a standard way to represent the concepts, labels, definitions, and relations of such systems.
  - The Semantic Web Rule Language (SWRL), which is a rule language for the semantic web that combines the sublanguages of OWL and RuleML. SWRL allows the definition of Horn-like rules that can be applied to the RDF and OWL data and ontologies.



### The Resource Description Framework (RDF) and RDF Schema

- RDF is a standard model for data interchange on the Web.
- RDF uses a graph-based data model to represent information as a set of triples, each consisting of a subject, a predicate, and an object.
- RDF allows data to be linked across different sources and domains, and supports the integration and reuse of data.
- RDF can be serialized in different formats, such as XML, JSON, Turtle, and N-Triples.
- RDF Schema (RDFS) is a set of classes and properties that extend the RDF data model and provide basic elements for the description of ontologies.
- RDFS defines the concepts of class, subclass, property, subproperty, domain, range, and literal, and provides mechanisms for defining vocabularies and schemas.
- RDFS uses the idea of the XML namespace to allow RDF statements to reference a particular RDF vocabulary or schema.
- RDFS can be used to express constraints, hierarchies, and semantics of RDF data, and to enable reasoning and inference.



### The Web Ontology Language (OWL)

- OWL is a Semantic Web language that is designed to process and integrate information over the web, making sense of it in a manner similar to human reasoning.
- OWL is intended to facilitate interpretability among web content using vocabulary and formatting that allows automatic machine processing.
- OWL can be used to describe the classes and relations between them that are inherent in Web documents and applications.
- OWL can also describe the properties of Web resources, such as their characteristics, constraints, and behaviors.
- OWL is a family of knowledge representation languages for authoring ontologies.
- Ontologies are a formal way to describe taxonomies and classification networks, essentially defining the structure of knowledge for various domains.
- OWL has three sublanguages with different levels of expressiveness and complexity: OWL Lite, OWL DL, and OWL Full.
- OWL Lite is the simplest and least expressive sublanguage, suitable for simple classification hierarchies and constraints.
- OWL DL is based on description logic, a subset of first-order logic, and supports more expressive features such as cardinality, disjointness, and equality.
- OWL Full is the most expressive and complex sublanguage, which allows arbitrary combinations of OWL constructs and RDF syntax.
- OWL is a W3C recommendation and has a standard syntax in XML, as well as alternative syntaxes in RDF/XML, Turtle, Manchester, and Functional.
- OWL can be used for various applications, such as semantic annotation, information integration, ontology engineering, natural language processing, and reasoning.



### Comparison to the Unified Modelling Language (UML)

- UML is a graphical language for modeling software systems, whereas OWL is a language for knowledge representation in a semantic perspective.
- UML focuses on the structure and behavior of software systems, including their classes, objects, behaviors, and interactions  , whereas OWL focuses on the meaning and inference of concepts, properties, and individuals in a domain.
- UML provides a standard way to visualize the design of a system, whereas OWL provides a standard way to express the semantics of a domain.
- UML supports multiple diagram types, such as use case, class, sequence, activity, state, etc., to capture different aspects of a system   , whereas OWL supports only one syntax, which is based on RDF/XML, to represent the ontology of a domain.
- UML is mainly used for software engineering, whereas OWL is mainly used for semantic web applications.

- The E/R model can handle complex and hierarchical data, whereas the relational model can handle simple and flat data   .
- The E/R model is more suitable for the initial phases of database development, whereas the relational model is more suitable for the implementation and operation of a database   .



### Comparison to the Extensible Markup Language (XML) and XML Schema

- XML is a markup language that defines a set of rules for encoding documents in a format that is both human-readable and machine-readable.
- XML Schema (XSD) is a language that specifies how to formally describe the elements and attributes in an XML document.
- XML and XSD are both based on the Standard Generalized Markup Language (SGML), which is a meta-language for defining markup languages.
- Some of the differences between XML and XSD are :
  - XML is used to describe data, while XSD is used to define the structure and format of the data.
  - XML does not have any predefined tags or attributes, while XSD defines a set of built-in data types and allows the creation of custom data types.
  - XML does not enforce any validation rules on the data, while XSD provides mechanisms to check the validity and consistency of the data.
  - XML can be used independently of any schema, while XSD depends on XML for its syntax and structure.
  - XML can be processed by any XML parser, while XSD requires a schema-aware XML processor to validate the XML document against the schema.
- Some of the advantages of using XML and XSD are :
  - XML simplifies data exchange and integration between different systems and platforms, as it is independent of any software or hardware.
  - XML stores data in plain text format, which makes it easy to read and edit by humans and machines.

### State-of-the-art in network data representation for the notes of the Unit 3 - Modelling and Aggregating Social Network Data in the subject of SOCIAL NETWORK ANALYTICS

- Network data representation is the process of encoding network data into low-dimensional vectors, also known as network embeddings, that can preserve the network topology structure and other attribute information .
- Network embeddings can facilitate various tasks in social network analysis, such as link prediction, node classification, community detection, recommendation, etc .
- The state-of-the-art methods for network data representation can be categorized into three types: matrix factorization-based methods, random walk-based methods, and deep learning-based methods .
- Matrix factorization-based methods aim to factorize the network adjacency matrix or other similarity matrices into low-rank matrices, and use the rows or columns of the factorized matrices as the node embeddings . Examples of such methods are Laplacian Eigenmaps, Locally Linear Embedding, and Singular Value Decomposition.
- Random walk-based methods generate node sequences by performing random walks on the network, and then apply natural language processing techniques, such as Skip-gram, to learn node embeddings from the node sequences . Examples of such methods are DeepWalk, node2vec, and LINE.
- Deep learning-based methods leverage various deep neural network architectures, such as autoencoders, convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, and graph neural networks, to learn node embeddings from the network structure and node features . Examples of such methods are DNGR, GraphSAGE, and GCN.

### Evaluation of web-based social network extraction

- Web-based social network extraction is the process of identifying and describing the actors and their relations from online sources, such as web pages, social media platforms, blogs, etc.  
- Web-based social network extraction can benefit many applications, such as web mining, social network analysis, recommender systems, sentiment analysis, etc. 
- Web-based social network extraction can be performed using different techniques, such as superficial methods, supervised methods, semi-supervised methods, and unsupervised methods.   
- Superficial methods rely on the textual similarity between web pages to determine the actors and their relations. They are simple and fast, but they may not capture the semantic meaning of the web content.  
- Supervised methods use labeled data to train a classifier or a extractor that can identify the actors and their relations from web pages. They are accurate and robust, but they require a lot of annotated data and domain knowledge. 
- Semi-supervised methods use a small amount of labeled data and a large amount of unlabeled data to learn the features and patterns of the actors and their relations from web pages. They are flexible and scalable, but they may suffer from noise and inconsistency. 
- Unsupervised methods use clustering, topic modeling, or graph analysis to discover the actors and their relations from web pages without any prior knowledge. They are general and adaptable, but they may produce ambiguous or irrelevant results. 
- Web-based social network extraction can be evaluated using different criteria, such as precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, completeness, consistency, etc.  
- Precision measures the fraction of correctly extracted actors and relations among all the extracted ones.  
- Recall measures the fraction of correctly extracted actors and relations among all the existing ones.  
- F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. It balances the trade-off between precision and recall.  
- Accuracy measures the fraction of correctly extracted actors and relations among all the web pages. 
- Completeness measures the degree to which all the actors and relations in the web pages are extracted. 
- Consistency measures the degree to which the extracted actors and relations are coherent and compatible with each other. 
- Web-based social network extraction can be evaluated using different methods, such as manual evaluation, automatic evaluation, or hybrid evaluation.  
- Manual evaluation involves human experts or users to judge the quality of the extracted actors and relations. It is reliable and intuitive, but it is time-consuming and subjective.  
- Automatic evaluation involves computational tools or metrics to compare the extracted actors and relations with a gold standard or a baseline. It is fast and objective, but it may not capture the human perception or the domain context.  
- Hybrid evaluation combines manual and automatic evaluation to leverage the advantages of both. It is comprehensive and flexible, but it may introduce more complexity and cost.



### Data collection for social network analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is a method of studying the structure and dynamics of social relationships among a set of actors, such as individuals, groups, or organizations.
- Data collection for SNA involves identifying the actors and the ties that connect them, as well as any attributes or characteristics of the actors or the ties.
- There are different types of data collection methods for SNA, depending on the research question, the scope of the network, and the availability of data sources.
- Some common data collection methods for SNA are:

  - **Saturation surveys**: These are surveys that are administered to all actors in a defined network, such as a classroom, a village, or an organization. The respondents are provided with a roster of all network members, and they are asked to indicate their relationship with each member, such as friendship, communication, collaboration, or trust. This method allows for a complete and accurate mapping of the network structure and the strength of the ties. However, it can be costly, time-consuming, and difficult to implement, especially for large or dispersed networks.  
  - **Snowball sampling**: This is a technique that starts with a small set of actors, and then asks them to nominate other actors with whom they have a relationship. The process is repeated until a desired network size or saturation is reached. This method is useful for identifying hidden or hard-to-reach populations, such as drug users, homeless people, or activists. However, it can introduce bias and error, as the initial actors may not be representative of the whole network, and some actors may be missed or overrepresented. 
  - **Archival data**: This is data that is derived from existing sources, such as records, documents, databases, or online platforms. The data can be used to infer the actors and the ties in a network, such as co-authorship, email exchange, phone calls, or social media interactions. This method is convenient, inexpensive, and unobtrusive, as it does not require direct contact with the actors. However, it can be incomplete, outdated, or inaccurate, as it may not capture the full range of relationships or the context and meaning of the interactions. 
  - **Observation**: This is a method that involves observing and recording the behavior and interactions of the actors in a natural or artificial setting, such as a classroom, a workplace, or a laboratory. The observer can use a checklist, a coding scheme, or a video camera to document the frequency, duration, and type of the ties among the actors. This method can provide rich and detailed data on the network dynamics and the quality of the relationships. However, it can be intrusive, subjective, and ethically problematic, as it may affect the behavior of the actors or violate their privacy. 

- Data collection for SNA should follow the principles of validity, reliability, and ethics, as well as the specific requirements of the research design and the analysis techniques.

    - Network growth: the addition or removal of nodes and links over time.

- SNA studies the behavior of the individual at the micro level, the pattern of relationships (network structure) at the macro level, and the interactions between the two.
- SNA is based on theoretical constructs of sociology and mathematical foundations of graph theory.
- SNA can be used to study various phenomena, such as communication, collaboration, influence, diffusion, power, trust, and social capital.
- SNA can be applied to different domains, such as health, politics, marketing, criminology, and computer science.
- SNA uses various methods and techniques to collect, analyze, and visualize network data, such as surveys, interviews, observation, web scraping, network metrics, centrality measures, clustering, community detection, and network graphs  .
- SNA requires careful evaluation of the validity, reliability, and generalizability of the network data and analysis.
- SNA evaluation can involve different criteria, such as data quality, network representation, network comparison, network inference, network prediction, and network intervention.
- SNA evaluation can also consider the ethical, legal, and social implications of network data and analysis, such as privacy, consent, confidentiality, and fairness.



### Structural and cognitive effects on scientific performance

- Scientific performance is the outcome of scientific activities, such as publishing papers, obtaining grants, or receiving citations.
- Scientific performance can be influenced by various factors, such as individual characteristics, environmental conditions, and social networks.
- Social networks are the patterns of relationships and interactions among scientists, such as co-authorship, collaboration, or citation.
- Social network analysis is a method to study the structure and dynamics of social networks, using concepts such as nodes, links, centrality, density, and clustering.
- Social network analysis can help to evaluate the impact of social networks on scientific performance, by measuring how network properties affect individual and collective outcomes.
- Structural effects refer to how the shape and size of the network influence scientific performance, such as how central or peripheral a scientist is in the network, or how dense or sparse the network is.
- Cognitive effects refer to how the content and quality of the network influence scientific performance, such as how diverse or homogeneous the network is, or how innovative or conservative the network is.
- Some examples of structural and cognitive effects on scientific performance are:

  - Central positions in the network can have positive or negative effects on scientific performance, depending on the context and the type of centrality. For instance, degree centrality (the number of direct connections) can increase the visibility and reputation of a scientist, but also the competition and pressure. Betweenness centrality (the number of shortest paths passing through a node) can increase the access and control of information and resources, but also the workload and stress.
  - Network density (the proportion of existing links among all possible links) can have positive or negative effects on scientific performance, depending on the balance between cohesion and diversity. For instance, high network density can foster trust and cooperation among scientists, but also reduce creativity and novelty. Low network density can enhance diversity and innovation, but also increase uncertainty and conflict.
  - Network diversity (the variety of attributes and perspectives among network members) can have positive or negative effects on scientific performance, depending on the level and type of diversity. For instance, high cognitive diversity (the difference in knowledge and skills) can improve problem-solving and learning, but also hinder communication and coordination. High demographic diversity (the difference in age, gender, ethnicity, etc.) can increase social capital and representation, but also generate stereotypes and biases.
  - Network innovation (the degree of novelty and originality of the network output) can have positive or negative effects on scientific performance, depending on the trade-off between risk and reward. For instance, high network innovation can lead to breakthroughs and discoveries, but also to failures and rejections. Low network innovation can ensure reliability and acceptance, but also limit impact and recognition.
