## Unit 1 - Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is the study of the structure, patterns, and dynamics of social relationships among individuals, groups, or organizations.
- SNA uses mathematical and computational methods to represent and analyze social networks as graphs, where nodes are actors (such as people, organizations, or events) and edges are ties (such as friendship, collaboration, or communication).
- SNA can be used to answer various questions about social phenomena, such as how information, influence, or diseases spread in a network, how groups or communities form and evolve, how network structure affects individual or collective behavior, and how network interventions can improve social outcomes.
- SNA can be applied to various domains, such as sociology, psychology, economics, political science, public health, computer science, and communication.
- SNA involves several steps, such as defining the research question, collecting and preprocessing network data, choosing appropriate network measures and models, performing network analysis and visualization, and interpreting and reporting the results.
- SNA requires some basic concepts and terminology, such as:

  - Degree: the number of ties a node has with other nodes in the network.
  - Path: a sequence of nodes and edges that connects two nodes in the network.
  - Distance: the length of the shortest path between two nodes in the network.
  - Diameter: the maximum distance among any pair of nodes in the network.
  - Centrality: a measure of the importance or influence of a node in the network, based on its position, degree, or connections.
  - Density: the ratio of the number of actual edges to the number of possible edges in the network.
  - Clustering: the tendency of nodes to form groups or communities within the network, based on their shared ties or attributes.
  - Homophily: the tendency of nodes to associate with similar nodes in the network, based on their attributes or preferences.
  - Heterophily: the tendency of nodes to associate with dissimilar nodes in the network, based on their attributes or preferences.
  - Modularity: a measure of the quality of the network partition into communities, based on the density of edges within and between communities.
  - Network type: a classification of the network based on its characteristics, such as:

    - Directed or undirected: whether the edges have a direction or not, indicating the direction of the relationship or interaction.
    - Weighted or unweighted: whether the edges have a weight or not, indicating the strength or intensity of the relationship or interaction.
    - Binary or valued: whether the edges have a binary value (0 or 1) or a continuous value, indicating the presence or absence of the relationship or interaction.
    - Simple or complex: whether the network has only one type of node and edge or multiple types of nodes and edges, indicating the diversity or multiplexity of the relationship or interaction.
    - Static or dynamic: whether the network is fixed or changes over time, indicating the stability or evolution of the relationship or interaction.

# Development of Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is a research method that focuses on patterns of relations among people and among groups such as organizations and states.
- SNA can be used to study various phenomena such as communication, collaboration, influence, trust, social capital, diffusion, and power.
- SNA has its origins in the late nineteenth century with the precursor work of Auguste Comte, who proposed a positivist approach to sociology and coined the term "social physics" .
- SNA developed as a methodology in the twentieth century, influenced by mathematics, namely graph theory, which provided the tools to represent and analyze networks as sets of nodes and links .
- Some of the key methodological landmarks in the development of SNA are:
  - The work of Burt (1982), who introduced the concept of structural holes and showed how network positions affect individual and organizational outcomes.
  - The work of Freeman et al. (1989), who developed software for network analysis and visualization, and proposed measures of centrality, density, and cohesion.
  - The work of Wasserman and Faust (1994), who wrote a comprehensive textbook on SNA, covering topics such as data collection, network models, network statistics, and network dynamics.
  - The work of Wasserman and Galaskiewicz (1994), who edited a volume on social network analysis in organizations, highlighting the applications of SNA to management, innovation, and performance.
  - The work of Scott (2000; originally published 1991), who wrote an introductory text on SNA, explaining the basic concepts, methods, and examples of SNA in various fields.
  - The work of Carrington et al. (2005), who edited a collection on models and methods in social network analysis, presenting the latest developments and challenges in SNA.
- SNA has also been influenced by various theoretical perspectives, such as network theory, social physics, social networks in economics, relational sociology, culture, and agency.
- SNA has become a popular and interdisciplinary research method, with applications in fields such as sociology, communication, psychology, education, political science, public health, computer science, and information science .
- SNA has also been used to study web hyperlinks, as the web connects people and organizations and can host social networks.
- SNA faces some limitations and challenges, such as the quality and availability of network data, the ethical and legal issues of network research, the complexity and dynamics of network structures, and the integration of network analysis with other methods and theories.



# Key concepts and measures in network analysis

- Network analysis is a method of studying the structure and behavior of complex systems composed of many interacting elements, such as social networks, computer networks, biological networks, etc.  
- Network analysis involves the following steps:
  - Network data collection: gathering information about the elements and their interactions in the system of interest.
  - Network representation: choosing a suitable way to model the system as a network, such as a graph, a matrix, or a tensor.
  - Network analysis and review: applying various techniques and measures to analyze the network and its properties, such as centrality, density, clustering, community detection, etc.
  - Network interpretation and visualization: deriving insights and conclusions from the network analysis and presenting them in a clear and meaningful way, such as using charts, diagrams, or maps.
- Some of the key concepts and measures in network analysis are   :
  - Node: an element or an actor in the network, such as a person, a computer, a gene, etc.
  - Edge: a connection or a relation between two nodes in the network, such as a friendship, a communication, a interaction, etc.
  - Degree: the number of edges connected to a node, indicating its activity or popularity in the network.
  - Path: a sequence of edges that connects two nodes in the network, indicating their distance or closeness in the network.
  - Shortest path: the path with the minimum number of edges between two nodes in the network, indicating their efficiency or accessibility in the network.
  - Diameter: the maximum shortest path length among all pairs of nodes in the network, indicating the size or span of the network.
  - Centrality: a measure of the importance or influence of a node in the network, based on various criteria, such as degree, closeness, betweenness, eigenvector, etc.
  - Density: the ratio of the number of actual edges to the number of possible edges in the network, indicating the connectivity or cohesion of the network.
  - Clustering: the tendency of nodes to form groups or communities in the network, based on their similarity or affinity in the network.
  - Modularity: a measure of the quality of the network partition into clusters or communities, based on the difference between the actual and the expected number of edges within and between the clusters.
  - Network type: a classification of the network based on its characteristics, such as directed or undirected, weighted or unweighted, bipartite or multipartite, etc.
  - Network model: a mathematical or statistical representation of the network that captures its essential features, such as random graph, small-world, scale-free, etc.

# The macro-structure of social networks

- A social network is a social structure made up of a set of social actors (such as individuals or organizations), sets of dyadic ties, and other social interactions between actors.
- The macro-structure of social networks refers to the overall pattern of relationships and interactions among the actors in the network, as well as the properties and functions of the network as a whole.
- The macro-structure of social networks can be analyzed from three levels: micro-level, meso-level, and macro-level.
  - Micro-level: This level focuses on the behavior and attributes of individual actors and their immediate ties in the network. For example, how an actor's position, centrality, or embeddedness in the network affects their outcomes, such as power, influence, or performance.
  - Meso-level: This level focuses on the subgroups and clusters of actors within the network, and how they are connected and organized by social institutions and institutionalized social relationships. For example, how communities, cliques, or coalitions form and operate in the network, and how they are influenced by norms, values, or culture.
  - Macro-level: This level focuses on the global and historical aspects of the network, and how they affect and are affected by social change and social movements. For example, how the network structure influences or reflects civil uprising, state formation, policy diffusion, or economic development.
- The macro-structure of social networks can be measured and visualized by using various methods and techniques of social network analysis, such as network density, diameter, degree distribution, modularity, centrality, or network graphs.
- The macro-structure of social networks can provide insights into the social processes and mechanisms that shape and are shaped by the network, such as diffusion, contagion, homophily, reciprocity, or social capital.

# Electronic sources for network analysis

- Network analysis is the process of determining the electrical parameters of a circuit or a network of interconnected components, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, voltage sources, current sources, etc. 
- Electronic sources for network analysis are devices that provide electrical energy to the circuit or network, such as batteries, generators, solar cells, etc. 
- Electronic sources can be classified into two types: independent sources and dependent sources. 
- Independent sources are sources that have a fixed value of voltage or current, regardless of the rest of the circuit. For example, a battery is an independent voltage source that provides a constant voltage across its terminals. 
- Dependent sources are sources that have a value of voltage or current that depends on another voltage or current in the circuit. For example, a current-controlled voltage source is a dependent voltage source that provides a voltage proportional to a current elsewhere in the circuit. 
- Electronic sources can also be classified into two types: ideal sources and practical sources. 
- Ideal sources are sources that have no internal resistance or impedance, meaning that they can provide any amount of power to the circuit without any loss. For example, an ideal voltage source can maintain a constant voltage across its terminals, regardless of the current drawn from it. 
- Practical sources are sources that have some internal resistance or impedance, meaning that they cannot provide unlimited power to the circuit and have some voltage drop or power loss. For example, a practical voltage source has a finite internal resistance, which causes the voltage across its terminals to decrease as the current drawn from it increases. 
- Electronic sources for network analysis can be used to model and simulate various electrical phenomena, such as power generation, distribution, transmission, consumption, etc. 
- Electronic sources for network analysis can also be used to study and analyze various social phenomena, such as communication, collaboration, influence, diffusion, etc. 
- Electronic sources for network analysis can be accessed and manipulated using various tools and methods, such as search engines, databases, software, algorithms, etc.

# Knowledge Representation on the Semantic Web

- Some of the main KR languages and standards for the Semantic Web are:
  - RDF (Resource Description Framework): The data model and syntax for representing data as triples of subject, predicate and object.
  - RDFS (RDF Schema): The vocabulary and schema language for defining classes and properties of RDF resources.
  - OWL (Web Ontology Language): The schema language, or KR language, of the Semantic Web. OWL enables you to define concepts composably so that these concepts can be reused as much and as often as possible.
  - SPARQL (SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language): The query language for retrieving and manipulating RDF data across various systems.
- The Semantic Web also uses URIs (Uniform Resource Identifiers) to identify and link resources, and XML (Extensible Markup Language) to encode and exchange data.
- The benefits of using KR on the Semantic Web are:
  - It enables the integration and reuse of data from different sources and domains.
  - It facilitates the discovery and access of relevant and reliable information.
  - It supports the automation and enhancement of tasks and services on the Web.
  - It enables the development of intelligent applications and agents that can reason and infer new knowledge from the data.

# Ontology languages for the Semantic Web

- Ontology languages are formal languages that are used to represent the meaning and structure of knowledge domains on the web.
- Ontology languages enable the creation, sharing, and reuse of ontologies, which are explicit specifications of the concepts, properties, and relations in a domain.
- Ontology languages also support automated reasoning and inference, which can enhance the functionality and intelligence of web applications and services.
- Some of the main ontology languages for the semantic web are:

  - **XML (eXtensible Markup Language)**: A general-purpose markup language that defines a set of rules for encoding documents in a human-readable and machine-readable format. XML can be used to represent the syntax and structure of ontologies, but not their semantics. XML is the basis for other ontology languages, such as RDF and OWL.
  - **RDF (Resource Description Framework)**: A framework for describing and linking resources on the web using triples of the form (subject, predicate, object). RDF can represent the basic semantics of ontologies, such as classes, instances, and properties, but not more complex axioms and constraints. RDF is the standard data model for the semantic web.
  - **OWL (Web Ontology Language)**: A family of ontology languages that extend RDF with more expressive features, such as cardinality, disjointness, equivalence, and inheritance. OWL can represent the rich and complex knowledge about things, groups of things, and relations between things on the web. OWL is based on description logic and supports various levels of reasoning and consistency checking .
  - **RDFS (RDF Schema)**: A simple ontology language that defines the basic vocabulary and schema for RDF, such as classes, subclasses, properties, and domains. RDFS can be seen as a subset of OWL, but with less expressivity and reasoning capabilities.
  - **SHOE (Simple HTML Ontology Extensions)**: An ontology language that extends HTML with tags for defining classes, instances, properties, and relations. SHOE can be embedded in HTML documents and can be processed by SHOE-aware browsers or agents.
  - **OIL (Ontology Interchange Language)**: An ontology language that combines the features of RDF, RDFS, and description logic. OIL can represent the semantics and constraints of ontologies, as well as the mappings and translations between different ontologies.
  - **DAML (DARPA Agent Markup Language)**: An ontology language that extends OIL with additional constructs, such as collections, defaults, and rules. DAML is designed to facilitate the communication and interoperability of agents on the web.

- Ontology languages for the semantic web are standardized and recommended by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), which is the main international organization for developing web standards.



# The Resource Description Framework (RDF) and RDF Schema

- RDF is a standard model for data interchange on the Web.
- RDF uses a graph-based data model to represent information as a set of triples, each consisting of a subject, a predicate, and an object.
- RDF allows data to be linked across different sources and domains, and supports the integration and reuse of data.
- RDF can be serialized in different formats, such as XML, JSON, Turtle, and N-Triples.
- RDF Schema (RDFS) is a set of classes and properties that extend the RDF data model and provide basic elements for the description of ontologies.
- RDFS defines the concepts of class, subclass, property, subproperty, domain, range, and other terms that can be used to define the structure and semantics of RDF data.
- RDFS also provides a vocabulary for expressing metadata about RDF resources, such as labels, comments, and definitions.
- RDFS can be used to create custom vocabularies or schemas that can be referenced by RDF statements using XML namespaces .
- RDFS is a simple and expressive language that can be used to define hierarchies, constraints, and inference rules for RDF data.



# The Web Ontology Language (OWL)

# State-of-the-art in network data representation for social network analysis

- Network data representation, also known as network embedding, is the process of encoding network data into low-dimensional vectors that can preserve the network structure and attributes .

- Network data representation can facilitate various tasks in social network analysis, such as node classification, link prediction, community detection, network visualization, and recommendation systems .
- The state-of-the-art methods for network data representation can be categorized into four types: matrix factorization, random walk, deep learning, and graph neural networks .
- Matrix factorization methods aim to decompose the network adjacency matrix or other similarity matrices into low-rank matrices, and use the rows or columns of these matrices as the node embeddings. Examples of these methods are Laplacian Eigenmaps, Locally Linear Embedding, and Singular Value Decomposition .
- Random walk methods generate node sequences by performing random walks on the network, and then apply natural language processing techniques, such as Skip-gram or GloVe, to learn node embeddings from these sequences. Examples of these methods are DeepWalk, node2vec, and LINE .
- Deep learning methods use neural networks to learn node embeddings from network data, either in an unsupervised or supervised manner. Examples of these methods are SDNE, DNGR, and GraphGAN .
- Graph neural networks are a class of deep learning methods that can directly operate on graph data and learn node embeddings by aggregating information from neighboring nodes. Examples of these methods are GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE .
- The state-of-the-art methods for network data representation have different advantages and disadvantages, depending on the network characteristics, such as size, density, heterogeneity, and dynamics. Therefore, there is no single best method for all scenarios, and the choice of the method should be based on the specific application and data .

# The generic architecture of Semantic Web applications

Semantic Web applications are software systems that use Semantic Web technologies to provide intelligent and interoperable services on the Web. Semantic Web technologies include standards and languages for representing and querying data, such as RDF, OWL, SPARQL, etc. Semantic Web applications can also use ontologies, which are formal and explicit specifications of the concepts and relationships in a domain of interest.

The generic architecture of Semantic Web applications can be described by the following layers:

- **URI and Unicode layer**: This layer follows the existing features of the Web, such as using Uniform Resource Identifiers (URIs) to identify resources and Unicode to encode international character sets.
- **XML layer**: This layer provides a common syntax for data exchange and validation, using Extensible Markup Language (XML), XML namespaces, and XML Schema definitions.
- **RDF layer**: This layer provides a data model and a syntax for representing and exchanging data as graphs of resources and properties, using Resource Description Framework (RDF), RDF Schema, and RDF serializations (such as RDF/XML, Turtle, JSON-LD, etc.).
- **Ontology layer**: This layer provides a language and a logic for defining and reasoning about the concepts and relationships in a domain, using Web Ontology Language (OWL) and its profiles (such as OWL 2 EL, OWL 2 QL, OWL 2 RL, etc.).
- **Query and Inference layer**: This layer provides a language and a mechanism for querying and inferring data from RDF graphs and ontologies, using SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language (SPARQL) and its extensions (such as SPARQL 1.1 Federated Query, SPARQL 1.1 Update, SPARQL 1.1 Entailment Regimes, etc.).
- **Rules layer**: This layer provides a language and a mechanism for defining and executing rules on RDF graphs and ontologies, using Rule Interchange Format (RIF) and its dialects (such as RIF Basic Logic Dialect, RIF Production Rule Dialect, RIF Core, etc.).
- **Trust and Proof layer**: This layer provides a framework and a mechanism for establishing and verifying the trustworthiness and provenance of data and services on the Semantic Web, using Web of Trust, digital signatures, certificates, etc.
- **User Interface and Applications layer**: This layer provides the tools and the methods for developing and interacting with Semantic Web applications, such as browsers, editors, visualizers, annotators, etc.

# Optimizing goodness of fit for the notes of the Unit 5 - Evaluation of Social Network Analysis

- Goodness of fit is a measure of how well a statistical model fits the observed data. It can be used to compare different models and select the best one for a given dataset.
- In social network analysis, goodness of fit can be applied to various types of models, such as exponential random graph models (ERGMs), relational event models (REMs), or two-mode networks.
- ERGMs are models that specify the probability of observing a network based on a set of structural features, such as density, reciprocity, transitivity, homophily, etc. ERGMs can capture complex dependencies among network ties and account for network heterogeneity.
- REMs are models that describe the occurrence and timing of events in a network, such as communication, collaboration, or conflict. REMs can capture the dynamics and evolution of network structures over time and account for temporal dependencies among events.
- Two-mode networks are networks that consist of two sets of nodes, such as actors and events, and links between nodes of different sets, such as participation or affiliation. Two-mode networks can reveal the dual nature of social structures and the interplay between individual agency and institutional constraints.
- To assess the goodness of fit of social network models, various methods and criteria can be used, such as:
  - Likelihood ratio tests: These tests compare the log-likelihood of a model with a null model or a nested model and determine whether the model provides a significantly better fit to the data.
  - Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC): These criteria balance the fit and complexity of a model and penalize models with more parameters. The model with the lowest AIC or BIC is preferred.
  - Graphical diagnostics: These methods compare the observed network statistics with the simulated network statistics from the fitted model and check whether the model can reproduce the empirical patterns in the data.
  - Predictive accuracy: These methods evaluate how well a model can predict future network events or missing network data and measure the error or accuracy of the predictions.
- To optimize the goodness of fit of social network models, various strategies can be used, such as:
  - Model selection: This involves choosing the most appropriate type of model and the most relevant set of parameters for a given network dataset and research question.
  - Model specification: This involves defining the functional form and the distributional assumptions of the model and ensuring that they are consistent with the data and the theory.
  - Model estimation: This involves finding the best-fitting values of the parameters and ensuring that the estimation method is reliable and convergent.
  - Model validation: This involves testing the robustness and sensitivity of the model and checking for potential problems, such as overfitting, underfitting, degeneracy, or misspecification.

# Predicting the goodness of fit for the notes of the Unit 5 - Evaluation of Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is a method of studying the patterns and structures of relationships between individuals or groups in a network.
- SNA can be used to understand various social phenomena, such as diffusion of information, influence, cooperation, conflict, and community formation.
- SNA can also be used to evaluate the effectiveness and impact of interventions that aim to change or improve the network structure or behavior of the actors.
- To evaluate SNA, one needs to compare the observed network data with some expected or theoretical network model that represents the underlying social process or mechanism that generates the network.
- A network model can be specified by a set of parameters that determine the probability of forming or maintaining a tie between any pair of actors in the network.
- A common way of estimating the parameters of a network model is to use maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), which finds the values of the parameters that maximize the likelihood of observing the given network data.
- However, MLE does not provide any information about how well the network model fits the data, or how well the model captures the salient features and characteristics of the network.
- Therefore, one needs to use some goodness-of-fit (GOF) measures or tests to assess the adequacy and validity of the network model.
- GOF measures or tests compare the observed network data with the expected network data generated by the model, and quantify the degree of similarity or discrepancy between them.
- There are different types of GOF measures or tests, depending on the level of analysis, the type of network model, and the type of network data.
- Some examples of GOF measures or tests are:

  - Chi-square test: A statistical test that compares the observed and expected frequencies of ties or attributes in the network, and tests whether the difference is significant or due to chance. The test statistic follows a chi-square distribution, and the null hypothesis is that the observed and expected frequencies are equal. The test is usually right-tailed, and the expected frequency for each cell needs to be at least five. 
  - Kolmogorov-Smirnov test: A non-parametric test that compares the observed and expected cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of network statistics, such as degree, clustering, or distance, and tests whether the difference is significant or due to chance.
