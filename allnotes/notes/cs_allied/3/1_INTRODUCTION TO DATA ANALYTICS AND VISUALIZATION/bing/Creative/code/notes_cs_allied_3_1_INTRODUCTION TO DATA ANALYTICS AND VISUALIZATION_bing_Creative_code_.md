### Sources and nature of data

- Data is any type of information that can be measured, accessed or used in any way in a given study.
- Data can be gathered from two places: internal and external sources.
- Internal sources are those that are generated within the organization, such as sales records, customer feedback, inventory data, etc.
- External sources are those that are obtained from outside the organization, such as government reports, market surveys, social media, etc.
- The sources of data can be classified into two types: statistical and non-statistical .
- Statistical sources refer to data that is gathered for some official purposes, such as censuses, and officially administered surveys .
- Non-statistical sources refer to the collection of data for other administrative purposes or for the private sector .
- The nature of data can be classified into two types: qualitative and quantitative.
- Qualitative data is data that is descriptive and subjective, such as opinions, feelings, attitudes, etc.
- Quantitative data is data that is numerical and objective, such as measurements, counts, percentages, etc.
- The nature of data can also be classified into two types: primary and secondary.
- Primary data is data that is collected directly by the researcher for the specific purpose of the study, such as interviews, experiments, observations, etc.
- Secondary data is data that is collected by someone else for a different purpose, and is reused by the researcher for the study, such as books, journals, websites, etc.

### Classification of Data

- Data can be classified into different types based on the nature, source, format, and level of abstraction of the information they contain.
- Some of the common types of data are:

  - **Quantitative data**: Data that can be measured or counted using numerical values, such as height, weight, temperature, income, etc. Quantitative data can be further classified into discrete data (data that can only take certain values, such as number of students, coins, etc.) and continuous data (data that can take any value within a range, such as height, weight, etc.).
  - **Qualitative data**: Data that can be described or categorized using words, symbols, images, sounds, etc., such as color, gender, name, emotion, etc. Qualitative data can be further classified into nominal data (data that can only be named or labeled, such as gender, blood type, etc.) and ordinal data (data that can be ordered or ranked, such as grade, satisfaction, etc.).
  - **Structured data**: Data that can be organized and stored in a predefined format, such as tables, spreadsheets, databases, etc. Structured data is easy to process and analyze using standard tools and techniques, such as SQL, Excel, etc.
  - **Unstructured data**: Data that does not have a predefined format or structure, such as text, images, videos, audio, etc. Unstructured data is difficult to process and analyze using standard tools and techniques, and may require specialized methods, such as natural language processing, computer vision, etc.
  - **Semi-structured data**: Data that has some elements of structure, such as tags, labels, metadata, etc., but not enough to fit into a predefined format, such as XML, JSON, HTML, etc. Semi-structured data is easier to process and analyze than unstructured data, but may still require some parsing and extraction techniques, such as XPath, JSONPath, etc.
  - **Primary data**: Data that is collected directly from the source or the original phenomenon, such as surveys, experiments, observations, etc. Primary data is usually more accurate, reliable, and relevant, but may also be more costly, time-consuming, and difficult to obtain.
  - **Secondary data**: Data that is collected from other sources or derived from primary data, such as books, reports, websites, databases, etc. Secondary data is usually less accurate, reliable, and relevant, but may also be more accessible, affordable, and convenient to obtain.
  - **Aggregated data**: Data that is summarized or grouped based on some criteria, such as average, sum, count, etc. Aggregated data is useful for providing an overview or a general trend of the data, but may also lose some details or variations of the data.
  - **Disaggregated data**: Data that is broken down or separated into smaller units or categories, such as gender, age, location, etc. Disaggregated data is useful for providing more insights or comparisons of the data, but may also increase the complexity or volume of the data.

### Characteristics of Data

- Data is any information that can be collected, stored, processed, analyzed, and communicated.
- Data can be classified into different types based on its nature, source, format, and quality.
- Some common types of data are:
  - **Quantitative data**: Data that can be measured or counted, such as numbers, percentages, or ratios. Examples: height, weight, temperature, sales, etc.
  - **Qualitative data**: Data that can be described or categorized, such as words, images, sounds, or opinions. Examples: color, shape, taste, feedback, etc.
  - **Structured data**: Data that has a predefined format or schema, such as tables, spreadsheets, or databases. Examples: student records, inventory, transactions, etc.
  - **Unstructured data**: Data that has no fixed format or schema, such as text, audio, video, or social media posts. Examples: emails, tweets, blogs, videos, etc.
  - **Primary data**: Data that is collected directly from the source or original observation, such as surveys, interviews, or experiments. Examples: customer feedback, product testing, market research, etc.
  - **Secondary data**: Data that is collected from other sources or existing data, such as reports, publications, or websites. Examples: census data, industry data, academic data, etc.
- Data can have different characteristics that affect its quality, usability, and value, such as:
  - **Accuracy**: The degree to which the data reflects the true or correct value of the phenomenon or attribute it represents. Examples: spelling errors, measurement errors, rounding errors, etc.
  - **Completeness**: The degree to which the data covers all the relevant aspects or dimensions of the phenomenon or attribute it represents. Examples: missing values, incomplete records, insufficient samples, etc.
  - **Consistency**: The degree to which the data follows the same rules, standards, or formats across different sources, systems, or time periods. Examples: different units, different scales, different formats, etc.
  - **Relevance**: The degree to which the data is useful or applicable for the purpose or context of the analysis or decision making. Examples: outdated data, irrelevant data, redundant data, etc.
  - **Timeliness**: The degree to which the data is available or updated at the right time or frequency for the analysis or decision making. Examples: real-time data, historical data, periodic data, etc.
  - **Validity**: The degree to which the data conforms to the rules, constraints, or expectations of the domain or application it belongs to. Examples: data types, data ranges, data formats, data relationships, etc.



# Introduction to Big Data Platform

- A big data platform is an integrated computing solution that combines numerous software systems, tools, and hardware for big data management.
- Big data refers to the large volume, variety, and velocity of data that exceeds the capacity of traditional data processing systems.
- A big data platform works to wrangle this amount of information, storing it in a manner that is organized and understandable enough to extract useful insights.
- A big data platform typically consists of the following components:
  - Data sources: The origin of the data, such as sensors, web logs, social media, etc.
  - Data ingestion: The process of collecting, transferring, and loading the data into the platform, using tools such as Kafka, Flume, Sqoop, etc.
  - Data storage: The system that stores the data in a scalable and reliable way, using technologies such as Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS), Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, etc.
  - Data processing: The system that transforms, analyzes, and queries the data, using frameworks such as MapReduce, Spark, Hive, Pig, etc.
  - Data visualization: The system that presents the data in a graphical or interactive way, using tools such as Tableau, Power BI, D3.js, etc.
- A big data platform can provide various benefits for businesses, such as:
  - Improved decision making: By analyzing large and diverse data sets, businesses can gain deeper insights into their customers, markets, operations, and risks.
  - Enhanced customer experience: By leveraging big data analytics, businesses can personalize their products, services, and marketing campaigns to meet the needs and preferences of their customers.
  - Increased operational efficiency: By optimizing their processes, resources, and performance, businesses can reduce costs, waste, and errors, and increase quality, productivity, and innovation.
  - Competitive advantage: By harnessing the power of big data, businesses can create new value propositions, products, and services, and differentiate themselves from their competitors.

### Evolution of analytic scalability

- Analytic scalability refers to the ability to handle large and complex data sets for analysis purposes.
- In the past, analytic scalability was limited by the separation of data and analytic environments, the lack of parallel processing, and the high cost of data storage and computation.
- With the emergence of big data, new paradigms and technologies have enabled greater analytic scalability, such as:
  - The convergence of data and analytic environments, which allows analysts to access and manipulate data directly in the data warehouse or data lake, without the need to extract, transform, and load (ETL) data into a separate analytic environment .
  - Massively parallel processing (MPP) architectures, which distribute data and computation across multiple nodes or servers, allowing for faster and more efficient processing of large data sets  .
  - The cloud, which offers scalable, on-demand, and pay-as-you-go data storage and computation services, reducing the upfront and operational costs of data analytics .
  - Grid computing, which leverages the idle resources of a network of computers to perform distributed and parallel computation, increasing the availability and reliability of data analytics .
  - MapReduce, which is a programming model and framework for processing large data sets in parallel using a cluster of computers, simplifying the development and execution of data analytics   .
- The evolution of analytic scalability has enabled new opportunities and challenges for data analytics, such as:
  - The ability to perform more complex and sophisticated analysis on larger and more diverse data sets, such as text, images, audio, video, social media, web logs, sensor data, etc.   .
  - The need to adopt new skills and tools for data analytics, such as programming languages, frameworks, libraries, platforms, etc.   .
  - The need to ensure the quality, security, privacy, and ethics of data and analytics, especially when dealing with sensitive, personal, or confidential data   .

### Analytic Process and Tools

- The analytic process is a systematic approach to identify, collect, analyze, and communicate data and information to support decision making and problem solving.
- The analytic process consists of four main steps: define the problem, collect and prepare the data, analyze the data, and communicate the results.

### Analysis vs Reporting

- Reporting is the process of organizing data into informational summaries in order to monitor how different areas of a business are performing .
- Analysis is the process of exploring data and reports to extract meaningful insights, which can be used to better understand and improve business performance .
- Some of the key differences between reporting and analysis are :
  - Required skills: Analysis requires more advanced skills than reporting, such as knowledge of different analytical models, statistical techniques, and business problems. Reporting requires basic skills of data organization, presentation, and communication.
  - Order of operations: Reporting is usually done before analysis, as it provides the data and summaries that can be used for analysis. Analysis is usually done after reporting, as it uses the reports to generate insights and recommendations.
  - Time needed to implement: Reporting is usually faster and easier to implement than analysis, as it involves less complex and more standardized processes. Analysis is usually slower and harder to implement than reporting, as it involves more complex and customized processes.
  - Ease of automation: Reporting is usually more suitable for automation than analysis, as it involves repetitive and predictable tasks. Analysis is usually less suitable for automation than reporting, as it involves creative and contextual tasks.
  - Impact on strategy: Analysis usually has more impact on strategy than reporting, as it provides actionable insights and recommendations that can influence business decisions. Reporting usually has less impact on strategy than analysis, as it provides descriptive summaries and facts that can inform business decisions.
  - Data context: Analysis usually requires more data context than reporting, as it involves interpreting and explaining the data and its implications. Reporting usually requires less data context than reporting, as it involves displaying and summarizing the data and its features.
- Reporting and analysis are both important aspects of data analytics, as they complement each other and provide different benefits for a business .
  - Reporting helps to monitor the current state of a business, track its progress, and identify any issues or opportunities.
  - Analysis helps to understand the root causes of a business problem, explore different scenarios and solutions, and optimize the business performance and outcomes.

## Unit 2 - Data Analytics Lifecycle

The data analytics lifecycle is a framework for planning and executing data analysis projects. It consists of six phases:

- **Discovery**: This phase involves identifying the business problem, defining the objectives and scope of the analysis, and gathering the relevant data sources and stakeholders.
- **Data preparation**: This phase involves cleaning, transforming, and integrating the data into a suitable format for analysis. It also involves exploring the data to understand its characteristics, quality, and distribution.
- **Model planning**: This phase involves selecting the appropriate analytical techniques and tools for the data and the problem. It also involves creating and validating hypotheses, and designing experiments or tests to evaluate them.
- **Model building**: This phase involves applying the selected techniques and tools to the data, and generating insights, patterns, or predictions. It also involves assessing the accuracy, validity, and reliability of the results, and refining the models as needed.
- **Communicating results**: This phase involves presenting and visualizing the results to the stakeholders, and explaining the methods, assumptions, and limitations of the analysis. It also involves providing recommendations, actions, or solutions based on the results.
- **Operationalizing**: This phase involves implementing the recommendations, actions, or solutions into the business processes or systems, and monitoring and evaluating their impact and performance. It also involves documenting the analysis project and its outcomes, and identifying the lessons learned and the best practices.

### Data Analytics Lifecycle

- Data analytics lifecycle is a framework that describes the stages involved in a data analytics project, from identifying the business problem to deploying the solution.
- The data analytics lifecycle consists of six phases: Discovery, Data Preparation, Model Planning, Model Building, Communicate Results, and Operationalize.
- Each phase has its own objectives, tasks, and deliverables that guide the data analyst through the project.

#### Discovery

- The discovery phase is the first and most important phase of the data analytics lifecycle. It involves understanding the business problem, defining the project scope, identifying the stakeholders, and determining the success criteria.
- The discovery phase also involves exploring the available data sources, assessing the data quality, and performing some preliminary analysis to gain insights into the data.
- The deliverables of the discovery phase include a problem statement, a project plan, a data inventory, and a data quality report.

#### Data Preparation

- The data preparation phase is the second phase of the data analytics lifecycle. It involves transforming the raw data into a suitable format for analysis, such as cleaning, filtering, aggregating, merging, and enriching the data.
- The data preparation phase also involves performing some exploratory data analysis (EDA) to understand the data distribution, identify outliers, detect patterns, and generate hypotheses.
- The deliverables of the data preparation phase include a data dictionary, a data schema, a data quality report, and some descriptive statistics and visualizations.

#### Model Planning

- The model planning phase is the third phase of the data analytics lifecycle. It involves selecting the appropriate analytical techniques and tools to address the business problem, such as regression, classification, clustering, association, or sentiment analysis.
- The model planning phase also involves defining the metrics and methods to evaluate the performance and accuracy of the models, such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC curve, or confusion matrix.
- The deliverables of the model planning phase include a model selection matrix, a model evaluation plan, and a model validation plan.

#### Model Building

- The model building phase is the fourth phase of the data analytics lifecycle. It involves applying the selected analytical techniques and tools to the prepared data, such as building, training, testing, and tuning the models.
- The model building phase also involves validating the models against the predefined metrics and methods, such as comparing the actual and predicted outcomes, calculating the error rates, and assessing the model fit and robustness.
- The deliverables of the model building phase include a model report, a model summary, and a model validation report.

#### Communicate Results

- The communicate results phase is the fifth phase of the data analytics lifecycle. It involves presenting the findings and recommendations of the analysis to the stakeholders, such as explaining the insights, highlighting the key takeaways, and suggesting the next steps.
- The communicate results phase also involves creating and delivering the reports and dashboards that convey the results in a clear and compelling way, such as using charts, tables, graphs, and narratives.
- The deliverables of the communicate results phase include a presentation, a report, a dashboard, and a feedback form.

#### Operationalize

- The operationalize phase is the sixth and final phase of the data analytics lifecycle. It involves deploying the solution into the production environment, such as integrating the models with the existing systems, automating the data pipelines, and monitoring the performance and impact of the solution.
- The operationalize phase also involves maintaining and updating the solution as needed, such as fixing the bugs, improving the features, and adapting to the changing requirements and data.
- The deliverables of the operationalize phase include a deployment plan, a maintenance plan, and a performance report.



### Key Roles for Successful Analytic Projects

According to the Data Analytics Lifecycle, a successful analytic project requires the collaboration of different roles and skills. Some of the key roles are:

- **Project sponsor**: The project sponsor is responsible for the start of the project and provides all the requirements for the project and defines the core business problem. The project sponsor also approves the budget, timeline, and scope of the project, and ensures that the project aligns with the strategic goals of the organization. The project sponsor can be a senior executive, a business owner, or a key stakeholder.  
- **Project manager**: The project manager ensures that key milestones and objectives are met on time and at the expected quality. The project manager also coordinates the communication, collaboration, and integration of the project team and other stakeholders. The project manager can be a certified professional, a business analyst, or a subject matter expert.  
- **Business user**: A business user is someone who understands the domain area and usually benefits from the results. This person can consult and advise the project team on the context of the project, the value of the results, and how the outputs will be operationalized. A business user can be a business analyst, a line manager, or a deep subject matter expert.  
- **Business Intelligence Analyst**: A business intelligence analyst is someone who can collect, analyze, and report on data from various sources, such as databases, spreadsheets, or web services. This person can also create dashboards, visualizations, and reports to communicate the insights and recommendations to the business users and other stakeholders. A business intelligence analyst can be a data analyst, a data engineer, or a data scientist.  
- **Database Administrator (DBA)**: A database administrator is someone who can design, implement, and maintain the databases that store and manage the data for the project. This person can also ensure the security, performance, and availability of the databases, and troubleshoot any issues that arise. A database administrator can be a data engineer, a data architect, or a data scientist.  
- **Data engineer**: A data engineer is someone who can build, test, and deploy the data pipelines and platforms that enable the ingestion, processing, and transformation of data from various sources and formats. This person can also implement the data quality, governance, and validation rules, and optimize the data for analysis and modeling. A data engineer can be a data architect, a data scientist, or a software engineer.  
- **Data scientist**: A data scientist is someone who can apply advanced statistical and machine learning techniques to analyze and model the data, and generate insights and predictions that address the business problem. This person can also communicate the findings and recommendations to the business users and other stakeholders, and evaluate the impact and value of the project. A data scientist can be a statistician, a machine learning engineer, or a domain expert.

# Regression modeling for the notes of the Unit 3 - Data Analysis in the subject of INTRODUCTION TO DATA ANALYTICS AND VISUALIZATION

- Regression modeling is a process of estimating the relationships between independent variables (also called predictors, covariates, explanatory variables or features) and a dependent variable (also called outcome, response, target or label) in data    .
- Regression modeling can be used to understand how different factors are related to one another, and to predict future values for the target variable based on the values of the independent variables    .
- Regression modeling can be classified into different types based on the nature of the target variable and the relationship between the independent and dependent variables. Some common types of regression models are  :
  - Linear regression: The target variable is continuous and the relationship between the independent and dependent variables is linear. The function that describes the relationship is of the form `y = a + bx + e`, where `y` is the target variable, `x` is the independent variable, `a` and `b` are coefficients, and `e` is the error term. Example: The relationship between height and weight may be described by a linear regression model.
  - Logistic regression: The target variable is binary (0 or 1) and the relationship between the independent and dependent variables is nonlinear. The function that describes the relationship is of the form `p = 1 / (1 + e^(-z))`, where `p` is the probability of the target variable being 1, `z` is a linear combination of the independent variables, and `e` is the base of the natural logarithm. Example: The probability of a customer buying a product may be described by a logistic regression model.
  - Polynomial regression: The target variable is continuous and the relationship between the independent and dependent variables is nonlinear and can be modeled by a polynomial function. The function that describes the relationship is of the form `y = a + b1x + b2x^2 + ... + bnx^n + e`, where `y` is the target variable, `x` is the independent variable, `a`, `b1`, `b2`, ..., `bn` are coefficients, `n` is the degree of the polynomial, and `e` is the error term. Example: The relationship between the speed and the braking distance of a car may be described by a polynomial regression model.
  - Multiple regression: The target variable is continuous and the relationship between the independent and dependent variables is linear or nonlinear and involves more than one independent variable. The function that describes the relationship is of the form `y = a + b1x1 + b2x2 + ... + bnxn + e`, where `y` is the target variable, `x1`, `x2`, ..., `xn` are the independent variables, `a`, `b1`, `b2`, ..., `bn` are coefficients, and `e` is the error term. Example: The relationship between the sales of a product and the factors such as price, advertising, and competition may be described by a multiple regression model.
- Regression modeling involves the following steps    :
  - Data collection: The data should be relevant, reliable, and sufficient to answer the research question or hypothesis.
  - Data preparation: The data should be cleaned, transformed, and checked for missing values, outliers, and multicollinearity (high correlation among independent variables).
  - Model selection: The appropriate type of regression model should be chosen based on the nature of the target variable and the relationship between the independent and dependent variables. The model should also be validated using techniques such as cross-validation, residual analysis, and goodness-of-fit measures.
  - Model estimation: The coefficients of the regression model should be estimated using methods such as ordinary least squares (OLS), maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), or gradient descent. The significance and confidence intervals of the coefficients should also be calculated and interpreted.

# Multivariate Analysis

Multivariate analysis (MVA) is a method of analyzing data that involves more than two variables. It is used to examine the relationships and patterns among the variables and to test hypotheses about them. MVA can provide a more complete and accurate picture of the data than univariate or bivariate analysis, which only consider one or two variables at a time.

Some of the objectives of MVA are:

- To describe the main features and characteristics of the data
- To reduce the dimensionality of the data by identifying the most important variables or combinations of variables
- To explore the associations and dependencies among the variables
- To compare the differences among groups or samples based on the variables
- To predict the values of one or more variables based on the values of other variables

There are many types of MVA, each with its own assumptions, techniques, and applications. Some of the most common ones are:

- Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA): A generalization of analysis of variance (ANOVA) that tests whether the means of several dependent variables are equal across different groups or levels of one or more independent variables.
- Multivariate regression: A generalization of linear regression that models the relationship between one or more independent variables and a vector of dependent variables.
- Principal component analysis (PCA): A technique that transforms a set of correlated variables into a smaller set of uncorrelated variables called principal components, which capture the most variation in the data.
- Factor analysis (FA): A technique that identifies the underlying factors or latent variables that explain the common variance among a set of observed variables.
- Cluster analysis (CA): A technique that groups the observations or cases into clusters based on their similarity or proximity in the multivariate space.
- Discriminant analysis (DA): A technique that classifies the observations or cases into predefined groups based on their values on a set of predictor variables.
- Canonical correlation analysis (CCA): A technique that measures the linear relationship between two sets of variables and identifies the pairs of variables that have the highest correlation.

MVA requires some basic knowledge of statistics, such as descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing, correlation, and regression. It also requires some specialized software or tools, such as R, Python, SPSS, or SAS, that can perform the calculations and produce the outputs for MVA. MVA can be applied to various fields and domains, such as business, economics, psychology, education, biology, and social sciences, where data with multiple variables are collected and analyzed.



### Bayesian modeling

Bayesian modeling is a statistical approach that uses probability to represent all uncertainty within a model, both the uncertainty regarding the output and the uncertainty regarding the input (parameters) to the model. Bayesian modeling is based on Bayes' theorem, which describes how to update the probability of an event or a parameter after obtaining new data.

Some of the advantages of Bayesian modeling are:

- It can incorporate prior knowledge or beliefs into the analysis, which can improve the accuracy and efficiency of the inference.
- It can handle complex and nonlinear models that are difficult to fit using frequentist methods.
- It can provide a full posterior distribution of the parameters and the output, which can be used to quantify the uncertainty and make predictions.
- It can be used for decision-making and policy evaluation by providing a quantitative assessment of various risks and scenarios.

Some of the challenges of Bayesian modeling are:

- It requires specifying a prior distribution for the parameters, which can be subjective and influence the results.
- It often involves computationally intensive methods, such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), to sample from the posterior distribution.
- It can suffer from overfitting or underfitting if the model or the prior is not appropriate for the data.

Some of the applications of Bayesian modeling are:

- Bayesian linear regression, which models the relationship between a response variable and one or more explanatory variables using a linear function and a normal prior.
- Bayesian hierarchical modeling, which models the variation and dependence among groups of data using multiple levels of parameters and hyperparameters.
- Bayesian network, which models the conditional dependence and independence among a set of variables using a directed acyclic graph and a joint probability distribution.
- Bayesian inference, which estimates the posterior distribution of the parameters and the output given the data and the prior.
- Bayesian decision theory, which evaluates the optimal action or policy based on the expected utility or loss function and the posterior distribution.

### Support Vector and Kernel Methods

Support vector machines (SVMs) are a class of supervised learning algorithms that can perform classification and regression tasks. They are based on the idea of finding a hyperplane that separates the data into different classes, such that the margin between the classes is maximized  .

However, not all data sets are linearly separable, meaning that there may not exist a hyperplane that can perfectly separate the classes. In such cases, SVMs use a technique called the kernel trick, which allows them to map the data into a higher-dimensional feature space, where a linear separation may be possible  .

A kernel is a function that computes the similarity between two data points, without explicitly transforming them into the feature space. There are different types of kernels, such as linear, polynomial, radial basis function (RBF), and sigmoid, that can capture different patterns and relationships in the data  .

Some of the advantages of using SVMs and kernel methods are:

- They are theoretically sound and have strong generalization ability, based on the principles of statistical learning theory .
- They can handle nonlinear and complex data sets, by using appropriate kernels  .
- They are robust to noise and outliers, as they only depend on the support vectors, which are the data points closest to the margin  .

Some of the disadvantages of using SVMs and kernel methods are:

- They can be computationally expensive and slow, especially for large data sets, as they require solving a quadratic optimization problem and computing the kernel matrix  .
- They can be sensitive to the choice of kernel parameters, such as the degree of polynomial or the width of RBF, which may require tuning and cross-validation  .
- They can suffer from overfitting, if the kernel is too complex or the regularization parameter is too small  .

Some of the applications of SVMs and kernel methods are:

- Image recognition and classification, such as face detection, handwritten digit recognition, and object recognition  .
- Text analysis and natural language processing, such as sentiment analysis, document classification, and spam filtering  .
- Bioinformatics and medical diagnosis, such as protein structure prediction, gene expression analysis, and cancer detection  .

### Linear Systems Analysis & Nonlinear Dynamics

- Linear systems are systems whose behavior can be described by linear equations or functions, which means that the change in one variable is proportional to the change in another variable.
- Nonlinear systems are systems whose behavior cannot be described by linear equations or functions, which means that the change in one variable is not proportional to the change in another variable, or that there are interactions or feedbacks among the variables.
- Linear systems can be solved exactly, while nonlinear systems can only be solved approximately or by using transformations or numerical methods.
- Linear systems are often simpler and easier to analyze than nonlinear systems, but they may not capture the complexity and richness of the real-world phenomena.
- Nonlinear systems can exhibit chaotic, unpredictable, or counterintuitive behavior, such as bifurcations, limit cycles, strange attractors, or sensitivity to initial conditions.
- Linear systems analysis involves finding the equilibrium points, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, the stability and the phase portraits of the system.
- Nonlinear systems analysis involves finding the equilibrium points, the Jacobian matrix, the linearization and the local stability of the system, as well as the bifurcation diagrams, the Lyapunov exponents, the Poincaré maps, and the fractal dimensions of the system.

### Neural Networks for Data Analysis

Neural networks are a type of machine learning technique that can learn from data and perform various tasks, such as regression, classification, image recognition, speech recognition, etc. Neural networks are inspired by the structure and function of the human brain, and consist of interconnected units called neurons that process information and transmit signals. Neural networks can adapt to different types of data and problems, and can handle imprecise or noisy input data.

Some of the main concepts and components of neural networks are:

- **Input layer**: The first layer of a neural network that receives the data and passes it to the next layer.
- **Hidden layer**: One or more layers of a neural network that perform computations and transformations on the data and pass it to the next layer. Hidden layers can have different numbers and types of neurons, depending on the architecture and design of the neural network.
- **Output layer**: The last layer of a neural network that produces the final output or prediction, such as a numerical value, a class label, an image, etc.
- **Neuron**: A basic unit of a neural network that receives input from other neurons or data, applies a weighted sum and an activation function, and produces an output signal. Neurons can have different activation functions, such as sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, etc., that determine the output range and nonlinearity of the neuron.
- **Weight**: A numerical value that represents the strength or importance of the connection between two neurons. Weights are learned or updated during the training process of a neural network, based on the error or loss function and the optimization algorithm.
- **Bias**: A numerical value that represents the offset or intercept of a neuron. Bias can help the neuron to produce a nonzero output even when the input is zero, and can also improve the flexibility and expressiveness of the neural network.
- **Learning rate**: A hyperparameter that controls the speed or magnitude of the weight and bias updates during the training process of a neural network. A high learning rate can lead to faster convergence, but also to instability or divergence. A low learning rate can lead to slower convergence, but also to higher accuracy or generalization.
- **Epoch**: A complete cycle of passing all the data through the neural network and updating the weights and biases. An epoch is a unit of measurement for the training process of a neural network, and can be used to monitor the performance or progress of the neural network.
- **Batch**: A subset or chunk of the data that is passed through the neural network at a time. A batch can have different sizes, depending on the memory and computational resources available. A batch can also affect the speed and accuracy of the training process of a neural network.
- **Backpropagation**: A technique that calculates the error or loss of the neural network output with respect to the desired output or target, and propagates it backwards through the neural network, updating the weights and biases accordingly. Backpropagation is the main algorithm for training neural networks, and can use different optimization methods, such as gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent, Adam, etc.

Some of the main types and applications of neural networks are:

- **Feedforward neural network**: A simple and basic type of neural network that has no loops or cycles in its structure, and passes the data from the input layer to the output layer in a forward direction. Feedforward neural networks can be used for regression or classification tasks, such as predicting house prices, identifying handwritten digits, etc.

# Sampling Data in a Stream

- Stream sampling is the process of collecting a representative sample of the elements of a data stream  .
- The sample is usually much smaller than the entire stream, but can be designed to retain many important characteristics of the stream, and can be used to estimate many important aggregates on the stream  .
- Stream sampling can be useful for many applications, such as anomaly detection, clustering, classification, frequent itemset mining, and sketching .
- Stream sampling can be challenging due to the following factors :
  - The stream is unbounded and potentially infinite, so the sample size and memory usage must be bounded and independent of the stream size.
  - The stream is dynamic and evolving, so the sample must be adaptive and responsive to the changes in the stream distribution and characteristics.
  - The stream is fast and transient, so the sample must be updated efficiently and incrementally, without requiring multiple passes over the stream or storing the entire stream.
- Stream sampling can be classified into two main categories :
  - Uniform sampling: The sample is selected such that each element in the stream has an equal probability of being included in the sample. This can be achieved by using reservoir sampling, which maintains a fixed-size sample and replaces elements randomly as new elements arrive.
  - Weighted sampling: The sample is selected such that each element in the stream has a probability of being included in the sample proportional to some weight function. This can be achieved by using priority sampling, which maintains a fixed-size sample and replaces elements based on their weights and random keys as new elements arrive.
- Stream sampling can also be performed over different time windows  :
  - Landmark window: The sample is selected from the elements that arrived since the beginning of the stream or a fixed point in time. This can be achieved by using reservoir sampling or priority sampling with a fixed-size sample.
  - Sliding window: The sample is selected from the elements that arrived within a fixed time interval or a fixed number of elements before the current time. This can be achieved by using reservoir sampling or priority sampling with a variable-size sample that discards expired elements.
  - Damped window: The sample is selected from the elements that arrived within a variable time interval or a variable number of elements before the current time, where the importance of older elements decays exponentially. This can be achieved by using priority sampling with a weight function that assigns lower weights to older elements.

# Counting Distinct Elements in a Stream

- A stream is a sequence of data items that arrive in an online fashion, i.e., one by one or in batches, and cannot be stored entirely in memory.

- There are two main types of algorithms for counting distinct elements in a stream: exact and approximate.
- Exact algorithms guarantee to return the exact number of distinct elements, but they require memory proportional to the cardinality of the stream, which can be impractical for large or unbounded streams.
- Approximate algorithms trade off accuracy for memory efficiency, and provide probabilistic guarantees on the error of their estimates. They use various techniques such as hashing, sketching, sampling, etc. to compress the stream information into a small data structure, called a synopsis or a sketch, that can be queried or updated in constant time.

  - Count-Min sketch: uses a two-dimensional array of counters and multiple hash functions to estimate the frequency of any element in the stream, and then applies an inequality to bound the number of distinct elements.

### Counting oneness in a window

- Counting oneness in a window is a problem of estimating the number of 1's in the last k bits of a data stream, where k is a large number that cannot be stored in memory.
- One possible solution is to use the DGIM algorithm, which uses a compact representation of the stream using buckets of 1's with timestamps and sizes.
- The DGIM algorithm works as follows :
  - Each bit of the stream has a timestamp, the position in which it arrives. The first bit has timestamp 1, the second has timestamp 2, and so on.
  - The algorithm maintains a set of buckets, each consisting of the timestamp of its rightmost bit and the number of 1's in the bucket.
  - The buckets are stored in a circular array of size N, where N is the length of the window. The algorithm also stores the total number of bits ever seen in the stream modulo N, which allows to determine the position of any bit in the current window.
  - The buckets are subject to two invariants:
    - There are at most two buckets of the same size.
    - The buckets are ordered by their timestamps, from oldest to newest.
  - Whenever a new bit arrives, the algorithm performs the following steps:
    - If the bit is 0, it is ignored.
    - If the bit is 1, it is placed in a new bucket of size 1 with the current timestamp.
    - If there are three buckets of size 1, the oldest two are merged into a bucket of size 2 with the timestamp of the newer one.
    - If there are three buckets of size 2, the oldest two are merged into a bucket of size 4 with the timestamp of the newer one, and so on.
    - If the oldest bucket falls out of the window, it is discarded.
  - To estimate the number of 1's in the last k bits, the algorithm performs the following steps:
    - Find the oldest bucket that is entirely within the last k bits, call it B.
    - Sum the sizes of all the buckets newer than B, call it S.
    - Add half the size of B to S, call it E.
    - Return E as the estimate.
- The DGIM algorithm has the following properties :
  - It uses O(log2 N) bits to represent a window of N bits.
  - It allows to estimate the number of 1's in the window with an error of no more than 50%.
  - It can handle arbitrary streams of 0's and 1's, and can adapt to changes in the stream.
  - It can answer queries for any value of k less than N in constant time.

- The idea is to give more importance to the recent data and less importance to the older data, as they may be less relevant or outdated.
- A common way to implement a decaying window is to use an exponentially decaying function, such as `e^(-c*t)`, where `c` is a constant and `t` is the time difference between the current element and the previous element in the stream.
- A decaying window can be used to compute various statistics or metrics on the data stream, such as the sum, the average, the frequency, or the characteristic function of the elements.

### Apriori algorithm for frequent itemsets

The Apriori algorithm is an algorithm for finding frequent itemsets in a transactional database and generating association rules from them. Frequent itemsets are sets of items that appear together in a minimum number of transactions, where the minimum number is specified by a threshold called support. Association rules are implications of the form X -> Y, where X and Y are itemsets and X is a subset of Y, meaning that transactions that contain X are likely to contain Y as well. The strength of an association rule is measured by two metrics: confidence and lift. Confidence is the ratio of the number of transactions that contain both X and Y to the number of transactions that contain X. Lift is the ratio of the confidence to the expected confidence, where the expected confidence is the product of the support of X and the support of Y.

The Apriori algorithm works by applying the following steps:

1. Scan the database and count the occurrences of each item. Collect the items that satisfy the minimum support and form the frequent 1-itemsets, denoted by L1.
2. Generate the candidate 2-itemsets by taking the Cartesian product of L1 with itself, denoted by C2. This is also called the join step.
3. Scan the database and count the occurrences of each candidate 2-itemset. Collect the candidates that satisfy the minimum support and form the frequent 2-itemsets, denoted by L2. This is also called the prune step.
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 for k > 2, until no more frequent k-itemsets can be found. The final result is the union of all Lk.

The Apriori algorithm uses a property called the Apriori property, which states that any subset of a frequent itemset must also be frequent. This property allows the algorithm to reduce the number of candidates and avoid scanning the database for each candidate. For example, if {A, B, C} is a frequent 3-itemset, then {A, B}, {A, C}, {B, C}, {A}, {B}, and {C} must also be frequent. Therefore, if {A, B} is not frequent, then {A, B, C} cannot be frequent either, and can be pruned from the candidates.

The Apriori algorithm is one of the most widely used algorithms for frequent itemset mining and association rule learning. It has many applications in market basket analysis, recommender systems, web mining, and bioinformatics. However, it also has some limitations, such as:

- It can be inefficient when the database is large or the minimum support is low, as it generates a large number of candidates and scans the database multiple times.
- It can be ineffective when the itemsets are long or the items have different frequencies, as it may miss some interesting associations or generate too many trivial rules.
- It can only handle binary attributes, meaning that each item can only have two possible values: present or absent. It cannot handle numerical or categorical attributes with more than two values.

# Hierarchical Clustering

- Hierarchical clustering is a method of cluster analysis that seeks to build a hierarchy of clusters.
- Clustering is a technique used to create homogeneous groups of entities or objects based on some similarity measure.
- Hierarchical clustering can be performed in two ways: agglomerative or divisive.
  - Agglomerative clustering is a bottom-up approach that starts with each observation in its own cluster and then merges the closest pairs of clusters until all observations are in one cluster.
  - Divisive clustering is a top-down approach that starts with all observations in one cluster and then splits the cluster into smaller clusters based on some criterion until each observation is in its own cluster.
- Hierarchical clustering requires a distance measure to determine the similarity or dissimilarity between observations or clusters.
  - Some common distance measures are Euclidean distance, Manhattan distance, Minkowski distance, and cosine similarity.
  - Euclidean distance is the straight-line distance between two points in a Euclidean space.
  - Manhattan distance is the sum of the absolute differences between the coordinates of two points in a grid-like space.
  - Minkowski distance is a generalization of Euclidean and Manhattan distances that allows different weights for different dimensions.
  - Cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle between two vectors that represent the observations in a high-dimensional space.
- Hierarchical clustering also requires a linkage method to determine how to merge or split clusters based on the distance measure.
  - Some common linkage methods are single linkage, complete linkage, average linkage, and Ward's method.
  - Single linkage merges or splits clusters based on the minimum distance between any two observations in the clusters.
  - Complete linkage merges or splits clusters based on the maximum distance between any two observations in the clusters.
  - Average linkage merges or splits clusters based on the average distance between all pairs of observations in the clusters.
  - Ward's method merges or splits clusters based on the minimum increase in the total within-cluster variance.
- Hierarchical clustering can be visualized using a dendrogram, which is a tree-like diagram that shows the nested structure of the clusters and the distances between them .

# Frequent Pattern Based Clustering Methods

- Frequent pattern based clustering methods are a type of cluster analysis that use frequent patterns (such as sets of items or objects) that occur frequently in large data sets to group similar data points together.
- Frequent patterns can be extracted from different types of data, such as transactions, sequences, graphs, texts, images, etc.
- Frequent pattern based clustering methods can handle high-dimensional data and discover clusters in different subspaces of the data .
- Frequent pattern based clustering methods can be divided into two main categories: frequent itemset based clustering and frequent term based clustering.

## Frequent Itemset Based Clustering
- Frequent itemset based clustering methods use frequent itemsets (sets of items that appear together in a minimum number of transactions) to cluster data points that share common itemsets.
- Frequent itemset based clustering methods can be further classified into two subcategories: partitioning methods and hierarchical methods.
- Partitioning methods divide the data into a predefined number of clusters, such as k-means or k-medoids, based on the similarity or distance between data points and cluster centers, which are represented by frequent itemsets.
- Hierarchical methods build a tree-like structure of clusters, such as agglomerative or divisive, based on the inclusion or exclusion of frequent itemsets in the data points, and then cut the tree at a certain level to obtain the final clusters.

## Frequent Term Based Clustering
- Frequent term based clustering methods use frequent terms (sequences of characters or words that appear frequently in a large collection of text documents) to cluster text documents that share common terms.
- Frequent term based clustering methods can be further classified into two subcategories: document-pivot methods and term-pivot methods.
- Document-pivot methods assign each document to a cluster based on the similarity or distance between the document and the cluster representative, which is a document that contains the most frequent terms in the cluster.
- Term-pivot methods assign each term to a cluster based on the similarity or distance between the term and the cluster representative, which is a term that co-occurs with the most frequent terms in the cluster, and then group the documents that contain the terms in the same cluster.

## Advantages and Disadvantages of Frequent Pattern Based Clustering Methods
- Some advantages of frequent pattern based clustering methods are:
  - They can discover clusters in different subspaces of the data, which can capture the local structures and correlations of the data .
  - They can handle high-dimensional data and reduce the dimensionality by selecting the most relevant features (frequent patterns) for clustering .
  - They can deal with different types of data, such as transactions, sequences, graphs, texts, images, etc., by applying different frequent pattern mining algorithms.
- Some disadvantages of frequent pattern based clustering methods are:
  - They can be computationally expensive and time-consuming, especially when the data is large and the frequent patterns are numerous .
  - They can be sensitive to the choice of parameters, such as the minimum support threshold for frequent pattern mining and the number of clusters for partitioning methods .
  - They can produce overlapping or nested clusters, which may not reflect the true structure of the data or the user's preference .

- Human vision has a limited field of view and resolution, which affects how we perceive and process visual information.
- The field of view is the angular extent of the visible world that is seen at any given moment. It is about 200 degrees horizontally and 135 degrees vertically for humans.
- The resolution is the smallest detail that can be distinguished in an image. It depends on the distance from the object, the size of the object, the lighting conditions, and the visual acuity of the observer.
- The visual acuity is the ability to resolve fine details in an image. It is measured by the smallest angle of separation between two points that can be detected. It is highest at the center of the field of view, called the fovea, and decreases rapidly towards the periphery.
- The fovea is a small area of the retina, about 1.5 mm in diameter, that contains the highest density of cone cells, which are responsible for color vision and high-resolution vision. It covers about 2 degrees of the visual angle at the center of the field of view.
- The periphery is the rest of the retina, which contains mostly rod cells, which are sensitive to low light and motion, but have low resolution and no color vision. It covers about 198 degrees of the visual angle at the edges of the field of view.
- The implication of space limitation for data visualization is that we need to design visual displays that fit within the field of view and the resolution of the human vision, and that emphasize the most important information at the center of the display, where the visual acuity is highest. We also need to consider the trade-off between the amount of information and the level of detail that can be shown in a visual display.

### Time Limitations

- Human vision is limited by the speed of light and the processing power of the brain. The speed of light is about 300,000 km/s, which means that it takes about 0.000003 seconds for light to travel from one meter away to the eye. The brain, however, takes much longer to process the visual information and make sense of it. The brain has a limited capacity to store and retrieve information, and it also has to deal with other cognitive tasks such as attention, memory, and decision making. Therefore, human vision is subject to various time limitations that affect how we perceive and interact with data and visualizations.
- Some of the time limitations that affect human vision are:
  - **Persistence of vision**: This is the phenomenon that allows us to perceive smooth motion from a series of discrete images, such as in movies or animations. The human eye can retain an image for about 0.1 seconds after it disappears, which creates the illusion of continuity. However, this also means that we can miss some details or changes that occur in between the frames, especially if they are fast or subtle. Persistence of vision can be exploited to create visual effects such as motion blur, flicker, or stroboscopic motion.
  - **Change blindness**: This is the phenomenon that causes us to fail to notice changes in a visual scene, especially if they are gradual, unattended, or irrelevant. The human brain cannot process all the information in a complex scene at once, so it focuses on the most salient or important features and filters out the rest. However, this also means that we can overlook some changes that occur in the background, or when we are distracted or interrupted. Change blindness can affect our ability to detect errors, anomalies, or trends in data and visualizations.
  - **Attentional blink**: This is the phenomenon that causes us to miss a second target stimulus if it appears shortly after a first one, within a stream of distractors. The human brain has a limited capacity to process multiple stimuli at the same time, so it prioritizes the first one and suppresses the second one. However, this also means that we can miss some important information that is presented in rapid succession, such as in dashboards or alerts. Attentional blink can affect our ability to monitor and respond to data and visualizations.
  - **Visual short-term memory**: This is the part of the memory system that holds a limited amount of visual information for a short period of time, usually a few seconds. The human brain uses visual short-term memory to compare, integrate, and manipulate visual information, such as in mental imagery or reasoning. However, visual short-term memory is also prone to decay, interference, and distortion, which can affect the accuracy and reliability of our visual perception and cognition. Visual short-term memory can affect our ability to recall and communicate data and visualizations.

### Figure Caption in Visual Interface

- A figure caption is a text that accompanies a figure in a visual interface, such as a chart, a graph, a map, or an image.
- A figure caption serves several purposes, such as:
  - Providing a title or a summary of the figure
  - Explaining the data source, the variables, the units, and the scale of the figure
  - Highlighting the main findings, trends, patterns, or insights from the figure
  - Providing additional information or context that is not visible in the figure
  - Citing the original source or author of the figure, if applicable
- A figure caption should be concise, clear, accurate, and informative. It should not repeat the information that is already shown in the figure, but rather complement it and guide the reader's attention to the most important aspects of the figure.
- A figure caption should be placed below the figure, unless otherwise specified by the style guide or the instructor. It should be aligned with the left edge of the figure, and separated from the figure by a blank line. It should start with the word "Figure" followed by a number and a period, such as "Figure 1." or "Figure 2.".
- A figure caption should follow the formatting and citation rules of the style guide or the instructor, such as APA, MLA, Chicago, IEEE, etc. For example, in APA style, a figure caption should end with a parenthetical citation of the source, such as "(Smith, 2020)" or "(Adapted from Jones, 2019)".

### Visual Objects and Data Objects

- Visual objects are any identifiable, separate, and distinct parts of the visual world that have certain visual attributes, such as shape, color, size, position, orientation, texture, etc. 
- Data objects are any abstract entities that have certain data attributes, such as name, value, type, category, relation, etc. 
- Visual objects and data objects can be mapped to each other by using visual features to represent data values. For example, a bar chart maps data objects (such as sales, profit, etc.) to visual objects (such as bars) by using visual features (such as length, color, etc.) to represent data values (such as numbers, categories, etc.). 
- The mapping between visual objects and data objects can be used to organize, explore, and communicate data in a meaningful and effective way. The object metaphor is pervasive in the way we think about abstract data. 
- The mapping between visual objects and data objects can also be interactive, allowing users to manipulate, filter, drill down, or highlight data by using on-object actions on the visual objects. For example, a user can click on a bar in a bar chart to see more details, or drag a slider to change the range of data displayed.
- The mapping between visual objects and data objects should follow some principles and guidelines to ensure clarity, accuracy, and expressiveness. For example, the visual features should match the data values in terms of scale, order, and direction; the visual objects should avoid occlusion, distortion, or ambiguity; and the visual objects should support the tasks and goals of the users.



### Space Perception and Data in Space

- Space perception is the process through which humans and other organisms become aware of the relative positions of their own bodies and objects around them.
- Space perception provides cues, such as depth and distance, that are important for movement and orientation to the environment.
- Space perception is influenced by various factors, such as visual, auditory, haptic, vestibular, and proprioceptive information, as well as prior knowledge, expectations, and attention.
- Space perception can be divided into three main scales: near, middle, and far range.
  - Near range is the space that can be reached by the body, and is mostly perceived through haptic feedback and binocular vision.
  - Middle range is the space that can be walked to within a few minutes, and is mostly perceived through monocular vision and motion parallax.
  - Far range is the space that extends beyond the horizon, and is mostly perceived through aerial perspective and landmarks.
- Space perception can be affected by the absence of gravity, as experienced by astronauts in space.
  - Gravity provides a constant reference for the orientation of the body and the environment, and helps to stabilize the vestibular system.
  - In microgravity, the vestibular system can be disrupted, leading to symptoms such as nausea, dizziness, and spatial disorientation.
  - Astronauts also have to adapt to the changes in their visual perception of depth, distance, and size, as well as their proprioception of their limbs and posture.
  - NASA conducts research on how human senses adapt to space, and how to improve the design of spacecraft and habitats to enhance the comfort and performance of the crew.
- Data in space refers to the information collected by satellites, probes, rovers, and other instruments that explore the outer space.
  - Data in space can include images, videos, sounds, measurements, and observations of various celestial bodies, phenomena, and events.
  - Data in space can help to advance the scientific knowledge of the universe, the solar system, the Earth, and the life forms that inhabit it.
  - Data in space can also help to monitor the changes in the environment, the weather, the climate, and the natural disasters that affect the planet.
  - Data in space can also support the navigation, communication, and security of various human activities on Earth and in orbit.
  - NASA provides open access to its data in space, and encourages the public to explore, analyze, and visualize it using various tools and platforms.
