### Sources and nature of data

Data is a collection of facts, figures, and statistics related to an object. Data can be collected from various sources and can be of different types. Here are some common sources and types of data:

1. **Primary Data:** Primary data is the data collected by the researcher themselves for a specific purpose. This data is collected through methods such as surveys, interviews, and observations.

2. **Secondary Data:** Secondary data is the data that has already been collected by someone else for a different purpose. This data can be obtained from sources such as books, journals, and online databases.

3. **Quantitative Data:** Quantitative data is the data that can be measured and expressed in numerical form. This data is often collected through methods such as surveys and experiments.

4. **Qualitative Data:** Qualitative data is the data that cannot be measured in numerical form. This data is often collected through methods such as interviews and focus groups.

5. **Structured Data:** Structured data is the data that is organized in a specific format or schema. This data is often stored in databases and can be easily analyzed.

6. **Unstructured Data:** Unstructured data is the data that is not organized in a specific format or schema. This data can be in the form of text, images, or videos and can be difficult to analyze.

These are some of the common sources and types of data that can be used in data analytics and visualization. It is important to understand the nature of the data being used in order to effectively analyze and visualize it.



### Classification of Data

Classification of data is the process of organizing data into categories or groups based on their characteristics or attributes. This is done to make the data easier to understand and analyze. In the context of data analytics, classification is used to identify patterns and relationships within the data.

There are several methods for classifying data, including:

1. **Nominal Classification**: This method involves grouping data into categories that have no inherent order or structure. For example, data on the colors of cars could be classified into categories such as red, blue, and green.

2. **Ordinal Classification**: This method involves grouping data into categories that have a specific order or hierarchy. For example, data on the level of education could be classified into categories such as high school, college, and graduate school.

3. **Interval Classification**: This method involves grouping data into categories based on numerical ranges. For example, data on the ages of individuals could be classified into categories such as 0-10, 11-20, and 21-30.

4. **Ratio Classification**: This method involves grouping data into categories based on the ratio of one attribute to another. For example, data on the income of individuals could be classified into categories based on the ratio of their income to the median income.

Classification is an important step in the data analysis process, as it helps to simplify and organize the data, making it easier to identify patterns and relationships. It is also a useful tool for data visualization, as it allows data to be presented in a clear and concise manner.

# Evolution of Analytic Scalability

Analytic scalability refers to the ability of an analytics system to handle increasing amounts of data and users while maintaining performance. The evolution of analytic scalability has been driven by the need to process and analyze large and complex datasets in a timely and efficient manner. Here are some key points in the evolution of analytic scalability:

1. **Early data processing:** In the early days of data processing, data was stored on tapes and processed in batches. This approach was not scalable as the amount of data grew and the need for real-time analysis increased.

2. **Relational databases:** The introduction of relational databases allowed for more efficient storage and retrieval of data. However, as the volume of data grew, the performance of these systems began to suffer.

3. **Parallel processing:** The use of parallel processing techniques, such as dividing data into smaller chunks and processing them simultaneously, helped to improve the performance of analytics systems.

4. **Distributed computing:** The use of distributed computing, where data is stored and processed across multiple machines, further improved the scalability of analytics systems.

5. **Cloud computing:** The advent of cloud computing has made it easier and more cost-effective to scale analytics systems by providing on-demand access to computing resources.

6. **Big data technologies:** The development of big data technologies, such as Hadoop and Spark, has enabled the processing and analysis of large and complex datasets at scale.

In summary, the evolution of analytic scalability has been driven by the need to process and analyze large and complex datasets in a timely and efficient manner. Advances in technology, such as parallel processing, distributed computing, cloud computing, and big data technologies, have helped to improve the scalability of analytics systems.

# Analysis vs Reporting

Analysis and reporting are two important aspects of data analytics and visualization. Here are some key points to understand the difference between the two:

1. **Purpose:** The purpose of analysis is to extract insights and knowledge from data, while the purpose of reporting is to communicate information and data to others.

2. **Process:** Analysis involves exploring, manipulating, and modeling data to uncover patterns and relationships. Reporting, on the other hand, involves organizing and presenting data in a clear and concise manner.

3. **Outcome:** The outcome of analysis is often new knowledge or insights, while the outcome of reporting is typically a document or presentation that communicates information to others.

4. **Skills:** Analysis requires skills in data manipulation, statistical analysis, and critical thinking. Reporting requires skills in data visualization, communication, and storytelling.

5. **Tools:** Analysis often involves the use of specialized software and tools, such as statistical analysis software or data mining tools. Reporting often involves the use of visualization and presentation software, such as charting tools or presentation software.

In summary, analysis and reporting are two distinct but complementary aspects of data analytics and visualization. Analysis is focused on extracting insights from data, while reporting is focused on communicating information to others. Both are important for making data-driven decisions and for effectively communicating the results of data analysis.

## Unit 2 - Data Analytics Lifecycle

The Data Analytics Lifecycle is a framework for conducting data analysis projects. It consists of several phases that guide the process of transforming raw data into valuable insights. The phases of the Data Analytics Lifecycle are:

1. **Discovery**: In this phase, the analyst works with the business stakeholders to understand their objectives and requirements. This includes identifying the key business questions that need to be answered and determining the data sources that will be used to answer them.

2. **Data Preparation**: This phase involves collecting, cleaning, and transforming the data to make it ready for analysis. This may include dealing with missing or inconsistent data, combining data from multiple sources, and creating new variables.

3. **Model Planning**: In this phase, the analyst selects the appropriate analytical techniques and tools to use for the analysis. This may involve choosing between different statistical models, machine learning algorithms, or data visualization techniques.

4. **Model Building**: In this phase, the analyst builds and tests the analytical models using the prepared data. This may involve training machine learning algorithms, running statistical tests, or creating visualizations.

5. **Communicating Results**: In this phase, the analyst presents the results of the analysis to the business stakeholders. This may involve creating reports, dashboards, or presentations that clearly communicate the key insights and findings.

6. **Operationalizing**: In this final phase, the analytical models and processes are integrated into the business operations. This may involve automating the data collection and analysis process, or embedding the analytical models into business applications.

Each phase of the Data Analytics Lifecycle builds upon the previous one, and the entire process is iterative, with the analyst continually refining the analysis as new data and insights are gained. By following this framework, analysts can ensure that their data analysis projects are well-structured and focused on delivering valuable insights to the business.

# Regression Modeling

Regression modeling is a statistical technique used to analyze the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. It is commonly used in data analysis to make predictions and to understand the relationship between variables.

There are several types of regression models, including:

1. **Linear Regression**: This model assumes a linear relationship between the dependent and independent variables. It is used to predict the value of the dependent variable based on the values of the independent variables.

2. **Logistic Regression**: This model is used when the dependent variable is binary (e.g. yes/no, success/failure). It predicts the probability of the dependent variable being 1 (e.g. success) given the values of the independent variables.

3. **Polynomial Regression**: This model is used when the relationship between the dependent and independent variables is non-linear. It fits a polynomial equation to the data.

4. **Multiple Regression**: This model is used when there are multiple independent variables. It is an extension of linear regression and can be used to predict the value of the dependent variable based on the values of multiple independent variables.

Regression modeling is an important tool in data analysis and can provide valuable insights into the relationships between variables. It is commonly used in fields such as economics, finance, and social sciences.



# Unit 3 - Data Analysis: Multivariate Analysis

Multivariate analysis is a statistical technique used to analyze data that involves more than one variable. It is used to understand the relationships between multiple variables and to identify patterns in the data. Some common methods of multivariate analysis include:

1. **Multiple Regression:** This method is used to analyze the relationship between a dependent variable and multiple independent variables. It is used to predict the value of the dependent variable based on the values of the independent variables.

2. **Factor Analysis:** This method is used to identify underlying factors or dimensions that explain the correlations among a set of variables. It is used to reduce the number of variables in a dataset by grouping them into factors.

3. **Cluster Analysis:** This method is used to group observations into clusters based on their similarity. It is used to identify patterns in the data and to classify observations into groups.

4. **Discriminant Analysis:** This method is used to classify observations into groups based on their characteristics. It is used to predict group membership based on the values of the independent variables.

5. **Canonical Correlation Analysis:** This method is used to analyze the relationship between two sets of variables. It is used to identify the linear combinations of variables that are most highly correlated.

These are some of the common methods used in multivariate analysis. Each method has its own assumptions and requirements, and the appropriate method should be chosen based on the research question and the characteristics of the data.



# Bayesian Modeling

# Analysis of Time Series

Time series analysis is a statistical technique that deals with time series data, or trend analysis. Time series data means that data is in a series of particular time periods or intervals. The data is considered in three types: Time series data, cross-sectional data, and pooled data. Here, we will be interested in time series data and its analysis.

## Components of Time Series

There are four components of time series data. These are:

1. **Trend**: The trend shows a general tendency of the data to increase or decrease during a long period of time. A trend is observed when there is an increase or decrease in the data over a long period of time.

2. **Seasonality**: Seasonality is a characteristic of a time series in which the data experiences regular and predictable changes that recur every calendar year. Any predictable change or pattern in a time series that recurs or repeats over a one-year period can be said to be seasonal.

3. **Cyclic**: A cyclic pattern exists when data exhibit rises and falls that are not of a fixed period. The duration of these fluctuations is usually of at least 2 years.

4. **Irregular**: The irregular component is the residual part of the time series that cannot be explained by the trend, seasonal, or cyclic components. It is the random variation in the data.

## Methods of Time Series Analysis

There are several methods to analyze time series data. Some of the commonly used methods are:

1. **Graphical Method**: This method involves plotting the time series data on a graph and analyzing the pattern of the plotted points.

2. **Regression Analysis**: This method involves fitting a regression line to the time series data and using the line to make predictions.

3. **Exponential Smoothing**: This method involves using a weighted average of past observations to forecast future values.

4. **Box-Jenkins Method**: This method involves using autoregressive moving average (ARMA) or autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models to forecast future values.

5. **Decomposition Method**: This method involves decomposing the time series data into its trend, seasonal, cyclic, and irregular components and analyzing each component separately.

These are some of the key points to remember while analyzing time series data. It is important to choose the appropriate method based on the characteristics of the data and the objectives of the analysis.

### Stochastic Search Methods

Stochastic search methods are a type of optimization algorithm that use randomness to find the global optimum of a function. These methods are often used when the search space is large and the function being optimized is complex, making it difficult to find the global optimum using deterministic methods.

Some common stochastic search methods include:

1. **Simulated Annealing:** This method is inspired by the annealing process in metallurgy, where a material is heated and then slowly cooled to reduce defects and improve its structure. In simulated annealing, the algorithm starts with a high "temperature" and explores the search space randomly. As the temperature decreases, the algorithm becomes more likely to accept solutions that are worse than the current solution, allowing it to escape local optima and find the global optimum.

2. **Genetic Algorithms:** These algorithms are inspired by the process of natural selection and use concepts such as reproduction, mutation, and selection to evolve a population of solutions towards the global optimum. The algorithm starts with a population of randomly generated solutions and iteratively applies genetic operators to create new solutions. The best solutions are selected to survive and reproduce, while the worst solutions are discarded.

3. **Particle Swarm Optimization:** This method is inspired by the social behavior of bird flocks and fish schools. The algorithm represents each potential solution as a particle in a swarm, and the particles move through the search space based on their own best position and the best position of their neighbors. Over time, the swarm converges towards the global optimum.

These are just a few examples of stochastic search methods. These methods can be effective for solving complex optimization problems, but they may require more computational resources and time compared to deterministic methods. Additionally, the randomness inherent in these methods means that the results may not be consistent across multiple runs of the algorithm.

# Stream Data Model and Architecture

1. An **aggregator** that gathers event streams and batch files from a variety of data sources.
2. A **broker** that makes data available for consumption.
3. An **analytics engine** that analyzes the data, correlates values, and performs real-time processing, data manipulation, and analytics .

In contrast to traditional data solutions that focus on writing and reading data in batches, a streaming data architecture consumes data immediately as it is generated, persists it to storage, and may perform real-time processing, data manipulation, and analytics .

Data arrives in a stream or streams, and if it is not processed immediately or stored, then it is lost forever. The data arrives so rapidly that it is not feasible to store it all in active storage (i.e., in a conventional database), and then interact with it at the time of our choosing .

# Apriori Algorithm

The Apriori algorithm is an algorithm for frequent itemset mining and association rule learning over transactional databases. It was proposed by Agrawal and Srikant in 1994. The algorithm is designed to operate on databases containing transactions, such as purchases by customers of a store.

The key idea of the Apriori algorithm is to make use of the Apriori property, which states that all non-empty subsets of a frequent itemset must also be frequent. This property is used to prune the search space of candidate itemsets, reducing the number of itemsets that need to be checked for frequency.

The Apriori algorithm operates in two steps:

1. **Generate**: The algorithm generates candidate itemsets of length k from the frequent itemsets of length k-1. This is done by joining the frequent itemsets of length k-1 with themselves and pruning the resulting itemsets using the Apriori property.

2. **Test**: The algorithm tests the candidate itemsets for frequency by counting their occurrences in the database. Itemsets that are found to be frequent are kept and used to generate candidate itemsets of length k+1 in the next iteration.

The algorithm terminates when no more frequent itemsets can be generated.

The Apriori algorithm has been widely used in market basket analysis, where it is used to identify sets of items that are frequently purchased together. It can also be used in other domains, such as bioinformatics and text mining, where it can be used to identify sets of items that frequently co-occur.

The Apriori algorithm has several limitations. It can be slow when applied to large databases, as it needs to generate and test a large number of candidate itemsets. It also assumes that all items are equally likely to be frequent, which may not be the case in practice. Despite these limitations, the Apriori algorithm remains a popular and widely used algorithm for frequent itemset mining and association rule learning.

# Hierarchical Clustering

Hierarchical clustering is a method of cluster analysis which seeks to build a hierarchy of clusters. It is commonly used in the field of data analytics and visualization, particularly in the context of frequent itemsets and clustering.

There are two main approaches to hierarchical clustering: agglomerative and divisive.

1. **Agglomerative hierarchical clustering** involves starting with each data point as a separate cluster and then merging the clusters together iteratively until a single cluster remains. This is done by calculating the distance between clusters and merging the two closest clusters at each step.

2. **Divisive hierarchical clustering** involves starting with all data points in a single cluster and then iteratively splitting the cluster into smaller clusters until each data point is in its own cluster. This is done by calculating the distance between data points and splitting the cluster along the dimension with the greatest variance.

Both approaches can use a variety of distance metrics to determine the distance between clusters or data points, including Euclidean distance, Manhattan distance, and cosine similarity.

Hierarchical clustering can be visualized using a dendrogram, which is a tree-like diagram that shows the hierarchy of clusters and the order in which they were merged or split.

Hierarchical clustering is commonly used in exploratory data analysis to identify patterns and relationships in the data. It can also be used in conjunction with other clustering methods, such as k-means clustering, to improve the accuracy of the clustering results.



# K-means

K-means is a clustering algorithm that is used to partition a given set of observations into a specified number of clusters. It is a type of unsupervised learning algorithm that is used to classify data into different groups based on their similarity.

The algorithm works by iteratively assigning each data point to the cluster whose centroid is closest to it, and then updating the centroid of each cluster based on the mean of the data points assigned to it.

The steps involved in the K-means algorithm are as follows:

1. **Initialization**: The initial centroids are chosen randomly from the data points.
2. **Assignment**: Each data point is assigned to the cluster whose centroid is closest to it.
3. **Update**: The centroid of each cluster is updated based on the mean of the data points assigned to it.
4. **Repeat**: Steps 2 and 3 are repeated until the centroids no longer change or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

K-means is a simple and widely used clustering algorithm. However, it has some limitations. For example, it assumes that clusters are spherical and equally sized, which may not always be the case in real-world data. Additionally, the algorithm is sensitive to the initial choice of centroids and may converge to a suboptimal solution.

In summary, K-means is a clustering algorithm that partitions data into a specified number of clusters by iteratively assigning data points to the closest cluster centroid and updating the centroids based on the mean of the data points assigned to them. It is a simple and widely used algorithm, but has some limitations and assumptions that should be considered when using it.

- The human eye can only perceive changes in visual stimuli that occur within a certain time frame.
- For example, the human eye can perceive changes in light intensity that occur within 1/100th of a second.
- This means that if a visual stimulus changes faster than this time frame, the human eye may not be able to perceive the change.
- This time limitation is known as the critical flicker fusion frequency (CFF).
- The CFF varies depending on the individual and the conditions, such as the level of light and the location of the stimulus on the retina.
- The CFF is also affected by factors such as age, fatigue, and the use of certain medications.

# Figure Caption in Visual Interface

Figure captions are an important aspect of visual interfaces, particularly in the context of data analytics and visualization. Here are some key points to consider when using figure captions in visual interfaces:

1. Figure captions provide context and explanation for the visual content they accompany. They should be clear, concise, and accurately describe the content of the figure.

2. Figure captions should be placed in close proximity to the figure they describe, typically directly below the figure.

3. The font size and style of the figure caption should be consistent with the rest of the text in the visual interface.

4. Figure captions should be written in a way that is accessible to the intended audience. Technical terms and jargon should be avoided or clearly defined.

5. Figure captions can also include additional information such as the source of the data, the date the data was collected, and any relevant units of measurement.

6. In some cases, it may be appropriate to include a brief summary of the key findings or conclusions that can be drawn from the figure.

7. Figure captions can also be used to provide additional context or background information that may not be immediately apparent from the visual content alone.

Overall, figure captions play a crucial role in helping users understand and interpret the visual content in a visual interface. They should be carefully crafted and thoughtfully integrated into the overall design of the interface.



# Visual Objects and Data Objects

In the context of data analytics and visualization, visual objects and data objects are two important concepts.

- **Visual objects** refer to the graphical elements that are used to represent data in a visualization. These can include points, lines, bars, pie slices, and other shapes that are used to encode data values.

- **Data objects**, on the other hand, refer to the underlying data that is being represented in the visualization. This can include numerical data, categorical data, and other types of data that are used to create the visual objects.

In order to create effective visualizations, it is important to understand the relationship between visual objects and data objects. The visual objects should be chosen and designed in such a way that they accurately and effectively represent the data objects, allowing the viewer to easily understand and interpret the information being presented.
