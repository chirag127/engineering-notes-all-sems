# KCS

KCS stands for Knowledge-Centered Service, a best practice methodology for service organizations to work more effectively with knowledge. KCS aims to improve service delivery, productivity, cost reduction, and customer satisfaction by creating and maintaining documentation as part of the support process .

Some of the main features of KCS are:

- KCS is based on the principle of "capture, structure, reuse, and improve" knowledge as a by-product of solving problems .
- KCS involves the whole organization, not just the service desk, in creating and using knowledge. KCS encourages collaboration, feedback, and continuous learning among all stakeholders .
- KCS uses a demand-driven approach to knowledge creation and maintenance, meaning that knowledge is created based on the actual needs and requests of the customers, not on assumptions or predictions .
- KCS uses a structured format for knowledge articles, with a clear distinction between the problem, the cause, and the resolution. KCS also uses metadata, keywords, and links to make knowledge articles easy to find and reuse .
- KCS measures the value and quality of knowledge based on its usage and outcomes, not on its quantity or completeness. KCS uses metrics such as reuse rate, resolution time, customer satisfaction, and self-service success to evaluate the effectiveness of knowledge .

KCS is not a one-time project, but a long-term commitment to a culture of knowledge sharing and improvement. KCS requires a change in mindset, behavior, and processes for the service organization and its customers. KCS also requires a suitable technology platform that supports the KCS practices and principles .

### Introduction - History and Evolution

- Business intelligence (BI) is the process of collecting, analyzing, and presenting data to support decision making in various domains of business.
- The term BI was coined in 1989 by Howard Dresner, a former Gartner analyst, who defined it as "concepts and methods to improve business decision making by using fact-based support systems."
- However, the origins of BI can be traced back to the 19th century, when Richard Millar Devens described how a banker used information about market trends to gain a competitive advantage in his book "Cyclopaedia of Commercial and Business Anecdotes" in 1865.
- The evolution of BI can be divided into four stages, according to the development of technology and the needs of users:
  - **Pre-digital era (before 1950s)**: BI was mainly based on manual methods of data collection and analysis, such as census, surveys, and accounting records. The data was often limited, inaccurate, and outdated.
  - **Early digital era (1950s-1970s)**: BI was influenced by the emergence of computers, databases, and statistical software. The first computerized decision support systems (DSS) were developed to help managers with complex and unstructured problems. The data was stored in centralized mainframes and accessed through terminals.
  - **Modern digital era (1980s-1990s)**: BI was driven by the proliferation of personal computers, spreadsheets, graphical user interfaces, and online analytical processing (OLAP) tools. The data was extracted from various sources, transformed, and loaded into data warehouses and data marts. The users could perform multidimensional analysis and generate reports and dashboards.
  - **Post-modern digital era (2000s-present)**: BI was enhanced by the advent of the internet, cloud computing, big data, and artificial intelligence. The data was collected from multiple channels, such as web, social media, sensors, and mobile devices. The users could access and analyze data anytime, anywhere, and on any device. The BI tools became more interactive, visual, and self-service oriented.

- The future of BI is expected to be more automated, intelligent, and collaborative, leveraging technologies such as machine learning, natural language processing, and augmented reality. The data will be more diverse, dynamic, and real-time. The users will be able to ask questions, get insights, and share findings with ease and efficiency.

### Data Mining - Introduction to Data Mining

Data mining is the process of extracting useful information or knowledge from a large set of data. Data mining can help businesses to discover patterns, trends, and insights from their data, and use them to improve decision making, marketing, customer service, fraud detection, and other applications.

Some of the benefits of data mining are:

- It can help to identify new opportunities and markets for products or services.
- It can help to segment customers based on their preferences, behavior, or demographics, and offer personalized recommendations or promotions.
- It can help to detect anomalies, outliers, or frauds in the data, and take preventive or corrective actions.
- It can help to optimize business processes, operations, or resources, and reduce costs or risks.
- It can help to generate new knowledge or hypotheses from the data, and test them with further analysis or experiments.

Some of the techniques or methods of data mining are:

- Classification: It is the process of assigning a label or category to a data instance based on its features or attributes. For example, classifying an email as spam or not spam based on its content or sender.
- Clustering: It is the process of grouping data instances that are similar or related to each other based on some measure of similarity or distance. For example, clustering customers based on their purchase history or browsing behavior.
- Association: It is the process of finding rules or patterns that describe the relationships or dependencies among data items or variables. For example, finding the association between products that are frequently bought together or co-occur in a transaction.
- Regression: It is the process of modeling the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables, and predicting the value of the dependent variable based on the values of the independent variables. For example, predicting the sales of a product based on its price, features, or advertising.
- Outlier detection: It is the process of identifying data instances that deviate significantly from the normal or expected behavior or distribution of the data. For example, detecting fraudulent transactions or credit card applications based on their amount, frequency, or location.
- Text mining: It is the process of extracting information or knowledge from unstructured or semi-structured text data, such as documents, emails, social media posts, or reviews. For example, extracting topics, sentiments, opinions, or entities from text data.
- Web mining: It is the process of extracting information or knowledge from web data, such as web pages, links, logs, or queries. For example, extracting keywords, rankings, or recommendations from web data.

Data mining is a multidisciplinary field that involves computer science, statistics, mathematics, and domain knowledge. Data mining can be performed using various tools, languages, or frameworks, such as Python, R, SQL, SAS, SPSS, Weka, RapidMiner, or KNIME. Data mining is also a part of the broader field of data science, which includes data collection, data cleaning, data analysis, data visualization, and data communication.

# Architecture of Data Mining

Data mining is the process of discovering meaningful new correlations, patterns, and trends by shifting through large amounts of data stored in repositories, using pattern recognition technologies as well as statistical and mathematical techniques.

The data mining process involves several components, and these components constitute a data mining system architecture. The significant components of data mining systems are:

- A data source, which can be a database, a data warehouse, a data stream, or any other data repository.
- A data mining engine, which is a set of modules for tasks such as data preprocessing, data mining algorithms, data analysis, and data visualization.
- A data warehouse server, which is responsible for fetching the relevant data from the data source and delivering it to the data mining engine or the user interface.
- A pattern evaluation module, which is used to evaluate the patterns or models generated by the data mining engine according to some interestingness measures or user feedback.
- A graphical user interface, which allows the user to interact with the data mining system, specify the data mining queries and parameters, and view the results.
- A knowledge base, which is a domain-specific repository of information and data that can be used to guide the data mining process or evaluate the results.

There are different types of data mining architecture, depending on the degree of coupling between the data mining system and the data source. These are:

- No coupling, where the data mining system retrieves data from a particular data source and does not use any database or data warehouse functionality. The data mining system is independent of the data source and can work with any kind of data.
- Loose coupling, where the data mining system retrieves data from the database or data warehouse and stores the data in an intermediate file or memory. The data mining system does not use any database or data warehouse functionality, such as indexing or sorting, and can work with different data sources.
- Semi-tight coupling, where the data mining system uses some database or data warehouse functionality, such as indexing or sorting, to improve the performance of data retrieval and preprocessing. The data mining system is integrated with the database or data warehouse, but does not modify the data or the schema.
- Tight coupling, where the data mining system is embedded in the database or data warehouse and uses the database or data warehouse functionality to perform data mining tasks. The data mining system can modify the data or the schema, and can optimize the data mining queries using the database or data warehouse query optimizer.

### Functionalities & Classifications of Data Mining

Data mining is the process of discovering useful patterns and insights from large and complex data sets. Data mining can help organizations to improve decision making, enhance customer experience, optimize business processes, and generate new knowledge.

Data mining functionalities are used to represent the type of patterns that have to be discovered in data mining tasks. In general, data mining tasks can be classified into two types including descriptive and predictive.

- Descriptive mining tasks define the common features of the data in the database and summarize the characteristics of the data. Examples of descriptive mining tasks are:

  - Association rule mining: finding frequent patterns, associations, or correlations among a set of items or attributes in a transaction database.
  - Clustering: grouping a set of data objects into clusters such that objects within a cluster are similar to each other and dissimilar to objects in other clusters.
  - Summarization: providing a concise and general description of the data, such as statistics, charts, graphs, or reports.
  - Sequence analysis: finding sequential patterns or trends in temporal or spatial data, such as customer purchase sequences, web clickstreams, or DNA sequences.

- Predictive mining tasks act inference on the current data in order to make predictions or estimations for future or unknown data. Examples of predictive mining tasks are:

  - Classification: determining a model that describes and differentiates data classes with the intention of using it to predict a class of unknown or anonymous objects. For example, classifying customers into different segments based on their attributes and behaviors.
  - Regression: finding a function that models the relationship between one or more independent variables and a continuous dependent variable. For example, predicting the sales of a product based on its price, features, and demand.
  - Anomaly detection: identifying data objects that deviate significantly from the normal behavior or expectation. For example, detecting fraud, intrusion, or outliers in a data set.

Data mining can also perform other functionalities, such as:

- Data cleaning: removing noise, outliers, or inconsistent data from the data set.
- Data integration: combining data from multiple sources into a unified view.
- Data transformation: transforming or converting data into a suitable format for data mining.
- Data visualization: presenting the data or the results of data mining in a graphical or interactive way.
- Data evaluation: assessing the quality, validity, or usefulness of the data mining results.

### Representation of Input Data

- Input data is the raw data that is collected from various sources and stored in a database or a data warehouse for data mining purposes.
- Input data can be of different types, such as numerical, categorical, textual, spatial, temporal, multimedia, etc.
- Input data can also have different structures, such as flat files, relational tables, graphs, trees, sequences, etc.
- Input data needs to be represented in a suitable format that can be processed by data mining algorithms and techniques.
- Input data representation can affect the quality, efficiency, and effectiveness of data mining results.
- Input data representation can be done by using various techniques, such as:

  - Data preprocessing: This involves cleaning, transforming, integrating, and reducing the input data to remove noise, outliers, missing values, inconsistencies, and redundancies. Data preprocessing can improve the accuracy and speed of data mining algorithms and techniques.
  - Data encoding: This involves converting the input data into a numerical or symbolic form that can be manipulated by data mining algorithms and techniques. Data encoding can reduce the dimensionality and complexity of the input data and enhance its interpretability. Data encoding can be done by using techniques such as one-hot encoding, label encoding, ordinal encoding, etc.
  - Data discretization: This involves dividing the input data into a finite number of intervals or categories that can capture the essential characteristics of the data. Data discretization can reduce the noise and variability of the input data and simplify the data mining process. Data discretization can be done by using techniques such as binning, clustering, entropy-based, etc.
  - Data feature extraction: This involves selecting or creating a subset of attributes or variables that can represent the input data effectively and efficiently. Data feature extraction can reduce the dimensionality and redundancy of the input data and improve the performance and scalability of data mining algorithms and techniques. Data feature extraction can be done by using techniques such as principal component analysis, linear discriminant analysis, singular value decomposition, etc.
  - Data feature selection: This involves choosing a subset of attributes or variables that are relevant and informative for the data mining task. Data feature selection can eliminate the irrelevant and redundant attributes or variables that can affect the accuracy and reliability of data mining results. Data feature selection can be done by using techniques such as filter methods, wrapper methods, embedded methods, etc.
  - Data visualization: This involves presenting the input data in a graphical or pictorial form that can facilitate the understanding and exploration of the data. Data visualization can reveal the patterns, trends, outliers, and anomalies of the input data and support the data mining process. Data visualization can be done by using techniques such as histograms, scatter plots, box plots, heat maps, etc.

- Input data representation is an important and challenging task in data mining, as it can affect the outcome and quality of the data mining process. Input data representation should be done carefully and appropriately, considering the characteristics, structure, and type of the input data, as well as the objectives and requirements of the data mining task.



### Analysis Methodologies for Data Mining and Warehousing

Some of the analysis methodologies for data mining and warehousing are:

- **Extract, Transform, and Load (ETL)**: This is the most common methodology for data warehousing, which involves extracting data from various sources, transforming them into a consistent and compatible format, and loading them into a data warehouse. ETL enables data integration, quality, and consistency, and prepares data for further analysis.

- **Online Analytical Processing (OLAP)**: This is a methodology for multidimensional analysis of data in a data warehouse, which allows users to slice and dice data along different dimensions, such as time, geography, product, customer, etc. OLAP tools can perform complex analytical calculations, such as aggregation, drill-down, roll-up, pivot, etc., and support interactive and ad-hoc queries. OLAP enables data exploration, discovery, and visualization, and supports various business reporting functions, such as financial analysis, budgeting, forecasting, etc..

- **Data Mining Techniques**: These are the methodologies for discovering patterns, trends, and relationships in data, using various statistical and machine learning techniques, such as classification, clustering, association, regression, anomaly detection, etc. Data mining techniques can be applied to different types of data, such as numerical, categorical, textual, spatial, temporal, etc., and can handle noisy, incomplete, or uncertain data. ### Data Warehousing - Introduction to Data Warehousing

- Data warehousing involves data cleaning, data integration, and data consolidation .
- Data warehousing enables the organization to access and analyze historical and current data, identify trends and patterns, and support strategic business decisions  .

### Schema – Star and Snowflake Schemas

- A schema is a logical representation of the structure and organization of data in a data warehouse or data mart.
- A schema consists of fact tables and dimension tables that store the data and metadata of the data warehouse or data mart.
- A fact table contains the measures or metrics of the business processes, such as sales, revenue, profit, etc.
- A dimension table contains the attributes or characteristics of the business entities, such as product, customer, location, time, etc.
- A schema can be designed using different approaches, such as star schema and snowflake schema, depending on the requirements and complexity of the data warehouse or data mart.

#### Star Schema

- A star schema is the simplest and most common type of schema used in data warehousing and business intelligence projects.
- A star schema consists of a single fact table and multiple dimension tables that are directly connected to the fact table.
- A star schema is called so because its structure resembles a star, with the fact table at the center and the dimension tables as the points of the star.
- A star schema has the following advantages:
  - It is easy to understand and implement, as it has a simple and denormalized structure.
  - It is efficient for querying and analysis, as it requires fewer joins and scans to retrieve the data.
  - It is flexible and scalable, as new dimensions and measures can be easily added to the schema without affecting the existing structure.
- A star schema has the following disadvantages:
  - It may result in data redundancy and inconsistency, as the same data may be repeated in multiple dimension tables.
  - It may not capture the complex relationships and hierarchies among the dimensions, as each dimension table is independent and isolated from other dimension tables.

#### Snowflake Schema

- A snowflake schema is an extension and variation of the star schema, where some or all of the dimension tables are further normalized into sub-dimension tables.
- A snowflake schema is called so because its structure resembles a snowflake, with the fact table at the center and the dimension tables and sub-dimension tables as the branches of the snowflake.
- A snowflake schema has the following advantages:
  - It reduces data redundancy and inconsistency, as the same data is stored only once in the sub-dimension tables.
  - It captures the complex relationships and hierarchies among the dimensions, as each sub-dimension table is linked to its parent dimension table.
- A snowflake schema has the following disadvantages:
  - It is difficult to understand and implement, as it has a complex and normalized structure.
  - It is inefficient for querying and analysis, as it requires more joins and scans to retrieve the data.
  - It is less flexible and scalable, as new dimensions and measures may affect the existing structure and require changes in the sub-dimension tables.

### Iris Dataset

The Iris dataset is a multivariate dataset that contains measurements on four different attributes (in centimeters) for 150 flowers from three different species of iris plants . The attributes are:

- Sepal length
- Sepal width
- Petal length
- Petal width

The three species are:

- Iris setosa
- Iris versicolor
- Iris virginica

The dataset was introduced by the British statistician and biologist Ronald Fisher in his 1936 paper "The use of multiple measurements in taxonomic problems" as an example of linear discriminant analysis. It is sometimes called Anderson's Iris dataset because Edgar Anderson collected the data to quantify the morphologic variation of Iris flowers of three related species.

The Iris dataset is widely used as a beginner's dataset for machine learning purposes. ### Complex Event Processing

Complex event processing (CEP) is the use of technology to predict high-level events likely to result from specific sets of low-level factors. By identifying and analyzing cause-and-effect relationships among events in real time, CEP allows personnel to take effective actions in response to specific opportunities or threats.

Some of the key concepts and features of CEP are:

- CEP is based on event processing, which is a method of tracking and analyzing streams of information about things that happen (events), and deriving a conclusion from them.
- CEP combines data from multiple sources to infer events or patterns that suggest more complicated circumstances. For example, CEP can detect a fraud attempt by correlating a credit card transaction with a location change of the cardholder's mobile phone.
- CEP uses a query language to specify the patterns of events that are of interest, and the actions that should be taken when they occur. For example, CEP can trigger an alert when a stock price reaches a certain threshold, or when a customer abandons a shopping cart.
- CEP operates on data streams that are continuously updated, rather than on static data sets. CEP can handle high volumes and velocities of data, and provide low-latency results.
- CEP can be applied to various domains and use cases, such as business intelligence, financial services, e-commerce, health care, security, and smart cities .

Some of the benefits and challenges of CEP are:

- CEP can provide real-time insights and actions that can improve operational efficiency, customer satisfaction, risk management, and competitive advantage .
- CEP can reduce the need for storing and processing large amounts of data, as it can filter out irrelevant or redundant events, and focus on the most meaningful ones.
- CEP can be complex and costly to implement, as it requires a robust and scalable infrastructure, a well-defined event model, and a skilled workforce .
- CEP can be affected by the quality and reliability of the data sources, the accuracy and completeness of the event patterns, and the timeliness and relevance of the actions .

### Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis (RCA) is a method of problem solving used for identifying the root causes of faults or problems. A root cause is the core issue that sets in motion the entire cause-and-effect reaction that ultimately leads to the problem. RCA assumes that it is much more effective to systematically prevent and solve for underlying issues rather than just treating ad hoc symptoms and putting out fires.

Some of the benefits of RCA are:

- It helps to prevent the recurrence of problems by addressing the root causes.
- It improves the quality and reliability of products, services, and processes by eliminating the sources of defects and errors.
- It enhances customer satisfaction and loyalty by delivering value and meeting expectations.
- It reduces costs and wastes by avoiding rework, downtime, and litigation.
- It fosters a culture of continuous improvement and learning by encouraging teamwork and collaboration.

Some of the tools and techniques used for RCA are:

- The 5 Whys: A simple technique that involves asking "why" repeatedly until the root cause is revealed.
- The Fishbone Diagram: A visual tool that helps to identify and organize the possible causes of a problem into categories such as people, process, equipment, environment, etc.
- The Pareto Chart: A graphical tool that helps to prioritize the most significant causes of a problem based on the 80/20 rule, which states that 80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes.
- The Fault Tree Analysis: A logical tool that helps to identify and quantify the probability of failure modes and their consequences.
- The Cause and Effect Matrix: A matrix tool that helps to evaluate the relationship between the inputs and outputs of a process and identify the critical inputs that affect the quality of the output.
