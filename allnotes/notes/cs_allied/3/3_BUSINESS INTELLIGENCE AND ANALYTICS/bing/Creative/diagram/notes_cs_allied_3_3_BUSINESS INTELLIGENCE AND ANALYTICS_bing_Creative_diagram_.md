

# Business Intelligence and Analytics

- Business intelligence and analytics are related but distinct concepts that involve the use of data to support decision-making and performance improvement in organizations.
- Business intelligence (BI) refers to the process of collecting, integrating, transforming, and presenting data from various sources to provide insights and information for decision-makers .
- Business analytics (BA) refers to the process of applying statistical, mathematical, and computational techniques to data to discover patterns, trends, and insights .
- BI and BA are often used together to provide a comprehensive view of the data and its implications for the organization. BI focuses on what has happened and what is happening, while BA focuses on why it has happened and what will happen .
- BI and BA can be applied to various domains and functions, such as marketing, finance, operations, human resources, customer service, etc. They can help organizations to improve efficiency, effectiveness, quality, innovation, and competitiveness.
- BI and BA require the use of various tools and technologies, such as databases, data warehouses, data lakes, data integration tools, data visualization tools, reporting tools, dashboards, business intelligence platforms, data mining tools, predictive analytics tools, prescriptive analytics tools, etc.
- BI and BA also require the use of various skills and competencies, such as data literacy, data governance, data quality, data security, data ethics, data storytelling, data analysis, data modeling, data science, machine learning, artificial intelligence, etc.
- BI and BA are evolving and expanding fields that are influenced by the trends and challenges of the data-driven economy, such as big data, cloud computing, internet of things, social media, mobile devices, etc.

### Introduction - History and Evolution

- Business intelligence (BI) is the process of collecting, analyzing, and presenting data to support decision making in various domains of business.
- The term BI was coined in 1989 by Howard Dresner, a former Gartner analyst, who defined it as "concepts and methods to improve business decision making by using fact-based support systems" .
- However, the origins of BI can be traced back to the 19th century, when Richard Millar Devens described how a banker used information about market trends to gain a competitive advantage in his book "Cyclopaedia of Commercial and Business Anecdotes" .
- The evolution of BI can be divided into four stages: pre-digital, digital, internet, and modern    .
  - Pre-digital stage: Before the advent of computers, BI was mainly based on manual data collection and analysis, using tools such as paper records, punch cards, and calculators. This stage lasted from the 19th century to the mid-20th century.
  - Digital stage: With the emergence of computers and databases in the 1950s and 1960s, BI became more automated and efficient, allowing for faster and larger data processing and storage. This stage also saw the development of decision support systems (DSS), which were computer models that helped managers make decisions based on data and rules. This stage lasted from the mid-20th century to the late 20th century.
  - Internet stage: With the rise of the internet and the web in the 1990s and 2000s, BI became more accessible and interactive, enabling users to access and share data from anywhere and anytime. This stage also saw the emergence of online analytical processing (OLAP), which allowed users to perform multidimensional analysis and drill-down on data. This stage lasted from the late 20th century to the early 21st century.
  - Modern stage: With the advancement of technologies such as cloud computing, big data, artificial intelligence, and machine learning in the 21st century, BI became more scalable, flexible, and intelligent, allowing for real-time and predictive analysis and visualization of data. This stage also saw the emergence of self-service BI, which empowered users to create and customize their own reports and dashboards without relying on IT experts. This stage is still ongoing and evolving.

### Architecture of Data Mining

Data mining is the process of discovering meaningful new correlations, patterns, and trends by shifting through large amounts of data stored in repositories, using pattern recognition technologies as well as statistical and mathematical techniques.

The data mining process involves several components, and these components constitute a data mining system architecture. The significant components of data mining systems are:

- A data source, which provides the raw data to be mined. The data source can be a database, a data warehouse, a data stream, or any other data collection.
- A data mining engine, which applies data mining algorithms to the data and generates results. The data mining engine can be a single module or a collection of modules that perform different tasks, such as preprocessing, clustering, classification, association analysis, etc.
- A data warehouse server, which stores the data and metadata (such as data summary, data model, data schema, etc.) in a data warehouse or other multidimensional data structure. The data warehouse server facilitates efficient data analysis and mining.
- A pattern evaluation module, which evaluates the patterns or models generated by the data mining engine and ranks them according to some criteria, such as interestingness, novelty, usefulness, etc. The pattern evaluation module can also perform some post-processing tasks, such as visualization, transformation, or simplification of the patterns.
- A graphical user interface, which allows the user to interact with the data mining system and specify the data mining tasks and parameters. The graphical user interface also displays the results of data mining in an understandable and interactive way.
- A knowledge base, which stores the domain knowledge and user preferences that are used to guide the data mining process and evaluate the results. The knowledge base can also provide feedback to the data mining engine and improve its performance.

There are different types of data mining architecture, depending on how the data mining system is coupled with the data source or the data warehouse. The main types are:

- No coupling, where the data mining system retrieves data from a particular data source and does not use the data warehouse or the database system functionalities. This type of architecture is suitable for standalone data mining applications that do not require complex data analysis or integration.
- Loose coupling, where the data mining system retrieves data from the database or the data warehouse and stores the data in its own format. The data mining system uses its own data mining engine and does not rely on the database or the data warehouse system functionalities. This type of architecture is suitable for data mining applications that require some data preprocessing or transformation before mining.
- Semi-tight coupling, where the data mining system uses some of the database or the data warehouse system functionalities, such as indexing, sorting, aggregation, etc., to perform data mining. The data mining system also uses its own data mining engine and can perform some data preprocessing or transformation. This type of architecture is suitable for data mining applications that require efficient data access and manipulation.
- Tight coupling, where the data mining system is integrated with the database or the data warehouse system and uses its data mining engine as a module or a function of the system. The data mining system can access the data and metadata directly from the system and can use all its functionalities. This type of architecture is suitable for data mining applications that require complex data analysis and integration.

### Representation of Input Data

- Data mining is the process of discovering useful patterns and insights from large and complex data sets.
- Data representation is the way of organizing and displaying the data for data mining tasks.
- Data representation techniques can affect the quality and efficiency of data mining results.
- Data representation techniques can be classified into two categories: basic and advanced.

#### Basic Data Representation Techniques

- Basic data representation techniques are the ones that use simple and common formats to store and display the data.
- Some examples of basic data representation techniques are:

  - **Tabular format**: Data is organized in rows and columns, where each row represents an instance or a record, and each column represents an attribute or a feature. Tabular format is easy to understand and manipulate, but it may not capture the relationships or dependencies among the data.
  - **Matrix format**: Data is organized in a two-dimensional array, where each element represents the value of an attribute for an instance. Matrix format is useful for numerical and categorical data, but it may not handle missing or sparse data well.
  - **Graph format**: Data is represented as a set of nodes and edges, where each node represents an instance or an attribute, and each edge represents a relationship or a dependency between them. Graph format is suitable for data that has complex and non-linear structures, such as social networks, web pages, or text documents.
  - **Visualization methods**: Data is displayed using graphical or pictorial tools, such as charts, plots, diagrams, or maps. Visualization methods can help to explore and understand the data, but they may not be able to show all the details or dimensions of the data.

#### Advanced Data Representation Techniques

- Advanced data representation techniques are the ones that use more sophisticated and specialized formats to store and display the data.
- Some examples of advanced data representation techniques are:

  - **Data cube**: Data is organized in a multi-dimensional array, where each dimension represents an attribute or a level of aggregation, and each cell represents a measure or a value. Data cube can support efficient and flexible data analysis, such as slicing, dicing, drilling, or rolling.
  - **Dimension reduction**: Data is transformed from a high-dimensional space to a low-dimensional space, by selecting, extracting, or combining the most relevant or informative features. Dimension reduction can help to reduce the complexity and redundancy of the data, and to improve the performance and accuracy of data mining algorithms.
  - **Data compression**: Data is encoded or represented using fewer bits or symbols, by exploiting the regularities or patterns in the data. Data compression can help to save storage space and transmission time, and to preserve data quality and integrity.
  - **Data discretization**: Data is converted from a continuous or numerical form to a discrete or categorical form, by dividing the range of values into intervals or bins. Data discretization can help to simplify the data and to handle noise or outliers.

### Data Mart

- A data mart is a **subset** of a data warehouse focused on a **particular line of business, department, or subject area**  .
- Data marts make specific data available to a defined group of users, which allows those users to quickly access critical insights without wasting time searching through an entire data warehouse.
- A data mart is a **subject-oriented** relational database that stores transactional data in rows and columns, which makes it easy to access, organize, and understand.
- As it contains historical data, this structure makes it easier for an analyst to determine data trends.
- A data mart can be **independent** or **dependent** on a data warehouse. An independent data mart is created directly from source data, while a dependent data mart is created from data already stored in a data warehouse .
- A data mart can also be **logical** or **physical**. A logical data mart is a view or query on a data warehouse that provides a specific perspective for a user group, while a physical data mart is a separate database that contains a subset of data from a data warehouse .
- A data mart can have different **design approaches**, such as top-down, bottom-up, or hybrid. A top-down approach starts with a data warehouse and then creates data marts from it, while a bottom-up approach starts with data marts and then integrates them into a data warehouse. A hybrid approach combines both methods .
- A data mart can have different **benefits**, such as faster query performance, lower cost, easier maintenance, increased security, and improved data quality .

### Difference between OLAP and OLTP

### Schema – Star and Snowflake Schemas

- A schema is a logical representation of the structure and organization of data in a data warehouse or data mart.
- A schema consists of fact tables and dimension tables that store the data and metadata of the data warehouse or data mart.
- A fact table contains the measures or metrics of the business processes, such as sales, revenue, profit, etc.
- A dimension table contains the attributes or characteristics of the business entities, such as product, customer, location, time, etc.
- A schema can be designed using different approaches, such as star schema and snowflake schema, depending on the requirements and complexity of the data.

#### Star Schema

- A star schema is the simplest and most common type of schema in data warehousing and business intelligence projects.
- A star schema consists of a single fact table and multiple dimension tables that are directly connected to the fact table.
- A star schema is called so because its structure resembles a star, with the fact table at the center and the dimension tables as the points.
- A star schema has the following advantages:
  - It is easy to understand and implement, as it has a simple and denormalized structure.
  - It is efficient for querying and analysis, as it requires fewer joins and scans to retrieve the data.
  - It is flexible and scalable, as new dimensions and measures can be easily added or modified without affecting the existing structure.
- A star schema has the following disadvantages:
  - It may have data redundancy and inconsistency, as the same data may be repeated in multiple dimension tables.
  - It may have limited data granularity and detail, as the dimension tables are not further normalized into sub-dimensions.
  - It may have performance issues, as the fact table and the dimension tables may grow very large over time.

#### Snowflake Schema

- A snowflake schema is an extension and variation of the star schema, where the dimension tables are further normalized into sub-dimension tables.
- A snowflake schema consists of a single fact table and multiple dimension tables that are connected to the fact table through intermediate sub-dimension tables.
- A snowflake schema is called so because its structure resembles a snowflake, with the fact table at the center and the dimension tables and sub-dimension tables as the branches.
- A snowflake schema has the following advantages:
  - It reduces data redundancy and inconsistency, as the same data is not repeated in multiple tables.
  - It increases data granularity and detail, as the dimension tables are further split into sub-dimensions.
  - It saves storage space, as the tables are normalized and have fewer columns and rows.
- A snowflake schema has the following disadvantages:
  - It is complex and difficult to understand and implement, as it has a normalized and hierarchical structure.
  - It is inefficient for querying and analysis, as it requires more joins and scans to retrieve the data.
  - It is rigid and less scalable, as new dimensions and measures may require significant changes to the existing structure.

### Data Discretization

- Data discretization is the process of transforming continuous data into discrete or categorical data by creating a finite set of values or intervals.
- Data discretization is useful for data analysis because it can reduce the complexity and noise of the data, improve the efficiency and accuracy of the data mining algorithms, and facilitate the interpretation and visualization of the results.
- Data discretization can be performed in two ways: supervised or unsupervised. Supervised discretization uses the class labels of the data to guide the discretization process, while unsupervised discretization does not use any class information and only considers the distribution of the data.
- There are different types of data discretization methods, such as:
  - Equal-width discretization: This method divides the range of the data into equal-sized intervals. It is simple and fast, but it can be sensitive to outliers and may not capture the underlying structure of the data.
  - Equal-frequency discretization: This method divides the data into intervals that have the same number of instances. It can handle outliers and skewed data better than equal-width discretization, but it may create intervals with different widths and lose some information.
  - Entropy-based discretization: This method uses the concept of entropy to measure the information gain of each possible cut point and selects the one that maximizes the information gain. It is a supervised method that can produce optimal intervals for classification, but it can be computationally expensive and may overfit the data.
  - Chi-square based discretization: This method uses the chi-square statistic to test the independence between the attribute values and the class labels and merges adjacent intervals that have no significant difference. It is also a supervised method that can produce optimal intervals for classification, but it can be sensitive to the number of instances and the initial intervals.

### Complex Event Processing

Complex event processing (CEP) is a technology for aggregating, processing, and analyzing massive streams of data in order to gain real-time insights from events as they occur . CEP can be used to identify meaningful events (such as opportunities or threats) in real-time situations and respond to them as quickly as possible . These events may be happening across the various layers of an organization as sales leads, orders, customer service calls, etc.

Some of the key concepts and components of CEP are:

- **Event**: An event is anything that happens or is observed, such as a click, a purchase, a sensor reading, a tweet, etc. Events have attributes that describe their properties, such as time, location, type, value, etc.
- **Event stream**: An event stream is a sequence of events that are ordered by time and share a common source or topic, such as a web server log, a stock ticker, a social media feed, etc.
- **Event processing**: Event processing is a method of tracking and analyzing (processing) streams of information (data) about things that happen (events), and deriving a conclusion from them.
- **Complex event**: A complex event is an event that is inferred from one or more events or patterns that suggest more complicated circumstances, such as a fraud attempt, a customer churn, a traffic jam, etc.
- **Complex event processing engine**: A complex event processing engine is a software system that can ingest, filter, transform, aggregate, correlate, and analyze event streams, and generate complex events or actions based on predefined rules or queries.
- **Complex event processing query**: A complex event processing query is a specification of the conditions or patterns that define a complex event or an action, such as "if the temperature of a sensor exceeds a threshold for more than 10 minutes, then send an alert" or "if a customer buys more than three items in a week, then offer a discount".
- **Complex event processing application**: A complex event processing application is a software system that uses a complex event processing engine and queries to perform a specific business function or goal, such as fraud detection, customer retention, traffic management, etc.

Some of the benefits and challenges of CEP are:

- **Benefits**: CEP can provide real-time insights and actions that can improve operational efficiency, customer satisfaction, risk management, and competitive advantage. CEP can also handle high-volume, high-velocity, and heterogeneous data sources that traditional data processing systems cannot cope with.
- **Challenges**: CEP requires a clear definition of the business problem and the relevant events and patterns. CEP also requires a scalable and reliable infrastructure and a robust and flexible complex event processing engine and query language. CEP may also face issues such as data quality, security, privacy, and compliance.



### Business Process Management

Business process management (BPM) is a discipline that uses various methods to discover, model, analyze, measure, improve and optimize business processes   . A business process is a set of coordinated activities that produce a specific outcome for a customer or stakeholder . BPM aims to align business processes with the strategic goals of the organization, improve efficiency and effectiveness, and enable innovation and agility  .

Some of the benefits of BPM are:

- It helps to reduce costs, errors, and delays by eliminating waste and redundancy in business processes .
- It helps to improve customer satisfaction and loyalty by delivering consistent and high-quality products and services .
- It helps to increase productivity and performance by streamlining and automating business processes .
- It helps to enhance compliance and risk management by ensuring adherence to standards, regulations, and policies .
- It helps to foster innovation and collaboration by enabling continuous improvement and learning from best practices .

Some of the challenges of BPM are:

- It requires a clear vision and strategy for business process improvement and alignment .
- It requires a strong leadership and governance structure to support and monitor the BPM initiatives .
- It requires a culture of change and adaptation to embrace new ways of working and thinking .
- It requires a skilled and motivated workforce to participate and contribute to the BPM efforts .
- It requires a robust and flexible technology infrastructure to enable and support the BPM solutions .

Some of the key components of BPM are:

- Business process modeling: The activity of creating graphical representations of business processes, using standard notations and tools  .
- Business process analysis: The activity of examining and evaluating the current state of business processes, identifying problems and opportunities for improvement  .
- Business process design: The activity of defining and specifying the desired future state of business processes, incorporating best practices and requirements  .
- Business process implementation: The activity of executing and deploying the designed business processes, using software applications and systems  .
- Business process monitoring: The activity of measuring and tracking the performance and outcomes of business processes, using indicators and dashboards  .
- Business process optimization: The activity of reviewing and refining the implemented business processes, using feedback and data to identify and implement improvements  .

Some of the common tools and techniques used in BPM are:

- Business process modeling notation (BPMN): A standard graphical notation for representing business processes, using symbols and diagrams  .
- Business process management suite (BPMS): A software platform that provides a comprehensive set of capabilities for designing, executing, monitoring, and optimizing business processes  .
- Business process reengineering (BPR): A radical approach to redesigning and transforming business processes, aiming to achieve dramatic improvements in performance and quality  .
- Business process outsourcing (BPO): A strategy of contracting out non-core business processes to external providers, aiming to reduce costs and increase efficiency  .
- Business process simulation (BPS): A technique of using mathematical models and software to mimic the behavior and outcomes of business processes, aiming to test and evaluate different scenarios and alternatives  .

### Root Cause Analysis

- Root cause analysis (RCA) is a method of problem solving used for identifying the root causes of faults or problems.
- A root cause is the core issue that sets in motion the entire cause-and-effect reaction that ultimately leads to the problem.
- RCA is a team approach methodology that works by understanding that systems and actions are related and that one action can trigger an event and that event can trigger another event and so on until you have a problem or multiple problems.
- There are four main steps in an RCA:
  - Identify the problem
  - Analyze the problem
  - Identify the root causes
  - Implement solutions
- RCA is often used in proactive management to identify the root cause of a problem, that is, the factor that was the leading cause.
- RCA is also useful for data analysis, as it can help discover the root causes of problems in data quality, data accuracy, data completeness, data timeliness, and data relevance.
- Some of the benefits of RCA are:
  - It can prevent recurrence of problems and reduce costs
  - It can improve customer satisfaction and loyalty
  - It can enhance quality and performance
  - It can foster a culture of continuous improvement and learning
- Some of the tools and techniques used for RCA are:
  - The 5 Whys: A simple technique that involves asking "why" repeatedly until the root cause is revealed
  - Fishbone Diagram: A visual tool that shows the relationship between a problem and its possible causes, organized into categories
  - Pareto Chart: A graphical tool that displays the frequency or impact of different causes, based on the 80/20 rule
  - Fault Tree Analysis: A logical tool that uses a tree-like structure to trace the events and conditions that lead to a failure
  - Cause and Effect Matrix: A matrix tool that ranks the causes of a problem based on their severity and frequency
