# KCS

- KCS stands for Knowledge-Centered Service, a methodology for creating and maintaining knowledge in a service organization.
- KCS is based on four principles: 
  - **Demand-driven**: Knowledge is created based on the issues and questions that customers and service agents encounter, not based on assumptions or predictions.
  - **Structured**: Knowledge is captured in a consistent and reusable format, following a standard template and taxonomy.
  - **Evolutionary**: Knowledge is continuously improved and updated based on feedback and usage, not based on a fixed schedule or review cycle.
  - **Collaborative**: Knowledge is shared and validated by the collective experience and expertise of the service organization, not based on individual opinions or authority.
- KCS has two main processes: 
  - **Solve Loop**: The process of creating, finding, and using knowledge during the service delivery, following the steps of Capture, Structure, Reuse, and Improve.
  - **Evolve Loop**: The process of managing, measuring, and improving the knowledge base and the KCS practices, following the steps of Content Health, Process Integration, Performance Assessment, and Leadership and Communication.
- KCS has six core concepts that guide the implementation and adoption of the methodology: 
  - **Buy-in**: The commitment and support of the service organization and its stakeholders to adopt KCS and its benefits.
  - **Roles**: The definition and assignment of specific responsibilities and accountabilities for the KCS participants, such as Knowledge Workers, Coaches, and Content Managers.
  - **Workflow**: The integration and alignment of the KCS practices with the service delivery processes and tools, such as incident management, problem management, and self-service portals.
  - **Content**: The quality and usability of the knowledge articles, following the KCS article quality criteria and the KCS article lifecycle.
  - **Technology**: The functionality and configuration of the knowledge management system and its integration with other service management systems, such as ticketing, reporting, and analytics.
  - **Performance**: The measurement and evaluation of the KCS outcomes and impacts, using the KCS measures and indicators, such as reuse rate, resolution time, and customer satisfaction.

### Introduction - History and Evolution

- Business intelligence (BI) is the process of collecting, analyzing, and presenting data to support decision making and improve performance in organizations.
- The term BI was coined in 1989 by Howard Dresner, a former Gartner analyst, who defined it as "concepts and methods to improve business decision making by using fact-based support systems" .
- However, the origins of BI can be traced back to the 19th century, when Richard Millar Devens described how a banker used information about market trends to gain a competitive advantage in his book "Cyclopaedia of Commercial and Business Anecdotes" .
- The evolution of BI can be divided into four main stages:   

  - **Pre-digital era (before 1950s):** The earliest forms of BI were based on manual data collection and analysis, such as census, surveys, and accounting records. The main challenges were the lack of data availability, accuracy, and timeliness.
  - **Early digital era (1950s-1980s):** The advent of computers and databases enabled the automation of data processing and storage, as well as the development of decision support systems (DSS) and executive information systems (EIS) that provided interactive and graphical interfaces for data analysis and reporting. The main challenges were the high cost, complexity, and inflexibility of these systems, as well as the lack of integration and standardization of data sources.
  - **Modern digital era (1990s-2010s):** The emergence of the internet, data warehousing, online analytical processing (OLAP), data mining, and business analytics (BA) expanded the scope and capabilities of BI, as well as the accessibility and usability of data for various users and purposes. The main challenges were the increasing volume, variety, and velocity of data, as well as the need for more advanced and predictive analytics.
  - **Future digital era (2020s and beyond):** The advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), cloud computing, big data, and data visualization are transforming BI into a more automated, intelligent, and agile process that can provide real-time, personalized, and prescriptive insights and recommendations. The main challenges are the ethical, legal, and social implications of data usage, as well as the need for human oversight and collaboration.

### BI – DATA MINING & WAREHOUSING: Key Concepts

- A data warehouse is a system that aggregates data from different sources into a single, central, consistent data store to support data analysis, data mining, artificial intelligence (AI), and machine learning.
- A data warehouse typically follows a dimensional model, which consists of fact tables and dimension tables. Fact tables store quantitative measures, such as sales, revenue, or profit. Dimension tables store descriptive attributes, such as product, customer, or time.
- A data warehouse can be organized into different levels of granularity, which refers to the level of detail or aggregation of the data. For example, a data warehouse can store daily, monthly, or yearly sales data.
- A data warehouse can also be organized into different schemas, such as star, snowflake, or galaxy. A schema is a logical structure that defines how the tables are related to each other. - A data warehouse can be implemented using various technologies, such as relational databases, columnar databases, or cloud-based platforms. Predictive data mining builds models that can forecast future outcomes, such as classification, regression, or time series. Prescriptive data mining suggests optimal actions or decisions, such as optimization, simulation, or recommendation.
- Data mining can also be classified into different tasks, such as classification, clustering, association rule mining, anomaly detection, or sentiment analysis. - Data mining can be applied to various domains and industries, such as finance, marketing, healthcare, education, or social media. Data mining can also be used for various purposes, such as customer segmentation, product recommendation, fraud detection, sentiment analysis, or trend analysis.

### Analysis Methodologies for Data Mining

Data mining is the process of discovering useful patterns and insights from large and complex data sets. It involves applying various techniques and methods to extract knowledge that can support decision making, problem solving, and business intelligence. Data mining can be applied to various domains, such as marketing, finance, healthcare, education, and social media.

There are different types of data mining methods, each with its own purpose, advantages, and limitations. Some of the common data mining methods are:

- **Association**: This method is used to find relationships or correlations between two or more items or variables in a data set. For example, finding the association between products purchased together in a supermarket, or between symptoms and diseases in a medical database. Association rules are often expressed in the form of "if-then" statements, such as "if a customer buys bread, then they are likely to buy butter". Association analysis can help identify cross-selling opportunities, customer preferences, and market segments.
- **Classification**: This method is used to assign items or instances in a data set to predefined classes or categories based on their attributes or features. For example, classifying emails as spam or not spam, or classifying customers as high-risk or low-risk based on their credit history. Classification techniques use supervised learning, which means they require labeled data for training and testing. Some of the common classification algorithms are decision trees, logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors, and support vector machines. Classification analysis can help predict outcomes, diagnose problems, and evaluate performance.
- **Clustering**: This method is used to group items or instances in a data set based on their similarity or dissimilarity. Unlike classification, clustering does not require predefined classes or labels, and it uses unsupervised learning, which means it does not need any prior knowledge or training data. For example, clustering customers based on their demographic and behavioral characteristics, or clustering documents based on their topics or keywords. Some of the common clustering algorithms are k-means, hierarchical clustering, and density-based clustering. Clustering analysis can help discover hidden patterns, segment data, and reduce complexity.
- **Outlier Detection**: This method is used to identify items or instances in a data set that deviate significantly from the normal or expected behavior. These items are also called anomalies, outliers, or exceptions, and they can indicate errors, fraud, or novelty. For example, detecting fraudulent transactions in a banking system, or detecting unusual network activity in a cybersecurity system. Outlier detection techniques can be based on statistical methods, distance-based methods, or density-based methods. Outlier detection analysis can help improve data quality, detect threats, and monitor changes.
- **Regression**: This method is used to model the relationship between a dependent variable (also called the target or response variable) and one or more independent variables (also called the predictors or explanatory variables) in a data set. For example, modeling the relationship between the sales of a product and its price, advertising, and customer satisfaction. Regression techniques use supervised learning, and they can be linear or nonlinear, depending on the nature of the data and the relationship. Some of the common regression algorithms are linear regression, polynomial regression, and logistic regression. Regression analysis can help estimate values, test hypotheses, and optimize resources.
- **Prediction**: This method is used to forecast the future values or outcomes of a variable or event based on the historical data and trends. For example, predicting the demand for a product in the next quarter, or predicting the weather for the next week. Prediction techniques can use supervised or unsupervised learning, and they can be based on statistical methods, machine learning methods, or time series methods. Some of the common prediction algorithms are linear regression, neural networks, and ARIMA models. Prediction analysis can help plan strategies, allocate resources, and anticipate risks.



### Data Warehousing - Introduction to Data Warehousing

- A data warehouse is a database designed to enable and support business intelligence (BI) activities, especially analytics.
- A data warehouse is a type of data management system that aggregates data from different sources into a single, central, consistent data store.
- A data warehouse is solely intended to perform queries and analysis and often contains large amounts of historical data.
- A data warehouse helps users understand and enhance their organization's performance by providing them with relevant, accurate, and timely information.
- A data warehouse can also support data mining, artificial intelligence (AI), and machine learning applications by providing them with high-quality and structured data.
- A data warehouse typically has a layered architecture that consists of the following components:
  - Data sources: The original data sources that provide the raw data for the data warehouse, such as transactional systems, log files, external data, etc.
  - Data integration: The process of extracting, transforming, and loading (ETL) the data from the data sources into the data warehouse, applying data quality, data cleansing, and data integration techniques.
  - Data storage: The physical storage of the data in the data warehouse, using a relational or multidimensional data model, and applying data compression, partitioning, indexing, and security techniques.
  - Data access: The interface that allows users and applications to access and query the data in the data warehouse, using various tools and languages, such as SQL, OLAP, reporting, dashboards, etc.
  - Data presentation: The process of presenting and visualizing the data in the data warehouse, using charts, graphs, tables, maps, etc., to support decision making and data analysis.



### Data Mart

- A data mart is a **subset** of a data warehouse focused on a **particular subject** or **line of business** .

### Online Analytical Processing (OLAP) – Tools

- Online Analytical Processing (OLAP) is a technology that is used to organize large business databases and support business intelligence.
- OLAP tools enable to analyze multidimensional data interactively from multiple perspectives.
- OLAP involves relational database, report writing and data mining and consists of three basic analytical operations: consolidation, drill down, and slicing and dicing.
- Consolidation is the aggregation of data along one or more dimensions, such as summing up sales by region or by product category.
- Drill down is the process of breaking down a higher level of data into more detailed levels, such as going from yearly sales to monthly sales.
- Slicing and dicing is the ability to view a subset of data by selecting specific values of one or more dimensions, such as viewing sales of a specific product in a specific month.
- OLAP tools can be classified into three types: MOLAP, ROLAP, and HOLAP .
- MOLAP or Multidimensional Online Analytical Processing uses a multidimensional cube that accesses stored data through various combinations of dimensions and measures.
- ROLAP or Relational Online Analytical Processing stores data in relational tables using columns and rows and uses SQL queries to access the data.
- HOLAP or Hybrid Online Analytical Processing combines the features of both MOLAP and ROLAP and allows the user to choose the best storage option for different types of data.

### Difference between OLAP and OLTP

- OLAP systems are used for business intelligence, data mining, data warehousing and reporting, while OLTP systems are used for e-commerce, banking, reservation and inventory management.

### Schema - Star and Snowflake Schemas

- A schema is a logical representation of the structure and organization of data in a data warehouse or data mart.
- A schema consists of fact tables and dimension tables that store the data and metadata of the data warehouse or data mart.
- A fact table contains the measures or metrics of the business processes, such as sales, revenue, profit, etc.
- A dimension table contains the attributes or characteristics of the business entities, such as product, customer, location, time, etc.
- A schema can be designed using different approaches, such as star schema, snowflake schema, galaxy schema, etc.
- The star schema and the snowflake schema are two popular approaches to organizing and structuring data in data warehousing and business intelligence projects.
- Each schema has its own unique characteristics and benefits, and the best fit for your specific needs will depend on the requirements of your project.

#### Star Schema

- The star schema is the simplest type of data warehouse schema.
- It is known as star schema as its structure resembles a star.
- In a star schema, all the dimension tables are directly connected to the fact table, forming a star-like shape.
- The dimension tables are not normalized, meaning they contain all the attributes in one table, without any further sub-dimensions.
- The star schema has the following advantages:
  - It is easy to understand and implement, as it has a simple and intuitive structure.
  - It is efficient for querying and analysis, as it requires fewer joins and less data processing.
  - It is compatible with most of the data warehouse tools and BI applications, as it is a widely used and accepted standard.
- The star schema has the following disadvantages:
  - It may result in data redundancy and inconsistency, as the same attribute may be repeated in multiple dimension tables.
  - It may not capture the complex relationships and hierarchies among the dimensions, as it does not allow sub-dimensions or snowflaking.
  - It may not support the changing business requirements, as it is rigid and inflexible to accommodate new dimensions or attributes.

#### Snowflake Schema

- The snowflake schema is an extension of the star schema, and it adds additional dimensions.
- It is called snowflake because its diagram resembles a snowflake.
- In a snowflake schema, some of the dimension tables are normalized, meaning they are split into sub-dimension tables, forming a snowflake-like shape.
- The snowflake schema has the following advantages:
  - It reduces data redundancy and inconsistency, as the same attribute is stored in only one table, avoiding duplication.
  - It captures the complex relationships and hierarchies among the dimensions, as it allows sub-dimensions or snowflaking.
  - It supports the changing business requirements, as it is flexible and adaptable to accommodate new dimensions or attributes.
- The snowflake schema has the following disadvantages:
  - It is difficult to understand and implement, as it has a complex and intricate structure.
  - It is inefficient for querying and analysis, as it requires more joins and more data processing.
  - It is incompatible with some of the data warehouse tools and BI applications, as it is a less used and accepted standard.

- Data reduction is a technique used in data mining to reduce the size of a dataset while still preserving the most important information.
- Data sampling is a data reduction technique that involves selecting a subset of the data to work with, rather than using the entire dataset .
- Data sampling can be useful for reducing the size of a dataset while still preserving the overall trends and patterns in the data .
- There are four types of sampling data reduction methods:
  - Simple random sample without replacement: This method selects a sample of size n from a dataset of size N without putting back the selected tuples. This ensures that each tuple has an equal probability of being selected and that no tuple is selected more than once.
  - Simple random sample with replacement: This method selects a sample of size n from a dataset of size N with putting back the selected tuples. This means that each tuple has an equal probability of being selected and that some tuples may be selected more than once.
  - Cluster sample: This method divides the dataset into clusters based on some criteria, such as geographic location, and then selects one or more clusters to form the sample. This can reduce the variability within the sample and increase the efficiency of the sampling process.
  - Stratified sample: This method divides the dataset into strata based on some attribute, such as gender, and then selects a proportional or equal number of tuples from each stratum to form the sample. This can ensure that the sample is representative of the population and that each stratum is adequately represented.

### PCA for the notes of the Unit 3 - BI – DATA PREPARTTION in the subject of BUSINESS INTELLIGENCE AND ANALYTICS KCS

- PCA stands for Principal Component Analysis, a very powerful technique that has wide applicability in data science, bioinformatics, and further afield.
- PCA is an unsupervised machine learning technique that aims to reduce the dimensionality of large data sets, by transforming a large set of variables into a smaller one that still contains most of the information in the large set.
- PCA can be used for various purposes, such as:
  - Exploratory data analysis, to discover patterns and relationships in the data.
  - Data visualization, to project high-dimensional data into lower dimensions for easier interpretation.
  - Data preparation, to remove noise, outliers, or redundant features from the data .
  - Information compression, to reduce the storage and computational costs of the data.
- PCA works by finding the directions of maximum variance in the data, called principal components, and projecting the data onto these directions.
- The principal components are orthogonal to each other, meaning they are uncorrelated and independent.
- The first principal component explains the most variance in the data, the second principal component explains the second most variance, and so on.
- The number of principal components is equal to or less than the number of original variables.
- PCA can be performed by using different methods, such as:
  - Singular value decomposition (SVD), which decomposes the data matrix into three matrices: U, S, and V, where U contains the principal components, S contains the singular values, and V contains the loadings.
  - Eigenvalue decomposition (EVD), which decomposes the covariance matrix of the data into two matrices: Q and D, where Q contains the eigenvectors, which are the principal components, and D contains the eigenvalues, which are the variances explained by each principal component.
- PCA requires some assumptions and considerations, such as:
  - The data should be standardized or normalized before applying PCA, otherwise the variables with larger scales will dominate the principal components.
  - The data should be linearly related, otherwise PCA may not capture the nonlinear patterns in the data.
  - The choice of the number of principal components to retain depends on the trade-off between information loss and dimensionality reduction.
  - The interpretation of the principal components may not be straightforward, as they are linear combinations of the original variables.

### Behavioral Data Analytics Process

Behavioral data analytics is the process of using behavioral data—or data generated by, or in response to, a customer’s engagement with a business, such as page views, email sign-ups, or other important user actions—to construct behavioral cohorts and analyze things like engagement, conversion, and retention.

Some steps for successful behavioral analysis are:

- Select goals, KPIs, and metrics: To determine whether users are reaching the right goals (e.g., purchase or conversion), select the KPIs and metrics that indicate progress toward those goals. Examples: revenue, retention rate, churn rate, etc.
- Map out the critical paths: Identify the key user actions or events that lead to the desired outcomes, such as signing up, adding items to cart, checking out, etc. These are the steps that users need to take to complete the goals.
- Create a tracking plan: Define the events and properties that need to be tracked for each user action, such as event name, event category, event value, user ID, device type, etc. A tracking plan helps to ensure consistent and accurate data collection across platforms and teams.
- Use unique identifiers to understand behavior across platforms: Assign a unique ID to each user that can be used to track their behavior across different devices and channels, such as web, mobile, email, etc. This helps to create a unified view of the user journey and avoid duplication or fragmentation of data.
- Implement analytics and begin event tracking: Choose an analytics tool or platform that can capture, store, and analyze the event data. Implement the tracking code or SDK on the platforms where the events occur, and verify that the data is flowing correctly and matches the tracking plan.
- Analyze the data and derive insights: Use the analytics tool or platform to segment the users into behavioral cohorts based on their actions, attributes, or outcomes. For example, segment users by frequency of usage, purchase behavior, retention status, etc. Then, use metrics and visualizations to compare and contrast the cohorts and identify patterns, trends, anomalies, and other useful insights. For example, use funnel analysis to see where users drop off in the critical path, or use retention analysis to see how loyal users are over time.
- Take action and optimize the user experience: Based on the insights from the data analysis, make data-driven decisions and take action to improve the user experience and achieve the business goals. For example, test different variations of the user interface, offer personalized recommendations or incentives, send targeted messages or notifications, etc. Then, measure the impact of the actions and iterate as needed.

### Complex Event Processing

Complex event processing (CEP) is a method of processing multiple streams of events and correlating seemingly unrelated events to identify opportunities or threats in real-time situations and respond to them as quickly as possible  .

Some examples of complex events are:

- A fraudulent credit card transaction detected by analyzing the location, amount, and frequency of the transactions.
- A stock market trend predicted by analyzing the price, volume, and sentiment of the trades.
- A customer churn risk identified by analyzing the customer behavior, feedback, and loyalty program data.

Some benefits of complex event processing are:

- It enables faster and more informed decision making by providing real-time insights from events as they occur.
- It improves operational efficiency and performance by automating actions and workflows based on event patterns and rules.
- It enhances customer experience and satisfaction by providing personalized and proactive services and offers based on event context and history.

Some challenges of complex event processing are:

- It requires a scalable and reliable infrastructure to handle the high volume, velocity, and variety of event data.
- It demands a complex and dynamic logic to define the event patterns and rules and to handle the uncertainty and ambiguity of event data.
- It involves a trade-off between the accuracy and timeliness of the event processing and the cost and complexity of the event processing.

Some technologies and tools for complex event processing are:

- Apache Kafka: A distributed streaming platform that provides high-throughput, low-latency, and fault-tolerant event ingestion and processing.
- Apache Spark: A unified analytics engine that supports batch, streaming, and complex event processing with high performance and scalability.
- TIBCO BusinessEvents: A complex event processing software that enables event-driven applications and analytics with a graphical user interface and a rule engine.

- Metadata is data that describes other data. It provides information about the characteristics, quality, origin, and usage of data.

- There are different types of metadata, such as technical, business, and operational metadata. Each type serves a different purpose and audience.

  - Business metadata describes data by mapping it to business terms, key performance indicators (KPIs), data domains, reports, glossary terms, and more. It helps data analysts and business users to understand the business context and meaning of data, and to perform data discovery and analysis.
  - Operational metadata includes information about the performance, usage, and quality of data, such as data sources, timestamps, frequency, volume, errors, and validations. It helps data managers and stewards to monitor, audit, and improve the data quality and governance.
- Metadata management can drive business value, improve innovation and collaboration, and mitigate risk. It can also enable data users to access high-quality and trusted data, thus ensuring that they work with the right data to deliver accurate insights .
- Metadata management can be implemented using various tools and techniques, such as metadata repositories, metadata standards, metadata extraction, metadata integration, metadata cataloging, and metadata search. These tools and techniques can help to automate, simplify, and enhance the metadata management process.



### Root Cause Analysis

- Root cause analysis (RCA) is a method of problem solving used for identifying the root causes of faults or problems.
- A root cause is the core issue that sets in motion the entire cause-and-effect reaction that ultimately leads to the problem(s).
- RCA assumes that it is much more effective to systematically prevent and solve for underlying issues rather than just treating ad hoc symptoms and putting out fires.
- RCA is a team approach methodology that works by understanding that systems and actions are related and that one action can trigger an event and that event can trigger another event and so on until you have a problem or multiple problems.
- There are four main steps in an RCA:
  - Identify the problem
  - Analyze the problem
  - Identify the root causes
  - Implement solutions
- RCA can be applied to various domains, such as engineering, science, business, healthcare, education, etc.
- RCA can benefit organizations by :
  - Improving quality and performance
  - Reducing costs and risks
  - Enhancing customer satisfaction and loyalty
  - Increasing employee engagement and morale
  - Fostering a culture of continuous improvement and learning
- Some of the common tools and techniques used for RCA are :
  - The 5 Whys: A simple technique that involves asking "why" repeatedly until the root cause is revealed.
  - Fishbone Diagram: A visual tool that helps organize the possible causes of a problem into categories and subcategories.
  - Pareto Chart: A graphical tool that shows the frequency or impact of different factors or causes of a problem.
  - Fault Tree Analysis: A logical tool that uses a tree-like structure to identify the possible failures and their causes in a system or process.
  - Cause and Effect Matrix: A matrix that helps prioritize the causes of a problem based on their severity and frequency.
