# Network Protocol- Data Dissemination

- Data dissemination is the process of delivering data that matches the interest of the querying nodes in a network.
- Data dissemination is essential for IoT applications that generate massive amounts of data from various sensors and devices.
- Data dissemination protocols aim to provide efficient, reliable, and secure communication among the nodes in the network.
- Data dissemination protocols can be classified into two categories: push-based and pull-based.
  - Push-based protocols: the data source initiates the data transmission to the interested nodes without waiting for their requests. This is suitable for time-sensitive and event-driven applications, but may cause redundant transmissions and network congestion.
  - Pull-based protocols: the interested nodes send requests to the data source and wait for the data delivery. This is suitable for on-demand and query-based applications, but may cause delay and overhead in the network.
- Data dissemination protocols can also be classified into three types: flooding, gossiping, and routing.
  - Flooding: the data source broadcasts the data to all its neighbors, and each neighbor repeats the same process until the data reaches all the nodes in the network. This is simple and robust, but may cause high energy consumption and network collision.
  - Gossiping: the data source randomly selects a subset of its neighbors and sends the data to them, and each neighbor repeats the same process until the data reaches a certain percentage of the nodes in the network. This is scalable and resilient, but may cause low reliability and coverage.
  - Routing: the data source sends the data to a specific node or a group of nodes based on some criteria, such as location, distance, or interest. This is efficient and accurate, but may cause high complexity and vulnerability.

### Improving Data and Service Interoperability with Structure, Compliance, Conformance and Context Awareness

- Interoperability is the ability of different systems, devices, or applications to exchange and use data or services without requiring human intervention or extra effort.
- Interoperability is essential for the Internet of Things (IoT), which involves a large number of heterogeneous and distributed entities that need to communicate and cooperate to achieve common goals.
- Interoperability can be achieved at different levels, such as syntactic, semantic, or pragmatic.
- Syntactic interoperability refers to the common format and structure of the data or messages exchanged.
- Semantic interoperability refers to the common meaning and interpretation of the data or messages exchanged.
- Pragmatic interoperability refers to the common understanding and expectations of the behavior and functionality of the services or applications exchanged.
- Interoperability can be improved by using the following concepts:
  - Structure: the organization and representation of the data or services, which can be based on standards, schemas, or formats that facilitate the exchange and processing of information.
  - Compliance: the adherence to a set of rules or specifications that define the structure, syntax, or semantics of the data or services, which can be verified by validation or verification mechanisms.
  - Conformance: the degree of similarity or compatibility between the data or services and a given standard, schema, or format, which can be measured by metrics or indicators.
  - Context awareness: the ability to adapt the data or services to the specific situation, environment, or preferences of the users or applications, which can be achieved by using contextual information or metadata.
- This chapter presents an architectural style, Structural Services, which combines the service modelling capabilities of Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA) with the flexibility of structured resources in Representational State Transfer (REST).
- Structural Services aim to enhance interoperability, at the data and service levels, by using the following features:
  - Partial interoperability: the possibility of achieving interoperability even when the data or services do not fully comply or conform to a given standard, schema, or format, by using compliance and conformance levels that indicate the degree of interoperability achieved.
  - Context model: the representation and management of the contextual information that influences the data or services, which includes forward and backward context, that is, the context that precedes and follows the data or service exchange.
- Structural Services can address some of the challenges of IoT, such as heterogeneity, scalability, or dynamism, by using compliance and conformance (less restrictive than schema sharing) and context awareness (which increases the range of applicability).

## Unit 2 - RFID False Authentications

- RFID stands for Radio Frequency Identification, a technology that uses radio waves to identify and track objects, such as tags, cards, or chips, that are attached to or embedded in the objects.
- RFID systems consist of three main components: a reader, a tag, and a backend database. The reader emits radio signals to query the tag, which responds with its unique identifier or other data. The reader then communicates with the database to verify the tag's authenticity and perform other operations.
- RFID false authentication is a type of attack that aims to impersonate a legitimate tag or reader, or to modify the data exchanged between them, in order to gain unauthorized access, steal information, or cause damage to the system.
- There are different types of RFID false authentication attacks, such as:

  - Tag cloning: The attacker copies the identifier or data of a legitimate tag and creates a fake tag that can respond to the reader's queries with the same information.
  - Tag spoofing: The attacker uses a device that can generate radio signals to mimic the response of a legitimate tag, without actually having a physical tag.
  - Reader spoofing: The attacker uses a device that can generate radio signals to mimic the query of a legitimate reader, and tries to elicit responses from the tags in the vicinity.
  - Replay attack: The attacker captures the radio signals exchanged between a legitimate reader and tag, and replays them later to fool the system.
  - Relay attack: The attacker uses two devices, one near the legitimate reader and one near the legitimate tag, to relay the radio signals between them, creating a false impression of proximity.
  - Man-in-the-middle attack: The attacker intercepts and modifies the radio signals exchanged between a legitimate reader and tag, altering the data or commands sent or received.

- RFID false authentication attacks pose serious threats to the security and privacy of RFID systems and their users, as they can lead to:

  - Unauthorized access: The attacker can gain access to restricted areas, services, or resources by impersonating a legitimate tag or reader.
  - Information leakage: The attacker can steal sensitive or personal information from the tags or the database by impersonating a legitimate reader or tag.
  - Data corruption: The attacker can alter the data stored on the tags or the database by impersonating a legitimate reader or tag, or by modifying the radio signals in transit.
  - System malfunction: The attacker can disrupt the normal operation of the RFID system by impersonating a legitimate reader or tag, or by sending false or malicious commands or data.

- To prevent or mitigate RFID false authentication attacks, various countermeasures can be employed, such as:

  - Cryptography: The use of encryption, decryption, hashing, digital signatures, or other cryptographic techniques to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity of the data exchanged between the reader and the tag.
  - Authentication protocols: The use of challenge-response, mutual authentication, or other protocols to verify the identity and legitimacy of the reader and the tag before exchanging data or commands.
  - Physical protection: The use of shielding, tamper-resistance, or other physical methods to prevent unauthorized access, cloning, or modification of the tags or the reader.
  - Distance bounding: The use of timing, signal strength, or other methods to measure the distance between the reader and the tag, and to reject any responses that exceed a certain threshold, indicating a relay attack.
  - Anomaly detection: The use of statistical, behavioral, or other methods to monitor the RFID system and detect any abnormal or suspicious activities, such as repeated queries, unusual responses, or inconsistent data.

# YA TRAP

- YA TRAP is a reader/tag authentication protocol for RFID systems .
- It aims to prevent unauthorized readers from accessing tag data and unauthorized tags from impersonating legitimate ones .
- It uses a hash function and a secret key shared between the reader and the tag to generate challenges and responses .
- The protocol consists of four steps :

  1. Reader -> Tag: T_r (a random number generated by the reader)
  2. Tag -> Reader: T_t (a random number generated by the tag)
  3. Tag -> Reader: H_r (a hash value computed by the tag using T_r, T_t and the secret key)
  4. Reader -> Tag: H_t (a hash value computed by the reader using T_r, T_t and the secret key)

- The protocol assumes that the tag can store the secret key and the hash function in its memory, and that the reader can verify the tag's response by comparing H_r and H_t .
- However, the protocol may suffer from false authentications, which means that a legitimate tag could be wrongly rejected by a reader due to various factors, such as tag memory limitations, tag power consumption, tag collisions, reader errors, etc .
- For example, if the tag cannot store the hash function in its memory, it may use a simplified version of it, which may result in different hash values than the reader's .
- Another example is if the tag runs out of power before sending H_r to the reader, the reader may assume that the tag is invalid and reject it .
- False authentications may cause security and privacy risks, such as denial of service, data loss, data leakage, etc .
- Therefore, it is important to design RFID authentication protocols that can prevent or minimize false authentications, and to evaluate their performance and robustness under realistic scenarios .

### Emerging Technologies in Health Information Systems

Health information systems (HIS) are systems that collect, store, process, and communicate health-related data for decision-making and service delivery. HIS can improve the quality, efficiency, and accessibility of health care, as well as empower patients and providers with timely and relevant information. However, HIS also face many challenges, such as interoperability, security, privacy, and ethical issues. Therefore, emerging technologies that can address these challenges and enhance the capabilities of HIS are of great interest and importance.

Some of the emerging technologies in HIS are:

- **Connected health devices**: These are devices that can monitor, measure, or transmit health-related data, such as vital signs, blood glucose, or activity levels. They can be wearable, implantable, or environmental, and can communicate with other devices or systems via wireless networks. Connected health devices can enable remote patient monitoring, personalized care, and self-management, as well as generate big data for analysis and research. Examples of connected health devices are smart watches, glucose meters, pacemakers, and smart inhalers.
- **Digital therapeutics**: These are software-based interventions that can deliver evidence-based treatments or therapies for various health conditions, such as chronic diseases, mental health, or substance abuse. They can be accessed via mobile apps, web platforms, or devices, and can provide feedback, guidance, or support to patients or providers. Digital therapeutics can complement or replace traditional therapies, and can improve adherence, outcomes, and cost-effectiveness. Examples of digital therapeutics are Omada Health, Pear Therapeutics, and Akili Interactive.
- **Artificial intelligence**: This is the technology that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as reasoning, learning, decision-making, or natural language processing. AI can be applied to various aspects of HIS, such as diagnosis, prognosis, treatment, management, research, and education. AI can enhance the accuracy, speed, and scalability of HIS, as well as discover new insights and innovations from large and complex data sets. Examples of AI applications in HIS are IBM Watson Health, Google Health, and Babylon Health .
- **Telehealth**: This is the delivery of health care services or information via telecommunications technologies, such as phone, video, or internet. Telehealth can enable access to health care for remote, rural, or underserved populations, as well as reduce travel time, costs, and risks. Telehealth can also facilitate communication and collaboration among health care providers, patients, and caregivers, as well as support education and training. Examples of telehealth services are Teladoc, Amwell, and Doctor on Demand.
- **Virtual reality**: This is the technology that can create immersive and interactive simulations of real or imagined environments, using devices such as headsets, gloves, or controllers. VR can be used for various purposes in HIS, such as pain management, rehabilitation, therapy, training, or education. VR can provide realistic and engaging experiences that can improve motivation, retention, and outcomes, as well as reduce stress, anxiety, and costs. Examples of VR applications in HIS are AppliedVR, MindMaze, and Osso VR.

These are some of the emerging technologies in HIS that have the potential to transform health care and improve health outcomes. However, they also pose some challenges and risks, such as technical, ethical, legal, and social issues, that need to be addressed and regulated. Therefore, it is important to evaluate the benefits and drawbacks of these technologies, and to adopt them in a responsible and ethical manner.



# GenomicsDriven Wellness Tracking and Management System (GO-WELL)

- GO-WELL is a concept of a health information system that uses genomic variation data, clinical, environmental, and behavioural observations to provide predictive and personalized care for patients .
- GO-WELL aims to engage patients in their own wellness management by providing them with feedback, recommendations, and alerts based on their genomic profile and lifestyle factors .
- GO-WELL consists of four main components: a genomic data repository, a wellness tracking and management engine, a web-based user interface, and a mobile application .
- The genomic data repository stores the genomic variation data of the patients, along with their clinical and demographic information, in a secure and standardized format .
- The wellness tracking and management engine analyzes the genomic data and integrates it with environmental and behavioural data collected from sensors and self-reports, to generate wellness scores, risk assessments, and personalized interventions for each patient .
- The web-based user interface allows the patients and their healthcare providers to access and visualize their genomic and wellness data, as well as to receive and provide feedback and guidance .
- The mobile application enables the patients to monitor their wellness status, receive notifications and reminders, and interact with the system and their healthcare providers anytime and anywhere .
- GO-WELL is designed to be scalable, interoperable, and adaptable to different diseases and populations, as well as to incorporate new genomic and environmental data sources and analytical methods .
- GO-WELL is demonstrated in the context of prostate cancer, a common and complex disease that is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors .
- GO-WELL can help prostate cancer patients to understand their genomic risk, to track their disease progression and response to treatment, and to adopt preventive and therapeutic strategies that are tailored to their individual needs .

### Personalized Medicine

Personalized medicine is an approach to health care that tailors medical treatments and interventions to the individual characteristics, needs, and preferences of each patient. Personalized medicine aims to improve the effectiveness, safety, and quality of health care by delivering the right treatment to the right patient at the right time.

Big data analytics is the process of collecting, storing, processing, and analyzing large and complex datasets to extract meaningful insights and patterns. Big data analytics can enable personalized medicine by:

- Integrating and analyzing multi-omics data (such as genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, etc.) to identify biomarkers, molecular signatures, and genetic variants that are associated with diseases, drug responses, and health outcomes.
- Applying machine learning and data mining algorithms to discover novel associations, causal relationships, and predictive models from multi-omics data and clinical data (such as electronic health records, images, device data, etc.).
- Providing decision support tools and recommendations to clinicians and patients based on the analysis of individual and population data, as well as the latest scientific evidence and guidelines.

Some of the challenges and limitations of big data analytics for personalized medicine are:

- The heterogeneity, complexity, and volume of biomedical data, which require scalable, robust, and interoperable data management and analysis platforms and tools.
- The quality, validity, and reliability of biomedical data, which depend on the data sources, collection methods, preprocessing techniques, and annotation standards.
- The ethical, legal, and social implications of big data analytics for personalized medicine, such as data privacy, security, ownership, consent, and governance, as well as the potential for bias, discrimination, and misuse of data and algorithms.

Big data analytics for personalized medicine is a promising and rapidly evolving field that can revolutionize health care and improve human health and well-being. However, it also poses significant challenges and risks that need to be addressed by multidisciplinary collaboration, innovation, and regulation.

### Social Network Analysis

- Social network analysis (SNA) is the process of investigating social structures through the use of networks and graph theory.
- SNA measures and maps the flow of relationships and relationship changes between knowledge-possessing entities, such as individuals, groups, organizations, or nations.
- SNA is based on theoretical constructs of sociology and mathematical foundations of graph theory.
- SNA is useful for examining the social structure and interdependencies (or work patterns) of individuals or organizations.
- SNA can be applied to various domains, such as health, communication, education, business, politics, and security.
- SNA can help identify influential actors, key connectors, subgroups, communities, and network properties, such as density, centrality, cohesion, and diversity.
- SNA can also help understand how network structure affects the diffusion of information, innovation, behavior, or disease.
- SNA can be performed using various methods, such as surveys, interviews, observations, experiments, or data mining.
- SNA can be visualized using graphs, where nodes represent entities and edges represent relationships.
- SNA can be analyzed using various metrics, such as degree, closeness, betweenness, eigenvector, and PageRank centrality, clustering coefficient, modularity, and assortativity.

### Citizen Actuation
