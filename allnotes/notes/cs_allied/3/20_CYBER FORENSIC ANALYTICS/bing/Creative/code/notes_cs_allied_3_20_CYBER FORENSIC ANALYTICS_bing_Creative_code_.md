

# CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- Cyber forensic analytics is the application of data analysis techniques and tools to investigate cyber incidents and collect digital evidence for legal purposes .
- Cyber forensic analytics involves the following steps:
  - Identification: Identifying the sources and scope of the cyber incident and the potential evidence to be collected.
  - Preservation: Isolating and securing the evidence from tampering, alteration, or destruction.
  - Extraction: Extracting the relevant data from the evidence using forensic tools and techniques.
  - Analysis: Analyzing the data to identify patterns, anomalies, indicators, and attribution of the cyber incident.
  - Presentation: Presenting the findings and conclusions in a clear and concise manner that can be understood by the intended audience and can withstand legal scrutiny.
- Cyber forensic analytics requires the use of various tools and technologies, such as:
  - Forensic software: Software that can perform tasks such as disk imaging, file recovery, data carving, malware analysis, memory analysis, network analysis, etc.
  - Forensic hardware: Hardware that can facilitate the acquisition and examination of evidence, such as write blockers, forensic duplicators, forensic workstations, etc.
  - Forensic standards: Standards that can ensure the reliability, validity, and admissibility of the evidence, such as ISO/IEC 27037, ISO/IEC 27041, ISO/IEC 27042, etc.
- Cyber forensic analytics can be applied to various domains and scenarios, such as  :
  - Cybercrime: Investigating crimes that involve the use of computers, networks, or digital devices, such as hacking, fraud, identity theft, cyberstalking, etc.
  - Cybersecurity: Detecting and preventing cyberattacks that target the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information systems, such as malware, denial-of-service, phishing, ransomware, etc.
  - Cyber intelligence: Gathering and analyzing information about the capabilities, intentions, and activities of cyber adversaries, such as nation-states, terrorists, hackers, etc.
  - Cyber law: Applying and enforcing the legal and ethical principles and regulations that govern the use of cyberspace, such as privacy, intellectual property, cyberwarfare, etc.



## Unit 1 - Cyber Crime

Cyber crime is any criminal activity that involves a computer, a network, or a device connected to the internet. Cyber crime can affect individuals, organizations, or governments, and can cause various types of harm, such as financial loss, identity theft, privacy breach, data corruption, or physical damage.

Some examples of cyber crime are:

- Hacking: unauthorized access to a computer system or network, often with malicious intent.
- Phishing: sending fraudulent emails or messages that appear to be from legitimate sources, in order to trick recipients into revealing personal or financial information, or clicking on malicious links or attachments.
- Ransomware: a type of malware that encrypts the victim's data and demands a ransom for its decryption.
- Cyberstalking: using electronic means to harass, threaten, or intimidate another person, often involving repeated or unwanted contact.
- Cyberterrorism: using cyber attacks to cause fear, panic, or disruption, or to advance a political or ideological agenda.
- Cyberwarfare: using cyber attacks to damage or disrupt the critical infrastructure or military capabilities of another state or entity.

Some of the challenges in combating cyber crime are:

- The global and anonymous nature of the internet, which makes it difficult to trace, identify, and prosecute cyber criminals across jurisdictions and borders.
- The rapid evolution and sophistication of cyber threats, which require constant vigilance and adaptation from law enforcement and security agencies.
- The lack of awareness and education among internet users, who may not take adequate precautions to protect themselves and their devices from cyber attacks.
- The legal and ethical issues involved in balancing the rights and interests of different stakeholders, such as privacy, security, freedom of expression, and intellectual property.

Some of the measures that can be taken to prevent and respond to cyber crime are:

- Implementing strong and updated security systems and protocols, such as firewalls, antivirus software, encryption, and authentication.
- Educating and training internet users and employees on how to recognize and avoid cyber threats, and how to report and recover from cyber incidents.
- Developing and enforcing clear and consistent laws and policies that define and deter cyber crime, and that facilitate cooperation and coordination among different agencies and countries.
- Promoting a culture of cyber resilience and responsibility, that encourages ethical and respectful behavior online, and that fosters trust and cooperation among different stakeholders.

### Cyber Crime

Cyber crime is any illegal activity that involves a computer, a network, or a device connected to the internet. Cyber crime can affect individuals, organizations, or governments, and can cause financial, reputational, or physical harm. Cyber crime can be classified into three main types:

- **A computer is the target of the attack**: This type of cyber crime involves unauthorized access, modification, or destruction of data or systems on a computer or network. For example, a data breach on a corporate network, a ransomware attack that encrypts files on a device, or a cryptojacking attack that uses a computer's resources to mine cryptocurrency without the owner's consent.
- **A computer is the weapon for an attack**: This type of cyber crime involves using a computer or network to launch an attack on another computer or network, or to facilitate other criminal activities. For example, a denial of service (DoS) attack that floods a website with traffic and prevents legitimate users from accessing it, a phishing attack that sends fraudulent emails to trick recipients into revealing sensitive information or downloading malware, or a spoofing attack that impersonates a legitimate entity to deceive or manipulate others.
- **A computer is an accessory to an attack**: This type of cyber crime involves using a computer or network to store, transmit, or process information related to other criminal activities. For example, identity fraud that uses stolen personal information to commit fraud or theft, cyberextortion that demands money to prevent a threatened attack or to release stolen data, or cyberstalking that harasses or threatens a person online.

Some examples of cyber crime are  :

- Email and internet fraud: Sending deceptive or fraudulent emails or messages to trick recipients into sending money, revealing personal or financial information, or downloading malware. For example, a Nigerian prince scam, a lottery scam, or a romance scam.
- Theft of financial or card payment data: Stealing or intercepting credit card or bank account information to make unauthorized transactions or withdrawals. For example, a skimming device, a keylogger, or a man-in-the-middle attack.
- Theft and sale of corporate data: Stealing or leaking confidential or proprietary information from a company or organization to sell it to competitors, hackers, or other parties. For example, a disgruntled employee, a hacker group, or a spy.
- Cyberterrorism: Using cyber attacks to cause fear, panic, or violence for political or ideological purposes. For example, a cyber attack on a critical infrastructure, a government website, or a media outlet.
- Cyberwarfare: Using cyber attacks to disrupt, damage, or destroy the capabilities or assets of an enemy state or group. For example, a cyber attack on a military network, a nuclear facility, or a satellite.
- Child pornography: Producing, distributing, or accessing images or videos of minors engaged in sexual activities or poses. For example, a pedophile, a child porn website, or a peer-to-peer network.
- Intellectual property theft: Stealing or infringing on the rights of the creators or owners of original works, such as music, movies, books, software, or patents. For example, a pirate, a torrent site, or a counterfeit product.

- Cyber crime can be motivated by various factors, such as greed, revenge, curiosity, boredom, ideology, or thrill .

  - **Lack of regulation**: Many countries do not have clear or consistent laws or policies to prevent or prosecute cyber crime, and there is a lack of international cooperation and coordination to combat cyber crime across borders .
  - **Lack of security**: Many systems and networks are vulnerable to cyber attacks due to weak or outdated security measures, such as passwords, encryption, firewalls, antivirus, etc .

### Jurisdictional Concerns

- Jurisdiction is the authority of a state or a court to apply its laws or rules to a person, a territory, or an event.
- Jurisdictional concerns arise in cybercrime cases because cybercrime is non-local in nature, meaning that it can cross geographical and legal boundaries without physical presence or contact.
- Jurisdictional concerns pose challenges for law enforcement agencies and prosecutors in investigating and prosecuting cybercrime, as they need to determine who has the authority and responsibility to deal with the crime, and how to cooperate and coordinate with other jurisdictions involved.
- Jurisdictional concerns also affect the rights and interests of the victims and the suspects of cybercrime, as they may face different legal systems and procedures, and may have difficulties in accessing justice and remedies.
- Jurisdictional concerns can be classified into two types: local cybercrime and transnational cybercrime.
  - Local cybercrime is cybercrime that occurs within the same jurisdiction, where the offender, the victim, and the computer system involved are all located in the same state or territory. Local cybercrime is relatively easier to deal with, as the law enforcement agency and the court of that jurisdiction have the authority and the capacity to investigate and prosecute the crime, and to apply the relevant laws and rules.
  - Transnational cybercrime is cybercrime that involves more than one jurisdiction, where the offender, the victim, or the computer system involved are located in different states or territories. Transnational cybercrime is more complex and challenging to deal with, as it requires the cooperation and coordination of multiple law enforcement agencies and courts across different jurisdictions, and the harmonization and application of different laws and rules.
- Jurisdictional concerns can be addressed by various principles and mechanisms, such as:
  - The principle of territoriality, which grants jurisdiction to the state or the court where the cybercrime occurs or has its effects, regardless of the nationality of the offender or the victim.
  - The principle of nationality, which grants jurisdiction to the state or the court of the nationality of the offender or the victim, regardless of where the cybercrime occurs or has its effects.
  - The principle of universality, which grants jurisdiction to any state or court that has an interest in prosecuting certain cybercrimes that are considered as serious threats to the international community, such as terrorism, child pornography, or human trafficking.
  - The principle of subsidiarity, which grants jurisdiction to the state or the court that is best suited or most capable of prosecuting the cybercrime, based on factors such as the availability of evidence, the efficiency of the process, or the protection of the rights of the parties involved.
  - The principle of comity, which requires the respect and recognition of the jurisdiction and the decisions of other states or courts, unless they are contrary to the public policy or the sovereignty of the state or the court concerned.
  - The principle of mutual legal assistance, which requires the cooperation and assistance of other states or courts in providing evidence, information, or other support for the investigation and prosecution of cybercrime, based on bilateral or multilateral treaties or agreements, or on the basis of reciprocity.
  - The principle of extradition, which requires the surrender of the offender or the suspect of cybercrime to another state or court that has jurisdiction over the crime, based on bilateral or multilateral treaties or agreements, or on the basis of reciprocity.

# Prepaid Cards

Prepaid cards are cards that are loaded with a certain amount of money and can be used for various purposes, such as gift cards, travel cards, payroll cards, etc. Prepaid cards are different from credit cards and debit cards, as they are not linked to a bank account or a line of credit. Prepaid cards can be issued by banks, retailers, or other entities, and can run on the networks of major card companies, such as Visa and Mastercard.

## Prepaid Cards and Cyber Crime

Prepaid cards are often involved in cyber crime, as they can be used to:

- Launder the proceeds of crime or fund terrorist activities, by transferring money from one prepaid card to another, or by using prepaid cards to purchase goods and services that can be resold or exchanged for cash .
- Commit fraud, by using stolen prepaid card information to make purchases, or by buying prepaid cards with stolen payment information. Prepaid cards are harder to trace and recover than regular cards, as they are often not tied to specific identities or accounts .
- Scam victims, by pressuring them to hand over prepaid cards, such as gift cards, as a form of payment for fake services, products, or taxes. Scammers can then redeem the prepaid cards online or sell them to other criminals .

## Prepaid Cards and Cyber Security

Prepaid cards pose a challenge for cyber security, as they have fewer controls and protections than regular cards, such as:

- Less verification and identification requirements, as prepaid cards can be purchased anonymously or with minimal personal information .
- Less fraud detection and prevention systems, as prepaid card issuers and networks may not have the same level of security and monitoring as banks and credit card companies .
- Less consumer protection and dispute resolution options, as prepaid card users may not have the same rights and remedies as credit card or debit card users in case of fraud or theft .

## Prepaid Cards and Cyber Forensic Analytics

Cyber forensic analytics is the process of applying analytical techniques and tools to investigate cyber crime and cyber security incidents. Cyber forensic analytics can be used to:

- Identify and track the sources and destinations of prepaid card transactions, by using data from card issuers, networks, merchants, and law enforcement agencies .
- Analyze and correlate the patterns and behaviors of prepaid card users, by using data mining, machine learning, and artificial intelligence techniques .
- Recover and preserve the evidence of prepaid card fraud or money laundering, by using digital forensics, encryption, and hashing techniques .

### Stored Values Cards for the notes of the Unit 1 - Cyber Crime in the subject of CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- Stored value cards (SVCs) are cards with data encoded in either a magnetic strip or a computer chip that are preloaded with a fixed amount of electronic currency or value.
- SVCs can be classified into three types: closed system, semi-closed system, and open system.
- Closed system cards are issued by a specific merchant or group of merchants and can only be used to purchase goods or services from those merchants. Examples are gift cards, phone cards, and transit cards.
- Semi-closed system cards are issued by a third-party provider and can be used to purchase goods or services from a network of merchants that accept the card. Examples are loyalty cards, campus cards, and payroll cards.
- Open system cards are issued by a financial institution and can be used to purchase goods or services from any merchant that accepts the card network (such as Visa or MasterCard). Examples are prepaid debit cards, travel cards, and remittance cards.
- SVCs offer convenience, security, and flexibility to consumers, merchants, and financial institutions. They can also facilitate financial inclusion for the unbanked and underbanked populations.
- However, SVCs also pose money laundering and cyber security risks, especially the open system and semi-closed system cards that have high loading and card balance value limits and offer anonymity to users.
- Money laundering is the process of concealing the origin, ownership, or destination of illicitly obtained money or other assets. Cyber security is the protection of information systems and networks from unauthorized access, use, modification, or destruction.
- SVCs can be used by organized criminals to launder their illicit proceeds, transfer funds across borders, evade taxes, finance terrorism, or purchase illegal goods or services. They can also be compromised by hackers, fraudsters, or insiders to steal or counterfeit the stored value, access personal or financial information, or disrupt the payment system .
- To mitigate these risks, SVCs are subject to various regulatory measures, such as customer identification and verification, transaction monitoring and reporting, record keeping, and cyber security standards. However, these measures vary across jurisdictions and may not be adequate or consistent to address the evolving threats and challenges posed by SVCs.
- Therefore, there is a need for more cooperation and coordination among law enforcement agencies, financial intelligence units, regulators, and the private sector to enhance the detection, prevention, and prosecution of money laundering and cyber security crimes involving SVCs.



### Mobile Payments

Mobile payments are transactions that use mobile devices such as smartphones, tablets, or smartwatches to initiate, authorize, or confirm the payment of goods or services. Mobile payments can be made through various methods, such as mobile wallets, QR codes, NFC, SMS, or biometrics.

Mobile payments offer convenience, speed, and flexibility for consumers and merchants, as well as new opportunities for innovation and inclusion in the digital economy. However, mobile payments also pose significant challenges and risks for cybersecurity, privacy, and consumer protection.

Some of the cyber threats and crimes that affect mobile payments are:

- Data breaches: Cybercriminals can hack into mobile payment systems or devices and steal sensitive data, such as card numbers, bank account details, personal identification numbers (PINs), or biometric information. Data breaches can result in identity theft, fraud, or blackmail. For example, in 2014, hackers stole the data of 56 million customers from Home Depot, a US retailer that used mobile payment terminals.

- Malware: Malicious software can infect mobile devices or payment applications and compromise their functionality, security, or performance. Malware can also capture, modify, or redirect mobile payment transactions, or send unauthorized payments to fraudsters. For example, in 2019, a malware called EventBot targeted Android devices and stole banking and cryptocurrency information from over 200 mobile payment apps.

- Phishing: Phishing is a form of social engineering that tricks users into revealing their personal or financial information, or clicking on malicious links or attachments, by impersonating legitimate entities or contacts. Phishing can target mobile payment users through emails, text messages, phone calls, or fake websites. For example, in 2020, a phishing campaign used fake SMS messages to lure users into downloading a malicious app that pretended to be a mobile payment service.

- SIM swapping: SIM swapping is a technique that involves transferring a user's phone number to a new SIM card, usually by convincing or bribing a mobile service provider's employee. SIM swapping can allow fraudsters to bypass two-factor authentication (2FA) or receive one-time passwords (OTPs) that are sent to the user's phone number. SIM swapping can enable fraudsters to access the user's mobile payment accounts, bank accounts, or cryptocurrency wallets. For example, in 2018, a SIM swapping ring stole over $2.4 million from cryptocurrency investors in the US.

Some of the best practices and measures that can help protect mobile payments from cyber threats and crimes are:

- Encryption: Encryption is a process that transforms data into an unreadable format that can only be decrypted by authorized parties. Encryption can protect data at rest (stored on devices or servers) or in transit (sent over networks or channels). Encryption can prevent unauthorized access, modification, or interception of mobile payment data. For example, mobile wallets use encryption to store and transmit card information.

- Tokenization: Tokenization is a process that replaces sensitive data, such as card numbers, with a unique and random identifier, called a token. Tokenization can reduce the exposure and impact of data breaches, as tokens are useless to cybercriminals and cannot be traced back to the original data. Tokenization can also enhance the security and privacy of mobile payments, as tokens can be limited to specific devices, merchants, or transactions. For example, Apple Pay uses tokenization to generate a device account number (DAN) for each card added to the mobile wallet.

- Authentication: Authentication is a process that verifies the identity or legitimacy of a user, device, or transaction. Authentication can prevent unauthorized access, use, or fraud of mobile payment accounts or services. Authentication can use various factors, such as passwords, PINs, OTPs, biometrics, or behavioral patterns. Authentication can also be enhanced by using multiple factors (MFA) or adaptive methods that adjust to the risk level of each transaction. For example, Google Pay uses MFA and risk-based authentication to secure mobile payments.

- Education: Education is a process that informs and trains users, merchants, and service providers about the benefits, risks, and best practices of mobile payments. Education can increase the awareness, knowledge, and skills of mobile payment stakeholders, and help them avoid or mitigate cyber threats and crimes. Education can also foster a culture of security and responsibility among mobile payment users and providers. For example, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) provides tips and resources for consumers and businesses on how to protect mobile payments.



# Internet Payment Services

- Internet payment services are methods of transferring money online using electronic devices such as computers, smartphones, or tablets.
- Internet payment services can be used for various purposes, such as online shopping, bill payment, peer-to-peer transfers, donations, subscriptions, etc.
- Internet payment services offer convenience, speed, security, and flexibility to users and merchants, as they can accept and process payments from different sources and currencies, and provide various features and benefits.
- Internet payment services can be classified into different types, such as:

  - **Card-based services**: These services allow users to pay online using their credit, debit, or prepaid cards, which are linked to their bank accounts or other financial institutions. Examples of card-based services are Visa, Mastercard, American Express, etc.
  - **Bank-based services**: These services allow users to pay online using their bank accounts, either by logging into their online banking platforms or by authorizing direct transfers. Examples of bank-based services are Net Banking, NEFT, ECS/NECS, IMPS, etc.

Users can fund their wallets using various methods, such as cards, bank transfers, cash, etc. Examples of wallet-based services are PayPal, Amazon Pay, Google Pay, etc.
  - **Platform-based services**: These services allow users to pay online using their accounts on specific platforms, such as e-commerce sites, social media networks, or online marketplaces. These platforms act as intermediaries between users and merchants, and provide payment processing and security features. Examples of platform-based services are Stripe, Braintree, Shopify, etc.



### Cyber stalking

Cyber stalking is a form of online harassment that involves the use of electronic communications to repeatedly target, intimidate, threaten, or monitor a person or a group. Cyber stalking can have serious consequences for the victims, such as psychological distress, fear, loss of privacy, damage to reputation, or physical harm. Cyber stalking is a criminal offense in many countries and can result in legal action against the perpetrators.

Some of the main points to note about cyber stalking are:

- Cyber stalking can take many forms, such as sending unwanted messages, emails, or calls, posting abusive or false information, spreading rumors, impersonating the victim, hacking into the victim's accounts, tracking the victim's location or activities, or soliciting personal or sexual information from the victim or others.
- Cyber stalking can be motivated by various reasons, such as anger, revenge, control, obsession, jealousy, or sexual interest. Cyber stalkers may have a personal or professional relationship with the victim, or may be strangers who randomly select their targets.
- Cyber stalking can affect anyone, regardless of age, gender, race, or background. However, some groups may be more vulnerable to cyber stalking, such as women, children, celebrities, activists, journalists, or online influencers.
- Cyber stalking can be prevented or stopped by taking some measures, such as reporting the harassment to the authorities, blocking or ignoring the cyber stalker, changing or securing the online accounts and passwords, limiting the personal information shared online, seeking support from friends, family, or professionals, and collecting evidence of the cyber stalking.



# Cyber extortion

Cyber extortion is a form of cybercrime in which hackers or cybercriminals demand money or something else of value from a victim in exchange for not carrying out a threat to harm their data, systems, or reputation. Some common types of cyber extortion are:

- **Ransomware**: A malware that encrypts the victim's files or locks their device and demands a ransom for the decryption key or the unlock code. Ransomware can affect individuals, businesses, or public institutions and can cause significant losses of data, productivity, and revenue. Some examples of ransomware are CryptoLocker, WannaCry, and Ryuk.
- **Distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks**: A cyberattack that floods the victim's website or network with a large amount of traffic, making it slow or unavailable for legitimate users. DDoS attackers may demand a payment from the victim to stop the attack or to prevent future attacks. DDoS attacks can disrupt online services, damage reputation, and incur costs for mitigation and recovery. Some examples of DDoS attacks are Mirai, Dyn, and GitHub.
- **Email ransom campaigns**: A phishing scheme that sends emails to a large number of recipients, claiming to have compromised their devices or accounts and threatening to expose their sensitive information, such as passwords, photos, or videos, unless they pay a ransom. Email ransom campaigns rely on scare tactics and social engineering to extort money from unsuspecting victims. Some examples of email ransom campaigns are Sextortion, Bomb Threat, and COVID-19 Scam.
- **Data breach extortion**: A cyberattack that steals the victim's confidential or personal data and demands a payment for not disclosing, deleting, or selling the data to third parties. Data breach extortion can affect individuals or organizations and can result in identity theft, fraud, blackmail, or legal liability. Some examples of data breach extortion are Ashley Madison, Sony Pictures, and Colonial Pipeline.

Cyber extortion is a serious and growing threat that can affect anyone who uses the internet. To protect yourself from cyber extortion, you should:

- Use strong and unique passwords for your accounts and devices and change them regularly.
- Install and update antivirus, firewall, and anti-malware software on your devices and scan them regularly.
- Avoid opening suspicious or unsolicited emails or attachments and do not click on unknown links or pop-ups.
- Backup your important data regularly and store it in a secure location, such as an external hard drive or a cloud service.
- Report any cyber extortion attempts to the relevant authorities, such as the police, the FBI, or the FTC, and do not pay the ransom or comply with the demands.



### Cyber terrorism

- Cyber terrorism means the **politically motivated use of computers and information technology to cause severe disruption or widespread fear in society**.
- Cyber terrorism can target **vital computer systems** that control critical infrastructure, such as power grids, transportation networks, communication systems, etc.
- Cyber terrorism can also aim to **damage or disrupt** the online presence or reputation of a government, organization, or individual, by spreading false or harmful information, hacking websites, stealing data, etc.
- Cyber terrorism can result in, or threaten, the **loss of life or significant bodily harm**, as well as economic, social, or psychological damage.
- Cyber terrorism can be carried out by **subnational groups or clandestine agents** who have ideological, religious, or political agendas .
- Cyber terrorism can be **premeditated** or opportunistic, depending on the availability and vulnerability of the targets.
- Cyber terrorism can be **challenging to prevent, detect, and respond to**, due to the anonymity, global reach, and complexity of the cyber domain .



### Cyber warfare

- Cyber warfare means the use of computer technology to disrupt the activities of a state or organization, especially the deliberate attacking of information systems for strategic or military purposes.

# ATM Frauds

ATM frauds are a type of cyber crime that involves stealing or compromising the debit card or personal identification number (PIN) of the users of automated teller machines (ATMs). ATM frauds can result in financial losses, identity theft, and other criminal activities. Some of the common types of ATM frauds are:

- **Skimming**: This is the process of installing a counterfeit device on the card reader or the keypad of the ATM that can capture the data on the card's magnetic strip and the user's PIN. The device can be a small camera, a fake overlay, or a wireless transmitter. The criminals can then use the stolen data to clone the card or make online purchases .
- **Shimming**: This is similar to skimming, but the device is inserted inside the card slot of the ATM, where it can intercept the data from the chip of the card. The device can be a thin metal or plastic shim with a microchip and flash storage. The criminals can then retrieve the device and use the data to create a magnetic strip version of the card.
- **Card trapping**: This is the process of inserting a device or a mechanism into the card slot of the ATM that can prevent the card from being ejected after the transaction. The device can be a metal loop, a spring, or a plastic sleeve. The criminals can then wait for the user to leave the ATM and retrieve the card, or they can distract the user and take the card while they are still at the ATM.
- **Cash trapping**: This is the process of attaching a device or a mechanism to the cash dispenser of the ATM that can prevent the cash from being dispensed or retrieved by the user. The device can be a metal plate, a lever, or a claw. The criminals can then wait for the user to leave the ATM and collect the cash, or they can distract the user and take the cash while they are still at the ATM.
- **PIN cracking**: This is the process of using software or hardware tools to guess or bypass the PIN of the user. The tools can be cracking programs, brute force attacks, or keyloggers. The criminals can then use the PIN to access the user's account or make unauthorized transactions.
- **Phishing**: This is the process of sending fraudulent emails, text messages, or phone calls to the user that claim to be from the bank or the ATM provider and ask for the user's card or account details, PIN, or security codes. The criminals can then use the information to access the user's account or make unauthorized transactions.
- **Bitcoin ATMs**: This is the process of using bitcoin ATMs, which are machines that allow users to buy or sell bitcoins using cash or debit cards, for criminal purposes. The criminals can use the bitcoin ATMs to launder money, evade taxes, or conduct illegal transactions, such as drug trafficking or fraud. The criminals can also target the users of the bitcoin ATMs by installing skimming devices, trapping devices, or hacking the machines.



### Phreaking

- Phreaking is a slang term coined to describe the activity of a culture of people who study, experiment with, or explore telecommunication systems, such as equipment and systems connected to public telephone networks.
- Phreaking involves fraudulent manipulation of telephone signaling in order to make free phone calls or to access restricted services.
- Phreaking originated in the 1960s and 1970s, when phreaks used devices such as whistles or electronic boxes to generate specific tones that could trigger the automatic switches of the phone network.
- Phreaking was also motivated by curiosity, exploration, and the challenge of understanding and manipulating complex systems.
- Phreaking techniques include:
  - Blue boxing: using a device that produces a 2600 Hz tone to seize control of a trunk line and route calls anywhere in the world.
  - Red boxing: using a device that simulates the tones of coins being inserted into a payphone to make free calls.
  - Black boxing: using a device that prevents the phone line from disconnecting after the called party hangs up, allowing the caller to make another call without paying.
  - Beige boxing: using a device that connects to a phone line outside a building and allows the user to make or receive calls from that line.
  - Green boxing: using a device that generates the tones used by operators to control certain functions of the phone network, such as conference calls or call forwarding.
- Phreaking is considered a form of cybercrime, as it violates the laws and regulations of the telecommunication providers and may cause financial losses or security breaches.
- Phreaking is also related to other forms of hacking, such as social engineering, spoofing, or wiretapping.
- Phreaking has become less common and less effective in the modern era, as the phone network has switched from analog to digital technology and implemented more security measures.
- Phreaking is still practiced by some hackers, hobbyists, or activists, who use it for various purposes, such as exploration, education, protest, or prank.

- Cyber forensics requires various skills and knowledge, such as:

### Disk imaging

- Disk imaging is the process of creating an exact copy of a storage device, such as a hard drive, SSD, CD, DVD, or USB flash drive.
- Disk imaging is used for various purposes, such as backup, recovery, forensic analysis, data migration, or duplication.
- Disk imaging can be done at different levels of abstraction, such as physical, logical, or file system level.
- Physical disk imaging creates a bit-by-bit copy of the entire storage device, including the unused and deleted sectors, the partition table, the boot sector, and the metadata. Physical disk imaging is useful for preserving the original state of the device and recovering deleted or corrupted data.
- Logical disk imaging creates a copy of the data stored in a specific partition or volume, without the unused or deleted sectors, the partition table, the boot sector, or the metadata. Logical disk imaging is useful for reducing the size of the image and focusing on the relevant data.
- File system disk imaging creates a copy of the files and folders stored in a specific partition or volume, without the unused or deleted sectors, the partition table, the boot sector, the metadata, or the file system structure. File system disk imaging is useful for extracting the data in a readable format and transferring it to a different file system.
- Disk imaging can be performed using various tools, such as dd, FTK Imager, EnCase, or Clonezilla. Some tools can create compressed, encrypted, or split images, or verify the integrity of the images using hash values. Some tools can also mount the images as virtual drives, or restore the images to a new device.



### Data Carving

Data carving is a technique used in cyber forensics to recover data from digital media without relying on the file system information. It is useful for finding hidden or deleted files that may contain evidence or clues for an investigation. Data carving can also recover data from damaged or corrupted media that cannot be accessed by normal means.

Some key points about data carving are:

- Data carving works by scanning the raw data blocks of the media and looking for patterns or signatures that indicate the start and end of a file. For example, a JPEG file usually starts with the bytes FF D8 and ends with FF D9, so a data carving tool can search for these bytes and extract the data between them as a JPEG file.
- Data carving can recover files from areas that are not allocated to any file system, such as unallocated clusters, slack space, lost clusters, or bad sectors. These areas may contain remnants of previous files that were deleted, overwritten, or fragmented.
- Data carving can also recover files from encrypted or compressed media, as long as the encryption or compression algorithm does not alter the file signatures. However, data carving cannot recover files that are encrypted or compressed at the file level, such as ZIP or RAR files, because they do not have recognizable signatures.
- Data carving requires a lot of processing power and storage space, as it has to scan the entire media and extract every possible file. It may also produce a lot of false positives or incomplete files, as some data blocks may match the file signatures by coincidence or may belong to different files. Therefore, data carving results need to be verified and filtered manually or by using additional tools.
- Data carving can be performed using various tools, such as Foremost, Scalpel, PhotoRec, or Autopsy. These tools have different features and capabilities, such as supporting different file types, allowing custom signatures, or providing graphical user interfaces. Some tools can also perform file carving based on file structure or content, rather than signatures, which can improve the accuracy and completeness of the recovery.

### Steganography

Steganography is the art or practice of concealing a message, image, or file within another message, image, or file  . It is an encryption technique that can be used along with cryptography as an extra-secure method in which to protect data. Steganography techniques can be applied to images, a video file or an audio file. Typically, however, steganography is written in characters including hash marking, but its usage within images is also common.

Some points to note about steganography are:

- Steganography is different from cryptography, which is the practice of making the message itself unreadable by using mathematical techniques. Steganography hides the existence of the message, while cryptography scrambles the message so that it cannot be understood.
- Steganography can be used for various purposes, such as hiding secret information, watermarking digital media, authenticating messages, or evading censorship or detection. It can also be used for malicious purposes, such as spreading malware, stealing data, or conducting cyber attacks.
- Steganography can be classified into different types based on the cover medium, the embedding method, or the extraction method. Some common types of steganography are:

  - Text steganography: hiding information within text files, such as using whitespace, invisible characters, or special formatting.
  - Image steganography: hiding information within image files, such as using the least significant bits, color palettes, or compression techniques.
  - Audio steganography: hiding information within audio files, such as using low-frequency sounds, phase coding, or echo hiding.
  - Video steganography: hiding information within video files, such as using frame insertion, motion vectors, or scene changes.
  - Network steganography: hiding information within network protocols, such as using packet headers, timing delays, or covert channels.

- Steganography can be detected and analyzed by using various techniques, such as statistical analysis, visual inspection, or steganalysis tools. Steganalysis is the science of discovering and revealing hidden information within steganographic data. Some examples of steganalysis tools are StegExpose, StegDetect, or StegSecret.

### Port scans

- A port scan is a technique used by cybercriminals to find out information about a system they are going to target .
- It involves scanning through a network system and determining which ports are open, which are sending data, and which are receiving data .
- A port is a logical endpoint of communication in a network, identified by a number from 0 to 65535.
- A port scan attack helps cyber criminals find open ports and figure out whether they are receiving or sending data. It can also reveal whether active security devices like firewalls are being used by an organization.
- Port scanning is a fundamental part of the pre-attack phase of a penetration test, where ethical hackers and penetration testers use port scanning techniques to locate vulnerabilities in a network.
- There are several different port scanning or checking techniques, including :
  - Ping scans: A ping is used to check whether a network data packet can reach an IP address without any issues. Ping scans can be used to discover which hosts are alive on a network.
  - Half-open or SYNC scans: Attackers can check the state of a port without creating a full connection, by sending a SYN packet and waiting for a response. If the response is a SYN-ACK packet, the port is open. If the response is a RST packet, the port is closed.
  - Connect scans: Attackers can establish a full TCP connection with each port and then close it. This is a reliable but noisy method that can be easily detected by firewalls and intrusion detection systems.
  - UDP scans: Attackers can send UDP packets to each port and wait for a response. If the response is an ICMP port unreachable message, the port is closed. If there is no response, the port is either open or filtered by a firewall.
  - Stealth scans: Attackers can use various techniques to avoid detection by firewalls and intrusion detection systems, such as fragmenting packets, using decoy addresses, or changing the source port.
- Port scanning can be detected by various methods, such as:
  - Monitoring network traffic and looking for anomalies, such as a large number of packets sent to different ports or hosts, or packets with unusual flags or options.
  - Using firewalls and intrusion detection systems to block or alert on suspicious packets or connections.
  - Implementing honeypots or honeynets, which are decoy systems or networks that attract and trap attackers, and allow for analysis and investigation of their activities.
- Port scanning can be prevented or mitigated by various methods, such as  :
  - Closing or disabling unnecessary ports and services on the network devices .
  - Using strong passwords and encryption for the ports and services that are open .
  - Configuring firewalls and intrusion detection systems to filter or block unwanted or malicious traffic  .
  - Updating and patching the network devices and applications regularly to fix any vulnerabilities .
  - Educating and training the network users and administrators on the risks and best practices of port scanning and security .

- Botnets can be used for various malicious purposes, such as spamming, distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, stealing sensitive information, or hosting illegal content.
- Botnet forensics is the science that determines the scope of the breach and applies the methodology to find out the type of the infection .

- Botnet forensics can provide valuable information for law enforcement, security analysts, or network administrators to prevent, detect, or mitigate botnet attacks .

### DDoS Attacks

- A DDoS attack is a distributed denial-of-service attack that targets websites and servers by disrupting network services .
- A DDoS attack attempts to exhaust an application’s resources by flooding it with HTTP requests and traffic from multiple sources, such as compromised computers or devices .
- A DDoS attack can result in degraded network performance, service outage, or loss of data and revenue for the targeted site or service .
- Some common techniques of DDoS attacks are:
  - SYN flood: The attacker sends a large number of TCP SYN packets to the target, but does not complete the handshake, leaving the target with many half-open connections that consume resources.
  - UDP flood: The attacker sends a large number of UDP packets to random ports on the target, causing the target to respond with ICMP packets that consume bandwidth and processing power.
  - HTTP flood: The attacker sends a large number of HTTP requests to the target, often using a botnet or a web application, that overwhelm the target's web server or application layer.
  - Amplification: The attacker sends a small number of packets with a spoofed source IP address to a third-party server that responds with a larger amount of data to the target, amplifying the attack traffic.
  - Reflection: The attacker sends a small number of packets with a spoofed source IP address to a third-party server that responds with a packet that contains the same data as the request, reflecting the attack traffic.
- Some common types of DDoS attacks are:
  - Volumetric: The attacker tries to consume the bandwidth of the target or its surrounding network by sending a large amount of traffic, such as UDP or ICMP packets .
  - Protocol: The attacker tries to consume the resources of the target or its intermediate network devices, such as routers or firewalls, by exploiting weaknesses in the network protocols, such as TCP or IP .
  - Application layer: The attacker tries to consume the resources of the target's web server or application layer, such as CPU or memory, by sending a large number of requests that require complex processing, such as HTTP or DNS queries .
- Some common motivations of DDoS attacks are:
  - Cybercrime: The attacker tries to extort money from the target by threatening or launching a DDoS attack, or to disrupt the operations of a competitor or rival .
  - Hacktivism: The attacker tries to protest or promote a political or social cause by launching a DDoS attack against a target that represents an opposing view or interest .
  - Cyberwarfare: The attacker tries to damage or disable the critical infrastructure or services of a nation or organization by launching a DDoS attack as part of a larger cyberattack or conflict .
  - Cyber vandalism: The attacker tries to cause mischief or annoyance by launching a DDoS attack against a target for fun or fame .



### Honey Pots

- A honey pot is a **cybersecurity mechanism** that uses a **manufactured attack target** to lure cybercriminals away from legitimate targets, and also gathers intelligence about the identity, methods and motivations of adversaries .
- A honey pot can be modeled after any digital asset, including software applications, servers or the network itself .
- Honeypots are network-attached systems intended to **mimic likely targets** of cyber attacks, such as vulnerable networks.
- Honeypots can be used to **attract, detect, and deflect** cybercriminals from hacking into legitimate targets.
- Honeypots can also serve **forensic purposes**, quietly gathering data about the hackers who stole it.
- Honeypots are strategically built to **hoax the cyber criminal** into believing they have found a way to alter rights and steal authorizations.
- When the trap is triggered, an alarm is sent to a **central deception server** that takes note of the affected decoy and the attack vectors that were used by the cyber criminal.
- Honeypots can be classified into **research honeypots** and **production honeypots** based on their purpose and deployment .
- Research honeypots are used to **collect information** about the techniques and strategies of cyber attackers, and are usually deployed by security researchers or organizations .
- Production honeypots are used to **enhance the security** of a network or system, and are usually deployed by businesses or government agencies .
- Honeypots can also be classified into **low-interaction honeypots** and **high-interaction honeypots** based on their level of complexity and involvement .
- Low-interaction honeypots are **simple and easy** to deploy and maintain, but they only provide limited information and interaction to the attackers .
- High-interaction honeypots are **complex and realistic** to deploy and maintain, but they provide detailed information and interaction to the attackers, and can also capture malware and exploits .
- Honeypots have several **advantages** such as:
  - They can **distract** the attackers from the real targets and waste their time and resources .
  - They can **detect** the attacks that are not caught by other security tools and alert the defenders .
  - They can **defend** the network or system by slowing down or stopping the attackers .
  - They can **collect** valuable information about the attackers' identity, methods, and motivations, and help improve the security measures .
- Honeypots also have some **disadvantages** such as:
  - They can **attract** unwanted attention and increase the risk of being attacked .
  - They can **mislead** the defenders by generating false positives or negatives, or by being compromised by the attackers .
  - They can **violate** the legal or ethical issues of privacy, entrapment, or liability, depending on the jurisdiction and the context .



# Malware

Malware is a term that refers to any software that is designed to harm, disrupt, or compromise a computer system or network. Malware can have various objectives, such as stealing data, extorting money, displaying unwanted ads, spying on user activity, or destroying files. Malware can also be used to facilitate cyberattacks, such as denial-of-service, phishing, or ransomware.

Some of the common types of malware are:

- **Virus**: A program that infects other software and replicates itself, spreading from one computer to another. A virus can corrupt or delete files, consume system resources, or display unwanted messages. A virus needs a host file to execute its code and infect other files .
- **Worm**: A program that replicates itself and spreads over a network, without the need for a host file. A worm can consume bandwidth, create backdoors, or install other malware on infected devices. A worm can also create a network of compromised devices, called a botnet, that can be used for malicious purposes .
- **Trojan**: A program that appears to be legitimate but contains hidden malicious functionality. A trojan can create a backdoor, steal data, install other malware, or perform other harmful actions. A trojan does not replicate itself, but relies on user interaction or social engineering to trick users into running it .
- **Ransomware**: A program that encrypts the user's data and demands a ransom for the decryption key. Ransomware can also threaten to delete or expose the data if the ransom is not paid. Ransomware can be delivered by other malware, such as trojans or worms, or by exploiting vulnerabilities in the system .
- **Bots or botnets**: A program that allows a remote attacker to control a compromised device, or a network of such devices. Bots or botnets can be used to perform various malicious activities, such as sending spam, launching denial-of-service attacks, stealing data, or mining cryptocurrency. Bots or botnets can be created by worms, trojans, or other malware .
- **Adware**: A program that displays unwanted or intrusive advertisements on the user's device. Adware can also redirect the user's browser, modify the user's settings, or collect the user's browsing data. Adware can be installed by other malware, such as trojans or worms, or by bundling with legitimate software .
- **Spyware**: A program that monitors and collects the user's personal information, such as keystrokes, passwords, browsing history, or location. Spyware can also modify the user's settings, redirect the user's browser, or display unwanted ads. Spyware can be installed by other malware, such as trojans or worms, or by phishing or social engineering .
- **Rootkit**: A program that hides itself and other malware from detection and removal. A rootkit can also modify the system's files, processes, or registry, or intercept the system's calls. A rootkit can be installed by other malware, such as trojans or worms, or by exploiting vulnerabilities in the system .
- **Fileless malware**: A program that does not create or modify any files on the disk, but resides in the memory or other system components. Fileless malware can evade traditional antivirus software and leave no traces on the system. Fileless malware can be delivered by other malware, such as trojans or worms, or by exploiting vulnerabilities in the system .
- **Malvertising**: A technique that uses malicious advertisements to deliver malware to the user's device. Malvertising can exploit vulnerabilities in the browser, the ad network, or the website to execute malicious code or redirect the user to a malicious website. Malvertising can be used to install various types of malware, such as ransomware, spyware, or trojans .



### Virus and Worms

- A virus is a malicious program that attaches itself to another program or file and executes when the host is run. A virus can infect other files or programs on the same system or on other systems through removable media, network connections, or email attachments. A virus can harm the system's data or performance by deleting, modifying, encrypting, or corrupting files, displaying unwanted messages, consuming resources, or opening backdoors for remote access. A virus requires human action to replicate and spread.   

- A worm is a malicious program that replicates itself without attaching to another program or file. A worm can spread through a computer network by exploiting vulnerabilities in software or protocols, or by using social engineering techniques to trick users into opening malicious attachments or links. A worm can harm the system's data or performance by consuming bandwidth, disk space, or CPU, deleting or modifying files, installing backdoors or other malware, or launching denial-of-service attacks. A worm can automatically replicate and spread without human action.    

- Some examples of virus and worm attacks are:

  - The ILOVEYOU virus was a Visual Basic script that was sent as an email attachment with the subject "ILOVEYOU" in 2000. It infected millions of computers worldwide by overwriting files, stealing passwords, and sending copies of itself to all the contacts in the user's address book. 

  - The Conficker worm was a Windows-based worm that was first detected in 2008. It infected millions of computers worldwide by exploiting a vulnerability in the Windows Server service, using dictionary attacks to guess weak passwords, and copying itself to removable media. It created a botnet that could be used for various malicious purposes, such as stealing data, installing ransomware, or launching denial-of-service attacks. 

  - The Stuxnet worm was a sophisticated cyberweapon that was designed to target the Iranian nuclear program in 2010. It infected Windows-based computers by using four zero-day exploits, spreading through USB drives, and using stolen digital certificates. It then targeted the programmable logic controllers that controlled the centrifuges used for uranium enrichment, causing them to malfunction and damage the equipment.

Cyber forensics can use various tools and techniques, such as antivirus software, malware analysis, network traffic analysis, file system analysis, memory analysis, or reverse engineering, to examine the infected systems and recover the relevant data. ## Unit 3 - Cyber Investigation

- Cyber investigation is the process of collecting, analyzing, and preserving digital evidence from various sources, such as computers, networks, mobile devices, cloud services, and social media platforms.

- Cyber investigation requires a combination of technical skills, legal knowledge, and ethical principles, as well as the ability to communicate effectively with various stakeholders, such as law enforcement, lawyers, judges, clients, and the public.
- Cyber investigation follows a general framework that consists of four main phases: preparation, collection, examination, and reporting.

### Preparation
- Preparation is the phase where the cyber investigator defines the scope, objectives, and methodology of the investigation, as well as the legal and ethical issues involved.
- Preparation involves identifying the sources of potential evidence, the tools and techniques to be used, the personnel and resources required, and the risks and challenges to be anticipated.

### Collection
- Collection is the phase where the cyber investigator acquires the digital evidence from the identified sources, using appropriate tools and techniques, and following the best practices and standards.
- Collection involves creating a forensic image or a copy of the original data, without altering or damaging it, and verifying its authenticity and integrity using cryptographic hashes or digital signatures.
- Collection also involves documenting the chain of custody, which is the record of who handled the evidence, when, where, how, and why, as well as the evidence inventory, which is the list of all the evidence items and their characteristics.

- Examination is the phase where the cyber investigator analyzes the digital evidence, using various tools and techniques, and following the relevant laws and policies.

- Reporting involves organizing, summarizing, and visualizing the evidence and the analysis, using tables, charts, graphs, timelines, maps, etc., and highlighting the key facts, issues, and recommendations.
- Reporting also involves writing a clear, concise, and accurate report, using proper language, style, and structure, and providing the necessary references, citations, and appendices.

### Investigating Web Attacks

Web attacks are malicious activities that target web applications, servers, or users through the internet. Web attacks can compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of web resources, data, or services. Web attacks can also cause financial losses, reputational damage, or legal consequences for the victims.

Some common types of web attacks are:

- Cross-site scripting (XSS): Injecting malicious code into web pages that execute in the browser of the visitors, allowing the attacker to steal cookies, session tokens, or other sensitive information, or perform actions on behalf of the users.
- SQL injection (SQLi): Injecting malicious SQL queries into web applications that access databases, allowing the attacker to read, modify, or delete data, or execute commands on the server.
- Cross-site request forgery (CSRF): Forging requests that are sent from the browser of a legitimate user to a web application, allowing the attacker to perform unauthorized actions on behalf of the user.
- Distributed denial-of-service (DDoS): Flooding a web server or application with a large number of requests, overwhelming its resources and preventing it from serving legitimate requests.
- Ransomware: Encrypting the files or data of a web server or application, and demanding a ransom for their decryption.

Web attack forensics is the process of collecting, analyzing, and investigating evidence related to web attacks, in order to identify the origin, method, motive, and impact of the attack, and to prevent or mitigate future attacks. Web attack forensics involves the following steps:

- Preservation: Securing the web environment and preserving the evidence in a forensically sound manner, without altering or destroying any data.
- Acquisition: Extracting the evidence from the web environment, such as web logs, configuration files, web content, database files, network traffic, or malware samples.
- Analysis: Examining the evidence using various tools and techniques, such as log analysis, network analysis, malware analysis, or code analysis, to determine the nature, scope, and timeline of the attack, and to identify the attacker's IP address, location, identity, or tools.
- Reporting: Documenting the findings and conclusions of the analysis, and presenting them in a clear, concise, and accurate manner, using charts, graphs, tables, or screenshots, to support the investigation or prosecution of the attack.

Web attack forensics requires a combination of technical skills, analytical skills, and legal knowledge, as well as a familiarity with the web environment and the web attack techniques. Web attack forensics can help web administrators, security professionals, law enforcement agencies, or forensic experts to detect, respond, and recover from web attacks, and to enhance the security and resilience of web applications and systems.

# Cyber Criminal Profiling

- Cyber criminal profiling is the process of identifying or determining the real identity of individual attackers or an attacker group involved in cyber crimes by analyzing their characteristics, their tools and their relationships .
- Cyber criminal profiling can help in deterring cyber crimes, enhancing cyber security, supporting cyber investigations and prosecutions, and improving cyber threat intelligence.
- Cyber criminal profiling can be based on different types of data, such as:
  - Technical data: the digital traces left by the attackers on the network, such as IP addresses, malware signatures, attack patterns, etc.
  - Behavioral data: the actions, motivations, preferences, and personality traits of the attackers, such as their targets, goals, methods, skills, etc.
  - Social data: the relationships, affiliations, and communication patterns of the attackers, such as their groups, networks, forums, etc.

### Evidence Management

Evidence management is the process of ensuring that the digital evidence collected from a cybercrime scene is properly preserved, documented, analyzed and presented in a way that is admissible and reliable in a court of law. Evidence management involves the following steps:

- **Identification**: The first step is to identify the potential sources and types of digital evidence that are relevant to the cybercrime investigation. This may include computers, mobile devices, network devices, cloud services, social media platforms, etc. The investigator should also classify the cybercrime according to its nature, such as identity theft, phishing, hacking, etc.
- **Preservation**: The second step is to preserve the digital evidence in its original state, without altering, deleting or modifying it in any way. This may involve creating a forensic image or a bit-by-bit copy of the original data storage device, using write blockers or other tools to prevent accidental or intentional changes, and securing the evidence in a safe and controlled environment. The investigator should also document the details of the evidence, such as its location, date, time, serial number, etc.
- **Analysis**: The third step is to analyze the digital evidence using various forensic tools and techniques to extract, recover, interpret and correlate the relevant information. This may involve examining the file system, metadata, deleted files, hidden files, encryption, passwords, logs, network traffic, etc. The investigator should also use analytical methods, such as timeline analysis, link analysis, keyword search, hash analysis, etc. to identify patterns, anomalies, connections and evidence of malicious activity.
- **Presentation**: The fourth step is to present the digital evidence in a clear, concise and accurate manner that can support the findings and conclusions of the investigation. This may involve preparing a forensic report, a summary, a timeline, a chart, a graph, a table, etc. that can illustrate the evidence and its significance. The investigator should also be prepared to testify as an expert witness in court and explain the evidence and the methods used to obtain and analyze it.

Evidence management is a critical aspect of cyber forensic analytics, as it ensures that the digital evidence is reliable, valid and admissible in legal proceedings. Evidence management also helps to maintain the integrity, authenticity and chain of custody of the digital evidence, which are essential for establishing its credibility and trustworthiness. Evidence management also helps to protect the rights and privacy of the parties involved in the cybercrime investigation, as well as the public interest and the rule of law.

### Information Technology Act 2000

The Information Technology Act 2000 (ITA-2000) is an Act of the Indian Parliament that was enacted on 17 October 2000 and came into force on 17 October 2000. It is the primary law in India dealing with cybercrime and electronic commerce. It is based on the United Nations Model Law on Electronic Commerce 1996. The main objectives of the ITA-2000 are:

- To provide legal recognition for transactions carried out by means of electronic data interchange and other means of electronic communication, commonly referred to as "electronic commerce".
- To facilitate electronic filing of documents with the Government agencies and further to amend the Indian Penal Code, the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891 and the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto.
- To provide for the constitution of the Cyber Regulations Advisory Committee and the Cyber Appellate Tribunal.
- To define various terms and concepts related to information technology, such as digital signature, electronic record, computer, computer network, computer resource, computer system, data, information, secure system, etc.
- To prescribe various offences and penalties for cybercrimes, such as hacking, tampering, publishing obscene information, breach of confidentiality, cyber terrorism, etc.
- To provide for the appointment of adjudicating officers and the procedure for adjudication of disputes.
- To provide for the establishment of the Controller of Certifying Authorities and the recognition of foreign certifying authorities.
- To provide for the regulation of certifying authorities and the issuance of digital signature certificates.
- To provide for the duties of subscribers and intermediaries in relation to electronic records and digital signatures.
- To provide for the preservation and retention of electronic records and audit of certifying authorities.
- To provide for the exemption from liability of intermediaries in certain cases.
- To provide for the powers of the Controller, the adjudicating officers and the Cyber Appellate Tribunal to access any computer, computer system or computer network, issue directions, call for information, etc.
- To provide for the protection of personal data and privacy of individuals in relation to electronic records and digital signatures.
- To provide for the amendment of the Indian Penal Code, the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891 and the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 to make them compatible with the ITA-2000.
- To provide for the repeal of the Information Technology Act, 1996.

The ITA-2000 has been amended by the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008 and the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2011 to incorporate new provisions and update existing ones. Some of the major changes introduced by the amendments are:

- The substitution of the term "digital signature" with "electronic signature" and the introduction of various types of electronic signatures, such as asymmetric crypto system, hash function, electronic signature certificate, etc.
- The inclusion of new offences and penalties, such as identity theft, cheating by personation, violation of privacy, cyber stalking, phishing, spamming, etc.
- The enhancement of the punishment for some existing offences, such as hacking, publishing obscene information, cyber terrorism, etc.
- The expansion of the scope of intermediaries to include telecom service providers, network service providers, internet service providers, web hosting service providers, search engines, online payment sites, online auction sites, online market places and cyber cafes.
- The introduction of the concept of "reasonable security practices and procedures" and "due diligence" for intermediaries and body corporates to prevent and deal with cyber incidents.
- The introduction of the concept of "sensitive personal data or information" and the obligation of body corporates to obtain consent, provide notice and ensure security of such data or information.
- The introduction of the concept of "cyber security" and the obligation of the Central Government to prescribe security practices and procedures for the protection of critical information infrastructure.
- The introduction of the concept of "cyber cafe" and the obligation of the owner or person in charge of a cyber cafe to maintain a register of the identity and address of the users and the computer resource used by them.
- The introduction of the concept of "protected system" and the prohibition of unauthorized access or attempt to access such system.
- The introduction of the concept of "communication device" and the inclusion of such device in the definition of "computer".

### Future Trends and Emerging Concerns for Cyber Laws

Cyber laws are the legal frameworks that govern the use, protection, and regulation of digital technologies, such as the internet, computers, mobile devices, cloud services, and artificial intelligence. Cyber laws aim to balance the rights and responsibilities of various stakeholders, such as individuals, organizations, governments, and service providers, in the cyberspace.

As the digital landscape evolves rapidly and becomes more complex and interconnected, cyber laws face various challenges and opportunities to adapt and respond to the emerging trends and issues in the cyberspace. Some of the future trends and emerging concerns for cyber laws are:

- **Cybercrime and cyberattacks**: Cybercrime is any illegal or unauthorized activity that involves the use of digital technologies, such as hacking, phishing, identity theft, fraud, ransomware, cyberstalking, cyberterrorism, etc. Cyberattacks are deliberate attempts to disrupt, damage, or gain unauthorized access to digital systems, networks, or data, such as denial-of-service attacks, malware infections, data breaches, etc. Cybercrime and cyberattacks pose serious threats to the security, privacy, and integrity of individuals, organizations, and governments, and can cause significant economic and social losses. Cyber laws need to address the challenges of preventing, detecting, investigating, prosecuting, and punishing cybercrime and cyberattacks, as well as enhancing the resilience and recovery of the victims. Cyber laws also need to cope with the cross-border and transnational nature of cybercrime and cyberattacks, and foster international cooperation and coordination among different jurisdictions and agencies  .

- **Internet of Things and cloud security**: The Internet of Things (IoT) is the network of physical objects, such as devices, vehicles, appliances, sensors, etc., that are embedded with digital technologies, such as software, hardware, and connectivity, that enable them to communicate, collect, and exchange data. Cloud computing is the delivery of computing services, such as storage, processing, networking, software, etc., over the internet, rather than on local devices or servers. IoT and cloud computing offer various benefits, such as convenience, efficiency, scalability, and innovation, but also pose various risks, such as data breaches, unauthorized access, cyberattacks, malfunction, etc. Cyber laws need to address the challenges of ensuring the security, privacy, and reliability of IoT and cloud services, as well as the rights and responsibilities of the users, providers, and regulators of such services. Cyber laws also need to cope with the diversity and complexity of IoT and cloud ecosystems, and the interoperability and compatibility of different standards and protocols .

- **Artificial intelligence and data protection**: Artificial intelligence (AI) is the simulation of human intelligence processes, such as learning, reasoning, and decision making, by machines, such as computers, robots, software, etc. AI relies on large amounts of data, such as personal information, behavioral patterns, preferences, etc., to perform various tasks, such as recognition, analysis, prediction, recommendation, etc. AI offers various benefits, such as automation, optimization, personalization, and innovation, but also poses various risks, such as bias, discrimination, manipulation, error, etc. Cyber laws need to address the challenges of ensuring the protection, quality, and governance of data, as well as the rights and responsibilities of the data subjects, data controllers, data processors, and data regulators. Cyber laws also need to cope with the ethical and social implications of AI, such as transparency, accountability, fairness, and human dignity .
