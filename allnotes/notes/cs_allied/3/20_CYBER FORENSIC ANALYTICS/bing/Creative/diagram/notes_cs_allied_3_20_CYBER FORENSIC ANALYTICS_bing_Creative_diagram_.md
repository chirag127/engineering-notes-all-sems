

# CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

Cyber forensic analytics is the field of study that combines the principles and techniques of cybersecurity and data analytics to identify, prevent, and respond to cyberattacks. Cyber forensic analytics involves the collection, preservation, analysis, and presentation of digital evidence from various sources, such as computers, networks, mobile devices, cloud services, and social media platforms. Cyber forensic analytics can help law enforcement, intelligence, and security professionals to investigate cybercrimes, such as hacking, fraud, espionage, terrorism, and sabotage.

Some of the key topics in cyber forensic analytics are:

- **Cyber forensics**: The process of extracting and examining digital evidence from various devices and systems, using specialized tools and methods, such as disk imaging, file carving, memory analysis, malware analysis, network forensics, and mobile forensics. Cyber forensics aims to establish the origin, authenticity, integrity, and relevance of digital evidence, and to comply with the legal and ethical standards for admissibility in court.
- **Network defense**: The practice of protecting networks and systems from unauthorized access, misuse, modification, or disruption, using various techniques, such as firewalls, encryption, authentication, intrusion detection and prevention systems, antivirus software, and honeypots. Network defense also involves monitoring and analyzing network traffic and logs, to detect and respond to potential threats and incidents.
- **Applied cyber data analytics**: The application of statistical and machine learning methods to analyze large and complex datasets related to cybersecurity, such as network flows, system logs, malware samples, user behavior, and threat intelligence. Applied cyber data analytics can help to identify patterns, anomalies, correlations, and causations, and to generate insights and predictions for cybersecurity decision making .
- **Security data visualization**: The use of graphical and interactive techniques to represent and explore cybersecurity data, such as charts, graphs, maps, dashboards, and animations. Security data visualization can help to enhance the understanding, communication, and exploration of cybersecurity data, and to support the detection and investigation of cyberattacks .
- **Auditing and intrusion detection**: The process of reviewing and verifying the compliance and performance of networks and systems, according to predefined policies, standards, and regulations, using various tools and methods, such as audits, assessments, scans, tests, and reports. Auditing and intrusion detection can help to identify and correct vulnerabilities, weaknesses, and deviations, and to detect and report unauthorized or malicious activities .



## Unit 1 - Cyber Crime

  - Intellectual property theft: violating the rights of the creators or owners of original works, such as music, movies, books, or software, by copying, downloading, or sharing them online without permission or payment.

### Jurisdictional Concerns

- Jurisdiction is the authority of a state or a court to apply its laws to a person, a territory, or an event.
- Jurisdictional concerns arise in cybercrime cases because cybercrime is non-local in nature, meaning that it can cross multiple geographic and legal boundaries.
- Jurisdictional concerns can pose challenges for law enforcement agencies and prosecutors in investigating and prosecuting cybercrime, such as:
  - Determining which agency or court has the authority and responsibility to handle the case.
  - Obtaining evidence and cooperation from other jurisdictions, especially foreign ones.
  - Respecting the sovereignty and human rights of other states and individuals involved in the case.
  - Applying the appropriate laws and standards of proof to the case.
  - Avoiding conflicts of laws and double jeopardy issues.
- Jurisdictional concerns can be addressed by various means, such as:
  - Establishing clear and consistent legal frameworks and definitions for cybercrime at the national and international levels.
  - Developing and implementing harmonized and cooperative mechanisms for mutual legal assistance and extradition among states.
  - Enhancing the capacity and coordination of law enforcement agencies and prosecutors to deal with cybercrime cases.
  - Raising awareness and education among the public and the private sector about cybercrime and its prevention.



### Jurisprudential Inconsistency

- Jurisprudential inconsistency refers to the situation where different judges deliver different rulings on similar cases involving cyber crime .
- This inconsistency may be caused by the lack of experience, knowledge, or training of the judges on cyber crime issues, such as the nature, scope, and impact of cyber crime, the technical aspects of digital evidence, and the legal frameworks and standards for cyber crime prosecution .
- Jurisprudential inconsistency may undermine the credibility, reliability, and fairness of the judicial system, and may create confusion, uncertainty, and frustration for the victims, witnesses, investigators, and prosecutors of cyber crime .
- Jurisprudential inconsistency may also affect the deterrence and prevention of cyber crime, as potential offenders may exploit the loopholes or inconsistencies in the judicial rulings to avoid or reduce their liability or punishment .
- Jurisprudential inconsistency may be reduced or resolved by enhancing the education, training, and awareness of the judges on cyber crime issues, by developing and applying consistent and uniform standards and guidelines for cyber crime adjudication, and by promoting cooperation and communication among the judges and other stakeholders involved in cyber crime investigations and prosecutions .

### Prepaid Cards

- Prepaid cards are different from regular credit and debit cards issued by banks, as they do not require a bank account, a credit check, or a personal identification number (PIN) to use them.

- Some measures that can be taken to prevent or mitigate prepaid card fraud and money laundering include:
  - Implementing stricter controls and verification processes for issuing and using prepaid cards, such as requiring identification, limiting the amount and frequency of transactions, and reporting suspicious activities.
  - Enhancing the cooperation and information sharing among financial institutions, card networks, law enforcement agencies, and regulators, both domestically and internationally .
  - Educating the public and raising awareness about the risks and signs of prepaid card fraud and scams, and encouraging them to report any incidents.



# Stored Values Cards

- Stored value cards (SVCs) are cards with data encoded in either a magnetic strip or a computer chip that are preloaded with a fixed amount of electronic currency or value.
- SVCs can be classified into three types: closed system, semi-closed system, and open system.
- Closed system cards are issued by a single merchant or group of merchants and can only be used to purchase goods or services from them. Examples are gift cards, phone cards, and transit cards.
- Semi-closed system cards are issued by a non-financial entity and can be used to purchase goods or services from a network of merchants that accept the card. Examples are loyalty cards, campus cards, and payroll cards.
- Open system cards are issued by a financial institution and can be used to purchase goods or services from any merchant that accepts the card. Examples are prepaid debit cards, travel cards, and remittance cards.
- SVCs offer convenience, security, and flexibility to consumers and merchants, but they also pose money laundering and cybercrime risks .
- The widespread availability of SVCs (particularly at non-financial outlets), the high loading and card balance value limits of open system cards, and the anonymity offered by closed and semi-closed system cards could be abused by organised criminals for illicit financial transactions, money laundering and bulk cash smuggling, particularly as value limits increase.
- SVCs have been identified in several reports as a potential tool for organised crime groups to launder their illicit crime proceeds.
- A study on cross-border electronic funds transfer systems raises similar concerns: "The use of stored value cards to move funds across borders is a relatively new phenomenon, but one that is likely to grow in the future. Stored value cards are attractive to money launderers because they are easy to obtain, can store large amounts of value, and can be used anonymously. They also allow money launderers to avoid the reporting and record-keeping requirements that apply to other forms of cross-border funds transfers."
- Cybercriminals can also exploit SVCs to commit fraud, theft, and counterfeiting. For example, cybercriminals can compromise a legitimate user’s account credentials and transfer funds to a SVC, or counterfeit a SVC and use it to make purchases or withdraw cash.
- The US Secret Service (USSS) operates Electronic Crimes Task Forces (ECTFs) to assist local law enforcement in investigating computer crimes, including those involving SVCs.
- The US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has also issued regulations to implement the provisions of the USA PATRIOT Act that require financial institutions to establish anti-money laundering programs and report suspicious activities involving SVCs.
- The Australian Institute of Criminology (AIC) has published a paper that describes the nature of the money laundering risks of SVCs and considers whether existing regulatory measures are adequate to address them.
- The paper concludes that "there is a need for a more comprehensive and coordinated approach to the regulation of stored value cards, both at the domestic and international levels. This would involve enhancing the existing anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing regime, as well as developing new measures to address the specific risks posed by stored value cards."



### Mobile Payments

Mobile payments are any payments that are made using a mobile device, such as a smartphone or a tablet. Mobile payments can be used to purchase goods or services, or to transfer money to other people. Mobile payments can be classified into three main types :

- **Mobile wallets**: These are applications that store the user's payment information, such as credit cards, debit cards, or bank accounts, and allow the user to make payments by scanning a QR code, tapping a NFC-enabled device, or entering a PIN. Examples of mobile wallets are Apple Pay, Google Pay, Samsung Pay, and PayPal.
- **Mobile money transfers**: These are services that enable the user to send or receive money using their mobile phone number, email address, or a unique identifier. - **Mobile payment platforms**: These are platforms that facilitate the processing of mobile payments between merchants and customers, or between peer-to-peer users. The platforms can provide various features, such as security, fraud prevention, loyalty programs, analytics, and customer support. Examples of mobile payment platforms are Square, Stripe, Shopify, and Alipay.

Mobile payments have several advantages over traditional payment methods, such as convenience, speed, security, and accessibility. However, mobile payments also face some challenges, such as interoperability, regulation, privacy, and adoption. Mobile payments are expected to grow rapidly in the future, as more consumers and businesses adopt them for various purposes. Mobile payments are also considered as a potential tool for financial inclusion, as they can provide access to financial services for the unbanked and underbanked populations. Mobile payments are also relevant for cyber forensic analytics, as they can generate digital traces that can be used for investigation, evidence, or intelligence. Mobile payments can also pose some risks for cybercrime, such as identity theft, fraud, hacking, or money laundering. Therefore, mobile payments require proper security measures, legal frameworks, and ethical standards to ensure their safe and responsible use.

### Cyber extortion

Cyber extortion is a form of cybercrime in which hackers or cybercriminals threaten to harm or damage an organization's data, systems, website, or reputation unless they receive a ransom or some other benefit. Cyber extortion can take various forms, such as:

- **Ransomware**: A type of malware that encrypts the victim's files or locks the victim's device and demands a payment to restore access. Ransomware can affect individual users, businesses, or public institutions. Some examples of ransomware are CryptoLocker, WannaCry, and Ryuk.
- **Distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks**: A type of cyberattack that floods the victim's network or server with a large amount of traffic or requests, making it slow down or crash. DDoS attackers may demand a payment to stop the attack or to prevent future attacks. Some examples of DDoS attacks are Mirai, Dyn, and GitHub.
- **Data breach extortion**: A type of cyberattack that involves stealing or accessing the victim's sensitive or confidential data, such as personal information, financial records, or trade secrets. Data breach extortionists may demand a payment to delete the data, return the data, or refrain from exposing or selling the data to third parties. Some examples of data breach extortion are Ashley Madison, Sony Pictures, and Colonial Pipeline.
- **Reputation damage extortion**: A type of cyberattack that involves threatening to harm the victim's reputation or credibility by spreading false or damaging information, such as fake news, negative reviews, or defamatory content. Reputation damage extortionists may demand a payment to remove the information, stop the campaign, or restore the reputation. Some examples of reputation damage extortion are Yelp, Ripoff Report, and Sextortion.

Cyber extortion is a serious and growing threat that can cause significant financial, operational, and reputational losses to organizations and individuals. Cyber extortion can also have legal, regulatory, and ethical implications, depending on the nature and scope of the attack and the response. Therefore, it is important to understand the risks and challenges of cyber extortion and to adopt appropriate measures to prevent, detect, and respond to cyber extortion incidents. Some of the best practices to mitigate cyber extortion are:

- Implementing robust cybersecurity policies and procedures, such as data encryption, backup, firewall, antivirus, and patch management.
- Educating and training employees and users on how to recognize and avoid phishing emails, malicious attachments, and suspicious links that may deliver ransomware or other malware.
- Monitoring and testing network and system performance and security regularly to identify and address any vulnerabilities or anomalies.
- Establishing and maintaining a crisis management and incident response plan that outlines the roles and responsibilities, communication channels, and escalation procedures in the event of a cyber extortion attack.
- Reporting and cooperating with law enforcement and regulatory authorities in the investigation and prosecution of cyber extortion cases.

### Phreaking

Phreaking is a term that refers to the activity of hacking into telecommunication systems, especially to obtain free calls or to manipulate the system in some way. Phreaking can be considered a form of cybercrime, as it involves unauthorized access, fraud, and damage to the telecommunication infrastructure.

Some of the main points to know about phreaking are:

- Phreaking originated in the 1960s and 1970s, when phone hackers discovered that they could use certain tones and devices to bypass the phone company's billing and routing mechanisms. They used whistles, blue boxes, black boxes, and red boxes to generate the tones and signals that controlled the phone switches.
- Phreaking was also motivated by curiosity and exploration, as phreakers wanted to learn about the inner workings of the phone system and to access hidden features and services, such as conference calls, test lines, and overseas operators.
- Phreaking was influenced by the hacker culture and the counterculture of the time, as phreakers saw themselves as rebels and outlaws who challenged the authority and monopoly of the phone companies. Some of the famous phreakers include John Draper (also known as Captain Crunch), Mark Bernay, and Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs (the founders of Apple).
- Phreaking declined in the 1980s and 1990s, as the phone companies switched to digital and encrypted systems that made phreaking more difficult and risky. However, phreaking did not disappear completely, as some phreakers adapted to the new technologies and found new ways to exploit the phone system, such as cloning cell phones, hacking voicemail, and spoofing caller ID.
- Phreaking is still relevant today, as the telecommunication system is constantly evolving and expanding, and as new vulnerabilities and opportunities emerge. Phreaking can pose a threat to the security and privacy of the users and the providers of the phone system, as well as to the national and international communication infrastructure. Phreaking can also be used for malicious purposes, such as harassment, extortion, espionage, and terrorism.

## Unit 2 - Cyber Forensics

Cyber forensics, also known as computer or digital forensics, is the branch of digital forensic science that deals with the investigation and analysis of evidence from electronic devices, such as computers, smartphones, tablets, etc.   

The main objectives of cyber forensics are:

- To identify the source, nature, and extent of a cyberattack or a cybercrime.
- To collect, preserve, and analyze the digital evidence in a forensically sound manner.
- To present the findings and conclusions in a clear and convincing way in a court of law or other legal proceedings.

### Botnets

- Botnet forensics can be divided into three phases: malware, botnet forensic investigator, and botnet forensic analyzer.
  - Malware phase: It involves propagation, infection, communication, and attack that are performed by the botnet malware. This phase can be further divided into four stages: infection, command and control (C&C), update, and action.
  - Botnet forensic investigator phase: This is the second phase of the botnet forensics framework. This phase focuses on the identification and collection of botnet evidence from the infected machines, the network traffic, and the C&C servers.   - Botnet forensic analyzer phase: This is the third and final phase of the botnet forensics framework. This phase involves the examination, analysis, and attribution of the botnet evidence collected in the previous phase. The analyzer uses various methods and tools to extract, correlate, and interpret the botnet evidence, such as static and dynamic analysis, reverse engineering, data mining, machine learning, etc. The analyzer also tries to identify the botmaster, the botnet architecture, the botnet protocol, and the botnet activity.
- Botnet forensics is a challenging and complex task, as botnets are constantly evolving and using various techniques to evade detection and analysis, such as encryption, obfuscation, polymorphism, fast-flux, peer-to-peer, etc  .

### Honey Pots

- A **honeypot** is a **cybersecurity mechanism** that uses a **manufactured attack target** to lure cybercriminals away from legitimate targets, and also gathers intelligence about the identity, methods and motivations of adversaries .
- A honeypot can be modeled after any digital asset, including software applications, servers or the network itself .
- Honeypots are typically configured to look like attractive targets to attackers—such as servers running vulnerable versions of operating systems or databases with easy passwords.
- When attackers exploit the honeypot, their activities are monitored and recorded.
- Honeypots can be used to **attract, detect, and deflect** cyberattacks, as well as to **collect data** for forensic analysis and threat intelligence .
- Honeypots can be classified into different types based on their purpose, interaction level, and deployment mode .
- The purpose of a honeypot can be either **research** or **production**. A research honeypot is used to study the behavior and techniques of attackers, while a production honeypot is used to protect a specific network or system .
- The interaction level of a honeypot can be either **low**, **medium**, or **high**. A low-interaction honeypot simulates only the basic services and protocols of a target, while a high-interaction honeypot provides a realistic and fully functional environment for the attacker. A medium-interaction honeypot offers a compromise between the two extremes .
- The deployment mode of a honeypot can be either **internal** or **external**. An internal honeypot is placed inside the network perimeter of an organization, while an external honeypot is placed outside the network perimeter. An internal honeypot can detect insider threats, while an external honeypot can detect external threats .
- Honeypots have several advantages and disadvantages for cybersecurity. Some of the advantages are: 
  - They can divert attackers from the real targets and waste their time and resources .
  - They can provide early warning and detection of attacks and vulnerabilities .
  - They can collect valuable information about the attackers and their methods .
  - They can improve the security awareness and education of the organization .
- Some of the disadvantages are:
  - They can be detected and avoided by sophisticated attackers .
  - They can be compromised and used as a launchpad for further attacks .
  - They can pose legal and ethical issues regarding the consent and privacy of the attackers .
  - They can require significant resources and expertise to maintain and analyze .

### Types of Evidence in Cyber Forensics

- **Logs**: Logs are records of events or activities that occur on a system, network, or application. - **Video footage and images**: Video footage and images are visual data that can capture the appearance, behavior, location, or identity of a person, object, or scene. - **Active data**: Active data is the data that is readily accessible and visible on a system or device. - **Metadata**: Metadata is the data that describes or provides information about other data. Residual data can include deleted files, fragments, cache, cookies, temporary files, swap files, slack space, etc. Residual data can be used to recover the data that was intentionally or unintentionally erased, overwritten, or corrupted. - E-governance aims to enhance the efficiency, transparency, accountability, and participation of the public sector by using ICTs.
- E-governance can be classified into four categories based on the direction of interaction: G2C, G2B, G2G, and G2E.
  - G2C: Government to citizen e-governance involves the delivery of public services and information to citizens through various channels, such as websites, portals, kiosks, mobile apps, call centers, etc. Examples of G2C services are online tax filing, voter registration, passport application, etc.
  - G2B: Government to business e-governance involves the facilitation of business activities and transactions with the government through online platforms, such as e-procurement, e-tendering, e-licensing, e-customs, etc. Examples of G2B services are online registration of businesses, online payment of taxes, online submission of bids, etc.
  - G2G: Government to government e-governance involves the integration and coordination of various government agencies and departments through ICTs, such as shared databases, networks, interoperability standards, etc. Examples of G2G services are online data sharing, online monitoring and evaluation, online decision support systems, etc.
  - G2E: Government to employee e-governance involves the improvement of internal administration and human resource management of the government through ICTs, such as intranets, e-learning, e-payroll, e-performance appraisal, etc. Examples of G2E services are online training, online leave application, online grievance redressal, etc.
- E-governance can also be classified into three stages based on the level of sophistication and maturity: informational, transactional, and transformational.
  - Informational: The informational stage of e-governance involves the dissemination of basic information and data to the public through static websites, brochures, newsletters, etc. The main objective of this stage is to increase the awareness and accessibility of government services and policies. The interaction is one-way and passive.
  - Transactional: The transactional stage of e-governance involves the provision of interactive and dynamic services and transactions to the public through online platforms, such as portals, applications, forms, etc. The main objective of this stage is to increase the convenience and efficiency of government services and transactions. The interaction is two-way and active.
  - Transformational: The transformational stage of e-governance involves the re-engineering and integration of various government processes and systems through ICTs, such as databases, networks, standards, etc. The main objective of this stage is to increase the transparency, accountability, and participation of government services and processes. The interaction is multi-way and collaborative.

### Future Trends and Emerging Concerns for Cyber Laws

Cyber laws are the legal frameworks that govern the use, protection, and regulation of digital technologies, such as the internet, computers, mobile devices, cloud services, and artificial intelligence. Cyber laws aim to balance the rights and responsibilities of various stakeholders, such as individuals, organizations, governments, and service providers, in the cyberspace.

Cyber laws are constantly evolving and adapting to the rapid changes and challenges posed by the digital world. Some of the future trends and emerging concerns for cyber laws are:

- **Cybercrime and cyberattacks**: Cybercrime is any illegal or unauthorized activity that involves the use of digital technologies, such as hacking, phishing, identity theft, ransomware, cyberstalking, cyberterrorism, etc. Cyberattacks are deliberate attempts to disrupt, damage, or gain unauthorized access to digital systems, networks, or data, such as denial-of-service attacks, malware, spyware, etc. Cybercrime and cyberattacks pose serious threats to the security, privacy, and integrity of individuals, organizations, and nations. According to a report from the National Cyber Security Centre, over 60 percent of law firms reported an information breach in 2020. Cyber laws need to address the prevention, detection, investigation, prosecution, and punishment of cybercrime and cyberattacks, as well as the protection and compensation of victims.
- **Internet of Things and cloud security**: Internet of Things (IoT) is the network of physical objects, such as devices, vehicles, appliances, sensors, etc., that are embedded with software, hardware, and connectivity, enabling them to communicate and exchange data with each other and the internet. Cloud computing is the delivery of computing services, such as storage, processing, networking, software, etc., over the internet, rather than on local devices or servers. IoT and cloud computing offer many benefits, such as convenience, efficiency, scalability, and innovation, but also pose many challenges, such as data privacy, data ownership, data sovereignty, data breach, data loss, data manipulation, etc. Cyber laws need to address the rights and obligations of IoT and cloud service providers and users, as well as the standards and regulations for ensuring the security, reliability, and accountability of IoT and cloud systems.
- **Artificial intelligence and machine learning**: Artificial intelligence (AI) is the simulation of human intelligence processes, such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving, decision-making, etc., by machines, such as computers, robots, etc. Machine learning (ML) is a subset of AI that involves the use of algorithms and data to enable machines to learn and improve from their own experience, without explicit programming. AI and ML offer many opportunities, such as automation, optimization, personalization, and innovation, but also raise many issues, such as bias, discrimination, transparency, explainability, accountability, liability, etc. Cyber laws need to address the ethical, legal, and social implications of AI and ML, as well as the governance and oversight of AI and ML systems and applications.
- **Digital identity and biometrics**: Digital identity is the representation of a person's identity in the digital world, such as usernames, passwords, email addresses, social media profiles, etc. Biometrics is the measurement and analysis of a person's physical or behavioral characteristics, such as fingerprints, face, voice, iris, etc., for identification or authentication purposes. Digital identity and biometrics offer many advantages, such as convenience, security, and accuracy, but also entail many risks, such as identity theft, identity fraud, identity discrimination, identity manipulation, etc. Cyber laws need to address the protection and management of digital identity and biometric data, as well as the rights and responsibilities of digital identity and biometric service providers and users.
- **Cyber sovereignty and jurisdiction**: Cyber sovereignty is the principle that each nation has the right and authority to regulate and control the digital activities and resources within its territory, according to its own laws and interests. Cyber jurisdiction is the legal power and authority of a nation or a court to apply and enforce its laws and regulations to the digital activities and resources that have a connection or impact on its territory, citizens, or interests. Cyber sovereignty and jurisdiction pose many challenges, such as conflicts of laws, extraterritoriality, enforcement, cooperation, etc., due to the global, borderless, and dynamic nature of the cyberspace. Cyber laws need to address the harmonization and coordination of cyber sovereignty and jurisdiction, as well as the respect and recognition of the diversity and sovereignty of different nations and regions in the cyberspace.
