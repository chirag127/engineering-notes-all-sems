

# Cyber Forensic Analytics

- Cyber forensic analytics is the application of data analysis techniques and tools to investigate cyber incidents and collect digital evidence for legal purposes.
- Cyber forensic analytics involves the following steps:
  - Identification: The process of identifying the sources and types of digital evidence relevant to a cyber incident, such as devices, networks, logs, files, etc.
  - Preservation: The process of securing and maintaining the integrity of digital evidence, such as by creating copies, hashes, or encryption.
  - Extraction: The process of accessing and retrieving digital evidence from various sources, such as by using forensic software, hardware, or cloud services.
  - Analysis: The process of examining and interpreting digital evidence, such as by using data visualization, anomaly detection, pattern recognition, or machine learning.
  - Reporting: The process of presenting and documenting the findings and conclusions of the analysis, such as by using charts, graphs, tables, or narratives.
- Cyber forensic analytics can help address various challenges and objectives, such as:
  - Detecting and responding to cyberattacks, such as malware, ransomware, phishing, denial-of-service, etc.
  - Identifying and attributing cybercriminals, such as hackers, fraudsters, terrorists, etc.
  - Recovering and reconstructing lost or damaged data, such as files, emails, passwords, etc.
  - Protecting and enhancing cybersecurity, such as by improving network defense, security awareness, or risk management.
  - Supporting and complying with legal and regulatory requirements, such as by preserving chain of custody, admissibility, or privacy.



## Unit 1 - Cyber Crime

Cyber crime is the use of a computer or a network as an instrument to commit illegal acts, such as fraud, identity theft, data theft, cyberextortion, ransomware, phishing, spoofing, etc. Cyber crime can harm individuals, organizations, or even nations by compromising their security, privacy, or financial well-being. Cyber crime can also affect the functioning of critical infrastructure, such as power grids, transportation systems, or health care services.

Some of the types and examples of cyber crime are:

- **Hacking**: Unauthorized access to a computer system or network, often with malicious intent. For example, hackers may steal confidential data, damage or delete files, install malware, or disrupt services.
- **Denial-of-service (DoS) attack**: An attempt to overwhelm a computer system or network with excessive traffic or requests, making it unavailable to legitimate users. For example, a DoS attack may target a website, an email server, or an online game.
- **Software piracy**: The illegal copying, distribution, or use of software without proper authorization or license. For example, software piracy may involve downloading or sharing copyrighted software, cracking or bypassing software protection, or selling counterfeit software.
- **Phishing**: A fraudulent technique of sending emails or other messages that appear to be from a legitimate source, but are actually designed to trick recipients into revealing personal or financial information, such as passwords, bank account details, or credit card numbers. For example, a phishing email may impersonate a bank, a government agency, or a well-known company, and ask the recipient to click on a link or open an attachment that leads to a fake website or a malware infection.
- **Spoofing**: A deceptive technique of falsifying the identity or origin of a communication, such as an email, a phone call, or a website. For example, spoofing may involve changing the sender's address, the caller ID, or the domain name, to make it look like the communication is coming from a trusted source, such as a friend, a colleague, or a reputable organization.
- **Identity theft**: The illegal acquisition and use of someone else's personal information, such as name, date of birth, social security number, or credit card number, for fraudulent purposes, such as opening bank accounts, applying for loans, or making purchases. For example, identity theft may occur when a cybercriminal obtains personal information from a data breach, a phishing email, a malware infection, or a stolen device.
- **Data theft**: The unauthorized copying, transfer, or disclosure of data, especially sensitive or confidential data, from a computer system or network. For example, data theft may involve stealing customer records, trade secrets, intellectual property, or classified information, for personal gain, espionage, or sabotage.
- **Cyberextortion**: The use of threats or coercion to extort money or other benefits from a victim, by exploiting their vulnerability or fear of a cyber attack. For example, cyberextortion may involve demanding a ransom to decrypt or restore data that has been encrypted or deleted by a ransomware attack, or to stop a DoS attack or a data leak.
- **Ransomware**: A type of malware that encrypts or locks the data or files on a victim's computer or device, and demands a ransom for the decryption key or the release of the data or files. For example, ransomware may infect a computer or device through a phishing email, a malicious attachment, or a compromised website, and display a message or a screen that instructs the victim to pay a certain amount of money, usually in cryptocurrency, to restore access to their data or files.
- **Cryptojacking**: The unauthorized use of someone else's computer or device resources to mine cryptocurrency, such as Bitcoin or Ethereum, without their knowledge or consent. For example, cryptojacking may involve embedding a malicious code or script on a website, an app, or an email, that runs in the background and consumes the CPU, memory, or battery power of the visitor's or user's computer or device, to generate cryptocurrency for the cybercriminal.

# Cyber Crime

Cyber crime is any illegal activity that involves a computer, a network, or a device connected to the internet. Cyber criminals use various methods and tools to harm individuals, organizations, or governments for their personal, financial, or political gain. Cyber crime can have serious consequences for the victims, such as loss of data, money, reputation, or privacy.

Some of the common types of cyber crime are:

- **Email and internet fraud**: This involves sending deceptive or fraudulent messages to trick the recipients into revealing their personal or financial information, or into paying money for fake products or services. For example, phishing, spoofing, or scam emails.
- **Identity fraud**: This involves stealing and using someone else's personal information, such as name, address, credit card number, or social security number, to commit fraud or other crimes. For example, identity theft, account takeover, or synthetic identity fraud.
- **Theft of financial or card payment data**: This involves stealing and using someone else's financial or card payment data, such as bank account number, debit or credit card number, or PIN, to make unauthorized transactions or withdrawals. For example, skimming, carding, or ATM fraud.
- **Theft and sale of corporate data**: This involves stealing and selling confidential or proprietary data from a company or an organization, such as trade secrets, customer data, or employee data, to competitors or other parties. For example, data breach, data exfiltration, or industrial espionage.
- **Cyberextortion**: This involves demanding money or other benefits from a victim in exchange for not carrying out a threatened cyber attack, or for restoring access to a compromised system or data. For example, ransomware, distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack, or sextortion.
- **Cryptojacking**: This involves using someone else's computer, network, or device resources without their consent to mine cryptocurrency, such as Bitcoin or Ethereum. For example, malware, browser-based mining, or cloud mining.

# Jurisdictional Concerns

- Jurisdiction is the authority of a state or a court to apply its laws to a person, a territory, or an event.
- Cybercrime is any criminal activity that involves the use of computers, networks, or the internet.
- Cybercrime poses jurisdictional challenges because it can be committed across borders, without physical presence, and with anonymity.
- Some of the jurisdictional issues that arise in cybercrime cases are:

  - Which state or court has the power to investigate, prosecute, or adjudicate a cybercrime that involves multiple jurisdictions?
  - Which law applies to a cybercrime that violates the laws of different jurisdictions?
  - How can evidence of cybercrime be collected, preserved, and shared across jurisdictions?
  - How can cooperation and coordination among different jurisdictions be facilitated and harmonized?
  - How can the rights and interests of the victims, suspects, and witnesses of cybercrime be protected and respected across jurisdictions?

- Some of the possible solutions to address the jurisdictional challenges of cybercrime are:

  - Adopting universal or harmonized definitions and classifications of cybercrime and cyber-related offences.
  - Establishing clear and consistent rules and criteria for determining jurisdiction based on the location, nationality, or effects of the cybercrime.
  - Developing and implementing mutual legal assistance treaties, extradition agreements, and other forms of international cooperation and assistance.
  - Enhancing the capacity and expertise of law enforcement, prosecutors, judges, and other stakeholders in dealing with cybercrime cases.
  - Promoting public awareness and education on cybercrime prevention and reporting.

- Jurisprudential inconsistency refers to the lack of uniformity or coherence in the legal system or the legal theory that deals with cyber crime and digital forensics.
- Cyber crime challenges traditional notions of crime and jurisdiction because it is non-local in nature, which throws law enforcement into confusion regarding who should investigate and prosecute the criminal.
- Digital forensics investigations jurisprudence is the theory and philosophy of the study of law and the principles upon which a law is based. For digital evidence to appear at court and be legally admissible, the evidence must be authentic, accurate, complete, and convincing to the jury.
- Jurisprudential inconsistency can arise from various sources, such as:
  - Different legal systems or jurisdictions that have different laws, rules, standards, or procedures for cyber crime and digital forensics.
  - Different interpretations or applications of the same laws, rules, standards, or procedures by different courts, judges, lawyers, or experts.
  - Different levels of technical knowledge or expertise among the legal actors or the digital forensics practitioners.
  - Different types or sources of digital evidence that have different characteristics, challenges, or limitations.
  - Different methods or tools for collecting, analyzing, or presenting digital evidence that have different reliability, validity, or accuracy.

  - Undermining the credibility or trustworthiness of the digital evidence or the digital forensics process.

  - Creating confusion, uncertainty, or ambiguity among the legal actors or the public.
  - Increasing the costs or risks of litigation or dispute resolution.
- Jurisprudential inconsistency can be addressed or reduced by various measures, such as:
  - Developing or harmonizing the laws, rules, standards, or procedures for cyber crime and digital forensics across different legal systems or jurisdictions.

# eCash Security

- eCash is a form of digital currency that allows for the secure, anonymous, and untraceable transfer of funds.
- eCash uses complex encryption algorithms to ensure that transactions are secure and cannot be intercepted or tampered with.
- eCash can be stored on hardware devices, such as smart cards or USB drives, that are not connected to the internet, providing a layer of security against the evolving threats emerging from being online .
- eCash can also be used without needing a bank account or credit card, which reduces the risk of identity theft or fraud.
- Some of the challenges of eCash security include:
  - Preventing double-spending, which is the act of using the same eCash for more than one transaction.
  - Ensuring the authenticity and integrity of eCash, which means verifying that the eCash is not counterfeit or altered.
  - Protecting the privacy and anonymity of eCash users, which means preventing the tracing or linking of eCash transactions to the identities of the users.
- Some of the solutions for eCash security include:
  - Using digital signatures, which are cryptographic codes that verify the identity and validity of eCash transactions.
  - Using blind signatures, which are digital signatures that hide the content of eCash transactions from the issuer or the verifier, preserving the anonymity of the users.
  - Using offline verification, which is a method of checking the validity of eCash transactions without needing to connect to a central authority or a network.



# Prepaid Cards

Prepaid cards are cards that can be loaded with a certain amount of money and used for various purposes, such as gift cards, travel cards, payroll cards, etc. Prepaid cards are different from credit cards and debit cards, as they do not require a bank account or a credit check to obtain. Prepaid cards can be issued by banks, retailers, or other entities, and can run on the networks of major card companies, such as Visa and Mastercard.

## Prepaid Cards and Cyber Crime

Prepaid cards are often involved in cyber crime, as they can be used to:

- Launder the proceeds of crime, such as drug trafficking, fraud, or tax evasion, by transferring funds from one prepaid card to another, or by using prepaid cards to purchase goods and services that can be resold for cash.
- Fund terrorist activities, by using prepaid cards to buy weapons, explosives, or other materials, or by using prepaid cards to travel across borders without detection.
- Commit fraud, by using stolen prepaid card information to make unauthorized purchases, or by using prepaid cards to buy other prepaid cards with stolen payment information.
- Scam victims, by coercing them to pay for fake products, services, or taxes with prepaid cards, such as iTunes or Google Play gift cards, and then redeeming the cards for cash or other value.

## Prepaid Cards and Cyber Security

Prepaid cards pose a challenge for cyber security, as they have fewer controls and regulations than regular credit and debit cards, such as:

- Prepaid cards are often not tied to specific identities, making it harder to track and verify the cardholders and their transactions.
- Prepaid cards are often not subject to the same anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorism financing (CTF) rules and reporting requirements as regular cards, making it easier to evade detection and scrutiny.
- Prepaid cards are often not protected by the same fraud prevention and customer dispute mechanisms as regular cards, making it harder to recover losses and resolve issues.

## Prepaid Cards and Cyber Forensic Analytics

Cyber forensic analytics is the process of applying analytical techniques and tools to investigate cyber crime and cyber security incidents, such as:

- Collecting and preserving digital evidence from various sources, such as computers, networks, devices, or cards.
- Analyzing and interpreting the digital evidence to identify the actors, methods, motives, and impacts of the cyber crime or cyber security incident.
- Reporting and presenting the findings and recommendations of the cyber forensic analysis to the relevant stakeholders, such as law enforcement, regulators, or courts.

Cyber forensic analytics can be applied to prepaid cards and cyber crime, such as:

- Tracing the origin, movement, and destination of funds on prepaid cards, by using data from card issuers, card networks, merchants, or other entities.
- Identifying the patterns, trends, and anomalies of prepaid card transactions, by using techniques such as data mining, machine learning, or artificial intelligence.
- Linking the prepaid card transactions to other cyber crime or cyber security activities, by using techniques such as network analysis, social media analysis, or geolocation analysis.



# Stored Values Cards

- Stored value cards (SVCs) are cards with data encoded in either a magnetic strip or a computer chip that are preloaded with a fixed amount of electronic currency or value.
- SVCs can be classified into three types: closed system, semi-closed system, and open system.
- Closed system cards are issued by a single merchant or group of merchants and can only be used to purchase goods or services from those merchants. Examples are gift cards, phone cards, and transit cards.
- Semi-closed system cards are issued by a third-party provider and can be used to purchase goods or services from a network of merchants that accept the card. Examples are store cards, loyalty cards, and payroll cards.
- Open system cards are issued by a financial institution and can be used to purchase goods or services from any merchant that accepts the card. Examples are prepaid debit cards, travel cards, and remittance cards.
- SVCs offer convenience, security, and flexibility to consumers and merchants, but they also pose money laundering and cybercrime risks .
- Money laundering risks include the use of SVCs to conceal the source, ownership, and movement of illicit funds, to avoid reporting and record-keeping requirements, and to evade law enforcement detection and confiscation .
- Cybercrime risks include the compromise of SVCs by hackers, malware, or phishing, to access or transfer funds, to counterfeit or clone cards, or to steal personal or financial information .
- To mitigate these risks, some regulatory measures have been implemented or proposed, such as imposing value limits, identification and verification requirements, transaction monitoring and reporting, and information sharing and cooperation among stakeholders .



# Mobile Payments

Mobile payments are transactions that use mobile devices such as smartphones, tablets, or smartwatches to initiate, authorize, or confirm the payment of goods or services. Mobile payments can be made through various methods, such as mobile wallets, QR codes, NFC, SMS, or biometrics.

Mobile payments offer convenience, speed, and flexibility for consumers and merchants, as well as new opportunities for innovation and inclusion in the digital economy. However, mobile payments also pose significant challenges and risks for cybersecurity, privacy, and consumer protection.

Some of the cyber crimes that can affect mobile payments are:

- Data breaches: Cyber criminals can hack into mobile payment systems or devices and steal sensitive information, such as card numbers, bank account details, personal identification numbers (PINs), or biometric data. Data breaches can result in identity theft, fraud, or unauthorized transactions.
- Malware: Cyber criminals can infect mobile devices or payment applications with malicious software that can monitor, manipulate, or disrupt the payment process. Malware can also compromise the security features of mobile devices, such as encryption, authentication, or firewall.
- Phishing: Cyber criminals can send fake emails, text messages, or calls that appear to be from legitimate payment providers, banks, or merchants, and trick users into revealing their payment credentials, account information, or personal details. Phishing can also direct users to fraudulent websites or applications that can capture their data or infect their devices with malware.
- Social engineering: Cyber criminals can use psychological techniques to manipulate users into performing actions that can compromise their payment security, such as sharing their PINs, passwords, or verification codes, or clicking on malicious links or attachments. Social engineering can also exploit users' emotions, such as fear, greed, or curiosity, to influence their behavior.
- SIM swapping: Cyber criminals can obtain a duplicate SIM card for a user's mobile number and use it to access their mobile payment accounts, messages, or calls. SIM swapping can enable cyber criminals to bypass the two-factor authentication (2FA) or one-time password (OTP) verification that many mobile payment services use.

To protect themselves from cyber crimes, mobile payment users and providers should adopt the following best practices:

- Use strong and unique passwords, PINs, or biometric authentication for mobile devices and payment applications, and change them regularly.
- Enable encryption, firewall, antivirus, and anti-malware software on mobile devices and payment applications, and update them frequently.
- Avoid using public or unsecured Wi-Fi networks or devices for mobile payments, and use VPNs or HTTPS connections when possible.
- Verify the identity and legitimacy of payment providers, banks, or merchants before initiating or accepting mobile payments, and check the URL, logo, and contact details carefully.
- Do not open or respond to suspicious emails, text messages, or calls that request payment information, account details, or personal data, and report them to the relevant authorities or entities.
- Do not share or disclose payment credentials, account information, or personal details with anyone, even if they claim to be from the payment provider, bank, or merchant, and do not store them on mobile devices or payment applications.
- Use tokenization, which replaces sensitive data with random symbols or codes, to enhance the security of mobile payments, and avoid storing card numbers or bank account details on mobile devices or payment applications.
- Monitor mobile payment transactions and account activities regularly, and report any suspicious or unauthorized activities to the payment provider, bank, or merchant immediately.
- Be aware of the terms and conditions, fees, and charges, and the rights and responsibilities of mobile payment users and providers, and seek clarification or assistance when needed.



# Internet Payment Services

- Internet payment services are methods of transferring money online using electronic devices such as computers, smartphones, or tablets.
- Internet payment services can be used for various purposes, such as online shopping, bill payment, peer-to-peer transfers, donations, subscriptions, etc.
- Internet payment services offer convenience, speed, security, and flexibility to users and merchants, as they can accept and process payments from different sources and currencies, and provide various features and benefits, such as fraud protection, dispute resolution, loyalty programs, etc.
- Internet payment services can be classified into different types, such as:

  - **Card-based services**: These services allow users to pay online using their credit, debit, or prepaid cards, which are linked to their bank accounts or other funding sources. Examples of card-based services are Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Discover, etc.
  - **Wallet-based services**: These services allow users to store their payment information, such as card details, bank account numbers, or digital currencies, in a secure online account or app, and use them to pay online without entering their information every time. Examples of wallet-based services are PayPal, Amazon Pay, Apple Pay, Google Pay, etc.
  - **Bank-based services**: These services allow users to pay online directly from their bank accounts, using online banking, electronic funds transfer (EFT), or automated clearing house (ACH) systems. Examples of bank-based services are Stripe, Due, GoCardless, SecurePay, etc.
  - **Cryptocurrency-based services**: These services allow users to pay online using digital currencies, such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, Litecoin, etc., which are decentralized and operate on blockchain technology. Examples of cryptocurrency-based services are Coinbase, BitPay, CoinPayments, etc.

- Internet payment services are regulated by various laws and standards, such as the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), the Electronic Fund Transfer Act (EFTA), the Fair Credit Billing Act (FCBA), the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA), the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), etc., which aim to protect the privacy, security, and rights of users and merchants.
- Internet payment services are also vulnerable to various risks and challenges, such as cyberattacks, fraud, identity theft, chargebacks, disputes, compliance issues, etc., which require effective measures and solutions, such as encryption, authentication, verification, monitoring, reporting, etc.



# Cyberstalking

- Cyberstalking is the repeated use of electronic communications to harass or frighten someone, for example by sending threatening emails.
- Cyberstalking can take many forms, including: harassment, embarrassment and humiliation of the victim, defamation, slander and libel, monitoring, identity theft, threats, vandalism, solicitation for sex, doxing, or blackmail.
- Cyberstalking is a criminal offense under various state anti-stalking, slander and harassment laws. A conviction can result in a restraining order, probation, or criminal penalties against the assailant, including jail.
- Cyberstalking is different from cyberbullying, which is the use of electronic communication to bully someone, typically by sending messages of an intimidating or threatening nature. Cyberbullying is more common among children and teenagers, while cyberstalking is more common among adults.
- Cyberstalking can have serious psychological and emotional effects on the victim, such as anxiety, depression, fear, paranoia, isolation, low self-esteem, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).
- Cyberstalking can be prevented or stopped by taking some measures, such as: blocking or reporting the stalker, changing passwords and privacy settings, saving evidence of the harassment, informing friends and family, contacting the authorities, and seeking professional help.



# Cyber Extortion

- Cyber extortion is an online crime in which hackers hold your data, website, computer systems, or other sensitive information hostage until you meet their demands for payment .
- It often takes the form of ransomware and distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, both of which could paralyze your business.
- Ransomware is a type of malware that encrypts your files or locks your devices and demands a ransom for their decryption or release.
- DDoS attacks are a type of cyberattack that floods your network or servers with traffic or requests, overwhelming your resources and preventing legitimate users from accessing your services.
- Cyber extortion can also involve threats to expose or damage your reputation, intellectual property, trade secrets, or customer data if you do not pay the extortionists .
- Cyber extortion is a serious and growing threat to businesses and individuals, as hackers become more sophisticated and demand higher ransoms.
- Cyber extortion can cause significant financial losses, operational disruptions, legal liabilities, and reputational damage to the victims.
- To prevent or mitigate cyber extortion, you should implement the following best practices:
  - Use strong passwords and encryption for your devices and data.
  - Install antivirus software and keep it updated.
  - Backup your data regularly and store it offline or in the cloud.
  - Avoid opening suspicious emails or attachments, or clicking on unknown links.
  - Educate your employees and customers about the risks and signs of cyber extortion.
  - Report any incidents or attempts of cyber extortion to the authorities and your cyber insurance provider.
  - Do not pay the ransom, as it may not guarantee the recovery of your data or the cessation of the attack, and it may encourage further extortion attempts.

- Cyber terrorism can target **vital computer systems** that control infrastructure, communication, transportation, finance, or security.

# Cyber warfare

- Cyber warfare means the use of computer technology to disrupt the activities of a state or organization, especially the deliberate attacking of information systems for strategic or military purposes.
- Cyber warfare is usually waged against government and military networks in order to disrupt, destroy, or deny their use.
- Cyber warfare can have intended outcomes such as espionage, sabotage, propaganda, manipulation or economic warfare.
- Cyber warfare can involve different types of attacks, such as:
  - Computer viruses: malicious programs that infect and damage computer files or systems.
  - Denial-of-service attacks: overwhelming a network or server with traffic or requests to make it unavailable or slow down its performance.
  - Phishing: sending fraudulent emails or messages that appear to be from legitimate sources to trick users into revealing sensitive information or installing malware.
  - Ransomware: encrypting the data or files of a victim and demanding payment for their decryption.
  - Distributed denial-of-service attacks: using multiple compromised devices or computers to launch a coordinated denial-of-service attack.
  - Botnets: networks of infected computers or devices that can be remotely controlled by hackers to perform malicious tasks.
  - Cyber espionage: stealing or accessing confidential or classified information from a target network or system.
  - Cyber sabotage: damaging or destroying physical or digital infrastructure or assets of a target.
  - Cyber propaganda: spreading false or misleading information or opinions online to influence public perception or behavior.
  - Cyber manipulation: altering or tampering with data or information to deceive or mislead a target or audience.
  - Cyber economic warfare: disrupting or harming the economic activities or interests of a target, such as trade, finance, or commerce.
- Cyber warfare can pose serious threats and challenges to national and international security, as well as to human rights, democracy, and the rule of law.
- Cyber warfare can also have ethical, legal, and social implications, such as:
  - The attribution of cyber attacks: identifying the source, origin, and responsibility of cyber attacks can be difficult and uncertain, as hackers can use various techniques to hide or disguise their identity and location.
  - The proportionality and necessity of cyber attacks: determining the appropriate and justified level and scope of cyber attacks in response to a provocation or threat can be complex and subjective, as cyber attacks can have unpredictable and unintended consequences and collateral damage.
  - The distinction between combatants and non-combatants: distinguishing between legitimate and illegitimate targets of cyber attacks can be challenging and controversial, as cyber attacks can affect civilians and civilian infrastructure, as well as military and government entities.

# ATM Frauds

ATM frauds are a type of cyber crime that involves stealing money or personal information from ATM users. ATM frauds can have serious consequences for the victims, such as financial losses, identity theft, or compromised accounts. Some of the common types of ATM frauds are:

- **Skimming**: This is when criminals attach a device to the ATM card reader that can capture the data on the magnetic strip of the card. They may also install a hidden camera or a fake keypad to record the PIN of the user. The criminals can then use the stolen data to clone the card or make online transactions.
- **Shimming**: This is similar to skimming, but the device is inserted inside the card slot of the ATM, where it can intercept the data from the chip of the card. The device is usually very thin and hard to detect. The criminals can then use the stolen data to create a fake card or make online transactions.
- **Card trapping**: This is when criminals insert a device or a substance into the card slot of the ATM that can prevent the card from being ejected. The user may think that the card is stuck or swallowed by the machine, and may leave the ATM or call for help. The criminals can then retrieve the card and use it to withdraw money or make online transactions.
- **Cash trapping**: This is when criminals attach a device or a substance to the cash dispenser of the ATM that can prevent the cash from being dispensed. The user may think that the ATM is out of order or has no cash, and may leave the ATM or call for help. The criminals can then collect the cash from the device.
- **Phishing**: This is when criminals send fake emails, text messages, or phone calls to the ATM users, pretending to be from the bank or the ATM provider. They may ask the users to provide their card details, PIN, or other personal information, or to click on a link that can install malware on their devices. The criminals can then use the stolen information or malware to access the users' accounts or make online transactions.
- **Bitcoin ATMs**: This is when criminals target the users of bitcoin ATMs, which are machines that allow the exchange of cash for cryptocurrency or vice versa. They may use various methods, such as posing as law enforcement officials, tax authorities, or utility companies, and demanding the users to pay them in bitcoin through the ATM. They may also use fake QR codes, malware, or counterfeit devices to steal the users' cryptocurrency or cash.

Some of the ways to prevent or avoid ATM frauds are:

- **Inspect the ATM**: Before using an ATM, check for any signs of tampering, such as loose or damaged parts, unfamiliar devices, or stickers. If you notice anything suspicious, do not use the ATM and report it to the bank or the ATM provider.
- **Cover the keypad**: When entering your PIN, use your hand or a shield to cover the keypad, so that any hidden camera or fake keypad cannot record it. Do not share your PIN with anyone, and change it regularly.
- **Be alert**: When using an ATM, be aware of your surroundings, and avoid any distractions, such as strangers offering help, phone calls, or messages. If you feel unsafe or uncomfortable, cancel the transaction and leave the ATM. Do not leave your card or cash unattended, and take your receipt with you.
- **Use secure ATMs**: Prefer to use ATMs that are located in well-lit, busy, and monitored areas, such as inside banks, malls, or stores. Avoid using ATMs that are isolated, unfamiliar, or in poor condition.
- **Monitor your accounts**: Check your account statements and online banking regularly, and report any unauthorized or suspicious transactions to your bank or card issuer. If you lose your card or suspect that it has been compromised, notify your bank or card issuer and request a replacement or a block.
- **Be cautious with bitcoin ATMs**: If you use bitcoin ATMs, make sure you understand how they work, and verify the legitimacy of the ATM provider and the transaction. Do not send bitcoin to anyone you do not know or trust, and do not fall for any scams that ask you to pay in bitcoin for taxes, fines, bills, or other reasons. Do not scan any QR codes that are not displayed on the ATM screen, and do not download any software or apps that are not authorized by the ATM provider.

# Data Carving

- Data carving is a term used in the field of cyber forensics, which is the process of acquisition, authentication, analysis, and documentation of evidence extracted from and/or contained in a computer system, computer network, and digital media   .
- Data carving is the technique of extracting data (file) out of undifferentiated blocks (raw data) without relying on the file system or metadata   .
- Data carving is useful for finding hidden or deleted files from digital media, which may contain valuable information for forensic investigation.
- Data carving can be performed on areas like lost clusters, unallocated clusters, and slack space of the disk or digital media, where files may be partially or fully stored.
- Data carving requires a file to have a standard file signature called a file header (start of the file) and optionally a file footer (end of the file), which can be used to identify the file type and boundaries .
- Data carving can be classified into two types: header/footer carving and semantic carving.
  - Header/footer carving is the simplest and most common type of data carving, which uses the file header and footer to locate and extract the file.
  - Semantic carving is a more advanced type of data carving, which uses the internal structure and content of the file to locate and extract the file, without relying on the file header and footer.
- Data carving can be performed using various tools, such as Foremost, Scalpel, PhotoRec, and Autopsy.
- Data carving has some limitations and challenges, such as:
  - Data carving may produce false positives, which are files that are incorrectly identified or extracted.
  - Data carving may produce incomplete or corrupted files, which are files that are missing some data or have incorrect data due to fragmentation, overwriting, encryption, or compression .
  - Data carving may be time-consuming and resource-intensive, especially for large or complex digital media .
  - Data carving may not be able to recover all types of files, especially those that do not have distinctive file signatures or internal structures .

# Port scans

- A port scan is a technique used by cybercriminals to find out information about a system they are going to target .
- It involves scanning through a network system and determining which ports are open, which are sending data, and which are receiving data .
- A port is a logical endpoint of communication in a network, identified by a number from 0 to 65535.
- A port scan can reveal the services, applications, and operating systems running on a system, as well as the security devices such as firewalls and intrusion detection systems .
- Port scanning is a fundamental part of the pre-attack phase of a penetration test, as it can help identify vulnerabilities and potential entry points for an attack.
- Port scanning can also be used for legitimate purposes, such as network monitoring, troubleshooting, and auditing.

## Port scanning techniques

- There are several different port scanning techniques, each with its own advantages and disadvantages .
- Some of the common port scanning techniques are:

  - Ping scans: A ping is used to check whether a network data packet can reach an IP address without any issues. Ping scans can determine if a host is alive and responsive on a network.
  - Half-open or SYN scans: Attackers can check the state of a port without creating a full connection, by sending a SYN (synchronization) packet and waiting for a response. If the response is a SYN-ACK (synchronization-acknowledgment) packet, the port is open. If the response is a RST (reset) packet, the port is closed .
  - Full connection or TCP connect scans: Attackers can establish a full TCP (transmission control protocol) connection with each port, by sending a SYN packet, receiving a SYN-ACK packet, and sending an ACK (acknowledgment) packet. This technique is more reliable but also more detectable and slower than a SYN scan .
  - Stealth or FIN scans: Attackers can send a FIN (finish) packet to a port, which normally indicates the end of a connection. If the port is closed, it will respond with a RST packet. If the port is open, it will ignore the packet. This technique can bypass some firewalls and intrusion detection systems, but it may not work on some operating systems .
  - UDP (user datagram protocol) scans: Attackers can send a UDP packet to a port, which is a connectionless protocol that does not require a handshake. If the port is closed, it will respond with an ICMP (internet control message protocol) error message. If the port is open, it will either respond with a UDP packet or remain silent. This technique can be used to find UDP services, but it is slow and unreliable, as some firewalls and routers may block or filter UDP packets .

## Port scan detection and prevention

- Port scanning can be detected and prevented by using various methods, such as:

  - Firewalls: Firewalls can filter and block incoming and outgoing traffic based on predefined rules and policies. Firewalls can also log and alert on suspicious or malicious activity, such as port scans.
  - Intrusion detection systems (IDS): IDS can monitor and analyze network traffic and detect anomalies and patterns that indicate a port scan. IDS can also generate alerts and reports on port scan incidents.
  - Intrusion prevention systems (IPS): IPS can perform the same functions as IDS, but also take actions to stop or mitigate a port scan, such as blocking the source IP address, dropping the packets, or resetting the connection.
  - Honeypots: Honeypots are decoy systems that are designed to attract and trap attackers. Honeypots can be used to divert port scans away from the real systems, and to collect information and evidence on the attackers and their techniques.

# Trojans and Backdoors

- Trojans and backdoors are two types of malicious software (malware) that can compromise the security and integrity of a computer system.

- A backdoor is a malware that creates a hidden or unauthorized entry point to a computer system, allowing remote attackers to gain control or access to the system. A backdoor can be installed by a trojan, or by exploiting a vulnerability in the system .
- Trojans and backdoors pose a serious threat to cyber security, as they can compromise the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the system and its data. They can also be used to launch further attacks, such as distributed denial-of-service (DDoS), ransomware, or botnet  .
- Cyber forensics is the discipline of collecting, analyzing, and presenting digital evidence related to cyber crimes or incidents. Cyber forensics can help identify, investigate, and prosecute the perpetrators of trojans and backdoors, as well as recover and protect the affected systems and data.
- Some of the techniques and tools used in cyber forensics to deal with trojans and backdoors are:

  - Static and dynamic malware analysis: This involves examining the code and behavior of the malware, using tools such as disassemblers, debuggers, sandboxes, and virtual machines, to understand its functionality, origin, and purpose.
  - Network traffic analysis: This involves monitoring and capturing the network packets sent and received by the malware, using tools such as Wireshark, tcpdump, or Nmap, to identify its communication protocol, destination, and payload.
  - Memory forensics: This involves analyzing the contents of the system's volatile memory (RAM), using tools such as Volatility, Rekall, or FTK Imager, to detect and extract the malware's artifacts, such as processes, files, registry keys, or strings.
  - File system forensics: This involves examining the structure and content of the system's non-volatile storage (hard disk, flash drive, etc.), using tools such as Autopsy, Sleuth Kit, or EnCase, to locate and recover the malware's files, timestamps, metadata, or traces.
  - Registry forensics: This involves analyzing the Windows registry, which stores various configuration and operational data for the system and its applications, using tools such as RegRipper, Registry Explorer, or RegShot, to identify and extract the malware's entries, values, or modifications.
  - Log analysis: This involves reviewing the system's and application's log files, which record various events and activities, using tools such as Log Parser, Log2Timeline, or Splunk, to correlate and reconstruct the malware's timeline, actions, or impact.

# DDoS Attacks

- DDoS stands for Distributed Denial of Service, which is a type of cyberattack that aims to disrupt the availability of a network or web service by sending multiple requests to it, so large and massive that the service becomes unavailable.
- DDoS attacks are often carried out by hackers who use compromised computers and networks, called botnets, to generate the traffic. Botnets can be created by infecting devices with malware, exploiting vulnerabilities, or hijacking credentials.
- DDoS attacks can target different layers of the network stack, such as the application layer, the protocol layer, or the volumetric layer. Each layer has different characteristics and requires different mitigation strategies .
  - Application layer attacks target the logic and functionality of a web service, such as HTTP requests, DNS queries, or SMTP commands. These attacks can be low in bandwidth but high in complexity and sophistication, and can bypass firewalls and other security measures. Examples of application layer attacks are HTTP floods, DNS amplification, and slowloris .
  - Protocol layer attacks target the network protocols that enable communication between devices, such as TCP, UDP, ICMP, or SYN. These attacks can consume server or network resources, such as memory, CPU, or bandwidth, and cause connection timeouts, packet loss, or network congestion. Examples of protocol layer attacks are SYN floods, UDP floods, ICMP floods, and TCP reset attacks .
  - Volumetric layer attacks target the network infrastructure and bandwidth, such as routers, switches, or links. These attacks can generate a large amount of traffic, measured in bits per second (bps) or packets per second (pps), and overwhelm the network capacity or the bandwidth of the target. Examples of volumetric layer attacks are NTP amplification, UDP fragmentation, and spoofed IP packets .
- DDoS attacks can have various motives and impacts, such as extorting money, disrupting competitors, protesting against policies, or damaging reputations. DDoS attacks can affect the availability, performance, and security of web services, and cause financial losses, customer dissatisfaction, or legal liabilities.
- Cyber forensics is the process of collecting, analyzing, and presenting digital evidence related to cyberattacks, such as DDoS attacks. Cyber forensics can help determine the source, method, intent, and extent of a cyberattack, and support the identification, prosecution, or mitigation of the attackers.
- The forensic procedure in investigating DDoS attacks can vary depending on the type, scale, and complexity of the attack, but it generally involves the following steps :
  - Preparation: This step involves defining the scope, objectives, and resources of the investigation, as well as obtaining the necessary authorization, tools, and equipment. It also involves establishing a chain of custody and a documentation process for the evidence.
  - Preservation: This step involves securing and isolating the affected network or web service, and preventing any alteration, deletion, or tampering of the evidence. It also involves identifying and collecting the relevant data sources, such as logs, packets, or malware samples, and creating forensic copies or images of them.
  - Analysis: This step involves examining and interpreting the collected data, using various techniques and tools, such as network traffic analysis, malware analysis, or data visualization. It also involves identifying and correlating the indicators of compromise, such as IP addresses, domains, ports, protocols, or patterns, and reconstructing the timeline, sequence, and impact of the attack.
  - Presentation: This step involves reporting and presenting the findings and conclusions of the analysis, using clear, concise, and accurate language, and supporting them with evidence and references. It also involves providing recommendations and suggestions for improving the security and resilience of the network or web service, and preventing or mitigating future attacks.



# Honey Pots

- A **honeypot** is a **cybersecurity mechanism** that uses a **manufactured attack target** to lure cybercriminals away from legitimate targets, and also gathers intelligence about the identity, methods and motivations of adversaries .
- A honeypot can be modeled after any digital asset, including software applications, servers or the network itself .
- Honeypots are typically configured to look like attractive targets to attackers—such as servers running vulnerable versions of operating systems or databases with easy passwords.
- When attackers exploit the honeypot, their activities are monitored and recorded.
- Honeypots can be used to **attract, detect, and deflect** cyberattacks, as well as to **collect and analyze** data about the attackers and their techniques.
- Honeypots can also serve forensic purposes, quietly gathering data about the hackers who stole it.
- Honeypots can be classified into different types based on their purpose, interaction level, and deployment mode.
- The purpose of a honeypot can be either **research** or **production**. A research honeypot is used to study the behavior and tactics of attackers, while a production honeypot is used to protect a specific network or system.
- The interaction level of a honeypot can be either **low**, **medium**, or **high**. A low-interaction honeypot simulates only the basic services and protocols of a target, while a high-interaction honeypot provides a realistic and fully functional environment for the attacker. A medium-interaction honeypot offers a compromise between the two extremes.
- The deployment mode of a honeypot can be either **internal** or **external**. An internal honeypot is placed inside the network perimeter, while an external honeypot is placed outside the network perimeter. An internal honeypot can detect insider threats, while an external honeypot can detect external threats.
- Honeypots have several advantages and disadvantages in cybersecurity. Some of the advantages are:
  - They can divert attackers from the real targets and waste their time and resources.
  - They can provide early warning and detection of attacks and alert the defenders.
  - They can collect valuable information and evidence about the attackers and their methods.
  - They can improve the security awareness and skills of the defenders by exposing them to real attacks.
- Some of the disadvantages are:
  - They can be detected and avoided by skilled and cautious attackers.
  - They can be compromised and used as a launchpad for further attacks on the network or other targets.
  - They can pose legal and ethical issues, such as entrapment, privacy, and liability.
  - They can consume resources and increase the complexity and maintenance of the network.
- Honeypots are not the centerpiece of a cybersecurity strategy, but they should be considered a crucial component of one.
- Honeypots can be used by anyone who wants to protect their network or system from cyberattacks, or who wants to learn more about the attackers and their techniques.



# Malware

Malware is a term that refers to any software or code that is intentionally designed to harm, disrupt, or exploit a computer, network, or server. Malware can take many forms, including :

- **Virus**: A program that infects other software and replicates itself, spreading from one computer to another. A virus usually requires a host file or program to execute its code and cause damage.
- **Worm**: A program that replicates itself and spreads over a network, without the need for a host file. A worm can consume network bandwidth and resources, and create backdoors for other malware to enter.
- **Trojan**: A program that appears to be legitimate but contains hidden malicious functionality. A trojan can steal data, spy on user activity, or create a remote access for hackers.
- **Ransomware**: A program that encrypts the victim's files or locks the device and demands a ransom for the decryption key or access. A ransomware can cause data loss, financial loss, or operational disruption.
- **Spyware**: A program that monitors and collects the victim's personal information, browsing habits, keystrokes, or other sensitive data. A spyware can compromise the victim's privacy, identity, or security.
- **Adware**: A program that displays unwanted or intrusive advertisements on the victim's device or browser. An adware can affect the device performance, consume bandwidth, or redirect the victim to malicious websites.
- **Rootkit**: A program that hides itself or other malware from detection or removal by antivirus or security software. A rootkit can modify the system files, processes, or registry, and gain full control over the device.

Malware can be delivered through various methods, such as email attachments, phishing links, removable media, software downloads, or network vulnerabilities. Malware can cause various impacts, such as data theft, identity fraud, financial loss, system damage, or network compromise.

To prevent malware infection, users should follow some best practices, such as:

- Use a reputable antivirus or security software and keep it updated.
- Avoid opening suspicious or unsolicited emails or attachments.
- Avoid clicking on unknown or untrusted links or pop-ups.
- Avoid downloading or installing software from unverified or illegitimate sources.
- Scan removable media before using it on the device.
- Keep the device and the applications updated with the latest patches and security fixes.
- Use strong and unique passwords and enable multi-factor authentication.
- Backup the important data regularly and store it in a secure location.

# Warranted Searches

# Warrantless searches for the notes of the Unit 3 - Cyber Investigation in the subject of CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- Warrantless searches are searches conducted by law enforcement without obtaining a warrant from a judge based on probable cause.
- Warrantless searches are generally prohibited by the Fourth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution, which protects the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures.
- However, there are some exceptions to the warrant requirement, such as consent, exigent circumstances, search incident to a lawful arrest, plain view, and border search.
- The border search exception allows the government to conduct routine inspections and searches of individuals entering at the U.S. border without a warrant or any individualized suspicion of criminal activity.
- The border search exception applies to searches of electronic devices such as computers and cell phones, which may contain digital evidence of cybercrimes.
- However, courts have disagreed over whether more intrusive, forensic examinations of such devices require heightened suspicion of criminal activity.
- Forensic examinations of electronic devices involve using software or other tools to copy, analyze, or process the data stored on the devices, which may reveal more personal and sensitive information than manual inspections.
- Some courts have held that forensic searches of electronic devices require reasonable suspicion of a crime, based on the Supreme Court's reasoning in Riley v. California that cell phone searches implicate greater privacy concerns than searches of most other physical items .
- Other courts have held that forensic searches of electronic devices do not require any suspicion of criminal activity, based on the government's broad authority at the border and the diminished privacy expectations of travelers.
- The Supreme Court has not addressed whether the routine border search exception extends to searches of electronic devices, and whether Riley v. California applies to searches at the border.
- Therefore, the legality of warrantless searches of electronic devices at the border remains an unsettled and controversial issue in cyber forensics.

# Circumstantial evidence

- Circumstantial evidence is evidence that **indirectly** proves a fact or a connection between facts by **inference** or **reasoning**.
- Circumstantial evidence is **not** direct evidence that **explicitly** shows the existence or non-existence of a fact or a connection between facts.
- Circumstantial evidence is **often** used in cyber forensics to **support** or **corroborate** other types of evidence, such as digital evidence, physical evidence, testimonial evidence, etc .
- Circumstantial evidence can be **strong** or **weak** depending on the **probability** and **plausibility** of the inference or reasoning that links the evidence to the fact or connection.
- Circumstantial evidence can be **challenged** or **refuted** by **contradictory** evidence, **alternative** explanations, or **logical** flaws.

## Examples of circumstantial evidence in cyber forensics

- The **presence** or **absence** of certain files, programs, or devices on a computer or a network that **suggests** the involvement or the motive of a suspect in a cybercrime .
- The **timestamp** or **metadata** of a digital file or a communication that **indicates** the time or the location of a cyber activity or a cyber event .
- The **pattern** or **frequency** of a network traffic or a user behavior that **implies** the existence or the absence of a cyber attack or a cyber defense .
- The **content** or **tone** of a social media post or a message that **reveals** the identity or the intention of a cyber actor or a cyber victim .
- The **similarity** or **difference** between a digital signature or a cryptographic hash and a known or a standard value that **confirms** or **denies** the authenticity or the integrity of a digital evidence or a digital source .

- Evidence preservation: This involves protecting the integrity and authenticity of the digital evidence by preventing any unauthorized access, modification, deletion, or contamination. Evidence preservation should also comply with the relevant laws and regulations regarding the retention and disposal of the evidence.
- Evidence analysis: This involves examining, interpreting, and extracting relevant information from the digital evidence using various tools, techniques, and methods. Evidence analysis should aim to answer the questions of who, what, when, where, how, and why related to the cyber crime or incident. Evidence analysis should also follow the principles of accuracy, completeness, objectivity, and repeatability.
- Evidence presentation: This involves reporting, summarizing, and communicating the findings and conclusions of the evidence analysis to the relevant stakeholders, such as law enforcement, prosecutors, judges, juries, or clients. Evidence presentation should use clear, concise, and logical language, supported by visual aids, charts, graphs, tables, etc. Evidence presentation should also highlight the strengths and limitations of the evidence, and address any potential challenges or objections.

Cyber laws will need to ensure the security, integrity, and availability of IoT devices and data, as well as the protection of the rights and interests of the users and owners of these devices and data .
- **Shift to remote and hybrid work environments**: The COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated the adoption of remote and hybrid work models, where employees can work from anywhere, using various devices and platforms. This has increased the flexibility, productivity, and efficiency of work, but also the exposure, complexity, and diversity of cyber risks and threats. Cyber laws will need to establish the roles, responsibilities, and liabilities of employers, employees, and third parties in ensuring the cyber security, privacy, and compliance of remote and hybrid work environments .
- **Diversity and disparity in cyber laws**: Cyber laws vary widely across different countries, regions, and sectors, reflecting the different legal systems, cultures, values, and interests of the stakeholders involved. This creates challenges and opportunities for harmonization, cooperation, and enforcement of cyber laws, especially in cross-border and transnational contexts. Cyber laws will need to balance the need for consistency and interoperability of cyber laws with the respect for diversity and sovereignty of cyber laws .
