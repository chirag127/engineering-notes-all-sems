

# CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- Cyber forensic analytics is the application of data analysis techniques and tools to investigate cyber incidents and collect digital evidence for legal purposes .
- Cyber forensic analytics involves the following steps:
  - Identification: Identify the sources and scope of the cyber incident and the potential evidence to be collected.
  - Preservation: Secure and isolate the evidence from tampering or alteration.
  - Extraction: Extract the relevant data from the evidence using forensic tools and methods.
  - Analysis: Analyze the data to reconstruct the events, identify the actors, and discover the motives and methods of the cyber incident.
  - Presentation: Present the findings and conclusions in a clear and concise manner that can be understood by the intended audience, such as law enforcement, courts, or management.
- Cyber forensic analytics faces several challenges, such as :
  - The volume and complexity of data to be analyzed, which may require advanced tools and techniques, such as machine learning, artificial intelligence, and data visualization.
  - The encryption and obfuscation of data by malicious actors, which may hinder the extraction and analysis of evidence.
  - The dynamic and evolving nature of cyber threats, which may require constant updating of forensic knowledge and skills.
  - The legal and ethical issues of cyber forensic analytics, such as privacy, consent, jurisdiction, and chain of custody.
- Cyber forensic analytics is a vital component of cybersecurity, as it helps to detect, prevent, and respond to cyberattacks, as well as to hold the perpetrators accountable and deter future incidents  .

- Cyber space is composed of various **layers** such as physical, logical, informational, and social.
- Cyber space is influenced by various **factors** such as technology, law, policy, culture, and ethics.

### Cyber Crime

Cyber crime is the use of a computer or a network as an instrument to commit illegal acts, such as fraud, identity theft, data theft, cyberextortion, ransomware, phishing, spoofing, etc. Cyber crime can harm individuals, organizations, or even nations by compromising their security, privacy, or financial well-being. Cyber crime can also affect the functioning of critical infrastructure, such as power grids, transportation systems, or health care services.

Some of the main types of cyber crime are:

- **Hacking**: The unauthorized access to or manipulation of a computer system or data, often for malicious purposes. Examples of hacking include stealing passwords, defacing websites, deleting files, or installing malware.
- **Denial of service (DoS) attack**: The intentional disruption of a computer system or network by overwhelming it with traffic or requests, making it unavailable to legitimate users. Examples of DoS attacks include flooding a server with ping requests, sending malformed packets, or exploiting a vulnerability in the system.
- **Software piracy**: The illegal copying, distribution, or use of software without the permission of the owner or the license agreement. Examples of software piracy include downloading cracked software from torrent sites, using counterfeit software keys, or sharing software with others.
- **Phishing**: The fraudulent attempt to obtain sensitive information, such as usernames, passwords, credit card numbers, or bank account details, by pretending to be a trustworthy entity in an email or other online communication. Examples of phishing include sending fake emails from banks, social media platforms, or online retailers, asking the recipients to click on a link or open an attachment that leads to a malicious website or downloads malware.
- **Spoofing**: The falsification of the identity or origin of an online communication, such as an email, a website, or an IP address, to deceive the recipient or hide the true source. Examples of spoofing include sending emails with forged headers, creating fake websites that mimic legitimate ones, or using proxy servers to mask the real IP address.

### Jurisdictional Concerns

- Jurisdiction is the authority of a state or a court to apply the law to a specific case or person.
- Jurisdictional concerns arise in cybercrime cases because cybercrime is non-local in nature, meaning that it can cross geographical and legal boundaries easily and quickly.
- Jurisdictional concerns can pose challenges for law enforcement agencies and prosecutors in investigating and prosecuting cybercrime, such as:
  - Determining which agency or court has the authority and responsibility to handle the case, especially when multiple jurisdictions are involved or affected by the cybercrime.
  - Obtaining evidence and cooperation from other jurisdictions, which may have different laws, procedures, and interests.
  - Respecting the sovereignty and human rights of other states and individuals, while pursuing the cybercrime effectively and efficiently.
  - Balancing the need for global cooperation and coordination, with the respect for national and regional differences and preferences.
- Jurisdictional concerns can be addressed by various means, such as:
  - Applying the existing principles and rules of jurisdiction, such as the territorial principle, the nationality principle, the passive personality principle, and the protective principle, to cybercrime cases, as long as there is a sufficient connection or genuine link between the cybercrime and the state exercising jurisdiction.
  - Developing and adopting new laws and regulations that specifically address cybercrime and its jurisdictional aspects, such as the Information Technology Act of India, which provides for extra-territorial jurisdiction for certain cyber offences.
  - Establishing and enhancing international and regional cooperation and coordination mechanisms, such as treaties, conventions, protocols, agreements, and networks, that facilitate the exchange of information, evidence, and assistance among states and agencies in cybercrime matters, such as the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime, the African Union Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection, and the 24/7 Network of Contact Points for Cybercrime.



### Jurisprudential Inconsistency

- Jurisprudential inconsistency refers to the lack of coherence or harmony in the application of legal principles and rules to different cases of cybercrime and digital forensics.
- Jurisprudential inconsistency can arise from various sources, such as:
  - The non-local and transnational nature of cybercrime, which challenges the traditional notions of territorial jurisdiction and sovereignty .
  - The intangible and volatile nature of digital evidence, which requires special methods of preservation, collection, analysis and presentation .
  - The evolving and complex nature of cyber technologies and digital objects, which may not fit into the existing legal categories and definitions.
  - The diversity and disparity of legal systems and standards across different countries and regions, which may create conflicts of law and gaps in legal protection.
- Jurisprudential inconsistency can have negative impacts on the effectiveness and legitimacy of cybercrime prevention and prosecution, such as:
  - Reducing the admissibility and reliability of digital evidence in court, which may undermine the rights of the victims and the accused .
  - Creating legal uncertainty and ambiguity for the law enforcement agencies, the digital forensic experts and the judicial authorities, which may hamper the investigation and adjudication process .
  - Eroding the public trust and confidence in the rule of law and the justice system, which may encourage more cybercrime and cyber-attacks .
- Jurisprudential inconsistency can be addressed by various measures, such as:
  - Developing and harmonizing the legal frameworks and standards for cybercrime and digital forensics at the national and international levels, which may enhance the legal certainty and consistency .
  - Adopting and implementing the best practices and guidelines for digital evidence management and digital forensic investigations, which may improve the quality and integrity of digital evidence .
  - Promoting and facilitating the cooperation and coordination among the relevant stakeholders, such as the law enforcement agencies, the digital forensic experts, the judicial authorities and the private sector, which may increase the efficiency and effectiveness of cybercrime response .

### Stored Values Cards

- Stored value cards (SVCs) are cards with data encoded in either a magnetic strip or a computer chip that are preloaded with a fixed amount of electronic currency or value.
- SVCs can be classified into three types: closed system, semi-closed system, and open system.
- Closed system cards are issued by a single merchant or group of merchants and can only be used to purchase goods or services from those merchants. Examples are gift cards, phone cards, and transit cards.
- Semi-closed system cards are issued by a third-party provider and can be used to purchase goods or services from a network of merchants that accept the card. Examples are store cards, loyalty cards, and payroll cards.
- Open system cards are issued by a financial institution and can be used to purchase goods or services from any merchant that accepts the card. Examples are prepaid debit cards, travel cards, and remittance cards.
- SVCs offer convenience, security, and flexibility to consumers, merchants, and financial institutions. They can also facilitate financial inclusion for the unbanked and underbanked populations.
- However, SVCs also pose money laundering risks, especially for open system and semi-closed system cards. These risks include:
  - The widespread availability of SVCs (particularly at non-financial outlets), the high loading and card balance value limits of open system cards, and the anonymity offered by closed and semi-closed system cards could be abused by organised criminals for illicit financial transactions, money laundering and bulk cash smuggling, particularly as value limits increase.
  - SVCs can be used to transfer funds across borders without triggering reporting or identification requirements. Criminals can use SVCs to avoid detection by law enforcement and financial intelligence units.
  - SVCs can be used to purchase goods or services that are associated with criminal activities, such as online gambling, pornography, or drugs.
  - SVCs can be used to facilitate cyber crimes, such as fraudulent monetary transfers and counterfeiting of stored value cards. This is typically done through the compromise of a legitimate user’s account credentials.
- To address these risks, some regulatory measures have been implemented or proposed, such as:
  - Requiring the identification and verification of customers who purchase, load, or redeem SVCs above a certain threshold.
  - Requiring the reporting of suspicious or unusual transactions involving SVCs to the relevant authorities.
  - Requiring the registration and licensing of SVC issuers and providers.
  - Requiring the cooperation and information sharing among SVC issuers, providers, merchants, law enforcement, and financial intelligence units.
  - Requiring the implementation of anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing policies and procedures by SVC issuers and providers.
- However, these measures also face some challenges, such as:
  - The lack of a consistent and harmonised definition and classification of SVCs across jurisdictions.
  - The difficulty of enforcing and monitoring compliance with the regulatory requirements, especially for non-financial SVC issuers and providers.
  - The potential impact on the cost, efficiency, and accessibility of SVCs for legitimate users.
  - The need to balance the privacy and security of SVC users with the prevention and detection of money laundering and other crimes.



### Mobile Payments

Mobile payments are transactions that use mobile devices such as smartphones, tablets, or smartwatches to initiate, authorize, or confirm payment for goods or services. Mobile payments can be made through various methods, such as mobile wallets, QR codes, NFC, SMS, or biometrics.

Mobile payments offer convenience, speed, and flexibility for consumers and merchants, but they also pose significant security risks and challenges for cyber forensic analysts. Some of the common cyber crimes related to mobile payments are:

- Data theft: Cyber criminals can steal sensitive data such as card numbers, bank account details, personal identification numbers (PINs), or biometric information from mobile devices or payment terminals. They can use this data to conduct fraudulent transactions, identity theft, or blackmail. Data theft can occur through malware, phishing, skimming, or hacking  .
- Account takeover: Cyber criminals can gain unauthorized access to mobile payment accounts by using stolen credentials, social engineering, or exploiting vulnerabilities in the authentication or verification processes. They can then use the accounts to make unauthorized purchases, transfer funds, or change settings .
- Transaction manipulation: Cyber criminals can alter, intercept, or redirect mobile payment transactions by compromising the communication channels, devices, or applications involved. They can also create fake or duplicate transactions to deceive the parties or the payment systems .
- Denial of service: Cyber criminals can disrupt or disable the availability or functionality of mobile payment services by overwhelming the network, servers, or devices with excessive traffic, requests, or commands. They can also damage or destroy the hardware or software components of the payment systems .

To protect themselves from mobile payment cyber crimes, consumers and merchants should:

- Use secure and updated devices, applications, and networks for mobile payments.
- Enable encryption, password protection, and biometric authentication for mobile devices and payment accounts.
- Avoid using public or untrusted Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or NFC connections for mobile payments.
- Monitor and verify mobile payment transactions and statements regularly.
- Report and block any suspicious or fraudulent activity or messages related to mobile payments.
- Use tokenization, which replaces sensitive data with random codes, for mobile payments.



### Internet Payment Services

- Internet payment services are online platforms that allow users to send and receive money electronically, such as PayPal, Venmo, Stripe, etc.
- Internet payment services are convenient, fast, and widely accepted, but they also pose some risks and challenges for cyber crime investigation.
- Some of the common cyber crimes involving internet payment services are:
  - Identity theft: Cyber criminals may steal personal or financial information of users and use it to access their accounts, make fraudulent transactions, or open new accounts in their names.
  - Phishing: Cyber criminals may send fake emails or messages that appear to be from legitimate payment service providers, asking users to click on a link, download an attachment, or provide sensitive information, such as passwords, PINs, or verification codes.
  - Money laundering: Cyber criminals may use internet payment services to transfer illicit funds from one account to another, or to purchase goods or services that can be resold for cash, in order to conceal the origin or destination of the money.
  - Ransomware: Cyber criminals may infect users' devices with malicious software that encrypts their data or locks their access, and then demand a ransom, usually in cryptocurrency, to restore their functionality.
  - Cryptojacking: Cyber criminals may hijack users' devices or networks and use their computing power to mine cryptocurrency without their consent or knowledge, consuming their resources and increasing their electricity bills.
- Some of the challenges and strategies for cyber crime investigation involving internet payment services are:
  - Obtaining evidence: Cyber crime investigators may face difficulties in obtaining digital evidence from internet payment service providers, due to their privacy policies, encryption methods, or jurisdictional issues. Investigators may need to use legal tools, such as subpoenas, warrants, or mutual legal assistance treaties, to request or compel cooperation from the providers or their intermediaries.
  - Tracing transactions: Cyber crime investigators may face challenges in tracing the flow of funds through internet payment services, especially when they involve multiple accounts, platforms, or currencies. Investigators may need to use analytical tools, such as transaction mapping, network analysis, or blockchain forensics, to identify the source, destination, and purpose of the transactions.
  - Identifying suspects: Cyber crime investigators may face obstacles in identifying the perpetrators or beneficiaries of cyber crimes involving internet payment services, due to their use of anonymity techniques, such as fake identities, proxy servers, or encryption. Investigators may need to use investigative techniques, such as undercover operations, online surveillance, or social engineering, to gather intelligence or evidence about the suspects.



### Cyber stalking

- Cyber stalking is the repeated use of electronic communications to harass or frighten someone, for example by sending threatening emails.
- Cyber stalking can take many forms, such as harassment, embarrassment, humiliation, defamation, slander, libel, monitoring, identity theft, threats, vandalism, solicitation for sex, doxing, or blackmail.
- Cyber stalking is a criminal offense under various state anti-stalking, slander and harassment laws. A conviction can result in a restraining order, probation, or criminal penalties against the assailant, including jail.
- Cyber stalking is different from cyberbullying, which is the use of electronic communication to bully someone, typically by sending messages of an intimidating or threatening nature. Cyberbullying is more common among children and teenagers, while cyberstalking is more common among adults.
- Cyber stalking can have serious psychological and emotional effects on the victim, such as anxiety, depression, fear, paranoia, isolation, low self-esteem, and post-traumatic stress disorder.
- Cyber stalking can be prevented or stopped by taking some measures, such as blocking or reporting the stalker, changing passwords and privacy settings, saving evidence of the harassment, informing friends and family, contacting the authorities, and seeking professional help .



### Cyber extortion

- Cyber extortion is an online crime in which hackers hold your data, website, computer systems, or other sensitive information hostage until you meet their demands for payment .
- It often takes the form of ransomware and distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, both of which could paralyze your business.
- Ransomware is a type of malware that encrypts your files or locks your devices and demands a ransom for their decryption or release.
- DDoS attacks are a type of cyberattack that floods your network or servers with traffic or requests, overwhelming your resources and preventing legitimate users from accessing your services.
- Cyber extortion can also involve threats to expose or damage your reputation, intellectual property, trade secrets, or customer data if you do not pay the extortionists .
- Cyber extortion is a serious threat to your organization's security, privacy, and continuity. It can cause financial losses, operational disruptions, legal liabilities, and reputational damage.
- The FBI's cyber strategy is to impose risk and consequences on cyber adversaries through proactive investigations and partnerships with the private sector, other government agencies, and international counterparts.
- The FBI advises victims of cyber extortion to not pay the ransom, as it does not guarantee the recovery of your data or systems, and may encourage further attacks. Instead, victims should report the incident to the FBI or other law enforcement agencies, and seek professional assistance to restore their operations.

### Cyber warfare

- Cyber warfare means the use of computer technology to disrupt the activities of a state or organization, especially the deliberate attacking of information systems for strategic or military purposes.
- Cyber warfare is usually waged against government and military networks in order to disrupt, destroy, or deny their use.

### Cyber weapons

- Cyber weapons are pieces of computer software or hardware that are used to commit cyberwarfare .
- Cyberwarfare is the use of cyberattacks to damage, disrupt, or destroy the computers, networks, or information systems of another nation or organization.
- Cyberattacks are malicious actions that exploit the vulnerabilities of digital devices or systems to compromise their confidentiality, integrity, or availability.
- Cyber weapons can have different objectives, such as:
  - Espionage: stealing or altering sensitive information or data from the target.
  - Sabotage: causing physical, functional, or mental harm to the target's structures, systems, or living things .
  - Influence: manipulating or deceiving the target's perception, behavior, or decision-making.
- Cyber weapons can have different characteristics, such as:
  - Lethality: the degree of damage or harm that a cyber weapon can cause to the target.
  - Effect: the type and duration of the impact that a cyber weapon can have on the target, such as temporary, permanent, reversible, or irreversible.
  - Employment: the mode and method of using a cyber weapon, such as remotely, locally, actively, or passively.
- Cyber weapons can have different types, such as:
  - Computer viruses: programs that can infect and replicate themselves on other computers, altering or deleting files or programs.
  - Trojans: programs that appear to be harmless or useful, but contain hidden malicious code that can execute unauthorized actions on the target's computer.
  - Spyware: programs that can monitor and collect information or data from the target's computer, such as keystrokes, browsing history, or personal details.
  - Worms: programs that can spread across networks without human intervention, consuming bandwidth or resources, or creating backdoors for further attacks.
- Cyber weapons can have different examples, such as:
  - Stuxnet: a computer worm that targeted and damaged the Iranian nuclear program by causing centrifuges to malfunction.
  - Flame: a spyware program that targeted and collected information from computers in the Middle East, such as audio, screenshots, or network traffic.
  - NotPetya: a ransomware program that targeted and encrypted the files of computers in Ukraine and other countries, demanding payment for decryption.



### ATM frauds

- ATM frauds are cyber crimes that involve stealing or misusing the debit card number, personal identification number (PIN), or other data of the ATM users for illegal purposes.
- ATM frauds can take various forms, such as:
  - Skimming: This is the use of counterfeit devices or software to capture the data on the card's magnetic strip or the user's PIN when they insert their card into the ATM or point-of-sale (POS) terminal .
  - Shimming: This is the use of a thin device inserted into the card slot of the ATM or POS terminal to intercept the data from the chip of the card.
  - Card trapping: This is the use of a device or mechanism to prevent the card from being ejected from the ATM or POS terminal, allowing the fraudster to retrieve it later.
  - Cash trapping: This is the use of a device or mechanism to prevent the cash from being dispensed from the ATM, allowing the fraudster to collect it later.
  - PIN capturing: This is the use of a hidden camera, keypad overlay, or shoulder surfing to observe or record the user's PIN when they enter it at the ATM or POS terminal.
  - ATM hacking: This is the use of malware, phishing, or brute force attacks to compromise the ATM software or network and manipulate the cash dispensing or transaction processing.
  - ATM robbery: This is the use of physical force or threats to coerce the user to withdraw cash from the ATM or hand over their card or PIN.
- ATM frauds can have serious consequences for the victims, such as:
  - Financial losses: The victims may lose their money or incur unauthorized charges on their accounts due to the fraudulent transactions.
  - Identity theft: The victims may have their personal or financial information compromised or used for other illegal activities, such as opening new accounts, applying for loans, or committing tax fraud.
  - Emotional distress: The victims may suffer from stress, anxiety, fear, or anger due to the violation of their privacy and security.
- ATM frauds can be prevented or mitigated by the following measures:
  - User awareness: The users should be aware of the common signs and methods of ATM frauds and report any suspicious or unusual activity or device at the ATM or POS terminal to the authorities or the card issuer .
  - User caution: The users should protect their card and PIN from being stolen or captured by covering the keypad when entering the PIN, avoiding using ATMs or POS terminals that look tampered or damaged, and checking their account statements regularly for any unauthorized transactions .
  - User security: The users should use strong and unique PINs for their cards, change them frequently, and never share them with anyone or write them down. They should also use secure and updated devices and networks when accessing their accounts online and avoid clicking on any suspicious or unsolicited links or attachments .
  - Card issuer security: The card issuers should implement security features and protocols to protect the card and transaction data, such as encryption, authentication, fraud detection, and alert systems. They should also provide customer service and support to the users in case of any fraud or dispute .
  - Law enforcement: The law enforcement agencies should investigate and prosecute the perpetrators of ATM frauds and cooperate with other authorities and stakeholders to prevent and combat the cyber crime .



### Phreaking

- Phreaking is a slang term coined to describe the activity of a culture of people who study, experiment with, or explore telecommunication systems, such as equipment and systems connected to public telephone networks.
- Phreaking involves fraudulent manipulation of telephone signaling in order to make free phone calls or to access restricted services.
- Phreaking originated in the 1960s and 1970s, when phreaks used devices such as whistles or electronic boxes to generate specific tones that could trigger the automatic switches of the phone network .
- Phreaking was also motivated by curiosity, exploration, and the challenge of understanding and manipulating complex systems.
- Phreaking techniques include:
  - Blue boxing: using a device that produces a 2600 Hz tone to seize control of a trunk line and route calls anywhere in the world .
  - Red boxing: using a device that simulates the sound of coins being deposited in a payphone to make free calls .

### Port scans

- A port scan is a technique used by cybercriminals to find out information about a system they are going to target .
- It involves scanning through a network system and determining which ports are open, which are sending data, and which are receiving data .
- A port is a logical endpoint of communication in a network, identified by a number from 0 to 65535.
- A port scan can reveal whether active security devices like firewalls are being used by an organization, and what services or applications are running on the system .
- Port scanning is a fundamental part of the pre-attack phase of a penetration test, as it can help locate vulnerabilities in a network that malicious hackers can use to gain access.
- Port scanning can also be used by ethical hackers and network administrators to monitor and secure their own networks.
- There are several different port scanning techniques, including :
  - Ping scans: A ping is used to check whether a network data packet can reach an IP address without any issues. Ping scans can identify which hosts are alive on a network.
  - Half-open or SYN scans: Attackers can check the state of a port without creating a full connection, by sending a SYN (synchronize) packet and waiting for a response. If the response is a SYN-ACK (synchronize-acknowledge) packet, the port is open. If the response is a RST (reset) packet, the port is closed.
  - Connect scans: Attackers can establish a full connection with each port by sending a SYN packet, receiving a SYN-ACK packet, and sending an ACK (acknowledge) packet. This is a reliable but noisy method, as it can be easily detected by firewalls and intrusion detection systems.
  - Stealth or FIN scans: Attackers can send a FIN (finish) packet to a port, which normally indicates the end of a connection. If the port is closed, it will send back a RST packet. If the port is open, it will ignore the packet. This is a stealthy but unreliable method, as some systems may respond differently to FIN packets.
  - Xmas scans: Attackers can send a packet with multiple flags set, such as FIN, URG (urgent), and PSH (push). If the port is closed, it will send back a RST packet. If the port is open, it will ignore the packet. This is a stealthy but unreliable method, as some systems may respond differently to Xmas packets.
  - ACK scans: Attackers can send an ACK packet to a port, which normally indicates the acknowledgment of a previous packet. If the port is filtered by a firewall, it will drop the packet. If the port is unfiltered, it will send back a RST packet. This can help identify the presence and rules of a firewall.
  - UDP scans: Attackers can send a UDP (user datagram protocol) packet to a port, which is a connectionless protocol. If the port is closed, it will send back an ICMP (internet control message protocol) error message. If the port is open, it will either send back a UDP response or no response at all. This is a slow and unreliable method, as some systems may rate-limit or block ICMP messages.
- Port scanning can be detected and prevented by various methods, such as:
  - Using firewalls and intrusion detection systems to monitor and filter incoming and outgoing traffic
  - Implementing network segmentation and encryption to limit the exposure and access of sensitive data
  - Applying patches and updates to fix known vulnerabilities and bugs in the system
  - Configuring the system to respond in a consistent manner to different types of packets, to avoid revealing information
  - Using honeypots and honeynets to lure and trap attackers into fake systems, and collect information about their activities
  - Performing regular audits and scans on the network to identify and remove any unauthorized devices or services

### DoS for the notes of the Unit 2 - Cyber Forensics in the subject of CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- DoS stands for **Denial-of-Service**, which is a type of cyber-attack that occurs when an attacker seeks to render a computer or other network inaccessible to its authorized users by momentarily or permanently interrupting the normal operations of a host linked to the Internet.
- DoS attacks can have various motivations, such as extortion, sabotage, activism, or cyberwarfare. They can also be used as a distraction or a cover for other malicious activities.
- DoS attacks can be classified into two main categories: **volumetric** and **application layer**. Volumetric attacks aim to overwhelm the network bandwidth or resources of the target by sending a large amount of traffic or requests. Application layer attacks target the specific functionality or logic of the application or service running on the target, such as web servers, databases, or email servers.
- Some common types of DoS attacks are:
  - **SYN flood**: The attacker sends a large number of TCP SYN packets to the target, initiating a connection request, but never completing the handshake. This causes the target to allocate resources for half-open connections, eventually exhausting its memory or CPU.
  - **UDP flood**: The attacker sends a large number of UDP packets to random ports on the target, causing the target to generate ICMP packets in response, which consume network bandwidth and processing power.
  - **Ping of death**: The attacker sends a malformed or oversized ICMP packet to the target, causing the target to crash or reboot due to a buffer overflow.
  - **HTTP flood**: The attacker sends a large number of HTTP requests to the target, either legitimate or malformed, with the intention of exhausting the target's web server resources or application pool.
  - **Slowloris**: The attacker sends partial HTTP requests to the target, keeping the connections open as long as possible, and sending additional headers at regular intervals. This prevents the target from closing the connections or serving other requests, eventually leading to a denial of service.
- DoS attack forensics is the process of identifying, analyzing, and mitigating DoS attacks. It has several steps, such as:
  - **Detection**: The first step is to detect the presence of a DoS attack, by monitoring the network traffic, system performance, and application logs. Some indicators of a DoS attack are abnormal spikes in traffic volume, high CPU or memory usage, slow response times, or error messages.
  - **Identification**: The next step is to identify the source and type of the DoS attack, by examining the characteristics of the attack traffic, such as IP addresses, ports, protocols, payloads, or patterns. This can help to distinguish the attack traffic from the legitimate traffic, and to employ appropriate mitigation techniques.
  - **Mitigation**: The final step is to mitigate the impact of the DoS attack, by blocking, filtering, or diverting the attack traffic, or by increasing the capacity or resilience of the target system. Some mitigation techniques are firewalls, routers, load balancers, proxies, or cloud-based services.
- DoS attack forensics is important for the investigation of crime and law enforcement, as it can help to trace the origin and motive of the attack, and to collect evidence for prosecution or litigation. It can also help to prevent or reduce the damage caused by the attack, and to improve the security and reliability of the system.



### DDoS Attacks

- A DDoS attack is a **distributed denial-of-service** attack that targets websites and servers by disrupting network services.
- A DDoS attack attempts to **exhaust** an application's resources by sending multiple requests to it, so large and massive that the service becomes **unavailable**.
- A DDoS attack works by using multiple **exploited** machines as a source to attack network traffic. These machines are called **botnets** and they are controlled by the attacker .
- DDoS attacks are one of the powerful cyber weapons threat actors use today. They can cause significant **damage** and **loss** to the victims, such as financial losses, reputation damage, customer dissatisfaction, and legal consequences.
- Cyber forensics can help **investigate** and **analyze** DDoS attacks by collecting and examining network traffic data, identifying the source and nature of the attack, determining the intent and extent of the breach, and providing evidence for legal actions .
- The forensic procedure in investigating DDoS attacks may include the following steps:
  - **Preparation**: Define the scope and objectives of the investigation, obtain the necessary tools and authorization, and document the process and findings.
  - **Preservation**: Isolate and secure the affected network and devices, and collect and store the network traffic data in a forensically sound manner.
  - **Analysis**: Examine the network traffic data using various tools and techniques, such as packet capture, flow analysis, signature analysis, anomaly detection, and traceback methods, to identify the characteristics and patterns of the attack, such as the type, duration, frequency, intensity, and origin of the attack.
  - **Reporting**: Summarize and present the findings and conclusions of the analysis, and provide recommendations and suggestions for mitigation and prevention of future attacks.



### Honey Pots

- A **honeypot** is a **cybersecurity mechanism** that uses a **manufactured attack target** to lure cybercriminals away from legitimate targets, and also gathers intelligence about the identity, methods and motivations of adversaries .
- A honeypot can be modeled after any digital asset, including software applications, servers or the network itself .
- Honeypots are typically configured to look like attractive targets to attackers—such as servers running vulnerable versions of operating systems or databases with easy passwords.
- When attackers exploit the honeypot, their activities are monitored and recorded.
- Honeypots can be used to **attract, detect, and deflect** cybercriminals from hacking into legitimate targets.
- Honeypots can also serve **forensic purposes**, quietly gathering data about the hackers who stole it.
- Honeypots can be classified into different types based on their level of interaction, purpose, and deployment.
  - **Low-interaction honeypots** provide minimal services and functionality to the attackers, and are easier to deploy and maintain, but offer less information and realism.
  - **High-interaction honeypots** provide full services and functionality to the attackers, and are harder to deploy and maintain, but offer more information and realism.
  - **Research honeypots** are used to study the behavior and techniques of attackers, and are usually deployed by academic or security organizations.
  - **Production honeypots** are used to protect the network and assets of an organization, and are usually deployed by businesses or government agencies.
  - **Pure honeypots** are real systems that are exposed to the attackers, and are not isolated or monitored by any other system.
  - **Honeynets** are networks of honeypots that simulate a real network environment, and are isolated and monitored by a separate system.



### Malware

Malware is a term that refers to any software or code that is intentionally designed to harm, disrupt, or exploit a computer, network, or server. Malware can take many forms, including :

- **Virus**: A program that infects other software and replicates itself, spreading from one computer to another. A virus usually requires a host file or program to execute its code and cause damage.
- **Worm**: A program that replicates itself and spreads over a network, without the need for a host file. A worm can consume network bandwidth and resources, and create backdoors for other malware to enter.
- **Trojan**: A program that appears to be legitimate but contains hidden malicious functionality. A trojan can steal data, install other malware, or create a remote access for hackers.
- **Ransomware**: A program that encrypts the data or files on a device or network, and demands a ransom for the decryption key. A ransomware can cause data loss, downtime, or financial losses.
- **Spyware**: A program that monitors and collects the user's activity, behavior, or personal information. A spyware can compromise the user's privacy, security, and identity.
- **Adware**: A program that displays unwanted or intrusive advertisements on a device or browser. An adware can slow down the device, redirect the user to malicious websites, or install other malware.
- **Rootkit**: A program that hides itself and other malware from detection and removal. A rootkit can modify the system files, processes, or registry, and gain full control over the device or network.

Malware can be prevented by using antivirus software, firewall, and other security tools, and by following good security practices, such as avoiding suspicious links or attachments, updating the system and software regularly, and backing up the data .



### Virus and Worms

- Virus and worms are two types of malicious software (malware) that can infect and harm computer systems and networks.
- Virus is a program that attaches itself to another executable file and executes when the host file is run. Virus can corrupt, delete, or modify data, or perform other malicious actions on the infected system.
- Worm is a program that can replicate itself and spread to other systems without requiring human action or a host file. Worm can consume network bandwidth, create backdoors, or install other malware on the infected systems.
- Some of the differences between virus and worm are:

| Virus | Worm |
| --- | --- |
| Requires a host file to spread | Does not require a host file to spread |
| Needs human action to execute | Can execute automatically |
| Infects individual files or programs | Infects entire systems or networks |
| Examples: ILOVEYOU, Code Red, Melissa | Examples: Stuxnet, Conficker, Morris |

- Virus and worms can pose serious threats to cyber security and forensics. They can compromise the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data and systems, and hinder the investigation and analysis of digital evidence.
- Cyber forensics is the process of identifying, preserving, analyzing, and presenting digital evidence in a legal context. Cyber forensics can help detect, prevent, and respond to virus and worm attacks, and hold the attackers accountable.
- Some of the techniques and tools used in cyber forensics to deal with virus and worm attacks are:

  - Antivirus software: A program that can scan, detect, and remove virus and worm infections from a system or a network. Antivirus software can also prevent future infections by blocking malicious files or activities. Examples: Norton, McAfee, Kaspersky.
  - Malware analysis: A process of examining the behavior, functionality, and origin of malware using static or dynamic methods. Malware analysis can help understand the purpose, impact, and source of virus and worm attacks, and develop countermeasures. Examples: IDA Pro, OllyDbg, Wireshark.
  - Incident response: A process of managing and resolving a security breach or attack by following a set of steps or procedures. Incident response can help contain, eradicate, and recover from virus and worm attacks, and prevent recurrence. Examples: NIST SP 800-61, SANS Incident Handling, ISO/IEC 27035.

### Warranted Searches

- A warranted search is a search of a computer or electronic media that is authorized by a search warrant issued by a judge or magistrate based on probable cause.
- Probable cause means that there is a reasonable belief that the computer or electronic media contains or is contraband, evidence of a crime, fruits of crime, or an instrumentality of a crime.

- A search warrant may be challenged in court if it is overly broad, lacks probable cause, or violates the Fourth Amendment.

- A search warrant may be issued for various types of cyber investigations, such as cybercrime, cyberterrorism, cyberespionage, or cyberwarfare.

- A search warrant may require the assistance of the owner or custodian of the computer or electronic media, or a third-party service provider, to access, copy, or preserve the data.
- A search warrant may also authorize the use of remote access techniques, such as hacking, malware, or network intrusion, to search or seize data that is located outside the jurisdiction of the issuing authority.
- A search warrant may be subject to time limits, reporting requirements, or other conditions imposed by the issuing authority or the law.



### Warrantless searches for the notes of the Unit 3 - Cyber Investigation in the subject of CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- Warrantless searches are searches conducted by law enforcement without obtaining a warrant from a judge based on probable cause.
- Warrantless searches are generally prohibited by the Fourth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution, which protects the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures.
- However, there are some exceptions to the warrant requirement, such as consent, exigent circumstances, plain view, search incident to arrest, and border search.
- The border search exception allows the government to conduct routine inspections and searches of individuals and their belongings entering or leaving the U.S. border without a warrant or any individualized suspicion of criminal activity.
- The border search exception is based on the government's interest in protecting the nation's sovereignty and preventing the entry of contraband, illegal aliens, and potential terrorists.
- The border search exception applies not only to physical searches of persons and their luggage, but also to searches of electronic devices such as computers and cell phones, which may contain digital evidence of cybercrimes or other offenses.
- However, the scope and limits of the border search exception for electronic devices are not clearly defined by the Supreme Court or by a uniform rule among the lower courts.
- Some courts have held that manual searches of electronic devices at the border are permissible as routine searches, while forensic searches of electronic devices require reasonable suspicion of criminal activity, based on the greater privacy interests and potential harm involved in such searches.
- Other courts have held that any search of electronic devices at the border, whether manual or forensic, requires reasonable suspicion of criminal activity, based on the analogy to the Supreme Court's ruling in Riley v. California, which held that warrantless searches of cell phones incident to arrest are generally unconstitutional.
- Yet other courts have held that no suspicion of criminal activity is required for any search of electronic devices at the border, whether manual or forensic, based on the diminished privacy expectations and the broad government authority at the border.
- The conflicting rulings among the courts create uncertainty and inconsistency for law enforcement and travelers regarding the legality and constitutionality of warrantless searches of electronic devices at the border.
- The issue may eventually be resolved by the Supreme Court or by Congress, which has the power to regulate the border search exception by statute.

### Digital Evidence

- Digital evidence is any information stored or transmitted in binary form that may be relied on in court .
- Digital evidence can be found on various devices, such as computers, hard drives, mobile phones, cameras, etc .
- Digital evidence is often associated with electronic crimes, such as child pornography, credit card fraud, cyberstalking, hacking, etc.
- Digital evidence can be classified into four categories: stored data, transient data, metadata and network data.
  - Stored data is the data that is permanently saved on a device, such as files, folders, emails, etc.
  - Transient data is the data that is temporarily stored in the memory of a device, such as cache, cookies, RAM, etc.
  - Metadata is the data that describes the characteristics or context of other data, such as file name, date, location, etc.
  - Network data is the data that is transmitted or received over a network, such as packets, logs, protocols, etc.
- Digital evidence can be volatile or non-volatile, depending on whether it can be easily altered or deleted.
  - Volatile evidence is the evidence that can be lost or changed if the device is powered off, disconnected, rebooted, etc, such as transient data and network data.
  - Non-volatile evidence is the evidence that can be preserved even if the device is powered off, disconnected, rebooted, etc, such as stored data and metadata.
- Digital evidence can be direct or circumstantial, depending on whether it can prove or imply a fact or event.
  - Direct evidence is the evidence that directly proves a fact or event, such as a video recording of a crime scene, a confession email, etc.
  - Circumstantial evidence is the evidence that indirectly implies a fact or event, such as a log file of a suspicious activity, a deleted file, etc.
- Digital evidence can be authentic or inauthentic, depending on whether it can be verified or tampered with.
  - Authentic evidence is the evidence that can be verified by its source, integrity, reliability and relevance, such as a digital signature, a hash value, a chain of custody, etc.
  - Inauthentic evidence is the evidence that can be tampered with by its source, integrity, reliability and relevance, such as a forged document, a malware, a spoofed IP address, etc.



### Types of Evidence in Cyber Forensics

Cyber forensics is the process of collecting, preserving, analyzing and presenting digital evidence from electronic devices and networks. There are different types of evidence that can be found in digital media, depending on the nature and source of the data. Some of the common types of evidence in cyber forensics are:

- **Logs**: Logs are records of events or activities that occur on a system or a network. They can include operating system logs, application logs, web server logs, firewall logs, router logs, etc. Logs can provide information about the actions, identities, locations and timestamps of the users or devices involved in an incident .
- **Video footage and images**: Video footage and images are visual data that can capture the scenes or activities of interest. They can include surveillance cameras, dash cams, body cams, webcams, mobile phones, etc. Video footage and images can provide evidence of the presence, appearance, behavior and movements of the suspects or victims.
- **Archives**: Archives are collections of files or data that are compressed, encrypted or organized in a specific format. They can include zip files, rar files, tar files, iso files, etc. Archives can contain evidence of the contents, sources, destinations and modifications of the data.
- **Active data**: Active data is the data that is readily accessible and visible to the user or the system. It can include files, folders, documents, emails, messages, etc. Active data can provide evidence of the creation, deletion, modification and usage of the data .
- **Metadata**: Metadata is the data that describes or provides information about other data. It can include file names, file sizes, file types, file dates, file permissions, etc. Metadata can provide evidence of the attributes, properties and history of the data .
- **Residual data**: Residual data is the data that is not easily accessible or visible to the user or the system. It can include deleted files, temporary files, swap files, cache files, etc. Residual data can provide evidence of the previous states, traces and remnants of the data .
- **Volatile data**: Volatile data is the data that is stored in the memory or the cache of the system or the device. It can include processes, services, network connections, open files, etc. Volatile data can provide evidence of the current state, activity and configuration of the system or the device .
- **Replicant data**: Replicant data is the data that is copied, duplicated or replicated from the original source. It can include backups, clones, images, snapshots, etc. Replicant data can provide evidence of the integrity, consistency and authenticity of the data .

These are some of the types of evidence that can be found in cyber forensics. Each type of evidence has its own characteristics, challenges and methods of collection, preservation, analysis and presentation. A cyber forensics examiner should be familiar with the different types of evidence and how to handle them properly and effectively.

### Circumstantial evidence

- Circumstantial evidence is evidence that **indirectly** proves a fact or a connection between facts by **inference** or **reasoning**.
- Circumstantial evidence is **not** direct evidence that **explicitly** shows the existence or non-existence of a fact or a connection between facts.
- Circumstantial evidence is often used in **cyber forensics** to establish the **identity** or the **intent** of the **suspect** or the **victim** of a cybercrime.
- Circumstantial evidence can be derived from various sources of **digital evidence**, such as **computer hard drives**, **mobile phones**, **smart devices**, **social media accounts**, **network logs**, etc .
- Circumstantial evidence can be **corroborated** or **contradicted** by other types of evidence, such as **eyewitness testimony**, **physical evidence**, **confessions**, etc.
- Circumstantial evidence can be **strong** or **weak** depending on the **quality** and the **quantity** of the evidence, the **reliability** of the source, the **plausibility** of the inference, and the **consistency** with other evidence.
- Circumstantial evidence can be **challenged** or **disputed** by the **defense** or the **prosecution** based on the **admissibility**, the **relevance**, the **sufficiency**, or the **interpretation** of the evidence.
- Circumstantial evidence can be **presented** or **explained** in court by using **charts**, **diagrams**, **timelines**, **scenarios**, **hypotheticals**, or **expert witnesses**.
- Circumstantial evidence can be **evaluated** or **weighed** by the **judge** or the **jury** based on the **standard of proof**, the **burden of proof**, the **presumption of innocence**, and the **common sense** of the fact-finders.



### Network Evidence

Network evidence is any data that is transmitted or received over a network and can be used to prove or disprove a fact in a legal case. Network evidence can include:

- Network traffic: The packets of data that are exchanged between devices on a network. Network traffic can be captured and analyzed using tools such as Wireshark, tcpdump, or Nmap. Network traffic can reveal information such as source and destination IP addresses, ports, protocols, timestamps, payload, and encryption methods. Network traffic can be captured using two methods:

  - Catch it as you can method: All network traffic is captured and stored for later analysis. This method guarantees that no important data is missed, but it requires a lot of storage space and processing power.
  - Stop, look and listen method: Only selected data packets are captured and analyzed in real time. This method reduces the storage and processing requirements, but it may miss some relevant data.

- Network logs: The records of events that occur on a network device, such as a router, firewall, server, or workstation. Network logs can provide information such as user activity, system configuration, network connections, errors, and security incidents. Network logs can be stored locally on the device or remotely on a centralized server. Network logs can be analyzed using tools such as Splunk, Logstash, or ELK Stack.
- Network devices: The physical or virtual components that make up a network, such as routers, switches, hubs, access points, firewalls, servers, workstations, or mobile devices. Network devices can contain evidence such as configuration files, firmware, memory, hard disks, or removable media. Network devices can be examined using tools such as FTK Imager, EnCase, or Autopsy.
- Network applications: The software programs that run on a network device and provide various functions, such as web browsing, email, file sharing, or instant messaging. Network applications can generate evidence such as web history, cookies, cache, bookmarks, email messages, attachments, chat logs, or files. Network applications can be analyzed using tools such as FTK, X-Ways, or Magnet Forensics.

Network evidence can be volatile, meaning that it can be easily lost, modified, or overwritten. Therefore, network evidence should be collected and preserved as soon as possible, following the principles of digital forensics:

- Identify: Identify the sources and types of network evidence that are relevant to the case.
- Collect: Collect the network evidence using appropriate tools and methods, while minimizing the impact on the network and the evidence.
- Preserve: Preserve the network evidence by creating a copy or an image of the original data, and verifying its integrity using a hash value.
- Analyze: Analyze the network evidence using various techniques and tools, such as filtering, sorting, searching, decoding, or correlating.
- Report: Report the findings and conclusions of the network evidence analysis, using clear, accurate, and unbiased language.

### Information Technology Act 2000

- The Information Technology Act 2000 (also known as ITA-2000, or the IT Act) is an Act of the Indian Parliament (No 21 of 2000) notified on 17 October 2000 .
- It is the primary law in India dealing with cybercrime and electronic commerce .
- It came into force on October 17, 2000 and applies to the whole of India and to any offense committed inside or outside India by any person   .
- It is based on the United Nations Model Law on Electronic Commerce 1996 (UNCITRAL Model) and the recommendations of the Information Technology Act Expert Committee.
- It aims to provide legal recognition for transactions carried out by means of electronic data interchange and other means of electronic communication, commonly referred to as "electronic commerce", which involve the use of alternatives to paper-based methods of communication and storage of information.
- It also aims to facilitate electronic filing of documents with the Government agencies and further to amend the Indian Penal Code, the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891 and the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto.
- It has 13 chapters, 94 sections and 4 schedules.
- Some of the important provisions of the IT Act are:
  - Legal recognition of electronic records and digital signatures.
  - Regulation of certifying authorities and electronic signature certificates.
  - Offenses and penalties for cybercrimes such as hacking, tampering, publishing obscene material, breach of confidentiality, cyber terrorism, etc.
  - Establishment of Cyber Appellate Tribunal and Cyber Regulations Advisory Committee.
  - Admissibility of electronic records and digital signatures as evidence in courts.
  - Protection of personal data and privacy of individuals.
  - Intermediaries' liability and due diligence.
  - Power of Controller, Adjudicating Officer and police to access, search, seize and intercept electronic records and devices.
  - Exemption from liability of Government for any damage caused by any error in electronic record.
  - Power of Central Government to make rules and regulations for the implementation of the IT Act.
- The IT Act was amended in 2008 to address new challenges and issues arising from the advancement of technology and the growth of cyber space.
- The IT (Amendment) Act 2008 introduced new provisions such as:
  - Recognition of electronic signatures as a mode of authentication of electronic records.
  - Expansion of the scope of cyber offenses and penalties.
  - Inclusion of new offenses such as identity theft, phishing, spamming, child pornography, cyber stalking, etc.
  - Establishment of Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) as the national agency for incident response.
  - Protection of critical information infrastructure from unauthorized access or attacks.
  - Obligation of intermediaries to preserve and retain information and cooperate with law enforcement agencies.
  - Power of Central Government to monitor and intercept any information transmitted through any computer resource.
  - Power of Central Government to block access to any information on the internet in the interest of sovereignty, security, public order, etc.
  - Power of Central Government to prescribe guidelines for data security and data protection.



### Digital signature for the notes of the Unit 5 - Cyber Laws and Authorities in the subject of CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- A digital signature is a mathematical scheme to verify the authenticity of digital documents or messages.
- A digital signature uses a digital certificate from a trust service provider (TSP), such as a certificate authority (CA), to authenticate a signer's identity.
- A digital signature also provides integrity and non-repudiation of the electronic document or message, meaning that it cannot be altered or denied by the sender.
- A digital signature is created by encrypting the hash result of the message with the private key of the sender.
- A digital signature can be verified by decrypting the signature with the public key of the sender and comparing the hash result with the original message.
- A digital signature is different from an electronic signature, which is a broader term that refers to any method of signing an electronic document or message, such as a typed name or a click of a button.
- A digital signature is legally valid and enforceable in the United States under the Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce (ESIGN) Act of 2000 and the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) of 1999 .
- A digital signature is also recognized and regulated by various international laws and standards, such as the European Union's eIDAS Regulation and the International Organization for Standardization's ISO/IEC 27001.
- A digital signature is widely used in various domains and applications, such as e-commerce, e-government, e-banking, e-health, e-learning, and e-voting.
- A digital signature is an essential tool for ensuring the security and trustworthiness of electronic transactions and communications in the digital era.



### Electronic Governance

- Electronic governance or e-governance is the application of information technology for delivering government services, exchange of information, communication transactions, integration of various stand-alone systems between government to citizen (G2C), government-to-business (G2B), government-to-government (G2G), government-to-employees (G2E) as well as back-office processes and interactions within the entire governance framework .
- E-governance aims to enhance the efficiency, transparency, accountability, and participation of the public sector and to improve the quality of life of the citizens .
- E-governance can be classified into four main categories based on the type of interaction involved :
  - G2C: Government to citizen, where the government provides information and services to the citizens through various channels such as websites, portals, kiosks, mobile apps, etc.
  - G2B: Government to business, where the government facilitates the transactions and processes related to the business sector such as taxation, licensing, procurement, etc.
  - G2G: Government to government, where the government coordinates and cooperates with other government agencies at different levels (local, state, national, international) for sharing data, resources, and services.
  - G2E: Government to employees, where the government manages the human resources and internal functions of the public sector such as payroll, training, performance appraisal, etc.
- E-governance can also be classified into three main stages based on the level of sophistication and maturity of the ICT applications:
  - Information: The government provides basic information and data to the public through online platforms such as websites, portals, etc.
  - Interaction: The government enables two-way communication and feedback with the public through online platforms such as email, chat, forums, etc.
  - Transaction: The government allows the public to complete transactions and access services online such as paying bills, applying for licenses, filing complaints, etc.
- E-governance faces various challenges and barriers such as lack of infrastructure, digital divide, cyber security, privacy, legal issues, organizational change, capacity building, etc .
- E-governance requires a holistic and integrated approach that involves the collaboration and coordination of various stakeholders such as government agencies, private sector, civil society, academia, media, etc .

### Regulation of certifying authorities

- A certifying authority (CA) is a trusted third party or entity that issues digital signature certificates for electronic authentication of users.
- The Information Technology Act, 2000 (IT Act) has established a Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA) to regulate the electronic transactions and the activities of CAs .
- The CCA is appointed by the Central Government under Section 17 of the IT Act and has the following functions  :
  - To exercise supervision over the activities of CAs.
  - To lay down the standards, conditions and procedures for the issue of digital signature certificates.
  - To specify the qualifications and experience of the employees of CAs.
  - To specify the contents of written, printed or visual materials and advertisements that may be distributed or used in respect of a digital signature certificate and the public key.
  - To specify the form and content of a digital signature certificate and the key pair.
  - To maintain a database containing the disclosure record of every CA containing such particulars as may be specified by regulations.
  - To certify public keys of the CAs.
  - To resolve any conflict of interests between the CAs and the subscribers.
  - To lay down the duties of the CAs.
  - To maintain a database of public keys in such a manner as may be specified by regulations.
- The CCA also has the power to grant, renew, suspend or revoke the licence of a CA under Section 21 of the IT Act  .
- The CCA may also investigate any contravention of the provisions of the IT Act, rules or regulations made thereunder by a CA and take appropriate action  .
- The CCA may also delegate its powers to a Deputy Controller, an Assistant Controller or any other officer under Section 18 of the IT Act  .
- The CCA is also required to follow the directions of the Central Government on questions of policy relating to national security and public interest under Section 19 of the IT Act  .
- The CCA is also bound by the rules and regulations made by the Central Government under Sections 35 and 87 of the IT Act  .
- The CCA is also subject to the oversight of the Cyber Appellate Tribunal (CAT) under Section 57 of the IT Act  .
- The CCA is also responsible for ensuring compliance with the cybersecurity and privacy laws and regulations of the country, such as the Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) program for the defense supply chain and the new federal cybersecurity legislation passed in June 2022.



### CERNTin for the notes of the Unit 5 - Cyber Laws and Authorities in the subject of CYBER FORENSIC ANALYTICS

- CERNTin stands for **Cyber Emergency Response Team International**. It is a global network of national and sectoral computer security incident response teams (CSIRTs) that cooperate and coordinate to respond to cyber threats and incidents.
- CERNTin was established in 2003 by the European Network and Information Security Agency (ENISA) and the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) to facilitate information sharing and collaboration among CSIRTs across the world.
- CERNTin aims to improve the global cyber resilience by enhancing the capabilities and cooperation of CSIRTs, promoting best practices and standards, and providing training and awareness programs.
- CERNTin operates under the following principles:
  - Voluntary participation and mutual trust
  - Respect for the sovereignty and jurisdiction of each country
  - Protection of the confidentiality and integrity of the shared information
  - Compliance with the applicable laws and regulations
- CERNTin has four main functions:
  - Information exchange: CERNTin facilitates the exchange of technical and operational information among CSIRTs, such as alerts, advisories, indicators of compromise, and mitigation strategies.
  - Coordination: CERNTin coordinates the response to cross-border or large-scale cyber incidents that affect multiple countries or sectors, such as denial-of-service attacks, ransomware, or cyber espionage.
  - Support: CERNTin provides support to CSIRTs in need of assistance, such as technical expertise, tools, or resources, to handle cyber incidents or improve their capabilities.
  - Outreach: CERNTin promotes the awareness and understanding of cyber threats and incidents among the public and private sectors, as well as the general public, through publications, events, and campaigns.
- CERNTin has a steering committee that oversees its strategic direction and governance, and a secretariat that manages its daily operations and activities. CERNTin also has working groups that focus on specific topics or issues, such as malware analysis, cybercrime, or critical infrastructure protection.
- CERNTin is not a legal entity or a formal organization, but rather a network of trusted partners that collaborate on a voluntary basis. CERNTin does not have any enforcement or regulatory powers, nor does it interfere with the national sovereignty or jurisdiction of its members.
- CERNTin is one of the key actors in the global cyber security landscape, along with other international organizations, such as the United Nations, the International Telecommunication Union, the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe, and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization. CERNTin works closely with these organizations to harmonize and complement their efforts and initiatives.

### Penalties and Compensation for Cyber Laws Violations

- Cyber laws are the legal framework that governs the use, protection, and regulation of information and communication technologies (ICTs) in various domains, such as e-commerce, data privacy, cybercrime, intellectual property, and national security.
- Cyber laws vary from country to country, depending on the jurisdiction, the level of development, and the socio-cultural values of the society. However, some common principles and standards have been established by international organizations, such as the United Nations, the Council of Europe, and the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD).
- Cyber laws aim to balance the rights and obligations of different stakeholders, such as individuals, businesses, governments, and civil society, in the digital environment. They also seek to prevent, deter, and punish cyberattacks, cybercrimes, and cyberthreats that may harm the security, integrity, and availability of ICTs and the data and services they provide.
- Penalties and compensation are the legal remedies that can be imposed or awarded by the courts or other authorities for the violation of cyber laws. They are meant to enforce compliance, redress harm, and deter future misconduct. Penalties and compensation can take various forms, such as fines, imprisonment, injunctions, damages, restitution, and disgorgement.
- The type and amount of penalties and compensation depend on several factors, such as the nature and severity of the violation, the intent and motive of the violator, the impact and harm caused to the victim and the public interest, the previous record and cooperation of the violator, and the applicable laws and regulations.
- Some examples of penalties and compensation for cyber laws violations are:

  - Under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996, which protects the privacy and security of health information in the United States, the fine is calculated based on the number of medical records exposed, with fines ranging from $50-$50,000 per record. Fines are capped at $1.5 million per year, but organizations may receive the maximum fine for multiple years.
  - Under the Information Technology Act, 2000, which regulates the use of ICTs in India, a person who damages a computer, computer system, or computer network without the permission of the owner or the person in charge is liable for penalty and compensation to the affected person. The penalty can range from Rs. 1 lakh to Rs. 1 crore, depending on the extent of the damage .
  - Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) of 2018, which harmonizes the data protection laws in the European Union, the fine for non-compliance can be up to 20 million euros or 4% of the annual global turnover of the violator, whichever is higher. The fine is based on the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, the number of data subjects affected, the level of cooperation and remediation by the violator, and the previous record of the violator.
  - Under the Cybersecurity Law of 2016, which regulates the cybersecurity and data protection in China, the fine for non-compliance can be up to 10 times the illegal gains or 1 million yuan, whichever is higher. The fine is based on the circumstances and consequences of the violation, the degree of fault and cooperation by the violator, and the previous record of the violator.
  - Under the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA) of 2020, which enhances the data privacy rights and protections for consumers in California, the fine for non-compliance can be up to $7,500 per intentional violation and $2,500 per unintentional violation. The fine is based on the factors such as the nature and seriousness of the violation, the persistence and frequency of the violation, the number of consumers affected, the willfulness and negligence of the violator, and the assets, liabilities, and net worth of the violator.



### Future Trends and Emerging Concerns for Cyber Laws

- Cyber law is the branch of law that deals with the legal issues related to the use of digital technology, especially the internet and cyberspace.
- Cyber law is constantly evolving and adapting to the rapid changes and challenges posed by the advancement of technology and the increasing reliance on digital platforms and devices.
- Some of the future trends and emerging concerns for cyber law are:

  - **Mobile devices and applications**: As more and more people use mobile devices and applications for various purposes, such as communication, entertainment, education, banking, shopping, etc., there is a need to ensure the security, privacy, and legality of these devices and applications. Mobile devices and applications may pose risks such as data theft, malware infection, unauthorized access, fraud, cyberbullying, etc. Cyber law needs to address these issues and provide clear guidelines and regulations for the development, distribution, and use of mobile devices and applications.

  - **Internet of Things and cloud security**: The Internet of Things (IoT) refers to the network of physical objects, such as sensors, cameras, vehicles, appliances, etc., that are connected to the internet and can collect and exchange data. Cloud computing refers to the delivery of various services, such as storage, processing, software, etc., over the internet. Both IoT and cloud computing offer many benefits, such as convenience, efficiency, scalability, etc., but also pose significant challenges for cyber law, such as data protection, access control, liability, jurisdiction, etc. Cyber law needs to ensure the security and reliability of IoT and cloud services and protect the rights and interests of the users and providers .

  - **Cybercrime and cyberattacks**: Cybercrime and cyberattacks are the malicious and illegal activities that are carried out using digital technology, such as hacking, phishing, ransomware, denial-of-service, identity theft, etc. Cybercrime and cyberattacks can cause significant damage and harm to individuals, organizations, and nations, such as financial losses, data breaches, reputation damage, physical harm, etc. Cyber law needs to prevent, detect, and prosecute cybercrime and cyberattacks and provide effective remedies and sanctions for the victims and perpetrators .

  - **Artificial intelligence and machine learning**: Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are the technologies that enable machines and systems to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as reasoning, learning, decision making, etc. AI and ML have many applications and benefits, such as automation, personalization, optimization, etc., but also raise many ethical and legal issues, such as accountability, transparency, fairness, privacy, etc. Cyber law needs to regulate the development, deployment, and use of AI and ML and ensure that they are aligned with the human values and rights .

  - **Legal technology and innovation**: Legal technology and innovation refer to the use of digital technology to improve the delivery and accessibility of legal services, such as online dispute resolution, smart contracts, blockchain, e-discovery, etc. Legal technology and innovation can enhance the efficiency, quality, and affordability of legal services, but also pose challenges for cyber law, such as quality assurance, ethical standards, professional responsibility, etc. Cyber law needs to support and facilitate the adoption and integration of legal technology and innovation and ensure that they comply with the existing laws and regulations .
