### Brief history of microbiology

1. Microbiology is the study of microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa.

### Proteobacteria
- Proteobacteria is a phylum of gram-negative bacteria discovered by Carl Woese in the 1980s based on nucleotide sequence homology.
- Proteobacteria are further classified into the classes alpha-, beta-, gamma-, delta- and epsilonproteobacteria, each class having separate orders, families, genera, and species.
- The first class of Proteobacteria is the Alphaproteobacteria. The unifying characteristic of this class is that they are oligotrophs, organisms capable of living in low-nutrient environments such as deep oceanic sediments, glacial ice, or deep undersurface soil.
- Proteobacteria is the second largest phylum of hydrogenogenic CO oxidizers, which are composed of mesophilic and neutrophilic bacteria.
- The phylum Proteobacteria includes a wide variety of pathogenic genera, such as Escherichia, Salmonella, Vibrio, Yersinia, Legionella, and many others. Others are free-living (non-parasitic) and include many of the bacteria responsible for nitrogen fixation.
- The renaming of phyla in 2021 remains controversial among microbiologists, many of whom continue to use the earlier names of long standing in the literature.

### Lichens

- Lichens are a complex life form that is a symbiotic partnership of two separate organisms, a fungus and an alga.
- The dominant partner is the fungus, which gives the lichen the majority of its characteristics, from its thallus shape to its fruiting bodies.

### Protozoa

- Protozoa are unicellular eukaryotic microorganisms.
- They are classified as protists, a diverse group of organisms that do not fit neatly into other categories.
- Protozoa are found in a wide range of habitats, including soil, water, and inside other organisms.
- They are heterotrophic, meaning they obtain their food by consuming other organisms or organic matter.
- Some protozoa are parasites, living inside a host organism and causing harm.
- Others are free-living and play important roles in their ecosystems, such as decomposing organic matter or serving as food for other organisms.
- Protozoa reproduce both sexually and asexually, depending on the species.

- Protozoa are studied in the field of microbiology, and their role in disease and ecology is an important area of research.




### Helminths
- Helminths are a group of parasitic worms that belong to the phylum Platyhelminthes (flatworms) and Nematoda (roundworms).
- They are multicellular organisms that can live in the host's intestines, tissues, or blood vessels.
- Helminths can cause a variety of diseases in humans and animals, including intestinal infections, tissue infections, and blood-borne infections.
- Common examples of helminths include tapeworms, flukes, and roundworms.
- Helminths have complex life cycles that often involve multiple hosts and stages of development.
- They can be transmitted through contaminated food or water, contact with infected soil or feces, or by insect bites.
- Prevention and control of helminth infections involve measures such as improved sanitation, safe food and water handling, and the use of antihelminthic drugs.
- Helminths are also used in research to study the immune system and to develop new treatments for autoimmune diseases.



### Viral Structures

- Viruses are small, infectious agents that can only replicate inside the living cells of other organisms.
- They are composed of genetic material, either DNA or RNA, surrounded by a protein coat called a capsid.
- Some viruses also have an outer envelope made of lipids, derived from the host cell membrane.
- The shape of the virus can vary, with some being helical, icosahedral, or complex in shape.
- The genetic material of the virus can be single or double-stranded, linear or circular, and can vary in size.
- The capsid is made up of protein subunits called capsomeres, which can self-assemble to form the capsid.
- The capsid serves to protect the genetic material of the virus and to aid in the transfer of the virus to new host cells.
- The envelope, if present, can contain viral proteins that aid in the attachment of the virus to host cells.
- The structure of the virus plays a crucial role in its ability to infect host cells and replicate.




### Viral Multiplication

Viral multiplication, also known as viral replication, is the process by which viruses produce abundant copies of their genome and infect new hosts. To multiply, a virus must first infect a cell. Susceptibility defines the capacity of a cell or animal to become infected. The host range of a virus defines both the kinds of tissue cells and the animal species which it can infect and in which it can multiply .

The life cycle of viruses varies significantly depending on the species, but there are six fundamental stages: attachment, penetration, uncoating, biosynthesis, assembly, and release. The multiplication of viruses or viral replication can be classified into two types based on the life cycle, i.e., the lytic cycle or the lysogenic cycle.

A virus is an infectious particle that reproduces by "commandeering" a host cell and using its machinery to make more viruses. A virus is made up of a DNA or RNA genome inside a protein shell called a capsid. Some viruses have an external membrane envelope. Viruses are very diverse.

### Arrangement of Bacterial Cells

Bacterial cells can be arranged in several ways, depending on their shape and the plane of division. Here are some common arrangements of bacterial cells:

1. **Singles:** Single cells that are not attached to each other.
2. **Pairs:** Two cells attached to each other, often called diplococci (for cocci) or diplobacilli (for bacilli).
3. **Chains:** Cells attached end-to-end, forming a chain. This arrangement is common in streptococci and streptobacilli.
4. **Clusters:** Cells attached in a grape-like cluster. This arrangement is common in staphylococci.
5. **Tetrads:** Groups of four cells arranged in a square.
6. **Cubical Packets:** Groups of eight or more cells arranged in a cube.
7. **Palisades:** Cells arranged side-by-side in a row, resembling a picket fence.

These are some of the common arrangements of bacterial cells. The arrangement of cells can provide important information about the identity of the bacteria. For example, Streptococcus pneumoniae is often found in pairs (diplococci), while Staphylococcus aureus is often found in clusters.

### Energy production mechanism

Energy production mechanisms are the processes by which cells convert energy from one form to another. These mechanisms are essential for the maintenance of cellular function and the continuation of life.

- One of the primary energy production mechanisms in cells is **glycolysis**, which is the process by which cells convert glucose into ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the primary energy currency of the cell.
- The first phase of glycolysis requires energy, while the second phase completes the conversion to pyruvate and produces ATP and NADH for the cell to use for energy.
- Overall, the process of glycolysis produces a net gain of two pyruvate molecules, two ATP molecules, and two NADH molecules for the cell to use for energy.
- Another energy production mechanism is **thermonuclear fusion**, which takes place in the sun and maintains its luminosity.
- Two forces that keep the sun in equilibrium are the gravitational force and nuclear energy force.
- Energy production from unconventional geothermal and petroleum resources relies on reservoir stimulation by hydraulic fracturing.

### Metabolic Diversity & Pathways of Energy Use

Metabolic diversity refers to the variety of metabolic pathways that exist in different organisms. These pathways allow organisms to use different sources of energy and materials to sustain life. Metabolic pathways can be divided into two main categories: catabolic and anabolic.

Catabolic pathways involve the breakdown of complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy in the process. This energy can be used to fuel other cellular processes. An example of a catabolic pathway is cellular respiration, where glucose is broken down to produce ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the main energy currency of the cell.

Anabolic pathways, on the other hand, involve the synthesis of complex molecules from simpler ones, using energy in the process. An example of an anabolic pathway is photosynthesis, where plants use energy from sunlight to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose.

Different organisms have evolved to use different metabolic pathways depending on their environment and available resources. For example, some bacteria can use sulfur or nitrogen compounds as a source of energy, while others can use light or organic compounds.

In terms of energy production, there are three main pathways that organisms can use: glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. Glycolysis is the first step in the breakdown of glucose and produces a small amount of ATP. The citric acid cycle, also known as the Krebs cycle, produces more ATP and also generates electron carriers that are used in oxidative phosphorylation. Oxidative phosphorylation is the final stage of cellular respiration and produces the majority of ATP.

In summary, metabolic diversity allows organisms to use a variety of energy sources and materials to sustain life. Catabolic and anabolic pathways are involved in the breakdown and synthesis of molecules, respectively. Energy production involves several pathways, including glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. These pathways allow organisms to efficiently produce ATP, the main energy currency of the cell.

### Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is the process by which cells convert nutrients into energy. This process takes place in the mitochondria, which are organelles found in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells.

#### Structure of Mitochondria

- Mitochondria are double-membrane-bound organelles.
- The outer membrane is smooth, while the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
- The cristae increase the surface area for the electron transport chain, which is involved in ATP production.
- The matrix, the space enclosed by the inner membrane, contains enzymes for the citric acid cycle.

#### Cellular Respiration

- Cellular respiration involves the breakdown of organic molecules to produce ATP.
- The process can be divided into four stages: glycolysis, formation of acetyl co-A, the citric acid cycle, and the electron transport chain.

##### Glycolysis

- Glycolysis is the first stage of cellular respiration.
- It takes place in the cytoplasm and involves the breakdown of one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate.
- This process produces a net gain of two ATP molecules and two NADH molecules.

##### Formation of Acetyl Co-A

- The pyruvate produced in glycolysis is transported into the mitochondria.
- There, it is converted into acetyl co-A by the removal of a molecule of carbon dioxide.
- This process also produces one molecule of NADH.

##### Citric Acid Cycle (Kreb Cycle)

- The acetyl co-A produced in the previous stage enters the citric acid cycle.
- This cycle takes place in the matrix of the mitochondria.
- It involves a series of chemical reactions that produce two molecules of ATP, six molecules of NADH, and two molecules of FADH2.

##### Electron Transport Chain and Oxidative Phosphorylation

- The electron transport chain is the final stage of cellular respiration.
- It takes place on the inner membrane of the mitochondria.
- The NADH and FADH2 produced in the previous stages donate their electrons to the electron transport chain.
- The energy released by the transfer of electrons is used to pump protons across the inner membrane, creating a proton gradient.
- This gradient is used to drive the production of ATP through a process called oxidative phosphorylation.

#### ATP

- ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the main energy currency of the cell.
- It is produced during cellular respiration and is used to power various cellular processes.

#### Factors Affecting Respiration

- Several factors can affect the rate of cellular respiration.
- These include the availability of nutrients, oxygen, and the presence of inhibitors or activators of the enzymes involved in the process.

#### Relationship of Carbohydrate Metabolism to Other Compounds

- Carbohydrate metabolism is closely related to the metabolism of other compounds, such as lipids and proteins.
- For example, the breakdown of fatty acids and amino acids can also produce acetyl co-A, which can enter the citric acid cycle.
- Additionally, intermediates of the citric acid cycle can be used to synthesize other compounds, such as amino acids.

### Glycolysis
- Glycolysis is a metabolic pathway that converts glucose (C6H12O6) into pyruvate. 
- The free energy released in this process is used to form the high-energy molecules adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH). 
- Glycolysis is a sequence of ten reactions catalyzed by enzymes.
- Glycolysis is a series of reactions that extract energy from glucose by splitting it into two three-carbon molecules called pyruvates.
- Glycolysis is an ancient metabolic pathway, meaning that it evolved long ago, and it is found in the great majority of organisms alive today.
- In glycolysis, the beginning process of all types of cellular respiration, two molecules of ATP are used to attach 2 phosphate groups to a glucose molecule, which is broken down into 2 separate 3-carbon PGAL molecules.
- PGAL releases electrons and hydrogen ions to the electron carrier molecule NADP+.
- Glycolysis does not require oxygen (In anaerobic conditions pyruvate is converted to lactic acid).
- The wide occurrence of glycolysis in other species indicates that it is an ancient metabolic pathway.



### Formation of Acetyl Co-A

- Acetyl-CoA formation most commonly occurs during glucose catabolism. After carbohydrates have been broken down by digestive enzymes, the first stage of cellular glucose metabolism or glycolysis can begin. Glycolysis is the breaking down of glucose molecules.
- Acetyl-CoA formation occurs inside or outside the cell mitochondria. As a metabolite (a substance necessary for metabolism), acetyl-CoA must be freely available. It can be produced via the catabolism (breakdown) of carbohydrates (glucose) and lipids (fatty acids).
- The overall formation reaction of acetyl CoA may be represented as: pyruvic acid + CoA + NAD + ---> acetyl CoA + NADH + H + + CO 2. This reaction may be called the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvic acid to acetyl CoA.
- Acetyl-CoA (acetyl coenzyme A) is a molecule that participates in many biochemical reactions in protein, carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Its main function is to deliver the acetyl group to the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) to be oxidized for energy production.
- The acetyl CoA combines with a four-carbon molecule and goes through a cycle of reactions, ultimately regenerating the four-carbon starting molecule. ATP (or, in some cases, GTP), NADH, and FADH_2 are made, and carbon dioxide is released.



### Kreb Cycle

- The Kreb Cycle, also known as the Citric Acid Cycle, is a series of chemical reactions used by all aerobic organisms in their energy conversion processes .
- It is an important part of many biochemical pathways and is suggested to be one of the earliest parts of cellular metabolism to evolve .
- The Kreb Cycle is an aerobic process consisting of eight definite steps .
- In order to enter the Kreb Cycle, pyruvate must first be converted into Acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex found in the mitochondria .
- The Kreb Cycle is used by organisms that respire, as opposed to those that ferment, to generate energy through either anaerobic or aerobic respiration .
- In addition to generating energy, the cycle also provides precursors of certain amino acids and the reducing agent NADH, which are used in numerous other reactions .
- The Kreb Cycle is likely the most important part of the process of aerobic respiration because it drives the formation of electron carriers .
- These electron carriers are important because they carry the energy used to create a large number of ATP molecules in the final steps of aerobic respiration .



### Electron Transport System and Oxidative Phosphorylation

- Oxidative phosphorylation is linked to a process known as electron transport.
- The electron transport system (also called the Electron Transport Chain, or ETC), located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, transfers electrons donated by the reduced molecules NADH and FADH2 through a series of electrons acceptors, to oxygen.
- Electrons are passed from one member of the transport chain to another in a series of redox reactions.
- Energy released in these reactions is captured as a proton gradient, which is then used to make ATP in a process called chemiosmosis.
- Together, the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis make up oxidative phosphorylation.



### ATP

- ATP stands for Adenosine Triphosphate.
- ATP is a molecule that carries energy within cells.
- It is the main energy currency of the cell, and it is an end product of the processes of cellular respiration and fermentation.
- All living organisms utilize ATP to carry out their cellular processes.
- ATP is composed of a ribose sugar, an adenine base, and three phosphate groups.
- The energy stored in ATP is released when the bond between the second and third phosphate groups is broken, forming ADP (Adenosine Diphosphate) and an inorganic phosphate molecule.
- This process is called hydrolysis and releases energy that can be used by the cell to carry out various functions.
- ATP can be regenerated from ADP and inorganic phosphate through a process called phosphorylation, which is carried out by the enzyme ATP synthase during cellular respiration or photosynthesis.
- ATP is a crucial component in the process of cellular respiration, which is the process by which cells generate energy from the oxidation of organic molecules.
- During cellular respiration, ATP is generated through the processes of glycolysis, the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle), and oxidative phosphorylation.
- ATP is also involved in other cellular processes such as muscle contraction, active transport, and DNA synthesis.
