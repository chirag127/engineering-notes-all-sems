

# KCS

KCS stands for Knowledge-Centered Service, which is a methodology for creating and maintaining documentation as part of the service delivery process. KCS aims to improve the quality and efficiency of service organizations by capturing, structuring, and reusing the knowledge of service agents and customers. Some of the benefits of KCS are:

- Reduced resolution time and costs
- Increased customer satisfaction and loyalty
- Enhanced agent productivity and morale
- Improved service quality and consistency
- Increased organizational learning and innovation

KCS is based on four basic principles:

- Integrate: Knowledge creation and maintenance should be integrated with the service delivery process, not a separate activity.
- Evolve: Knowledge should be continuously improved and updated based on feedback and usage.
- Collaborate: Knowledge should be shared and reused across the organization and with customers, not hoarded or siloed.
- Reward: Knowledge workers should be recognized and rewarded for their contributions and outcomes, not their activities.

KCS follows a set of practices and processes that are organized into six core elements:

- Strategy: Define the vision, goals, and governance of KCS.
- Content: Define the standards, structure, and quality of knowledge articles.
- Process: Define the workflow, roles, and responsibilities of knowledge workers.
- Technology: Define the tools, systems, and integrations that support KCS.
- People: Define the skills, competencies, and culture of knowledge workers.
- Measurement: Define the metrics, indicators, and feedback mechanisms that monitor and improve KCS.

KCS is not a one-size-fits-all solution, but a flexible and adaptable framework that can be customized to fit different service contexts and needs. KCS is also not a static or fixed methodology, but a dynamic and evolving one that incorporates new learnings and best practices from the KCS community. KCS is designed to be a self-sustaining and self-correcting system that leverages the collective wisdom and experience of service workers and customers.

### Brief history of microbiology

- Robert Hooke, an English natural philosopher and architect, was another pioneer of microscopy, who coined the term "cell" after observing the structure of cork and plant tissues, and also reported the presence of fungi and microfossils.
- In the 19th century, microbiology developed as a distinct field of science, with many discoveries and contributions from various researchers, such as:
  - Louis Pasteur, a French chemist and biologist, who demonstrated the role of microorganisms in fermentation, disproved the theory of spontaneous generation, developed the germ theory of disease, and invented the process of pasteurization .
  - Robert Koch, a German physician and bacteriologist, who established the criteria for proving the causal relationship between a microbe and a disease (Koch's postulates), and identified the causative agents of anthrax, tuberculosis, and cholera .
  - Ferdinand Cohn, a German botanist and microbiologist, who founded the field of bacteriology, classified bacteria into four groups based on their shape and mode of reproduction, and discovered bacterial spores and endospores .
  - Joseph Lister, a British surgeon and pioneer of antiseptic surgery, who applied the principles of germ theory to prevent wound infections by using carbolic acid as a disinfectant .
  - Alexander Fleming, a Scottish biologist and pharmacologist, who discovered the first antibiotic, penicillin, from a mold culture, and opened the era of antimicrobial chemotherapy .
- In the 20th century, microbiology expanded to include the study of viruses, immunology, molecular biology, genetics, biotechnology, and environmental microbiology, with many advances and applications in medicine, agriculture, industry, and ecology . ### Types of microorganisms

- Microorganisms or microbes are microscopic organisms that exist as unicellular, multicellular, or cell clusters. Microorganisms are widespread in nature and are beneficial to life, but some can cause serious harm .
- Microorganisms are divided into seven major types: bacteria, archaea, protozoa, algae, fungi, viruses, and multicellular animal parasites (helminths) .
- Each type has a characteristic cellular composition, morphology, mean of locomotion, and reproduction .
- Bacteria are prokaryotic cells, meaning they lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are classified into two groups based on their cell wall structure: gram-positive and gram-negative. Gram-positive bacteria have a thick layer of peptidoglycan in their cell wall, while gram-negative bacteria have a thin layer of peptidoglycan and an outer membrane. Bacteria can be further classified based on their shape, metabolism, oxygen requirement, and other features.
- Archaea are also prokaryotic cells, but they differ from bacteria in their genetic, biochemical, and structural features. Archaea are often found in extreme environments, such as hot springs, salt lakes, and deep-sea vents. They are classified into three main groups based on their metabolism: methanogens, halophiles, and thermophiles.
- Protozoa are eukaryotic cells, meaning they have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They are unicellular and motile, using cilia, flagella, or pseudopodia for movement. They can be classified based on their mode of nutrition: autotrophic (photosynthetic), heterotrophic (ingesting organic matter), or mixotrophic (both photosynthetic and heterotrophic). Some protozoa are parasites, causing diseases such as malaria, amoebiasis, and giardiasis.
- Algae are eukaryotic cells that are photosynthetic, meaning they use light energy to synthesize organic molecules. They can be unicellular or multicellular, and they have various pigments that give them different colors. They can be classified based on their cell wall composition, morphology, and life cycle. Some algae are beneficial, producing oxygen and serving as food sources, while some algae are harmful, causing algal blooms and toxins.
- Fungi are eukaryotic cells that are heterotrophic, meaning they obtain organic matter from other sources. They can be unicellular (yeasts) or multicellular (molds and mushrooms). They have cell walls made of chitin, a polysaccharide. They reproduce by spores, which can be sexual or asexual. Some fungi are decomposers, breaking down organic matter and recycling nutrients, while some fungi are pathogens, causing diseases such as ringworm, athlete's foot, and candidiasis.
- Viruses are acellular entities, meaning they are not composed of cells. They consist of a nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein coat (capsid). Some viruses also have an envelope, a lipid layer derived from the host cell membrane. Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites, meaning they can only replicate inside a living host cell. They can infect bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes, and even other viruses. They are classified based on their nucleic acid type, capsid shape, envelope presence, and host range. Some viruses cause diseases such as influenza, measles, AIDS, and COVID-19 .
- Helminths are multicellular animals that are parasitic, meaning they live on or in another organism and cause harm. They belong to the phylum Platyhelminthes (flatworms) or Nematoda (roundworms). They have a simple body plan, with a digestive system, a nervous system, and a reproductive system. They can infect humans and animals, causing diseases such as schistosomiasis, tapeworm infection, and filariasis.



### Basic idea of domain bacteria

- Domain bacteria is one of the three domains of life, along with domain archaea and domain eukarya.
- Domain bacteria includes the prokaryotes people encounter on an everyday basis. Prokaryotes are organisms that lack a membrane-bound nucleus and other organelles.
- Most bacterial species are heterotrophic; that is, they acquire their food from organic matter. The largest number of bacteria are saprobic, meaning that they feed on dead or decaying organic matter. A few bacterial species are parasitic; these bacteria live within host organisms and cause disease.
- Domain bacteria can be classified into five main phyla: proteobacteria, cyanobacteria, firmicutes, actinobacteria, and spirochaetes.
- Proteobacteria are the largest and most diverse group of bacteria. They include many common pathogens, such as E. coli, Salmonella, and H. pylori, as well as many beneficial bacteria, such as nitrogen-fixing bacteria and bioluminescent bacteria.
- Cyanobacteria are also known as blue-green algae. They are capable of photosynthesis and produce oxygen as a by-product. They are important for the evolution of life on Earth, as they are believed to be the first organisms to perform oxygenic photosynthesis.
- Firmicutes are mostly Gram-positive bacteria, meaning that they have a thick cell wall that retains a purple dye in a staining technique. They include many bacteria that live in the human gut, such as Lactobacillus and Clostridium, as well as some pathogens, such as Staphylococcus and Streptococcus.
- Actinobacteria are also mostly Gram-positive bacteria, but they have a high GC content in their DNA, meaning that they have more guanine and cytosine bases than adenine and thymine bases. They include many soil bacteria, such as Streptomyces and Mycobacterium, which produce antibiotics and cause tuberculosis, respectively.
- Spirochaetes are bacteria that have a spiral shape and a flagellum that allows them to move in a corkscrew fashion. They include some bacteria that cause syphilis, Lyme disease, and leptospirosis.
- Domain bacteria play a vital role in the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals, such as ethanol, acetone, penicillin, insulin, and human growth hormone. They also help in bioremediation, which is the use of microorganisms to degrade or detoxify pollutants in the environment .



### Proteobacteria

- Proteobacteria is a **phylum** of **gram-negative** bacteria discovered by Carl Woese in the 1980s based on nucleotide sequence homology .
- Proteobacteria are further classified into the **classes** alpha-, beta-, gamma-, delta- and epsilonproteobacteria, each class having separate orders, families, genera, and species.
- Proteobacteria include a wide variety of **pathogenic** genera, such as Escherichia, Salmonella, Vibrio, Yersinia, Legionella, and many others.
- Proteobacteria also include many **free-living** (non parasitic) and **beneficial** bacteria, such as those responsible for nitrogen fixation, hydrogen oxidation, and bioremediation .
- Proteobacteria can live in diverse environments, such as low-nutrient, high-temperature, acidic, alkaline, or anaerobic conditions .
- Proteobacteria have different **morphologies**, such as rods, cocci, spirilla, or filamentous forms .
- Proteobacteria have different **metabolic** pathways, such as aerobic or anaerobic respiration, fermentation, photosynthesis, or chemolithotrophy .
- Proteobacteria have different **interactions** with other organisms, such as symbiosis, commensalism, parasitism, or predation .
- Proteobacteria are important for the **production** of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals, such as antibiotics, vitamins, enzymes, bioplastics, and biofuels .



### Nonproteobacteria Gram-Negative Bacteria

- Nonproteobacteria are a diverse group of bacteria that do not belong to the phylum Proteobacteria, which contains the majority of gram-negative bacteria.
- Nonproteobacteria can be either gram-negative or gram-positive, depending on their cell wall structure and staining properties.
- Nonproteobacteria can have various metabolic modes, such as photoautotrophy, chemoheterotrophy, or chemoautotrophy.
- Nonproteobacteria can be either motile or nonmotile, and some have distinctive shapes, such as spirals, rods, or filaments.
- Some examples of nonproteobacteria are:

  - Chlamydia: a group of obligate intracellular parasites that cause various diseases in humans and animals, such as chlamydia, trachoma, and psittacosis.
  - Spirochetes: a group of motile, spiral-shaped bacteria that have a unique flagellar structure and movement. Some spirochetes cause diseases, such as syphilis, Lyme disease, and leptospirosis.
  - CFB group: a group of gram-negative bacteria that includes the genera Cytophaga, Fusobacterium, and Bacteroides. They are mostly found in the oral cavity and the gastrointestinal tract, and some are involved in periodontal disease and anaerobic infections.
  - Planctomycetes: a group of gram-negative bacteria that have unusual features, such as a lack of peptidoglycan in their cell wall, a compartmentalized cytoplasm, and budding reproduction. They are mostly aquatic and play a role in the nitrogen cycle.
  - Phototrophic bacteria: a group of bacteria that use light as an energy source and can perform photosynthesis. They include cyanobacteria, green sulfur bacteria, green nonsulfur bacteria, purple sulfur bacteria, and purple nonsulfur bacteria. They have different pigments and electron donors for photosynthesis, and some can fix nitrogen.



### Gram-positive bacteria

- Gram-positive bacteria are bacteria that have a thick cell wall composed of peptidoglycan and teichoic acids .
- Gram-positive bacteria can be distinguished from gram-negative bacteria by the Gram stain test, which involves a chemical dye that stains the cell wall purple or blue  .
- Gram-positive bacteria include many important pathogens, such as Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus, Clostridium, and Listeria .
- Gram-positive bacteria can cause various infections, such as skin infections, pneumonia, meningitis, anthrax, botulism, and food poisoning .
- Gram-positive bacteria can be treated with antibiotics that target the cell wall, such as penicillin, vancomycin, and daptomycin .
- Gram-positive bacteria can also undergo bacterial transformation, which is the uptake and incorporation of DNA from the environment.



### Lichens

- Lichens are **simple, slow-growing plants** that typically form a **low crusty, leaflike, or branching growth** on rocks, walls, and trees .
- Lichens are **composite organisms** that consist of a **symbiotic association** of algae (usually green) or cyanobacteria and fungi (mostly ascomycetes and basidiomycetes) .
- Lichens are **found worldwide** and occur in a variety of environmental conditions.
- Lichens have different forms, such as **foliose** (leaflike), **crustose** (crusty), or **fruticose** (shrublike) .
- Lichens often play an important part in the **weathering of rocks** and include some that are sources of **natural dyes**.
- Lichens are also used as **indicators of air quality**, as they are sensitive to pollutants.
- Lichens have some **medicinal properties**, such as antibacterial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects.
- Lichens are also a source of **food and shelter** for some animals, such as insects, birds, and mammals.



### Algae

- Algae are a large and diverse group of photosynthetic eukaryotic organisms that are typically aquatic and include seaweeds and many single-celled forms .
- Algae are classified into several phyla, divisions, or classes based on their pigments, cell wall composition, flagella, and other features .
- Some of the major groups of algae are:
  - Green algae (Chlorophyta): They have chlorophyll a and b, store starch, and have cellulose in their cell walls. They are the most closely related to plants among algae. They can be unicellular, colonial, or multicellular. Examples: Spirogyra, Ulva, Volvox.
  - Brown algae (Phaeophyta): They have chlorophyll a and c, and a brown pigment called fucoxanthin. They store laminarin and mannitol, and have alginic acid in their cell walls. They are mostly marine and multicellular. Examples: Fucus, Sargassum, kelps.
  - Red algae (Rhodophyta): They have chlorophyll a and d, and a red pigment called phycoerythrin. They store floridean starch, and have agar and carrageenan in their cell walls. They are mostly marine and multicellular. Examples: Porphyra, Corallina, Gracilaria.
  - Diatoms (Bacillariophyta): They have chlorophyll a and c, and a yellow-brown pigment called diatoxanthin. They store oil or chrysolaminarin, and have silica in their cell walls. They are unicellular or colonial, and have two valves that fit together like a box. Examples: Navicula, Pinnularia, Coscinodiscus.
  - Dinoflagellates (Dinophyta): They have chlorophyll a and c, and a yellow-brown pigment called peridinin. They store starch, and have cellulose plates in their cell walls. They are unicellular, and have two flagella that make them spin. Some of them are bioluminescent or cause red tides. Examples: Ceratium, Noctiluca, Gonyaulax.
  - Euglenoids (Euglenophyta): They have chlorophyll a and b, and a yellow-green pigment called xanthophyll. They store paramylon, and have a proteinaceous pellicle instead of a cell wall. They are unicellular, and have one or two flagella and an eyespot. Some of them are heterotrophic or mixotrophic. Examples: Euglena, Phacus, Trachelomonas.
- Algae play an important role in the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals, such as:
  - Agar: A polysaccharide extracted from red algae, used as a solidifying agent in microbiology, biotechnology, and food industry.
  - Carrageenan: A polysaccharide extracted from red algae, used as a thickening, stabilizing, and gelling agent in food, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals.
  - Alginic acid: A polysaccharide extracted from brown algae, used as an emulsifier, stabilizer, and binder in food, textiles, and pharmaceuticals.
  - Iodine: A chemical element obtained from brown algae, used as an antiseptic, disinfectant, and dietary supplement.
  - Beta-carotene: A pigment and provitamin A synthesized by green algae and diatoms, used as a food colorant, antioxidant, and nutritional supplement.
  - Biodiesel: A renewable fuel derived from the oil of algae, used as an alternative to fossil fuels.



### Protozoa

- Protozoa are **unicellular, microscopic, and primitive types of animals**  that belong to the kingdom Protista .
- They are **heterotrophic**, meaning they obtain organic carbon from other sources, such as bacteria, algae, or other protozoa.
- They have a **true nucleus** and other membrane-bound organelles, such as mitochondria, Golgi apparatus, and endoplasmic reticulum.
- They can move in water by various means, such as **flagella, cilia, pseudopodia, or contractile vacuoles**.
- They can reproduce by **asexual** methods, such as binary fission, multiple fission, or budding, or by **sexual** methods, such as conjugation, syngamy, or gametogenesis .
- They can be classified into four major groups based on their mode of locomotion: **Sarcodina, Mastigophora, Ciliophora, and Sporozoa** .
- Some examples of protozoa are:

  - **Amoeba**, a sarcodine that moves by pseudopodia and feeds by phagocytosis .
  - **Paramecium**, a ciliate that moves by cilia and feeds by oral groove and food vacuoles .
  - **Euglena**, a flagellate that moves by flagella and has both plant-like and animal-like features, such as chloroplasts and eyespot .
  - **Plasmodium**, a sporozoan that causes malaria, a deadly disease transmitted by mosquitoes .



### Helminthes

- Helminthes are worm-like parasites that can infect humans and other animals  .
- They are invertebrates characterized by elongated, flat or round bodies.
- They belong to two major phyla: Platyhelminthes (flatworms) and Nematoda (roundworms) .
- Platyhelminthes include flukes (trematodes) and tapeworms (cestodes), which have flattened bodies and complex life cycles involving intermediate hosts .
- Nematoda include roundworms (nematodes), which have cylindrical bodies and simple life cycles involving eggs and larvae .
- Helminthes can inhabit various organs of the human body, such as the intestines, liver, blood vessels, lungs, and skin .
- Helminthes can cause various diseases, such as intestinal worms, schistosomiasis, filariasis, and trichinosis .
- Helminthes can be diagnosed by microscopic examination of stool, urine, blood, or tissue samples.
- Helminthes can be treated by anthelmintic drugs, which kill or expel the worms from the body.
- Helminthes can be prevented by improving sanitation, hygiene, and vector control .



### Viral Structures

- Viruses are non-living infectious particles that can only replicate inside living cells.
- Viruses have a simple structure that consists of three main components:
  - **Nucleic acid**: The genetic material of the virus, either DNA or RNA, that contains the information for protein synthesis and replication . The nucleic acid is found within the inner core of the virus.
  - **Capsid**: The protein layer or shell that encloses and protects the nucleic acid . The capsid is composed of subunits called capsomeres that can have different shapes and arrangements . The capsid determines the shape and size of the virus.
  - **Envelope**: A lipid bilayer membrane that surrounds the capsid in some viruses . The envelope is derived from the host cell membrane and contains viral proteins that help the virus attach and enter the host cell .
- Viruses can be classified into different types based on their nucleic acid, capsid, and envelope characteristics :
  - **DNA viruses**: Viruses that have DNA as their nucleic acid. They can be single-stranded (ssDNA) or double-stranded (dsDNA), linear or circular, and have icosahedral, helical, or complex capsids. Some examples are adenoviruses, herpesviruses, poxviruses, and papillomaviruses  .
  - **RNA viruses**: Viruses that have RNA as their nucleic acid. They can be single-stranded (ssRNA) or double-stranded (dsRNA), positive-sense (+) or negative-sense (-), and have icosahedral, helical, or complex capsids. Some examples are coronaviruses, influenza viruses, polioviruses, and retroviruses  .
  - **Enveloped viruses**: Viruses that have an envelope surrounding their capsid. They can be DNA or RNA viruses, and have icosahedral, helical, or complex capsids. Some examples are HIV, hepatitis B virus, measles virus, and rabies virus  .
  - **Non-enveloped viruses**: Viruses that do not have an envelope surrounding their capsid. They can be DNA or RNA viruses, and have icosahedral, helical, or complex capsids. Some examples are adenoviruses, polioviruses, rotaviruses, and noroviruses  .
- Viruses have different sizes and shapes, but they are generally much smaller than bacteria and can only be seen with an electron microscope  . The smallest viruses are about 20 nm in diameter, while the largest viruses are about 500 nm in length . The most common shapes of viruses are icosahedral, helical, and complex :
  - **Icosahedral viruses**: Viruses that have a capsid with 20 triangular faces and 12 vertices, forming a symmetrical polyhedron. The capsid can be smooth or have spikes or fibers on its surface. The nucleic acid is packed inside the capsid. Some examples are adenoviruses, herpesviruses, and polioviruses .
  - **Helical viruses**: Viruses that have a capsid with a cylindrical or rod-shaped structure, formed by a single or multiple helices of capsomeres. The nucleic acid is coiled inside the capsid. Some examples are tobacco mosaic virus, rabies virus, and influenza virus .
  - **Complex viruses**: Viruses that have a capsid with a combination of icosahedral and helical structures, or other additional components such as tails, fibers, or envelopes. The nucleic acid is located in the head or core of the virus. Some examples are bacteriophages, poxviruses, and coronavirus .



### Viral Multiplication

- Viral multiplication is the process of forming new viruses by using the host cell's machinery and resources.
- Viruses can only multiply inside living cells that are susceptible and permissive to their infection.
- The viral life cycle consists of six basic stages: attachment, penetration, uncoating, biosynthesis, assembly and release.
- Attachment: The virus binds to specific receptors on the surface of the host cell.
- Penetration: The virus or its genetic material enters the host cell by endocytosis or membrane fusion.
- Uncoating: The viral capsid is removed and the viral genome is released into the cytoplasm or nucleus of the host cell.
- Biosynthesis: The viral genome is replicated and the viral proteins are synthesized using the host cell's enzymes and ribosomes.
- Assembly: The newly synthesized viral components are assembled into mature virions.
- Release: The new virions are released from the host cell by budding or lysis.
- The viral multiplication can follow two different cycles: lytic cycle or lysogenic cycle.
- Lytic cycle: The virus replicates and destroys the host cell, releasing new virions. This cycle is typical of bacteriophages and some animal viruses.
- Lysogenic cycle: The virus integrates its genome into the host cell's genome and remains dormant until a stimulus triggers its activation. The virus then enters the lytic cycle and produces new virions. This cycle is typical of some bacteriophages and some animal viruses, such as herpesviruses and retroviruses.



### Role of microorganisms in the production of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals

- Microorganisms are used in industry to produce a variety of organic compounds including acids, growth stimulants, and enzymes.
- Microorganisms can be genetically modified or engineered to aid in large-scale production of desired products.
- Some examples of industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals produced by microorganisms are:

  - Amino acids: Microbes make only the L-isomer of amino acids, which are essential for protein synthesis and human nutrition. Some amino acids, such as lysine and glutamate, are used as food additives and flavor enhancers .
  - Citric acid: Produced by the mold Aspergillus niger from sugar or molasses. Citric acid is widely used as a preservative, flavoring agent, and acidulant in food and beverages .
  - Enzymes: Produced by various bacteria, fungi, and yeasts. Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions and have many applications in food, detergent, textile, paper, leather, and pharmaceutical industries. Some examples of enzymes are amylases, proteases, lipases, cellulases, and pectinases .
  - Antibiotics: Produced by bacteria and fungi that inhibit the growth of other microorganisms. Antibiotics are used to treat bacterial infections and diseases. Some examples of antibiotics are penicillin, streptomycin, tetracycline, and erythromycin .
  - Vaccines: Produced by attenuated or killed microorganisms that stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies against specific pathogens. Vaccines are used to prevent infectious diseases and epidemics. Some examples of vaccines are polio, measles, mumps, rubella, and hepatitis .
  - Vitamins: Produced by bacteria and fungi that synthesize essential organic compounds that cannot be made by humans. Vitamins are used to supplement the diet and prevent deficiency diseases. Some examples of vitamins are B12, riboflavin, and biotin .
  - Biofuels: Produced by microorganisms that convert organic substrates into combustible gases or liquids. Biofuels are used as alternative sources of energy and reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Some examples of biofuels are ethanol, methanol, hydrogen, and methane .

- In the industrial process, microorganisms act like chemical factories. To be effective, they should liberate a large amount of a single product that can be efficiently isolated and purified. The microorganisms should be easy to maintain and cultivate, and should have genetic stability with infrequent mutations.
- Microorganisms can be cultured in different types of bioreactors, such as batch, continuous, or fed-batch, depending on the product and the process requirements. The bioreactors provide optimal conditions for the growth and metabolism of the microorganisms, such as temperature, pH, oxygen, nutrients, and agitation.
- Microorganisms can also produce exopolysaccharides, which are sugar-composed polymers secreted by some bacteria and fungi into the external environment. Exopolysaccharides have various industrial applications, such as thickeners, stabilizers, emulsifiers, gelling agents, and biofilms.



## Unit 2 - Functional Anatomy of Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells: Size, shape, and arrangement of bacterial cells. Structure and function of cells

- Bacterial cells are microscopic organisms that cannot be seen by the naked eye. They are measured in micrometers (µm), which are one-thousandth of a millimeter. The average size of a bacterial cell varies between 0.2 and 2.0 µm  .

### Energy production mechanism

- Energy production is the process of converting energy from one form to another in living systems.

  - Glycolysis can occur in the absence or presence of oxygen. In the absence of oxygen, pyruvate is converted into lactate or ethanol, regenerating NAD+ for glycolysis to continue. This is called fermentation.

The citric acid cycle produces more ATP, NADH, and FADH2, which are used by the electron transport chain to generate a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. ### Relationship of carbohydrate metabolism to other compounds

### Glycolysis

- Glycolysis is a metabolic pathway that converts glucose, a six-carbon sugar, into two molecules of pyruvate, a three-carbon compound.
- Glycolysis does not require oxygen and can occur in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. In anaerobic conditions, pyruvate is further converted into lactic acid or ethanol.
- Glycolysis is an ancient metabolic pathway that is found in most living organisms. It is the first stage of cellular respiration, which produces energy in the form of ATP and NADH.
- Glycolysis consists of ten enzyme-catalyzed reactions that can be divided into two phases: the preparatory phase and the payoff phase.
- In the preparatory phase, two molecules of ATP are used to phosphorylate glucose and split it into two molecules of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P).
- In the payoff phase, each G3P molecule is oxidized and phosphorylated, producing two molecules of NADH and four molecules of ATP. The final product is two molecules of pyruvate.



### Formation of Acetyl Co-A

- Acetyl Co-A (acetyl coenzyme A) is a molecule that participates in many biochemical reactions in protein, carbohydrate and lipid metabolism.
- Its main function is to deliver the acetyl group to the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) to be oxidized for energy production.
- Acetyl Co-A formation most commonly occurs during glucose catabolism.
- After carbohydrates have been broken down by digestive enzymes, the first stage of cellular glucose metabolism or glycolysis can begin.
- Glycolysis is the breaking down of glucose molecules into two molecules of pyruvate, two molecules of ATP, and two molecules of NADH.
- Pyruvate then enters the mitochondria, where it is converted into acetyl Co-A by a complex enzyme called pyruvate dehydrogenase.
- The overall formation reaction of acetyl Co-A may be represented as: pyruvic acid + CoA + NAD+ → acetyl Co-A + NADH + H+ + CO2.
- This reaction may be called the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvic acid to acetyl Co-A, because it involves the removal of a carboxyl group (CO2) and the transfer of electrons to NAD+.
- Acetyl Co-A formation also occurs during the breakdown of fatty acids, which are first converted into acyl Co-A molecules and then cleaved into two-carbon units that form acetyl Co-A.
- Acetyl Co-A can also be synthesized from some amino acids, ketone bodies, and alcohol.
- Acetyl Co-A is the common link between glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and the citric acid cycle, where it is further oxidized to generate more ATP, NADH, and FADH2.



### Kreb cycle

- The Kreb cycle, also known as the citric acid cycle, is a series of chemical reactions that occur in the mitochondria of aerobic organisms  .
- The Kreb cycle is part of the cellular respiration process, which converts glucose and oxygen into carbon dioxide, water, and energy (ATP)  .
- The Kreb cycle requires the input of acetyl-CoA, which is derived from the oxidation of pyruvate, the end product of glycolysis  .
- The Kreb cycle consists of eight steps, each catalyzed by a specific enzyme   :
  - Step 1: Acetyl-CoA combines with oxaloacetate (a four-carbon molecule) to form citrate (a six-carbon molecule) and release coenzyme A.
  - Step 2: Citrate is rearranged to form isocitrate, a more reactive form of citrate.
  - Step 3: Isocitrate is oxidized to form alpha-ketoglutarate (a five-carbon molecule) and release carbon dioxide and NADH (a high-energy electron carrier).
  - Step 4: Alpha-ketoglutarate is oxidized to form succinyl-CoA (a four-carbon molecule) and release carbon dioxide and NADH.
  - Step 5: Succinyl-CoA is converted to succinate and release coenzyme A and GTP (a high-energy phosphate carrier).
  - Step 6: Succinate is oxidized to form fumarate and release FADH2 (another high-energy electron carrier).
  - Step 7: Fumarate is hydrated to form malate, a more reactive form of fumarate.
  - Step 8: Malate is oxidized to form oxaloacetate and release NADH.
- The Kreb cycle produces two molecules of carbon dioxide, three molecules of NADH, one molecule of FADH2, and one molecule of GTP per acetyl-CoA molecule   .
- The Kreb cycle also provides precursors for the synthesis of certain amino acids, such as aspartate, glutamate, and alanine .
- The Kreb cycle is regulated by the availability of substrates (such as acetyl-CoA, NAD+, and oxaloacetate) and the feedback inhibition of enzymes (such as citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase) by the products (such as ATP, NADH, and succinyl-CoA)   .
- The Kreb cycle is linked to the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation, which use the electrons from NADH and FADH2 to generate a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane and drive the synthesis of ATP by ATP synthase   .
- The Kreb cycle is one of the oldest metabolic pathways, as it is found in almost all living organisms that use oxygen for respiration  . It is thought to have evolved from an ancient anaerobic pathway that used sulfur instead of oxygen as the final electron acceptor .



### Electron Transport System and Oxidative Phosphorylation

- Electron transport system (ETS) is a series of proteins and electron carriers located in the inner mitochondrial membrane that transfers electrons from reduced molecules such as NADH and FADH2 to oxygen, the final electron acceptor .
- Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is a process that uses the energy released by the electron transport system to generate ATP, the main energy currency of the cell   .
- The main steps of oxidative phosphorylation are:
  - Electron transport chain (ETC): Electrons donated by NADH and FADH2 are passed from one member of the ETS to another in a series of redox reactions. Each member of the ETS has a different affinity for electrons, and the electrons move from a higher to a lower energy level. This releases energy that is used to pump protons (H+) from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient   .
  - Chemiosmosis: The proton gradient created by the ETC drives the synthesis of ATP by a protein complex called ATP synthase. ATP synthase allows protons to flow back to the matrix through a channel, and uses the energy of this flow to catalyze the formation of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi). This process is called chemiosmosis  .
- The overall equation of oxidative phosphorylation is:

  NADH + FADH2 + O2 + ADP + Pi → NAD+ + FAD + H2O + ATP

- The amount of ATP produced by oxidative phosphorylation depends on the type of electron donor and the efficiency of the ETS and ATP synthase. In general, one molecule of NADH can produce about 2.5 ATP, and one molecule of FADH2 can produce about 1.5 ATP .
- Oxidative phosphorylation is regulated by several factors, such as the availability of oxygen, electron donors, ADP, and Pi, as well as the pH and membrane potential of the mitochondria .



### ATP and Cellular Respiration

- ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the energy-carrying molecule used in cells because it can release energy very quickly.
- Cellular respiration refers to the breakdown of glucose and other respiratory substrates to make ATP in the presence of an inorganic electron acceptor such as oxygen .
- Cellular respiration is a series of chemical reactions that involve three main steps: glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation .
- Glycolysis is the first step of cellular respiration that occurs in the cytoplasm. It splits one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, producing a net of 2 ATP and 2 NADH (a reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, an electron carrier) per glucose molecule.
- Pyruvate is then transported into the mitochondria, where it is converted into acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) by a complex of enzymes called pyruvate dehydrogenase. This process also produces 1 NADH and 1 CO2 per pyruvate molecule.
- Acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle) in the mitochondrial matrix. It combines with oxaloacetate to form citrate, which then goes through a series of reactions that regenerate oxaloacetate and release 2 CO2, 3 NADH, 1 FADH2 (a reduced form of flavin adenine dinucleotide, another electron carrier), and 1 ATP per acetyl-CoA molecule.
- The NADH and FADH2 produced in glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, and the citric acid cycle donate their electrons to the electron transport chain (ETC) in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The ETC consists of a series of protein complexes and electron carriers that transfer the electrons from one to another, releasing energy along the way. The energy is used to pump protons (H+) across the membrane, creating a proton gradient .
- The proton gradient drives the synthesis of ATP by a protein complex called ATP synthase, which allows the protons to flow back into the matrix through its channel. This process is called oxidative phosphorylation, because it couples the oxidation of NADH and FADH2 to the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP. Oxidative phosphorylation can produce up to 28 ATP per glucose molecule .
- The final electron acceptor in the ETC is oxygen, which combines with the electrons and protons to form water. This is why cellular respiration requires oxygen and produces water as a by-product .
- The total number of ATP molecules produced by cellular respiration can vary depending on the efficiency of glycolysis, the availability of oxygen, and the type of respiratory substrate. However, a typical estimate is that one molecule of glucose can produce a net of 30-32 ATP molecules.
- The rate of cellular respiration can be affected by several factors, such as temperature, pH, substrate concentration, enzyme activity, oxygen availability, and hormonal regulation.
