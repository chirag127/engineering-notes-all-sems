## List of Experiments

- An experiment is a scientific procedure that tests a hypothesis or a prediction by manipulating one or more variables and observing the outcomes.
- Experiments are essential for testing scientific theories and discovering new phenomena and laws of nature.
- There are many types of experiments, depending on the field of study, the research question, the design, and the methods of data collection and analysis.
- Some examples of experiments are:

  - Controlled experiments: These are experiments where the researcher controls all the variables except one, which is manipulated to test its effect on the outcome. The outcome is measured and compared with a control group that does not receive the manipulation. This type of experiment is common in natural and physical sciences, such as biology, chemistry, and physics.
  - Randomized experiments: These are experiments where the researcher randomly assigns the participants or units to different groups that receive different treatments or interventions. The outcome is measured and compared across the groups to test the causal effect of the treatment or intervention. This type of experiment is common in social and behavioral sciences, such as psychology, economics, and education.
  - Natural experiments: These are experiments where the researcher exploits a natural occurrence or event that creates different groups that are similar in all aspects except one, which is affected by the occurrence or event. The outcome is measured and compared across the groups to test the causal effect of the occurrence or event. This type of experiment is common in fields where controlled or randomized experiments are not feasible or ethical, such as ecology, epidemiology, and history.
  - Quasi-experiments: These are experiments where the researcher uses existing groups that are not randomly assigned or controlled, but are similar in some aspects and different in others. The outcome is measured and compared across the groups to test the causal effect of the difference. This type of experiment is common in fields where natural or randomized experiments are not possible or practical, such as sociology, political science, and public policy.
  - Field experiments: These are experiments where the researcher conducts the experiment in a natural setting, such as a school, a workplace, or a community, rather than in a laboratory or a controlled environment. The outcome is measured and compared across the groups to test the causal effect of the manipulation or intervention. This type of experiment is common in fields where the researcher wants to test the generalizability or external validity of the findings, such as anthropology, criminology, and development studies.

##### 1. To determine the wavelength of sodium light by Newton’s ring experiment.

- Newton’s ring experiment is a method of measuring the wavelength of light using the phenomenon of interference.
- The setup consists of a plano-convex lens of large radius of curvature placed on a plane glass plate. A monochromatic light source, such as a sodium lamp, is used to illuminate the lens from above.
- The light rays reflected from the upper and lower surfaces of the air film between the lens and the plate interfere with each other to form a pattern of concentric bright and dark rings, called Newton’s rings, on a screen placed below the lens.
- The radius of the nth bright ring is given by the formula:

  `r_n = sqrt[(n + 1/2)Rλ]`

  where R is the radius of curvature of the lens, λ is the wavelength of light, and n is the order of the ring.

- To determine the wavelength of sodium light, the following steps are followed:

  - Measure the diameter of several bright rings using a traveling microscope and note down the corresponding values of n.
  - Plot a graph of D^2 (the square of the diameter) versus n and obtain the slope of the line.
  - Calculate the value of R using the formula:

    `R = (D_N^2 - D_1^2) / 4(N - 1)h`

    where D_N and D_1 are the diameters of the Nth and first bright rings, respectively, and h is the thickness of the air film at the point of contact of the lens and the plate.
  - Substitute the values of R and the slope of the graph in the formula:

    `λ = (slope / 4R)`

    and obtain the wavelength of sodium light.

##### Transmission Grating

- A transmission grating is a type of diffraction grating that transmits light rather than reflecting it.
- A transmission grating consists of a series of parallel slits or grooves on a transparent material that diffract light into different directions according to the wavelength and the angle of incidence.
- A transmission grating can be used to disperse light into a spectrum for spectral analysis, wavelength filtering, or beam shaping applications.
- A transmission grating can be either a surface relief grating or a volume Bragg grating.
  - A surface relief grating has a periodic modulation of the surface height of the material, which creates a phase difference between the transmitted rays.
  - A volume Bragg grating has a periodic modulation of the refractive index inside the material, which creates an interference pattern between the transmitted rays.
- A transmission grating has some advantages over a reflection grating, such as:
  - Lower polarization sensitivity, since the light is not reflected by a metallic coating.
  - Higher diffraction efficiency, since the light is not absorbed or scattered by the coating.
  - Smaller size and weight, since the grating can be integrated into a thin substrate or a fiber.
  - Simpler optical alignment, since the grating can be used in a collimated or a converging beam without additional optics.

- There are two main methods to measure attenuation in an optical fiber: the cut-back method and the insertion-loss method.
- The cut-back method involves measuring the optical power at the output of a fiber before and after cutting back a known length of the fiber. The difference in power divided by the length gives the attenuation coefficient.
- The insertion-loss method involves measuring the optical power at the output of a fiber before and after inserting an optical device, such as a connector, splice, or coupler, between two sections of the fiber. The measurements should be performed at the same wavelength and under the same conditions as the actual application. - The energy band gap of a semiconductor can be measured by various methods, such as absorption spectroscopy, photoluminescence spectroscopy, or electrical conductivity measurements.
- One of the common methods to determine the energy band gap of a semiconductor is to plot the variation of electrical conductivity with temperature and extrapolate the curve to zero conductivity.

- The number of charge carriers in a semiconductor is proportional to the probability of electrons jumping from the valence band to the conduction band, which is given by the Boltzmann factor: $$P \propto e^{-\frac{E_g}{2k_BT}}$$ where $P$ is the probability, $E_g$ is the energy band gap, $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $T$ is the absolute temperature.
