1. Electric Charge: Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter that causes it to experience a force when placed in an electromagnetic field. Positively charged substances have fewer electrons than protons while negatively charged substances have more electrons than protons.

2. Electric Current: The flow of electric charge is an electric current. It is measured in amperes (A) and flows through a complete circuit from the negative terminal to the positive terminal. The amount of current depends on the voltage and resistance in the circuit.

3. Voltage: Voltage is the amount of "push" that drives the electric current. It is measured in volts (V) and is provided by a voltage source like a battery or power outlet. Higher voltage leads to higher current when resistance is constant.

4. Resistance: Resistance opposes the flow of electric current and is measured in ohms (Ω). It depends on the material, length, and cross-sectional area of the object. Resistance causes the electrical energy to be converted into heat and light. Lower resistance leads to higher current when voltage is constant.

5. Ohm's Law: Ohm's law states that current is directly proportional to voltage and inversely proportional to resistance. It is expressed as I=V/R where I is current in A, V is voltage in V, and R is resistance in Ω. Ohm's law is a basic law that describes how voltage, current, and resistance relate to each other in an electrical circuit.

### Voltage and Current Sources

- Voltage source: It is a two terminal device which provides a fixed voltage across its terminals and can maintain the voltage constant irrespective of the current drawn from it. For example, battery, cell, etc. The voltage source is represented by a symbol consisting of a triangle pointing upwards or downwards.
- Current source: It is a two terminal device which provides a fixed current through its terminals and can maintain the current constant irrespective of the voltage across it. For example, constant current diode, etc. The current source is represented by a symbol consisting of a circle with an arrow pointing outwards or inwards.
- Independent and dependent sources: If the source output does not depend on any other voltage or current in the circuit, it is called an independent source. If the source output depends on some other voltage or current in the circuit, it is called a dependent source. An ideal voltage or current source is always an independent source.
- Practical sources: In practice, no source can maintain a perfectly constant output voltage or current. All practical sources have some internal resistance or impedance due to which their outputs get affected by the load connected. Hence, they show some deviation from ideal behavior. However, for analysis purposes, practical sources are often represented by their ideal models and the effect of internal resistance is considered separately.

### Concept of Linearity for the notes of the Unit 1 - DC Circuits

1. A linear system is one in which the output is directly proportional to the input. This means that if the input is doubled, the output is also doubled and if the input is halved, the output is also halved.
2. For a linear system, the output-input relationship can be represented by a straight line graph. The slope of the line is the gain (multiplier) of the system.
3. An important property of linear systems is that the output is the "sum" of the responses to each input separately. For example, if an input has two components (say, x(t) = x1(t) + x2(t)), the output will be the sum of the responses to each component separately (y(t) = y1(t) + y2(t)).
4. In linear circuits, the current and voltage relationships can be represented by straight line graphs. As the input voltage or current changes, the output voltage or current changes in direct proportion. The proportionality constant is the resistance or conductance of the element.
5. Linear elements in circuits include resistors, capacitors and inductors with unchanging parameter values. Diodes and transistors are nonlinear elements as their current-voltage relationships are not straight lines.
6. Analysis of linear circuits is simpler than analysis of nonlinear circuits. Superposition theorem can be applied to solve complex linear circuits. These methods cannot be applied to nonlinear circuits.

### Unilateral and Bilateral Elements

- **Unilateral elements**: Elements that have one-sided effect on the circuit, i.e. the current through them depends only on the voltage across them and is independent of the direction of current flow. Examples are resistors, capacitors, inductors, etc. The current-voltage relationship of these elements is the same irrespective of the direction of current.
- **Bilateral elements**: Elements that have a two-sided effect on the circuit, i.e. the current through them depends on the voltage across them as well as the direction of current flow. Examples are diodes and transistors. The current-voltage relationship is not the same for both directions of current flow. The relationship is different for forward bias and reverse bias.

The properties of unilateral and bilateral elements must be considered while analyzing DC circuits to determine currents and voltages across different elements. Unilateral elements can be connected in either direction but bilateral elements must be connected properly considering their polarity.

### Mesh and nodal methods of analysis for the notes of the Unit 1 - DC Circuits in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING

- Mesh analysis: In mesh analysis, the circuit is divided into meshes and mesh currents are defined. The mesh currents are the currents that flow around the loops or meshes of the circuit. Kirchhoff's voltage law is applied around each mesh to obtain a set of equations in terms of the mesh currents. These equations can be solved to obtain the mesh currents. Once the mesh currents are known, the voltage across and current through each element can be determined.
- Nodal analysis: In nodal analysis, the circuit is divided into nodes and node voltages are defined. Kirchhoff's current law is applied at each node to obtain a set of equations in terms of the node voltages. These equations can be solved to obtain the node voltages. Once the node voltages are known, the voltage across and current through each element can be determined.
- Advantages: The main advantage of mesh and nodal analysis is that the number of unknowns (mesh currents or node voltages) is usually less than the number of equations. This results in a set of simultaneous equations that can be easily solved to determine the currents and voltages in the circuit. Mesh and nodal analysis provide an alternative method for circuit analysis and are useful in cases where other methods may not be applicable.

2. RMS values: The heating effect produced in a resistor is proportional to the square of the current flowing through it. 5. Power factor: It is the measure of how effectively the current is being used in an AC circuit to perform useful work. It is equal to the ratio of active power to the apparent power and can range from 0 to 1. A higher power factor indicates a more efficient circuit.

### Form and Peak Factors for steady state analysis of single phase AC circuits

- Form factor (F.F.): Ratio of RMS value to the average value of a waveform. It indicates the shape of the waveform.
- For a sinusoidal waveform, form factor is 1.11 (RMS value/Average value)
- Peak factor (P.F.): Ratio of peak value to the RMS value of a waveform. It indicates the amplitude of the waveform.
- For a sinusoidal waveform, peak factor is sqrt(2) = 1.414 (Peak value/RMS value)
- These factors are useful in calculating power, current, voltage values in AC circuits from given RMS values or vice-versa.
- Examples:

### Apparent, active & reactive power

* Apparent power (S) - It is the total power drawn by the AC circuit from the supply source. It is the product of root mean square (rms) current and voltage. (S = IV)
* Active power (P) - It is the actual power consumed by the load to perform useful work. It is the product of rms current and voltage in phase with each other. (P = IV Cos φ)
* Reactive power (Q) - It is the power consumed by the load's inductive or capacitive reactance. It is the product of rms current and voltage 90° out of phase with each other. (Q = IV Sin φ)
* Power factor (cos φ) - It is the ratio of active power to apparent power. It indicates how effectively the apparent power is being used. (cos φ = P/S)

### Power factor for the notes of the Unit 2 - Steady State Analysis of Single Phase AC Circuits in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING

- Power factor (PF) is the ratio of actual power to apparent power in an AC circuit.
- Apparent power (S) is the product of RMS voltage and RMS current. It is the total VA drawn from the supply.
- Actual power (P) is the power dissipated or delivered to the load.

- A low PF means extra current is drawn from the supply, leading to higher losses and less efficient utilization of power.
- PF can be improved using power factor correction techniques like using capacitors or active power filters.

- Resonance occurs in an AC circuit when the inductive and capacitive reactances are equal in magnitude but cancel each other.
- This happens at a particular frequency called the resonant frequency.

### Three phase balanced circuits

1. Three phase circuits are commonly used in power distribution systems as they have several advantages over single phase circuits like better efficiency, less voltage drops, absence of power surges etc.
2. In a three phase balanced circuit, three AC voltages having the same amplitude and frequency and a phase difference of 120 degrees are applied to the circuit. The currents flowing through the loads also have the same amplitude and are displaced from each other by 120 degrees.
3. The power in a balanced three phase circuit is constant and pulsation free. This is because at any instant, the sum of the instantaneous powers of the three phases is equal to zero.
4. The steady state analysis of balanced three phase circuits can be done easily by applying the principles and methods discussed for single phase circuits. The voltages and currents can be represented as vectors and the vector algebra can be used to solve the circuits.
5. The line currents in a balanced three phase circuit are equal in magnitude and phase shifted by 120 degrees. The line voltage between any two lines is the vector sum of the individual phase voltages.
6. Three phase transformers and motors are designed based on the principles of balanced three phase circuits. The coils of the three phases are constructed in such a way that the mutual coupling between them is the same. This results in equal current distribution and absence of circulating currents.

### Voltage and Current Relations in Star and Delta Connections

- Star or Y connection: In star connection, the terminals of three resistors or impedances are connected to a common junction point. The voltage across each resistor is equal to the line voltage V multiplied by square root 3 divided by 2 (V√3/2). The current through each resistor is equal to the line current I.
- Delta or ∆ connection: In delta connection, the terminals of three resistors or impedances are connected in series forming a closed loop. The voltage across each resistor is equal to the line voltage V. The current through each resistor is equal to the line current I divided by square root 3 (I/√3).

### Losses in Transformers

- Copper losses: These losses occur due to the resistance of the copper winding. The current flowing through the winding produces heat leading to copper losses. These are also known as I^2R losses.
- Iron losses: These losses occur due to hysteresis and eddy current effects in the core material.
- - Hysteresis losses: They occur due to the repeated magnetization and demagnetization of the core material as the magnetic field reverses during each cycle. This causes energy dissipation.
- - Eddy current losses: As the flux in the core material changes, circulating currents are induced in the core which leads to energy dissipation in the form of heat. These are also known as I^2R losses.
- Load losses: Apart from the core and copper losses which are nearly constant, some additional losses occur due to winding resistance and core loss variations with load. These are known as load losses and increase with increase in load. The efficiency of a transformer reduces with increase in load due to these losses.

The total losses in a transformer must be minimized for efficient operation and to reduce heating. This can be achieved by proper selection of core material and windings. The core material is chosen based on a balance between hysteresis and eddy current losses while the number of turns and size of winding is chosen based on copper loss considerations. The losses must also be within the allowable limits based on the cooling capabilities of the transformer.

### Regulation and Efficiency

- Regulation refers to the ability of a transformer to maintain constant secondary voltage for fluctuating load conditions and primary voltage changes.
- The regulation of a transformer depends on the following factors:
- Magnetizing current: The magnetizing current required for core excitation causes the primary winding voltage to drop. This affects the regulation.
- Copper loss: The copper loss in the windings causes the winding resistance to increase with load resulting in voltage drop and poor regulation.
- Leakage reactance: The leakage reactance causes the voltage drop to increase with the load current, thereby affecting regulation.
- Efficiency refers to the ratio of output power to the input power. The efficiency of a transformer depends on:
- Core loss: The core loss increases with frequency and flux density, thereby reducing efficiency.
- Copper loss: The copper loss in the windings increases with the square of current and causes reduction in efficiency.
- Load: The efficiency is maximum at full load and reduces with fractional loads.

## Unit 4 - Electrical machines

1. DC machines
- DC generator: Converts mechanical energy into DC electrical energy. Parts: Stator, Rotor, Commutator, Brushes. Working principle: Rotation of rotor induces current in stator coils, commutator reverses current direction, resulting in DC.
- DC motor: Converts DC electrical energy into mechanical energy. Working principle: Current flows through rotor coils and interacts with magnetic field to produce rotation. Torque is proportional to current.

2. AC machines
- AC generator (alternator): Converts mechanical energy into AC electrical energy. Parts: Stator, Rotor. Working principle: Rotation of rotor induces AC current in stator coils. Frequency of AC depends on rotor speed.
- AC motor: Converts AC electrical energy into mechanical energy. Types: Induction motor, Synchronous motor. Working principle: Interaction of magnetic fields of stator and rotor coils produces rotation.

3. Transformers
- Step-up transformer: Increases voltage, decreases current. Primary coil has fewer turns than secondary coil.
- Step-down transformer: Decreases voltage, increases current. Primary coil has more turns than secondary coil.
- Working principle: Mutual induction between two coils transfers energy via alternating magnetic fields. No direct connection between coils. Ratios of turns determine voltage transformation ratio.

### DC machines

- DC machines are electric motors and generators that run on direct current electricity.
- The main components of a DC machine are:

1. Stator - The stationary part that produces a stationary magnetic field.
2. Rotor - The rotating part that rotates within the stator.
3. Commutator - A device that converts alternating current to direct current.
4. Brushes - Contacts that transmit electric current to the rotor windings through the commutator.

- Based on the type of excitation, DC machines are classified into:
- Separately excited DC machine - The field windings are externally excited by a separate DC source.
- Self-excited DC machine - The field windings are excited by the armature current.
- Further classified into - Series, shunt, and compound DC machines based on the interconnections of field and armature windings.
- DC machines are widely used in applications requiring variable speed control and high starting torque such as electric locomotives, cranes, elevators, etc.

### Principle & Construction for the notes of the Unit 4 - Electrical machines in the subject of FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING

1. DC Machine
- Components: Stator ( field system), Rotor (armature), Commutator
- Working: Conversion of electrical energy into mechanical energy and vice-versa based on the interaction between magnetic fields of stator and rotor.
- Types: Series, Shunt, Compound

2. Transformer
- Components: Primary winding, Secondary winding, Core
- Working: Transfer of electrical energy between two circuits through electromagnetic induction. Conversion of voltage and current levels as per the turns ratio.
- Types: Step-up, Step-down, Distribution, Power

3. Induction Machine
- Components: Stator, Rotor, Shaft
- Working: Rotor field is produced due to induction from rotating magnetic field of stator. Electrical energy converted into mechanical energy and vice-versa.
- Types: Three phase induction motor, single phase induction motor

4. Synchronous Machine
- Components: Stator, Rotor, Exciter
- Working: Rotor field is produced by DC excitation and rotates at constant speed in synchronization with rotating magnetic field of stator.
- Types: Alternator, Motor
