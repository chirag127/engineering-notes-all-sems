# Nuclear Fusion

- Nuclear fusion is a process in which two or more atomic nuclei are combined to form one or more different atomic nuclei and subatomic particles.
- The difference in mass between the reactants and products is converted into energy by the mass-energy equivalence formula E = mc^2, where E is energy, m is mass, and c is the speed of light.
- Nuclear fusion is the source of energy for the Sun and other stars, as well as hydrogen bombs and fusion reactors.
- The main challenge of achieving nuclear fusion on Earth is to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged nuclei, which requires high temperatures and pressures to initiate and sustain the fusion reaction.
- The most common fusion reaction in the Sun and other stars is the proton-proton chain, which fuses four hydrogen nuclei (protons) into one helium nucleus, releasing two positrons, two neutrinos, and gamma rays.
- The most studied fusion reaction for terrestrial applications is the deuterium-tritium reaction, which fuses one deuterium nucleus (a hydrogen isotope with one proton and one neutron) and one tritium nucleus (a hydrogen isotope with one proton and two neutrons) into one helium nucleus, releasing one neutron and 17.6 MeV of energy.
- The main advantages of nuclear fusion as a source of energy are that it produces no greenhouse gases or radioactive waste, it uses abundant and cheap fuels (hydrogen isotopes), and it has a high energy density (the amount of energy released per unit mass of fuel).
- The main disadvantages of nuclear fusion as a source of energy are that it requires complex and expensive technology, it poses safety and security risks (such as neutron radiation and nuclear proliferation), and it faces technical and scientific challenges (such as plasma confinement and stability, materials engineering, and fusion ignition and control).

# Concept of Linearity

Homogeneity means that if the input is scaled by a constant factor, the output is also scaled by the same factor. Additivity means that if the input is the sum of two or more signals, the output is the sum of the corresponding outputs for each signal.
- A linear component or circuit can be represented by a straight line on a graph of the input versus the output. For example, a resistor is a linear component because the voltage across it is proportional to the current through it (Ohm's law). A diode is a nonlinear component because the current through it is not proportional to the voltage across it (exponential relationship).
- Linearity is an important concept in electrical engineering because it simplifies the analysis and design of circuits. Many circuit analysis techniques, such as **superposition**, **Thevenin's theorem**, and **Norton's theorem**, are based on the assumption of linearity. Linear circuits can also be modeled by **linear differential equations**, which have well-known methods of solution.
- However, not all electrical components or circuits are linear. Some examples of nonlinear components or circuits are transistors, amplifiers, switches, and oscillators. Nonlinear circuits can exhibit complex behaviors, such as **hysteresis**, **bistability**, **harmonics**, and **chaos**. Nonlinear circuits require more advanced methods of analysis, such as **piecewise-linear approximation**, **small-signal analysis**, and **numerical simulation**.

# Kirchhoff‟s laws

- Kirchhoff‟s laws are two rules that can be used to analyze any electrical circuit, whether it is simple or complex.
- Kirchhoff‟s laws are based on the conservation of charge and energy in a circuit.
- Kirchhoff‟s laws are also known as Kirchhoff‟s circuit laws or Kirchhoff‟s rules.

## Kirchhoff‟s current law (KCL)

- Kirchhoff‟s current law states that the algebraic sum of the currents entering any node (or junction) in a circuit is zero.
- Mathematically, KCL can be expressed as:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n I_k = 0$$

- Where $I_k$ is the current entering or leaving the node, and $n$ is the number of branches connected to the node.
- KCL is based on the principle of conservation of charge, which means that charge cannot be created or destroyed in a circuit.
- KCL can be used to find the unknown currents in a circuit, by applying it to each node and forming a system of linear equations.

## Kirchhoff‟s voltage law (KVL)

- Kirchhoff‟s voltage law states that the algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed loop (or mesh) in a circuit is zero.
- Mathematically, KVL can be expressed as:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n V_k = 0$$

- Where $V_k$ is the voltage across the kth element in the loop, and $n$ is the number of elements in the loop.
- KVL is based on the principle of conservation of energy, which means that energy cannot be created or destroyed in a circuit.
- KVL can be used to find the unknown voltages in a circuit, by applying it to each loop and forming a system of linear equations.



# Mesh and Nodal Methods of Analysis

- Mesh and nodal methods of analysis are two systematic techniques for solving circuits that involve writing and solving a set of equations.
- In mesh analysis, the unknown quantities are the loop currents. A loop is a closed path that does not contain any other closed paths within it. A mesh is a loop that does not contain any other loops within it.
- In nodal analysis, the unknown quantities are the node voltages. A node is a point where two or more elements are connected. A reference node is a node that is assigned a voltage of zero volts.
- Both methods are based on applying Kirchhoff's laws: Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) states that the algebraic sum of currents entering a node is zero, and Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) states that the algebraic sum of voltages around a loop is zero.
- The steps for applying mesh analysis are:
  - Identify all the meshes in the circuit and assign a current variable to each mesh in a clockwise direction.
  - Write KVL equations for each mesh, expressing the voltages in terms of the mesh currents.
  - Solve the system of equations for the mesh currents.
  - Use Ohm's law to find the voltages across the elements if needed.
- The steps for applying nodal analysis are:
  - Identify all the nodes in the circuit and assign a voltage variable to each node, except the reference node.
  - Write KCL equations for each node, expressing the currents in terms of the node voltages.
  - Solve the system of equations for the node voltages.
  - Use Ohm's law to find the currents through the elements if needed.
- Both methods can handle circuits with resistors, independent sources, dependent sources, and linear elements. However, nodal analysis is more suited for circuits with many nodes and few loops, while mesh analysis is more suited for circuits with many loops and few nodes.

# Apparent, Active and Reactive Power

- In an AC circuit, the power delivered by the source is not always equal to the power consumed by the load. This is because the AC voltage and current may not be in phase with each other, due to the presence of reactive elements like capacitors and inductors.
- The power that is actually consumed by the load is called **active power** or **real power**. It is measured in watts (W) and is symbolized by the capital letter P. Active power is the product of the voltage and current that are in phase with each other, and it represents the useful work done by the circuit.
- The power that is stored and returned by the reactive elements is called **reactive power**. It is measured in volt-amperes reactive (VAR) and is symbolized by the capital letter Q. Reactive power is the product of the voltage and current that are 90 degrees out of phase with each other, and it represents the energy that oscillates between the source and the load.
- The power that is apparent to the source is called **apparent power**. It is measured in volt-amperes (VA) and is symbolized by the capital letter S. Apparent power is the product of the RMS voltage and current, without reference to phase angle, and it represents the total power that the source has to supply to the circuit.
- The relationship between active, reactive and apparent power can be expressed by the **power triangle**, which is a right-angled triangle with the following sides and angles:

  - The hypotenuse is the apparent power S, and it is the longest side of the triangle.
  - The adjacent side is the active power P, and it is the horizontal side of the triangle.

## Construction of DC Machines

# EMF equation of generator and torque equation of motor

## EMF equation of generator

- A generator is a device that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by rotating a coil in a magnetic field.
- The emf (electromotive force) generated by the coil is given by the equation :

  $$E_g = \phi Z N \frac{P}{60 A}$$

  where:

  - $E_g$ is the generated emf in volts
  - $\phi$ is the flux per pole in webers
  - $Z$ is the total number of armature conductors
  - $N$ is the speed of rotation in revolutions per minute (rpm)
  - $P$ is the number of poles
  - $A$ is the number of parallel paths in the armature

- The generated emf depends on the speed of rotation and the flux per pole. If either of them increases, the emf increases.

## Torque equation of motor

- A motor is a device that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy by passing a current through a coil in a magnetic field.
- The torque (rotational force) exerted on the coil is given by the equation :

  $$T = \phi Z I \frac{P}{2 \pi A}$$

  where:

  - $T$ is the torque in newton-meters (Nm)
  - $\phi$ is the flux per pole in webers
  - $Z$ is the total number of armature conductors
  - $I$ is the current in the armature in amperes (A)
  - $P$ is the number of poles
  - $A$ is the number of parallel paths in the armature

- The torque depends on the current in the armature and the flux per pole. If either of them increases, the torque increases.

## Back emf in motor

- When a motor is running, the coil in the motor also acts as a generator and produces an emf that opposes the applied voltage. This is called the back emf or counter emf and is denoted by $E_b$ .
- The back emf is given by the same equation as the generated emf in a generator :

  $$E_b = \phi Z N \frac{P}{60 A}$$

- The back emf reduces the effective voltage across the coil and limits the current and the speed of the motor. The back emf increases with the speed of the motor.

# Three Phase Induction Motor

A three phase induction motor is a type of AC motor that operates on three phase supply. It converts electrical energy into mechanical energy by creating a rotating magnetic field in the stator, which induces an electromotive force in the rotor. The rotor then rotates in the same direction as the rotating magnetic field and produces mechanical power.

## Types of Three Phase Induction Motor

There are two main types of three phase induction motor:

- **Squirrel cage induction motor**: This type of motor has a simple and rugged construction, with a cylindrical rotor that consists of copper or aluminum bars (called rotor bars) embedded in slots and short-circuited at both ends by end rings. The rotor bars and end rings form a cage-like structure, hence the name. The rotor bars are slightly skewed to reduce noise and improve starting performance. The rotor does not have any external connection, and the induced current in the rotor bars is due to the transformer action of the stator winding. This type of motor is widely used in industrial and domestic applications due to its low cost, high efficiency, and reliability.

- **Slip ring or wound rotor induction motor**: This type of motor has a wound rotor, which consists of insulated coils (called rotor winding) placed in slots and connected to slip rings mounted on the rotor shaft. The slip rings are externally connected to a variable resistor (called rotor resistance) through brushes. The rotor winding can be either star or delta connected. The rotor resistance can be adjusted to control the speed and torque of the motor. This type of motor is used in applications that require high starting torque, speed control, or frequent starting and stopping.

## Working Principle of Three Phase Induction Motor

The working principle of a three phase induction motor can be explained as follows:

- When a three phase supply is given to the stator winding of the motor, it produces a rotating magnetic field (RMF) of constant magnitude and synchronous speed. The synchronous speed (Ns) is given by the formula:

  Ns = 120f / P

  where f is the supply frequency and P is the number of poles.

- The RMF cuts the rotor conductors and induces an EMF in them according to Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. The direction of the induced EMF and current in the rotor conductors is given by Fleming's right hand rule.

- The induced current in the rotor conductors produces a magnetic field in the rotor, which interacts with the RMF and causes a torque on the rotor. The rotor starts to rotate in the same direction as the RMF.

- The rotor speed (Nr) is always less than the synchronous speed (Ns), because if the rotor speed is equal to the synchronous speed, there will be no relative motion between the RMF and the rotor, and hence no induced EMF or current in the rotor. The difference between the synchronous speed and the rotor speed is called the slip (s), and it is expressed as a percentage of the synchronous speed:

  s = (Ns - Nr) / Ns * 100%

- The slip is an important parameter that determines the performance of the motor. The slip is directly proportional to the rotor resistance, the load torque, and the rotor current. The slip is inversely proportional to the rotor power factor, the rotor EMF, and the motor efficiency.

- The slip-torque characteristic of an induction motor is the relationship between the slip and the torque for a given value of rotor resistance.
- The slip-torque characteristic can be derived from the equivalent circuit of the induction motor, by equating the mechanical power output to the electrical power input to the rotor.

  - Low slip region: This is the region where the slip is very small, and the rotor speed is near the synchronous speed. $$

    s = \frac{n_s - n_r}{n_s} = \frac{1200 - 1140}{1200} = 0.05
    $$

    $$
    f_r = s f_s = 0.05 \times 60 = 3 \text{ Hz}
    $$

    n_s = \frac{120 f_s}{p} = \frac{120 \times 50}{4} = 1500 \text{ rpm}

# Single Phase Induction Motor

A single phase induction motor is a type of electric motor that operates on single phase alternating current. It is similar to a three phase induction motor in construction, except that it has only one stator winding instead of three. The stator winding produces a pulsating magnetic field that induces currents in the rotor, which is a squirrel cage made of copper or aluminum bars. The rotor currents create a magnetic field that interacts with the stator field and produces torque, causing the rotor to rotate.

However, a single phase induction motor has no starting torque, because the stator field does not rotate and the rotor is stationary. To make the motor self-starting, various methods are used to create a rotating magnetic field at least at the start. Some of the common methods are:

- **Permanent-split capacitor motor**: This motor has a capacitor connected in series with a second stator winding, which is placed at an angle to the main winding. The capacitor creates a phase shift between the two windings, resulting in a rotating magnetic field. The capacitor remains in the circuit during the operation of the motor, and provides a high power factor and efficiency. This motor is suitable for applications that require constant speed and low starting torque, such as fans, blowers, pumps, etc.

- **Capacitor-start induction motor**: This motor has a capacitor connected in series with a second stator winding, which is placed at an angle to the main winding. The capacitor creates a phase shift between the two windings, resulting in a rotating magnetic field. The capacitor is disconnected from the circuit by a centrifugal switch once the motor reaches a certain speed, and the motor runs on the main winding only. This motor is suitable for applications that require high starting torque and moderate speed, such as compressors, refrigerators, air conditioners, etc.

- **Shaded-pole motor**: This motor has a salient-pole stator with a small copper ring or loop around a part of each pole, called the shaded pole. The shaded pole creates a phase shift in the magnetic flux, resulting in a rotating magnetic field. The shaded pole remains in the circuit during the operation of the motor, and provides a low power factor and efficiency. This motor is suitable for applications that require low starting torque and low speed, such as fans, clocks, toys, etc.

Some of the advantages of single phase induction motors are:

- They are simple, cheap, and easy to maintain.

  - Three-point starter: This is a device that connects the armature of a DC shunt or compound motor to the supply through a variable resistance. The resistance is gradually reduced as the motor picks up speed, until it is bypassed by a no-volt coil, which also acts as a protection device in case of power failure or overload.
  - Four-point starter: This is similar to the three-point starter, except that the no-volt coil is connected separately from the field winding, which provides better speed regulation and prevents the motor from running away in case of field failure.
  - Series-parallel starter: This is a device that connects the armature of a DC series motor to the supply through two resistances in series, which are then connected in parallel as the motor picks up speed. This reduces the starting current and torque, and prevents the motor from overspeeding.
- Starting methods for AC machines are mainly based on controlling the voltage or the frequency of the supply, which determines the starting current and torque. Some of the common starting methods for AC machines are:
  - Direct-on-line (DOL) starter: This is the simplest and most economical method of starting an AC machine, where the machine is directly connected to the supply without any intermediate device. This method provides the highest starting torque, but also draws the highest starting current, which may cause voltage drop, overheating and mechanical stress on the machine and the supply system.
  - Star-delta starter: This is a device that connects the stator of a three-phase induction motor to the supply in star configuration during the starting period, and then switches to delta configuration when the motor reaches a certain speed. This reduces the starting current and torque by a factor of three, but also reduces the starting performance of the motor.
  - Auto-transformer starter: This is a device that connects the stator of a three-phase induction motor to the supply through an auto-transformer, which reduces the voltage and the current during the starting period, and then bypasses the auto-transformer when the motor reaches a certain speed. This provides a smooth and adjustable starting method, but also increases the cost and complexity of the device.
  - Variable frequency drive (VFD): This is a device that converts the AC supply into a variable frequency and variable voltage output, which can be controlled to match the speed and torque requirements of the AC machine. This provides the most efficient and flexible starting method, but also requires sophisticated electronic components and software.

# Three Phase Synchronous Machines

- A three-phase synchronous machine is an AC machine that operates at a constant speed and converts electrical energy to mechanical energy or vice versa.
- A three-phase synchronous machine consists of two main parts: a stator and a rotor.
- The stator is the stationary part of the machine that contains a three-phase winding, which is connected to the AC supply. The stator winding produces a rotating magnetic field that rotates at the synchronous speed.
- The rotor is the rotating part of the machine that contains a DC winding, which is excited by a DC source. The rotor winding produces a constant magnetic field that aligns with the stator field.
- There are two types of rotors: salient pole and cylindrical (or round) rotor. Salient pole rotors have projecting poles with concentrated windings, while cylindrical rotors have smooth surfaces with distributed windings.
- The synchronous speed of the machine is given by the equation:

  $$n_s = \frac{120f}{P}$$

  where $n_s$ is the synchronous speed in revolutions per minute (rpm), $f$ is the frequency of the AC supply in hertz (Hz), and $P$ is the number of poles on the rotor.
- A three-phase synchronous machine can operate as a generator or a motor, depending on the direction of the power flow.
- A three-phase synchronous generator converts mechanical energy to electrical energy by rotating the rotor faster than the synchronous speed. The rotor field induces an AC voltage in the stator winding, which is delivered to the load.
- A three-phase synchronous motor converts electrical energy to mechanical energy by rotating the rotor at the synchronous speed. The stator field induces a torque on the rotor, which drives the load.
- A three-phase synchronous machine can be started by various methods, such as using a separate induction motor, using a damper winding, or using a brushless exciter.



# Principle of operation of alternator and synchronous motor

- An **alternator** or **synchronous generator** is a device that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by producing alternating current (AC).
- A **synchronous motor** is a device that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy by rotating at a constant speed that is synchronized with the frequency of the AC supply.
- Both devices work on the principle of **electromagnetic induction**, i.e., when the flux linking a conductor changes, an EMF is induced in the conductor.

## Alternator

- An alternator consists of a **stator** and a **rotor**. The stator is the stationary part that contains a set of coils called the **armature winding**. The rotor is the rotating part that contains a set of magnets or electromagnets called the **field winding**.
- The rotor is driven by a prime mover, such as a turbine, engine, or water wheel, and creates a **rotating magnetic field** that induces an EMF in the stator coils.
- The output voltage and frequency of the alternator depend on the speed of the rotor and the number of poles in the field winding. The output voltage can be regulated by adjusting the current in the field winding.
- Alternators are used to generate electricity in power plants, vehicles, and other applications.

## Synchronous motor

- A synchronous motor consists of a **stator** and a **rotor**. The stator is the stationary part that contains a set of coils called the **stator winding**. The rotor is the rotating part that contains a set of magnets or electromagnets called the **rotor winding**.
- The stator is connected to a three-phase AC supply that creates a **rotating magnetic field** that rotates at a constant speed determined by the supply frequency and the number of poles in the stator winding.
- The rotor is supplied with a direct current (DC) that creates a **constant magnetic field** that aligns with the rotating magnetic field of the stator. The rotor then rotates at the same speed as the stator field, and is said to be **synchronized** with the supply.
- Synchronous motors are used to drive loads that require constant speed, such as fans, pumps, compressors, and clocks. They can also be used to improve the power factor of the system by adjusting the current in the rotor winding.

# ELCB

ELCB stands for Earth Leakage Circuit Breaker. It is a safety device used in electrical installations with high earth impedance to prevent shock. It detects small stray voltages on the metal enclosures of electrical equipment, and interrupts the circuit if a dangerous voltage is detected  .

## Working Principle of ELCB

There are two types of ELCBs: voltage-operated and current-operated.

- Voltage-operated ELCB: This type of ELCB compares the voltage difference between a metal body (earthed) and the earth. If the voltage difference exceeds a preset value, the ELCB trips and disconnects the power supply. This type of ELCB is sensitive to voltage fluctuations and may cause nuisance tripping.
- Current-operated ELCB: This type of ELCB measures the residual current in the circuit, which is the difference between the current flowing in the live wire and the current returning in the neutral wire. If the residual current exceeds a preset value, the ELCB trips and disconnects the power supply. This type of ELCB is also known as Residual Current Device (RCD) or Residual Current Circuit Breaker (RCCB). This type of ELCB is more reliable and accurate than the voltage-operated ELCB .

## Advantages of ELCB

- ELCB provides protection against electric shocks and fire hazards caused by earth leakage currents.
- ELCB can detect very small leakage currents that may not be detected by conventional fuses or circuit breakers.
- ELCB can prevent damage to electrical equipment and appliances due to earth faults.
- ELCB can reduce the risk of electrocution for humans and animals in contact with the earthed metal bodies .

## Disadvantages of ELCB

- ELCB may not provide protection against direct contact with live wires, as the leakage current may not be sufficient to trip the ELCB.
- ELCB may cause nuisance tripping due to external factors such as lightning, moisture, or faulty wiring.
- ELCB may not work properly if the earth connection is loose or broken.
- ELCB may not be compatible with some types of electrical equipment that generate leakage currents as part of their normal operation, such as electronic devices or variable frequency drives .

# Fundamentals of Earthing and Lightning Protection

## Introduction

Earthing and lightning protection are important for everyone that uses electrical equipment and that includes the large majority of the world. Earthing, Lighting Arrestors and Surge Protection Devices are the leading devices that protect devices against current shock. Lightning protection grounding helps protect structures and equipment from direct strikes. Overhead ground wires and surge arresters, connected to ground, can limit dangerous system overvoltages to safe values.

## Objectives of Earthing and Lightning Protection

The main objectives of earthing and lightning protection are:

- To provide a low impedance path for fault currents to flow to the earth and clear the fault.
- To maintain the voltage at any part of the electrical system at a known value and prevent excessive voltage buildup.
- To provide a reference point for the system voltage and ensure proper operation of protective devices.
- To protect personnel and equipment from electric shock, fire, and damage due to lightning strikes and surges.

## Types of Earthing Systems

There are different types of earthing systems depending on the configuration of the source, the load, and the earth connection. Some common types are:

- TN system: The source neutral is connected to the earth and the load is directly connected to the source neutral. The exposed metal parts of the load are also connected to the source neutral or earth. There are three subtypes of TN system: TN-C, TN-S, and TN-C-S.
- TT system: The source neutral is connected to the earth but the load is not directly connected to the source neutral. The exposed metal parts of the load are connected to a separate earth electrode. The source and the load have independent earth connections.
- IT system: The source neutral is isolated from the earth or connected through a high impedance. The load is not directly connected to the source neutral. The exposed metal parts of the load are connected to a separate earth electrode. The source and the load have independent earth connections.

## Components of Earthing Systems

The main components of earthing systems are:

- Earth electrode: A conductor or a group of conductors that are buried in the soil and provide a low resistance contact with the earth. Examples are earth rods, earth plates, earth mats, etc.
- Earth conductor: A conductor that connects the earth electrode to the equipment or the system that needs to be earthed. Examples are copper wires, copper strips, steel tapes, etc.
- Earth clamp: A device that connects the earth conductor to the earth electrode or to the equipment. Examples are bolted clamps, compression clamps, etc.
- Earth pit: A hole in the ground that contains the earth electrode and is filled with soil or other materials to improve the earth contact. Examples are concrete pits, brick pits, etc.

## Principles of Lightning Protection

Lightning protection is the process of preventing or minimizing the harmful effects of lightning strikes on structures, equipment, and personnel. The basic principles of lightning protection are:

- Capture: A lightning protection system (LPS) should intercept or divert the lightning current before it reaches the structure or the equipment. This can be done by installing air terminals, masts, or overhead wires on the roof or around the perimeter of the structure.
- Conduction: A LPS should provide a low impedance path for the lightning current to flow to the ground and dissipate safely. This can be done by installing down conductors, bonding conductors, and grounding electrodes that are properly sized and connected.
- Dissipation: A LPS should spread the lightning current over a large area of the earth and reduce the potential difference between the LPS and the surrounding soil. This can be done by installing earth rods, earth plates, earth mats, or radial wires that are buried in the soil and have adequate length and spacing.
- Isolation: A LPS should prevent the lightning current from inducing dangerous voltages or currents in any parallel metallic systems near the LPS, such as power lines, communication lines, pipelines, etc. This can be done by installing surge arresters, surge protectors, or isolation transformers that limit the surge voltage or current to safe values.

There are various standards and codes that provide guidelines and recommendations for the design, installation, testing, and maintenance of earthing and lightning protection systems. Some of the most widely used standards and codes are:

- IEEE Std 81: Guide for Measuring Earth Resistivity, Ground Impedance, and Earth Surface Potentials of a Grounding System

- **Reserve batteries**: These are batteries that are activated only when needed. They have a separate electrolyte that is added to the battery when it is required to produce current. Examples of reserve batteries are thermal batteries, water-activated batteries, etc.
- **Fuel cells**: These are batteries that generate electrical energy from the continuous supply of fuel and oxidant. They do not store chemical energy, but rather convert it directly into electrical energy. Examples of fuel cells are hydrogen-oxygen, methanol, etc.

Each type of battery has its own advantages and disadvantages, depending on the application, cost, performance, safety, environmental impact, etc. Some of the factors that are used to compare different types of batteries are   :

- **Voltage**: The potential difference between the terminals of the battery. It determines the amount of electrical energy that can be delivered by the battery. Different types of batteries have different nominal voltages, ranging from 1.2 V to 3.6 V for common batteries.
- **Capacity**: The amount of electrical charge that can be delivered by the battery. It is measured in ampere-hours (Ah) or milliampere-hours (mAh). It depends on the size, chemistry, and discharge rate of the battery. Higher capacity means longer run time for the battery.
- **Energy density**: The amount of energy that can be stored by the battery per unit volume or mass. It is measured in watt-hours per liter (Wh/L) or watt-hours per kilogram (Wh/kg). Higher energy density means smaller and lighter batteries for the same amount of energy.
- **Power density**: The amount of power that can be delivered by the battery per unit volume or mass. It is measured in watts per liter (W/L) or watts per kilogram (W/kg). Higher power density means faster and stronger batteries for the same size and weight.
- **Cycle life**: The number of times that the battery can be charged and discharged before its capacity drops below a certain level. It depends on the depth of discharge, charging method, temperature, etc. Higher cycle life means longer lifespan for the battery.
- **Self-discharge**: The loss of charge that occurs in the battery when it is not in use. It is caused by internal chemical reactions, leakage currents, etc. It is expressed as a percentage of the initial charge per month or year. Lower self-discharge means longer shelf life for the battery.
- **Cost**: The price of the battery per unit of energy or power. It depends on the materials, manufacturing, availability, etc. Lower cost means more affordable batteries for the consumers.
