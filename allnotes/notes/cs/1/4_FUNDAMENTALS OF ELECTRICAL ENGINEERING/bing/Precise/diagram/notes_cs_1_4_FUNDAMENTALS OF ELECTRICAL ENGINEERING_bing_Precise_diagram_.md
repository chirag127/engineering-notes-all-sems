

# FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING

Electrical engineering is a branch of engineering that deals with the study and application of electricity, electronics, and electromagnetism. Here are some fundamental concepts in electrical engineering:

1. **Electric charge:** Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter that results from the presence or absence of electrons. It is measured in coulombs (C).

2. **Electric current:** Electric current is the flow of electric charge through a conductor. It is measured in amperes (A).

3. **Voltage:** Voltage, also known as electric potential difference, is the measure of the electric potential energy per unit charge in an electrical circuit. It is measured in volts (V).

4. **Resistance:** Resistance is the measure of the opposition to the flow of electric current through a conductor. It is measured in ohms (Ω).

5. **Ohm's Law:** Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points.

6. **Power:** Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or used. In electrical systems, it is measured in watts (W).

7. **Circuits:** An electric circuit is a closed path through which electric current flows. It consists of various components such as resistors, capacitors, and inductors.




## Unit 1 - DC Circuits

1. **Introduction to DC Circuits:** A direct current (DC) circuit is an electrical circuit that consists of any combination of constant voltage sources, constant current sources, and resistors. In a DC circuit, the direction of the flow of electrons is constant, flowing from the negative pole of the power source to the positive pole.

2. **Ohm's Law:** Ohm's Law states that the current flowing through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points. Mathematically, it is represented as V = IR, where V is the voltage, I is the current, and R is the resistance.

3. **Series and Parallel Circuits:** In a series circuit, the components are connected end-to-end, such that the current flows through each component in turn. In a parallel circuit, the components are connected across common points, providing separate paths for the current to flow through each component.

4. **Kirchhoff's Laws:** Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) states that the algebraic sum of currents entering a node (or a closed boundary) is zero. Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) states that the algebraic sum of all the voltages around any closed loop in a circuit is zero.

5. **Power in DC Circuits:** The power dissipated in a resistor is given by the formula P = IV, where P is the power, I is the current, and V is the voltage. Alternatively, it can be calculated using the formula P = I^2R or P = V^2/R, where R is the resistance.

6. **Capacitors and Inductors:** Capacitors and inductors are passive components used in DC circuits. A capacitor stores electrical energy in an electric field, while an inductor stores energy in a magnetic field. Capacitors and inductors can be used to smooth out voltage fluctuations, filter signals, and store energy.

7. **DC Circuit Analysis:** DC circuit analysis involves calculating the voltage, current, and power in each component of a DC circuit. This can be done using techniques such as Ohm's Law, Kirchhoff's Laws, and the voltage divider rule.



### Electrical circuit elements (R, L and C)

An electrical circuit is made up of various elements, each with its own properties and functions. The three basic elements of an electrical circuit are the resistor (R), the inductor (L), and the capacitor (C).

2. **Inductor (L):** An inductor, also known as a coil or reactor, is a passive two-terminal electrical component that stores energy in a magnetic field when electric current flows through it. 3. **Capacitor (C):** A capacitor is a passive two-terminal electrical component that stores electrical energy in an electric field. Capacitors are widely used in electronic circuits for blocking direct current while allowing alternating current to pass, in filter networks, for smoothing the output of power supplies, and for many other purposes.

These three elements, along with other components such as voltage and current sources, can be combined in various ways to form complex electrical circuits. Understanding the properties and functions of these basic elements is essential for the study of electrical engineering.

### Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's laws are two laws that deal with the conservation of charge and energy in electrical circuits. These laws are named after the German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff.

1. **Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)**: This law is also known as the "junction rule" or the "point rule". It states that the algebraic sum of currents entering a node (or a closed boundary) is zero. In other words, the total current entering a junction must equal the total current leaving the junction.

2. **Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)**: This law is also known as the "loop rule" or the "mesh rule". It states that the algebraic sum of the potential differences (voltages) around any closed loop is zero. In other words, the total voltage gained in a closed loop must equal the total voltage lost in the same loop.

These laws are fundamental principles in the analysis of DC circuits and are widely used in electrical engineering. They are based on the principles of conservation of charge and energy, and provide a powerful tool for analyzing complex circuits.



### Mesh and Nodal Methods of Analysis

Mesh and nodal analysis are two fundamental methods for analyzing DC circuits. These methods are used to determine the current and voltage in each branch of a circuit.

#### Mesh Analysis

Mesh analysis is a technique used to solve circuits with multiple loops or meshes. A mesh is a loop that does not contain any other loops within it. In mesh analysis, we apply Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) to each mesh in the circuit. This results in a system of equations that can be solved to determine the current in each mesh.

The steps for performing mesh analysis are as follows:

1. Identify all the meshes in the circuit.
2. Assign a current to each mesh.
3. Write KVL equations for each mesh.
4. Solve the system of equations to determine the current in each mesh.

#### Nodal Analysis

Nodal analysis is a technique used to solve circuits with multiple nodes. A node is a point in a circuit where two or more circuit elements meet. In nodal analysis, we apply Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) to each node in the circuit. This results in a system of equations that can be solved to determine the voltage at each node.

The steps for performing nodal analysis are as follows:

1. Identify all the nodes in the circuit.
2. Assign a voltage to each node.
3. Write KCL equations for each node.
4. Solve the system of equations to determine the voltage at each node.

Both mesh and nodal analysis are powerful techniques for analyzing DC circuits. They can be used to solve complex circuits with multiple sources and loads. It is important to understand these methods and be able to apply them to solve circuit problems.

### Form and Peak Factors

Form factor and peak factor are two important concepts in the steady state analysis of single phase AC circuits. These factors are used to describe the shape of an AC waveform.

1. **Form Factor:** The form factor is defined as the ratio of the RMS (root mean square) value of an AC waveform to its average value. It is a measure of the "peakedness" of the waveform. A higher form factor indicates a more peaked waveform, while a lower form factor indicates a flatter waveform.

2. **Peak Factor:** The peak factor is defined as the ratio of the peak value of an AC waveform to its RMS value. It is a measure of the "sharpness" of the waveform. A higher peak factor indicates a sharper waveform, while a lower peak factor indicates a smoother waveform.

These factors are important in the analysis of AC circuits because they provide information about the shape of the waveform, which can affect the behavior of the circuit. For example, a circuit with a high form factor may experience more power loss due to the higher peak currents. Similarly, a circuit with a high peak factor may experience more stress on its components due to the higher peak voltages.

In summary, form factor and peak factor are two important concepts in the steady state analysis of single phase AC circuits. They provide information about the shape of the AC waveform, which can affect the behavior of the circuit. Understanding these factors is essential for the proper analysis and design of AC circuits.

## Unit 3 - Transformers

Transformers are electrical devices that transfer electrical energy between two or more circuits through electromagnetic induction. A varying current in any one coil of the transformer produces a varying magnetic flux, which, in turn, induces a varying electromotive force across any other coils wound around the same core. Electrical energy can be transferred between the two coils, without a metallic connection between the two circuits.

- **Principle of operation:** Transformers operate on the principle of electromagnetic induction, which states that a changing magnetic field induces an electromotive force in a conductor.
- **Construction:** A transformer consists of two or more coils of insulated wire wound around a common iron core. The coil that is connected to the source of electrical energy is called the primary coil, and the coil that is connected to the load is called the secondary coil.
- **Types of transformers:** There are several types of transformers, including step-up transformers, step-down transformers, isolation transformers, and autotransformers.
- **Applications:** Transformers are used in a wide range of applications, including power generation, transmission, and distribution, as well as in electronic devices such as radios, televisions, and computers.

### Ideal and Practical Transformer

A transformer is a static device that transfers electrical energy from one circuit to another through electromagnetic induction. It is used to either increase or decrease the voltage of an alternating current (AC) supply.

#### Ideal Transformer

An ideal transformer is an imaginary transformer that has no losses, meaning that the power input is equal to the power output. In an ideal transformer, the following conditions are assumed to be true:

1. The windings have zero resistance.
2. The core has infinite permeability, so all the magnetic flux is confined to the core.
3. There are no leakage fluxes, meaning that all the magnetic flux links both the primary and secondary windings.
4. There are no iron losses (hysteresis and eddy current losses) in the core.
5. There is no magnetizing current, meaning that the magnetizing branch of the equivalent circuit can be neglected.

#### Practical Transformer

In reality, no transformer is ideal. A practical transformer has losses, meaning that the power input is greater than the power output. In a practical transformer, the following conditions are true:

1. The windings have some resistance, which causes copper losses (I^2R losses).
2. The core does not have infinite permeability, so some of the magnetic flux leaks out of the core.
3. There are leakage fluxes, meaning that not all the magnetic flux links both the primary and secondary windings.
4. There are iron losses (hysteresis and eddy current losses) in the core.
5. There is a magnetizing current, meaning that the magnetizing branch of the equivalent circuit cannot be neglected.

In summary, an ideal transformer is an imaginary transformer that has no losses, while a practical transformer has losses. The losses in a practical transformer include copper losses, leakage fluxes, iron losses, and magnetizing current. These losses reduce the efficiency of the transformer and must be taken into account when designing and operating a transformer.

### Losses in Transformers

Transformers are used to transfer electrical energy from one circuit to another through electromagnetic induction. However, during this process, some energy is lost in the form of heat. These losses are known as transformer losses. There are two main types of losses in transformers: core losses and copper losses.

1. **Core losses:** Core losses, also known as iron losses, occur in the magnetic core of the transformer. These losses are caused by two main factors: hysteresis and eddy currents. Hysteresis losses occur due to the magnetization and demagnetization of the core, while eddy current losses occur due to the circulation of currents within the core. Core losses can be reduced by using high-quality magnetic materials and by laminating the core to reduce eddy currents.

2. **Copper losses:** Copper losses, also known as winding losses, occur in the transformer windings. These losses are caused by the resistance of the windings and are directly proportional to the square of the current flowing through them. Copper losses can be reduced by using larger conductors or by increasing the number of turns in the windings.

In addition to these two main types of losses, there are also other minor losses such as stray losses and dielectric losses. These losses can be minimized by proper design and construction of the transformer.

Overall, transformer losses can be reduced by using high-quality materials, proper design, and construction. However, it is important to note that some losses are inevitable and cannot be completely eliminated. The efficiency of a transformer can be calculated by taking into account these losses and comparing the input and output power.



### Regulation and Efficiency

Transformers are used to transfer electrical energy from one circuit to another through electromagnetic induction. The efficiency and regulation of a transformer are important factors to consider when designing and operating a transformer.

#### Regulation

Regulation is the measure of how well a transformer maintains a constant secondary voltage as the load on the transformer changes. It is expressed as a percentage and is calculated as follows:

`% Regulation = (No-load voltage - Full-load voltage) / Full-load voltage * 100`

A transformer with good regulation will have a low percentage of regulation, meaning that the secondary voltage remains relatively constant as the load changes.

#### Efficiency

Efficiency is the measure of how well a transformer converts electrical energy from the primary circuit to the secondary circuit. It is expressed as a percentage and is calculated as follows:

`% Efficiency = (Output power / Input power) * 100`

A transformer with high efficiency will have a high percentage of efficiency, meaning that it is able to transfer a large amount of electrical energy from the primary circuit to the secondary circuit with minimal losses.

Factors that affect the efficiency of a transformer include the resistance of the windings, the core losses, and the leakage flux. These factors can be minimized through careful design and construction of the transformer.

In summary, regulation and efficiency are important factors to consider when designing and operating a transformer. A transformer with good regulation and high efficiency will be able to transfer electrical energy from one circuit to another with minimal losses and maintain a constant secondary voltage as the load changes.



## Unit 4 - Electrical machines

Electrical machines are devices that convert electrical energy into mechanical energy or vice versa. There are two main types of electrical machines: generators and motors.

1. **Generators** convert mechanical energy into electrical energy. They work on the principle of electromagnetic induction, where a conductor moving in a magnetic field induces an electromotive force (EMF) in the conductor. The most common type of generator is the alternator, which produces alternating current (AC) electricity.

2. **Motors** convert electrical energy into mechanical energy. They work on the principle of electromagnetic force, where a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force. The most common type of motor is the induction motor, which runs on AC electricity.

Electrical machines are used in a wide range of applications, including power generation, transportation, and industrial processes. They are essential components of modern society and play a critical role in the efficient use of energy.



### DC Machines

DC machines are a type of electrical machine that converts mechanical energy into direct current (DC) electrical energy or vice versa. These machines are commonly used in applications that require a constant and regulated output voltage.

#### Construction of DC Machines

A DC machine consists of two main components: the stator and the rotor. The stator is the stationary part of the machine and contains the field windings. The rotor, also known as the armature, is the rotating part of the machine and contains the armature windings.

#### Types of DC Machines

There are two main types of DC machines: DC generators and DC motors. DC generators convert mechanical energy into electrical energy, while DC motors convert electrical energy into mechanical energy.

#### Working Principle of DC Machines

The working principle of a DC machine is based on Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. When a conductor moves in a magnetic field, an electromotive force (EMF) is induced in the conductor. In a DC machine, the armature windings are placed on the rotor and rotate in the magnetic field produced by the field windings on the stator. This induces an EMF in the armature windings, which generates a current.

#### Applications of DC Machines

DC machines are used in a wide range of applications, including electric vehicles, elevators, and industrial machinery. DC motors are commonly used in applications that require precise speed control, while DC generators are used in applications that require a constant and regulated output voltage.

- The EMF equation of a generator is used to determine the induced EMF in a generator.
- The equation is given by: E = (Φ * Z * N * P) / (60 * A)

  - E is the induced EMF

  - Z is the total number of armature conductors
  - N is the speed of the armature in revolutions per minute (RPM)
  - P is the number of poles
  - A is the number of parallel paths in the armature

#### Torque Equation of Motor

- The equation is given by: T = (Φ * I * Z * P) / (2π * A)
- Where:
  - T is the torque produced by the motor
  - Φ is the flux per pole
  - I is the current flowing in the armature
  - Z is the total number of armature conductors
  - P is the number of poles
  - A is the number of parallel paths in the armature
  - 2π is a constant value

### Slip-torque characteristics

The slip-torque characteristics of an induction motor describe the relationship between the slip and the torque produced by the motor. The slip is defined as the difference between the synchronous speed of the rotating magnetic field and the rotor speed, expressed as a percentage of the synchronous speed.

The torque produced by an induction motor is directly proportional to the slip, up to a certain point. This point is known as the maximum torque point, and it occurs at a slip value known as the maximum torque slip. Beyond this point, the torque decreases as the slip increases.

The shape of the slip-torque curve is determined by the design of the motor, and it can be altered by changing the rotor resistance. A high rotor resistance results in a steep slip-torque curve, with a high starting torque and a low maximum torque. A low rotor resistance results in a flatter slip-torque curve, with a lower starting torque and a higher maximum torque.

In summary, the slip-torque characteristics of an induction motor describe the relationship between the slip and the torque produced by the motor. It can be calculated using the formula: `Slip(%) = (Ns - Nr) / Ns * 100`, where Ns is the synchronous speed and Nr is the rotor speed.

2. **Determining rotor speed:** Given the synchronous speed and slip of an induction motor, the rotor speed can be determined using the formula: `Nr = Ns * (1 - Slip(%)/100)`.

3. **Determining synchronous speed:** Given the rotor speed and slip of an induction motor, the synchronous speed can be determined using the formula: `Ns = Nr / (1 - Slip(%)/100)`.

### Fundamentals of Earthing and Lightning Protection

Earthing and lightning protection are important for everyone that uses electrical equipment. Earthing, Lighting Arrestors, and Surge Protection Devices are the leading devices that protect devices against current shock.

A lightning protection system intercepts or diverts lightning and provides a certain path for conducting the surges safely to the ground by adequate down conductors to grounding electrodes. Thus, it helps prevent disastrous events like fires, injuries, and deaths.

Lightning protection grounding helps protect structures and equipment from direct strikes. Overhead ground wires and surge arresters, connected to ground, can limit dangerous system overvoltages to safe values. Fundamentally, grounding an electronic system is the same as grounding any electrical system.

In a lightning protection system, the lightning rod is a single component of the system. The lightning rod requires a connection to earth to give a building protection. Lightning rods come in many different forms, including hollow, solid, pointed, or rounded. All lightning rods are made of conductive materials, such as copper and aluminum.

Common methods for earthing and lightning protection include providing an alternative path for the lightning current to flow to ground and ensuring that lightning current flowing in the lightning protection system (LPS) does not induce dangerous currents in any parallel metallic systems near the LPS.
