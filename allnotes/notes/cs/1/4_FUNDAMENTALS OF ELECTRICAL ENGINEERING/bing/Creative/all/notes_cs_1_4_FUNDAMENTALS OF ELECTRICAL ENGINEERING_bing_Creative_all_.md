# FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING

- Electrical engineering is the branch of engineering that deals with the study, design, and application of electrical and electronic systems, devices, and circuits.
- Electrical engineering covers a wide range of topics, such as power generation and distribution, communication and information systems, control and automation, signal processing and analysis, and computer hardware and software.
- Electrical engineering also involves the use of mathematics, physics, and engineering principles to solve complex problems and design innovative solutions.
- Some of the fundamental concepts of electrical engineering are:

  - Voltage, current, and power: Voltage is the measure of electric potential difference between two points in a circuit. Current is the rate of flow of electric charge in a circuit. Power is the rate of energy transfer or consumption in a circuit.
  - Ohm's law: Ohm's law states that the voltage across a resistor is proportional to the current through it. The constant of proportionality is called the resistance, which is a measure of how much a resistor opposes the current flow.
  - Kirchhoff's laws: Kirchhoff's laws are two rules that govern the conservation of charge and energy in a circuit. Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) states that the algebraic sum of currents at any node in a circuit is zero. Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) states that the algebraic sum of voltages around any loop in a circuit is zero.
  - Circuit elements: Circuit elements are the basic components that make up a circuit, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, diodes, transistors, switches, sources, and loads. Each circuit element has its own characteristic behavior and function in a circuit.
  - Circuit analysis: Circuit analysis is the process of finding the voltage, current, and power across or through each circuit element in a given circuit. Circuit analysis can be done using various methods, such as nodal analysis, mesh analysis, superposition, Thevenin's theorem, Norton's theorem, and maximum power transfer theorem.
  - AC and DC circuits: AC and DC circuits are two types of circuits that differ in the nature of the voltage and current sources. AC stands for alternating current, which means that the voltage and current vary sinusoidally with time. DC stands for direct current, which means that the voltage and current are constant or unidirectional. AC circuits are more common in power systems, while DC circuits are more common in electronic devices.
  - Single-phase and three-phase circuits: Single-phase and three-phase circuits are two types of AC circuits that differ in the number of voltage and current sources. Single-phase circuits have one voltage and one current source, while three-phase circuits have three voltage and three current sources that are phase-shifted by 120 degrees. Three-phase circuits are more efficient and reliable for power transmission and distribution.
  - Transformers: Transformers are devices that use electromagnetic induction to transfer electrical energy from one circuit to another. Transformers can step up or step down the voltage and current levels, depending on the ratio of the number of turns in the primary and secondary coils. Transformers are widely used in power systems, communication systems, and electronic devices.
  - Electromagnetic induction: Electromagnetic induction is the phenomenon of generating an electric current in a conductor when it is exposed to a changing magnetic field. Electromagnetic induction is the basis of many devices, such as generators, motors, transformers, and induction coils.
  - Motors and generators: Motors and generators are devices that convert electrical energy into mechanical energy, and vice versa. Motors use electric current to produce torque and rotation, while generators use mechanical motion to produce electric current. Motors and generators are widely used in industrial, domestic, and transportation applications.



# Unit 1 - DC Circuits

## Introduction

A DC circuit is a circuit that uses direct current (DC) as the source of electric power. DC is the type of current that flows in one direction only, unlike alternating current (AC) that changes direction periodically. DC circuits are commonly used in batteries, solar cells, and electronic devices.

## DC Current

DC current is the unidirectional flow of electric charge. The amount of charge that passes through a point in a circuit per unit time is called the electric current, measured in amperes (A). The direction of the current is defined as the direction that positive charges would flow, from the positive terminal to the negative terminal of the source.

## DC Circuit Types

There are three main types of DC circuits: series, parallel, and series-parallel. Each type has different characteristics and rules for calculating the voltage, current, and resistance in the circuit.

### Series DC Circuit

A series DC circuit is a circuit where the components are connected end to end, forming a single path for the current to flow. The current is the same in every component, and the total voltage across the circuit is equal to the sum of the voltages across each component. The total resistance in the circuit is equal to the sum of the resistances of each component.

### Parallel DC Circuit

A parallel DC circuit is a circuit where the components are connected in separate branches, forming multiple paths for the current to flow. The voltage is the same across every branch, and the total current in the circuit is equal to the sum of the currents in each branch. The total resistance in the circuit is equal to the reciprocal of the sum of the reciprocals of the resistances of each branch.

### Series-Parallel DC Circuit

A series-parallel DC circuit is a circuit that combines both series and parallel connections of components. The circuit can be simplified by applying the rules for series and parallel circuits to each sub-circuit, and then combining the results to find the total voltage, current, and resistance in the circuit.

## DC Circuit Formulas

The following formulas can be used to calculate the voltage, current, and resistance in DC circuits:

- Ohm's law: V = IR, where V is the voltage, I is the current, and R is the resistance.
- Kirchhoff's voltage law: The sum of the voltages around any closed loop in a circuit is zero.
- Kirchhoff's current law: The sum of the currents entering any node in a circuit is equal to the sum of the currents leaving that node.
- Series circuit: V<sub>T</sub> = V<sub>1</sub> + V<sub>2</sub> + ... + V<sub>n</sub>, I<sub>T</sub> = I<sub>1</sub> = I<sub>2</sub> = ... = I<sub>n</sub>, R<sub>T</sub> = R<sub>1</sub> + R<sub>2</sub> + ... + R<sub>n</sub>

# Electrical circuit elements (R, L and C)

- An **electrical circuit** is a closed loop of conductors and components that allows the flow of electric current and energy.
- An **electrical circuit element** is a device or component that has a specific function or property in a circuit.
- The most fundamental and passive electrical circuit elements are **resistor (R)**, **inductor (L)**, and **capacitor (C)**.
- A **resistor** is a circuit element that **opposes** the flow of current and **dissipates** electrical energy as heat. The resistance of a resistor is measured in **ohms (Ω)** and is given by the ratio of voltage across it to the current through it, according to **Ohm's law**.
- An **inductor** is a circuit element that **stores** electrical energy in a **magnetic field** when current flows through it. The inductance of an inductor is measured in **henries (H)** and is given by the ratio of the magnetic flux linkage to the current, according to **Faraday's law**.
- A **capacitor** is a circuit element that **stores** electrical energy in an **electric field** between two conductive plates separated by an insulator. The capacitance of a capacitor is measured in **farads (F)** and is given by the ratio of the electric charge on the plates to the voltage across them, according to **Gauss's law**.
- An **RLC circuit** is an electrical circuit that consists of a resistor, an inductor, and a capacitor, connected in **series** or in **parallel**. The name of the circuit is derived from the letters that are used to denote the constituent components of this circuit, where the sequence of the components may vary from RLC.
- An RLC circuit can exhibit **resonance**, which is a phenomenon where the circuit responds with maximum amplitude to an input signal of a specific frequency, called the **resonant frequency**. The resonant frequency of an RLC circuit depends on the values of the resistance, inductance, and capacitance, and whether the circuit is series or parallel.
- An RLC circuit can also exhibit **transient** and **steady-state** behaviors, which are the responses of the circuit to a change in the input signal or the initial conditions. The transient behavior is the short-term response that occurs immediately after the change, while the steady-state behavior is the long-term response that occurs after the transient behavior has died out.
- The analysis of RLC circuits involves applying **Kirchhoff's laws**, which are the laws of conservation of charge and energy in a circuit, and solving the resulting **differential equations** that describe the voltage and current in the circuit elements. The solutions can be expressed in terms of **complex numbers**, **phasors**, or **Laplace transforms**, depending on the type and frequency of the input signal.



# Concept of active and passive elements

- Active and passive elements are the two main types of electronic circuit elements that have different characteristics and functions.
- Active elements are the ones that can supply energy to a circuit or control the flow of current in a circuit. They can also provide amplification or gain for electronic signals. Examples of active elements are batteries, generators, transistors, op-amps, etc.
- Passive elements are the ones that can only receive, store, or dissipate energy in a circuit. They cannot control the current direction or provide amplification. They are mainly used for energy storage, discharge, oscillation, filtering, etc. Examples of passive elements are resistors, capacitors, inductors, etc.
- The main difference between active and passive elements is that active elements can deliver more power to a circuit than they receive, while passive elements can only deliver less power than they receive.
- Some of the criteria to distinguish between active and passive elements are:

| Criteria | Active Element | Passive Element |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy source | Has an internal or external energy source | Does not have an energy source |
| Current direction | Can control the current direction | Cannot control the current direction |
| Power gain | Can provide power gain for signals | Cannot provide power gain for signals |
| Function | Used for current and voltage control, signal processing, etc. | Used for energy storage, discharge, oscillation, filtering, etc. |
| Symbol | Usually has an arrow to indicate the direction of current or voltage | Usually does not have an arrow |

- The concept of active and passive elements is important for understanding the behavior and analysis of electrical circuits. Active elements can modify the input signals and perform various functions, while passive elements can store or release energy and affect the impedance and frequency response of the circuit.



# Voltage and Current Sources

## Introduction

- Voltage and current sources are devices that provide electrical energy to a circuit.
- Voltage sources maintain a constant voltage across their terminals, regardless of the current they deliver.
- Current sources maintain a constant current through their terminals, regardless of the voltage they produce.
- Voltage and current sources can be classified as ideal or practical, depending on how closely they match the ideal behavior.

## Ideal Voltage Source

- An ideal voltage source is a device that provides a constant voltage to any load connected to it, regardless of the current drawn from it.
- An ideal voltage source has zero internal resistance, meaning that it can supply any amount of current without losing any voltage.
- An ideal voltage source is represented by a circle with a plus and minus sign inside, indicating the polarity of the voltage.
- An example of an ideal voltage source is a battery that never runs out of charge or loses voltage.

## Practical Voltage Source

- A practical voltage source is a device that approximates the behavior of an ideal voltage source, but has some limitations and imperfections.
- A practical voltage source has a nonzero internal resistance, meaning that it loses some voltage when it supplies current to a load.
- A practical voltage source is represented by a circle with a plus and minus sign inside, in series with a resistor, indicating the internal resistance.
- An example of a practical voltage source is a real battery that has a finite charge and voltage.

## Ideal Current Source

- An ideal current source is a device that provides a constant current to any load connected to it, regardless of the voltage across it.
- An ideal current source has infinite internal resistance, meaning that it can produce any amount of voltage without changing the current.
- An ideal current source is represented by a circle with an arrow inside, indicating the direction of the current.
- An example of an ideal current source is a solar cell that generates a constant current from sunlight, regardless of the load.

## Practical Current Source

- A practical current source is a device that approximates the behavior of an ideal current source, but has some limitations and imperfections.
- A practical current source has a finite internal resistance, meaning that it cannot produce an infinite voltage and the current may vary with the load.
- A practical current source is represented by a circle with an arrow inside, in parallel with a resistor, indicating the internal resistance.
- An example of a practical current source is a real solar cell that has a limited voltage and current output.



# Concept of Linearity

- Linearity is a property of an element or a system that describes a linear relationship between cause and effect  .
- A linear element or system is one that obeys the principles of **homogeneity** and **additivity**  .
- Homogeneity means that if the input is scaled by a constant factor, the output is also scaled by the same factor  .
- Additivity means that if the input is the sum of two or more signals, the output is the sum of the corresponding outputs for each signal  .
- In electrical engineering, linearity is often applied to the current-voltage characteristics of circuit elements  .
- A linear element is one that has a straight-line current-voltage curve, such as a resistor  .
- A nonlinear element is one that has a curved or discontinuous current-voltage curve, such as a diode or a transistor  .
- Linearity is also applied to the input-output characteristics of systems, such as sensors, amplifiers, or filters  .
- A linear system is one that has a proportional and additive relationship between the input and the output signals  .
- A nonlinear system is one that has a distorted or nonlinear relationship between the input and the output signals  .
- Linearity is an important concept in electrical engineering because it simplifies the analysis and design of circuits and systems  .
- For example, the **superposition principle** states that the response of a linear system to a combination of inputs is the sum of the responses to each input individually .
- This principle allows us to break down complex circuits into simpler parts and solve them separately .
- Another example is the **Fourier transform** which states that any periodic signal can be decomposed into a sum of sinusoidal signals of different frequencies.
- This transform allows us to analyze the frequency response of linear systems and design filters and modulators.



# Unilateral and Bilateral Elements

- Unilateral and bilateral elements are the two different types of electrical / electronic circuit elements on the basis of their V-I characteristics on reversal of voltage polarity.
- Unilateral elements are those that allow the current in only one direction and offer different impedance in different directions of current flow . Examples: Diodes, Transistors, etc.
- Bilateral elements are those that allow the current in both directions and offer the same impedance in either direction of current flow . Examples: Resistors, Inductors, Capacitors, etc.
- Unilateral and bilateral circuits are the circuits that contain only unilateral or only bilateral elements, or a combination of both.
- Unilateral and bilateral circuits have different properties and applications, such as:
  - Unilateral circuits are used for rectification, switching, modulation, etc. Bilateral circuits are used for filtering, impedance matching, signal processing, etc.
  - Unilateral circuits are non-linear and asymmetric. Bilateral circuits are linear and symmetric.
  - Unilateral circuits are more sensitive to noise and distortion. Bilateral circuits are more robust and stable.
  - Unilateral circuits are more complex and difficult to analyze. Bilateral circuits are simpler and easier to analyze.



# Kirchhoff's laws for DC circuits

Kirchhoff's laws are two principles that govern the analysis of electric circuits. They are named after Gustav Kirchhoff, a German physicist who formulated them in 1845. They are:

- Kirchhoff's current law (KCL): The algebraic sum of the currents entering and leaving any node in a circuit is zero. This means that the total charge flowing into a node is equal to the total charge flowing out of it. This law is also known as the junction rule or the conservation of charge principle.
- Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): The algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed loop in a circuit is zero. This means that the total energy gained or lost by the charges in a loop is zero. This law is also known as the loop rule or the conservation of energy principle.

## Applications of Kirchhoff's laws

Kirchhoff's laws are used to find:

- The values of current, voltage and internal resistance in DC circuits.
- The unknown resistance in a circuit using methods such as Wheatstone bridge, mesh analysis or node analysis.
- The equivalent resistance of a complex circuit by simplifying it into series and parallel combinations of resistors.
- The power dissipated or delivered by each element in a circuit.

## Limitations of Kirchhoff's laws

Kirchhoff's laws are valid only for:

- Linear circuits, where the voltage and current are proportional to each other for each element.
- Time-invariant circuits, where the values of the elements do not change with time.
- Lumped circuits, where the physical dimensions of the elements are negligible compared to the wavelength of the signals.

Kirchhoff's laws do not apply to:

- Nonlinear circuits, where the voltage and current are not proportional to each other for some elements, such as diodes, transistors, etc.
- Time-varying circuits, where the values of the elements change with time, such as capacitors, inductors, etc.
- Distributed circuits, where the physical dimensions of the elements are comparable to or larger than the wavelength of the signals, such as transmission lines, antennas, etc.



# Mesh and Nodal Methods of Analysis

## Introduction

- Mesh and nodal methods of analysis are two systematic techniques for solving circuits that contain multiple sources and resistors.
- Both methods are based on applying Kirchhoff's laws to the circuit, but they differ in the choice of variables and equations.
- Mesh analysis uses loop currents as variables and Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) as equations.
- Nodal analysis uses node voltages as variables and Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) as equations.
- Both methods can be used to analyze circuits with dependent sources, but nodal analysis is more convenient for circuits with voltage-controlled sources, while mesh analysis is more convenient for circuits with current-controlled sources.

## Mesh Analysis

- A mesh is a loop that does not contain any other loops within it.
- A mesh current is a current that flows around a mesh in a clockwise or counterclockwise direction.
- To apply mesh analysis, the following steps are followed:

  1. Identify all the meshes in the circuit and assign a mesh current to each one. The direction of the mesh current can be chosen arbitrarily, but it is usually clockwise.
  2. Write a KVL equation for each mesh, expressing the voltage drops across the resistors and sources in terms of the mesh currents. If there are dependent sources in the circuit, express their controlling variables in terms of the mesh currents as well.
  3. Solve the system of linear equations for the mesh currents using any method, such as substitution, elimination, or matrix inversion.
  4. Calculate the voltages and currents of interest using Ohm's law and the mesh currents.

## Nodal Analysis

- A node is a point where two or more branches of a circuit meet.
- A node voltage is the voltage difference between a node and a reference node, which is usually chosen as the ground or the negative terminal of a source.
- To apply nodal analysis, the following steps are followed:

  1. Identify all the nodes in the circuit and assign a node voltage to each one, except the reference node, which is assigned zero volts.
  2. Write a KCL equation for each node, expressing the incoming and outgoing currents in terms of the node voltages and the source currents. If there are dependent sources in the circuit, express their controlling variables in terms of the node voltages as well.
  3. Solve the system of linear equations for the node voltages using any method, such as substitution, elimination, or matrix inversion.
  4. Calculate the currents and voltages of interest using Ohm's law and the node voltages.

- The voltage across each component is different and depends on the frequency of the source and the values of R, L and C.
- The current through each component is the same and is equal to the source current.

$$Z = R + j(X_L - X_C) = R + j\omega L - \frac{j}{\omega C}$$

where $j$ is the imaginary unit, $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the source, $X_L$ is the inductive reactance, and $X_C$ is the capacitive reactance.

$$Z = |Z| \angle \phi = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2} \angle \tan^{-1} \left(\frac{X_L - X_C}{R}\right)$$

- The phase angle indicates the phase difference between the source voltage and the source current. It can be positive, negative or zero depending on the relative values of R, L and C.
- If $X_L > X_C$, the circuit is inductive and the current lags the voltage by $\phi$.
- If $X_L < X_C$, the circuit is capacitive and the current leads the voltage by $\phi$.
- If $X_L = X_C$, the circuit is resonant and the current is in phase with the voltage. The impedance is purely resistive and has the minimum value of $R$.

$$V_R = IZ_R = IR \angle 0$$

$$P_R = \Re(V_R I^*) = \Re(IR \angle 0 \cdot I \angle -\phi) = IR^2 \cos \phi$$

# Apparent, Active and Reactive Power

- In an AC circuit, the power delivered by the source is not always equal to the power consumed by the load. This is because the AC voltage and current may not be in phase with each other, due to the presence of reactive elements like capacitors and inductors in the circuit.
- The power that is actually consumed by the load is called **active power** or **real power**. It is measured in watts (W) and is symbolized by the capital letter P. Active power is the product of the voltage and current that are in phase with each other, i.e. P = VI cos θ, where θ is the phase angle between voltage and current.
- The power that is stored and released by the reactive elements is called **reactive power**. It is measured in volt-amperes reactive (VAR) and is symbolized by the capital letter Q. Reactive power is the product of the voltage and current that are 90 degrees out of phase with each other, i.e. Q = VI sin θ.
- The power that is apparent to the source is called **apparent power**. It is measured in volt-amperes (VA) and is symbolized by the capital letter S. Apparent power is the product of the RMS voltage and current, without reference to phase angle, i.e. S = VI.
- The relationship between active, reactive and apparent power can be represented by a right-angled triangle called the **power triangle**. The active power is the horizontal side, the reactive power is the vertical side, and the apparent power is the hypotenuse of the triangle. The angle θ is also called the **power factor angle**, and the cosine of this angle is called the **power factor**. The power factor is a measure of how efficiently the power is used in the circuit. It ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 means that all the power is active and 0 means that all the power is reactive.
- The power triangle can be expressed by the following equations:

  - S^2 = P^2 + Q^2
  - P = S cos θ
  - Q = S sin θ
  - cos θ = P/S
  - sin θ = Q/S

- The power factor can be improved by adding or subtracting reactive elements to the circuit, such as capacitors or inductors. This is called **power factor correction** and it can reduce the apparent power and the losses in the circuit.



# Power factor

- Power factor is a dimensionless quantity that measures how effectively an AC circuit uses the supplied power.
- Power factor is defined as the ratio of real power (P) to apparent power (S) in an AC circuit. Real power is the average power that is converted to useful work, while apparent power is the product of the RMS values of voltage and current.
- Power factor can also be expressed as the cosine of the phase angle (θ) between the voltage and current waveforms in an AC circuit. The phase angle represents the degree of alignment or misalignment between the voltage and current.
- Power factor can range from 0 to 1, where 0 means no power is delivered to the load and 1 means all the power is delivered to the load. A power factor of 1 is also called unity power factor, and it indicates that the voltage and current are in phase and the circuit is purely resistive.
- A power factor less than 1 is called lagging power factor, and it indicates that the current lags behind the voltage and the circuit has some inductive reactance. A power factor greater than 1 is called leading power factor, and it indicates that the current leads the voltage and the circuit has some capacitive reactance.
- Power factor is important because it affects the efficiency and reliability of an AC power system. A low power factor means that more current is required to deliver the same amount of real power, which increases the losses and heating in the transmission lines and transformers. # Magnetic circuits

- A magnetic circuit is a closed path to which a magnetic field, represented as lines of magnetic flux, is confined.
- A magnetic circuit is analogous to an electric circuit, but with magnetic quantities instead of electric quantities. The main difference is that in a magnetic circuit, nothing actually flows, whereas in an electric circuit, electric charge flows.
- A magnetic circuit consists of a structure composed for the most part of high permeability magnetic material, such as iron, which guides the magnetic flux along the desired paths.
- The presence of high permeability material causes the magnetic flux to be concentrated in the paths defined by the structure, much as currents are confined to the conductors of an electric circuit.
- A magnetic circuit may have air gaps or other materials in the path, which increase the reluctance of the circuit and reduce the flux.
- Some examples of magnetic circuits are: horseshoe magnet with iron keeper (low-reluctance circuit), horseshoe magnet with no keeper (high-reluctance circuit), electric motor (variable-reluctance circuit), and transformer (multi-loop circuit).
- The main quantities used to analyze a magnetic circuit are: magnetic flux (Φ), magnetic flux density (B), magnetic field intensity (H), magnetomotive force (MMF), and reluctance (R).
- The basic laws of magnetic circuits are: Ohm's law for magnetic circuits, which relates MMF, flux, and reluctance; Kirchhoff's current law for magnetic circuits, which states that the algebraic sum of the fluxes at any node is zero; and Kirchhoff's voltage law for magnetic circuits, which states that the algebraic sum of the MMFs around any loop is zero.
- The analysis of magnetic circuits involves applying these laws to find the unknown quantities, such as flux, MMF, or reluctance, in a given circuit.
- The design of magnetic circuits involves choosing the appropriate materials, dimensions, and configurations to achieve the desired performance, such as maximum flux, minimum MMF, or optimal efficiency.



# Ideal and Practical Transformer

- A transformer is a device that transfers electrical energy from one circuit to another through mutual induction, without changing the frequency of the alternating current.
- An ideal transformer is a hypothetical transformer that has no energy losses, no winding resistance, no leakage flux, infinite permeability of the core, and 100% efficiency.
- A practical transformer is a real transformer that has some energy losses, finite winding resistance, some leakage flux, finite permeability of the core, and less than 100% efficiency.
- The main differences between an ideal and practical transformer are:

  - Efficiency: An ideal transformer has 100% efficiency, which means that the input power is equal to the output power. A practical transformer has less than 100% efficiency, which means that some power is lost as heat, core loss, and copper loss. The efficiency of a practical transformer depends on the power factor and loading of the transformer.
  - Winding resistance: An ideal transformer has zero (or negligible) winding resistance, which means that there is no voltage drop across the windings. A practical transformer has finite winding resistance, which means that there is some voltage drop across the windings, and hence the output voltage is less than the input voltage.
  - Leakage flux: An ideal transformer has no leakage flux, which means that the whole flux of the magnetic circuit links both the primary and secondary coils. A practical transformer has some leakage flux, which means that some flux does not link both the coils, and hence the mutual inductance is less than the self-inductance of the coils.
  - Core permeability: An ideal transformer has infinite core permeability, which means that the core can carry any amount of flux without saturation. A practical transformer has finite core permeability, which means that the core has a limit to the amount of flux it can carry, and beyond that it saturates and causes distortion in the output voltage.
  - Core loss: An ideal transformer has zero core loss, which means that there is no hysteresis loss or eddy current loss in the core. A practical transformer has some core loss, which means that some energy is dissipated as heat in the core due to the reversal of magnetization and the circulation of induced currents in the core.

# Losses in Transformers

A transformer is a device that transfers electrical energy from one circuit to another by electromagnetic induction. It consists of two or more coils of wire that are wound around a magnetic core. The primary coil is connected to the input voltage source, and the secondary coil is connected to the output load. The transformer works on the principle of mutual induction, which means that a changing current in one coil induces a voltage in the other coil.

However, a transformer is not a perfect device, and some energy is lost during the process of energy transfer. These losses reduce the efficiency and performance of the transformer. The losses in a transformer can be classified into four main types     :

- **Copper loss** or **I2R loss** or **resistive loss**: This is the power loss in the transformer windings due to their electrical resistance. The resistance of the windings causes heat to be generated when current flows through them. The copper loss depends on the current and the resistance of the windings, and it varies with the load. The copper loss can be calculated by the formula:

  `Pc = I1^2 * R1 + I2^2 * R2`

  where Pc is the copper loss, I1 and I2 are the currents in the primary and secondary windings, and R1 and R2 are the resistances of the primary and secondary windings.

- **Core loss** or **iron loss**: This is the power loss in the transformer core due to the magnetic properties of the core material. The core loss consists of two components:

  - **Hysteresis loss**: This is the power loss due to the repeated magnetization and demagnetization of the core material as the alternating current changes direction. The hysteresis loss depends on the frequency and the maximum flux density of the core, and it is proportional to the area of the hysteresis loop of the core material. The hysteresis loss can be calculated by the formula:

    `Ph = Kh * f * Bm^x`

    where Ph is the hysteresis loss, Kh is the hysteresis constant of the core material, f is the frequency of the alternating current, Bm is the maximum flux density of the core, and x is the Steinmetz exponent of the core material.

  - **Eddy current loss**: This is the power loss due to the currents induced in the core material by the changing magnetic flux. The eddy currents circulate within the core and generate heat. The eddy current loss depends on the frequency, the maximum flux density, the thickness, and the resistivity of the core material. The eddy current loss can be calculated by the formula:

    `Pe = Ke * f^2 * Bm^2 * t^2`

    where Pe is the eddy current loss, Ke is the eddy current constant of the core material, f is the frequency of the alternating current, Bm is the maximum flux density of the core, t is the thickness of the core, and ρ is the resistivity of the core material.

  The core loss is constant and does not vary with the load.

- **Stray loss**: This is the power loss due to the leakage of magnetic flux from the transformer. The leakage flux does not link with the secondary winding and induces eddy currents in the nearby conductive objects, such as the transformer tank, the clamps, and the bolts. The stray loss depends on the design and construction of the transformer, and it is difficult to measure and calculate. The stray loss is usually estimated as a percentage of the full-load copper loss.

- **Dielectric loss**: This is the power loss due to the heating of the insulating materials in the transformer, such as the oil, the paper, and the varnish. The dielectric loss is caused by the polarization and depolarization of the electric dipoles in the insulating materials as the alternating voltage changes direction. The dielectric loss depends on the frequency, the voltage, the temperature, and the quality of the insulating materials. The dielectric loss is usually negligible compared to the other losses in the transformer.

The total loss in a transformer is the sum of the copper loss, the core loss, the stray loss, and the dielectric loss. The total loss can be calculated by the formula:

`Pt = Pc + Pi + Ps + Pd`

where Pt is the total loss, Pc is the copper loss,

# DC Machines

## Introduction

- A DC machine is an electro-mechanical energy conversion device that can either convert mechanical power to electrical power (DC generator) or electrical power to mechanical power (DC motor).
- The principle of operation of a DC machine is based on the interaction of a current-carrying conductor and a magnetic field.
- A DC machine consists of two main parts: a stationary part called the stator and a rotating part called the rotor.
- The stator provides the magnetic field by means of permanent magnets or electromagnets, and the rotor carries the conductors that cut the magnetic field and induce the emf or torque.
- The rotor conductors are connected to a commutator, which is a device that reverses the direction of the current in the conductors every half rotation, so that the output voltage or torque is unidirectional.

## Construction of a DC Machine

- The main components of a DC machine are:

  - **Yoke**: The outer frame of the machine that supports and protects the other parts. It is usually made of cast iron or steel. It also acts as a part of the magnetic circuit and provides a low reluctance path for the magnetic flux.
  - **Poles**: The projections on the inner side of the yoke that carry the field windings. They are usually made of laminated steel to reduce eddy current losses. They have pole shoes at their ends to increase the area of contact with the air gap and distribute the flux uniformly.
  - **Field windings**: The coils of wire wound on the poles that produce the magnetic field when energized by a DC source. They can be connected in series or parallel with the armature winding, depending on the type of the machine.
  - **Armature**: The rotating part of the machine that carries the armature windings. It is usually made of laminated steel to reduce eddy current losses. It has slots on its surface to accommodate the armature conductors.
  - **Armature windings**: The coils of wire wound on the armature slots that cut the magnetic field and induce the emf or torque. They are connected in series-parallel arrangement to form a closed loop. They are also connected to the commutator segments by means of brushes.
  - **Commutator**: A cylindrical device mounted on the shaft of the armature that consists of a number of segments (equal to the number of armature coils) insulated from each other. It reverses the direction of the current in the armature coils every half rotation, so that the output voltage or torque is unidirectional.
  - **Brushes**: The sliding contacts that connect the armature windings to the external circuit. They are usually made of carbon or graphite and are pressed against the commutator segments by means of springs. They collect the current from the commutator and transfer it to the load.

## Types of DC Machines

  - **Series DC machine**: The field winding is connected in series with the armature winding. The current in the field winding is the same as the current in the armature winding. The field flux is proportional to the armature current. This type of machine has a high starting torque and a variable speed. It is used for applications that require constant speed and low torque, such as fans, blowers, pumps, etc.
  - **Compound DC machine**: The field winding is divided into two parts: a series field winding and a shunt field winding. The series field winding is connected in series with the armature winding, and the shunt field winding is connected in parallel with the armature winding. The field flux is the sum of the series field flux and the shunt field flux. This type of machine has a moderate starting torque and a variable speed. It is used for applications that require a combination of high torque and constant speed, such as generators, compressors, elevators, etc.



# Principle and Construction of Electrical Machines

Electrical machines are devices that convert mechanical energy to electrical energy and vice versa. They are classified into two main categories: direct current (DC) machines and alternating current (AC) machines. DC machines operate on direct current, while AC machines operate on alternating current.

## DC Machines

DC machines are further divided into DC generators and DC motors. DC generators produce direct current by converting mechanical power to electrical power, while DC motors consume direct current by converting electrical power to mechanical power.

### Principle of DC Machines

The working principle of a DC machine is based on the Lorentz force law, which states that when a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field, it experiences a force that is perpendicular to both the current and the magnetic field. This force causes the conductor to rotate, which is called torque. The direction of the force can be determined by Fleming's left-hand rule for motors and Fleming's right-hand rule for generators.

### Construction of DC Machines

The main components of a DC machine are:

- Yoke: The outer frame of the machine that supports and protects the other parts. It is usually made of cast iron or steel. It also provides a low reluctance path for the magnetic flux.
- Poles: The parts of the machine that produce the magnetic field. They consist of pole cores and pole shoes. The pole cores are made of laminated steel and carry the field coils. The pole shoes are attached to the pole cores and spread the flux over the armature.
- Field coils: The coils of wire that carry direct current and generate the magnetic flux. They are wound on the pole cores and connected in series or parallel.
- Armature: The rotating part of the machine that carries the armature conductors. It consists of armature core and armature winding. The armature core is made of laminated steel and provides a low reluctance path for the flux. The armature winding is the arrangement of conductors that carry the induced current in generators or the input current in motors. The conductors are insulated from each other and from the core.
- Commutator: The cylindrical device that connects the armature winding to the external circuit. It consists of segments of copper or brass that are insulated from each other and from the shaft. The commutator reverses the direction of the current in the armature conductors every half rotation, so that the torque remains constant.
- Brushes: The stationary contacts that slide on the commutator and transfer the current between the armature and the external circuit. They are usually made of carbon or graphite and are spring-loaded to maintain contact with the commutator.

## AC Machines

AC machines are further divided into transformers, induction machines, synchronous machines, and special machines. Transformers are static devices that transfer electrical power from one circuit to another by electromagnetic induction. Induction machines are asynchronous machines that operate on the principle of electromagnetic induction. Synchronous machines are machines that operate at a constant speed and are synchronized with the supply frequency. Special machines are machines that have specific applications or characteristics, such as stepper motors, brushless DC motors, switched reluctance motors, etc.

## EMF equation of generator

- A generator is a device that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by rotating a coil in a magnetic field.
- The emf induced in the coil is given by the Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, which states that the emf is equal to the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage.
- The magnetic flux linkage is the product of the magnetic flux and the number of turns in the coil, and it depends on the angle between the coil and the magnetic field.
- The emf equation of a generator can be derived by considering a single loop of wire with N turns and area A, rotating with a constant angular speed ω in a uniform magnetic field B.
- The magnetic flux through the loop is given by Φ = B A cos θ, where θ is the angle between the normal to the loop and the magnetic field.
- The flux linkage is then N Φ = N B A cos θ, and the emf is the negative of the rate of change of flux linkage, i.e.,

  E = - d/dt (N Φ) = - N B A d/dt (cos θ)

- Since the loop is rotating with a constant angular speed ω, the angle θ changes with time as θ = ω t, and the derivative of cos θ is - ω sin θ. Therefore, the emf is

  E = N B A ω sin θ = N B A ω sin (ω t)

- This equation shows that the emf is a sinusoidal function of time, with a frequency equal to the angular speed of the loop, and an amplitude equal to N B A ω, which is also called the peak emf, E0. Thus, the emf equation of a generator can be written as

  E = E0 sin (ω t)

- For a DC generator, the coil is connected to a commutator, which reverses the direction of the current every half cycle, so that the output emf is always positive. The emf equation of a DC generator is then

  E = E0 |sin (ω t)|

## Torque equation of motor

- A motor is a device that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy by passing a current through a coil in a magnetic field, so that a torque is exerted on the coil, and it starts to rotate.
- The torque on the coil is given by the Lorentz force law, which states that the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field is F = I l B sin θ, where I is the current, l is the length of the wire, B is the magnetic field, and θ is the angle between the wire and the magnetic field.
- The torque is then the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the wire, i.e.,

  τ = r F sin θ = I l B r sin θ sin θ = I l B r sin^2 θ

- For a single loop of wire with N turns and area A, the length of the wire is l = 2 π r, where r is the radius of the loop, and the area is A = π r^2. Therefore, the torque is

  τ = I N B A sin^2 θ = I N B π r^3 sin^2 θ

- This equation shows that the torque is a sinusoidal function of the angle θ, with a maximum value of I N B π r^3 when θ = 90°, and a zero value when θ = 0° or 180°. The torque equation of a motor can be written as

  τ = τ0 sin^2 θ

  where τ0 = I N B π r^3 is the peak torque.

# Three Phase Induction Motor

A three phase induction motor is a type of AC motor that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy by using the principle of electromagnetic induction. It is one of the most commonly used motors in various industrial applications.

## Working Principle

A three phase induction motor works on the following principle:

- A three phase AC supply is given to the stator windings, which creates a rotating magnetic field (RMF) in the air gap. The RMF has a constant magnitude and rotates at a synchronous speed (Ns), which depends on the supply frequency (f) and the number of poles (p) of the stator.

- The RMF induces an electromotive force (EMF) in the rotor conductors, which are either short-circuited or connected to an external resistance. The induced EMF causes a current to flow in the rotor conductors, which in turn produces a magnetic field in the rotor.

- The interaction between the stator RMF and the rotor magnetic field creates a torque on the rotor, which makes it rotate. The rotor speed (Nr) is always less than the synchronous speed (Ns), and the difference is called the slip (s).

- The slip is a measure of how much the rotor lags behind the stator RMF. The slip can be expressed as:

  s = (Ns - Nr) / Ns

- The slip varies from 0 to 1, depending on the load and the rotor resistance. A higher slip means a higher torque and a lower speed, and vice versa.

## Types of Three Phase Induction Motors

There are two main types of three phase induction motors, based on the construction and connection of the rotor:

- Squirrel cage induction motor: This type of motor has a rotor that consists of a cylindrical core with bars of copper or aluminum embedded in slots. The bars are short-circuited at both ends by end rings, forming a cage-like structure. The rotor has no external connection, and the induced current flows through the bars and the end rings. This type of motor is simple, rugged, and cheap, but has a low starting torque and a poor speed control.

- Slip ring or wound rotor induction motor: This type of motor has a rotor that consists of a laminated core with coils of insulated wire wound on it. The coils are connected to slip rings, which are mounted on the rotor shaft. The slip rings are connected to an external resistance or a rheostat, which can be varied to control the rotor resistance. This type of motor has a high starting torque and a better speed control, but is more complex, expensive, and requires more maintenance.

## Principle of operation

The principle of operation of electrical machines is based on the interaction between electric currents and magnetic fields. When a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field, it experiences a force that is perpendicular to both the current and the field. This force is called the Lorentz force and it can be used to rotate the conductor or the magnetic field. The direction of the force can be determined by the right-hand rule: if the thumb, index finger, and middle finger of the right hand are aligned with the current, field, and force respectively, then the fingers form a right angle.

In a DC machine, the magnetic field is produced by permanent magnets or electromagnets that are fixed to the stator (the stationary part of the machine). The current is supplied to the rotor (the rotating part of the machine) through brushes and commutators that switch the direction of the current as the rotor rotates. This ensures that the torque (the rotational force) on the rotor is always in the same direction and the rotor can rotate continuously.

In an AC machine, the magnetic field is produced by alternating currents that are supplied to the stator through a three-phase or single-phase power source. The rotor can be either a squirrel-cage type or a wound type. A squirrel-cage rotor consists of copper or aluminum bars that are short-circuited by end rings. A wound rotor consists of coils that are connected to slip rings and brushes. The current in the rotor is induced by the magnetic field of the stator through electromagnetic induction. The torque on the rotor depends on the phase difference between the stator and rotor currents. The rotor can rotate synchronously (at the same speed as the stator field) or asynchronously (at a different speed than the stator field).

## Methods of starting

The methods of starting electrical machines depend on the type and size of the machine, the load characteristics, and the power supply. Some of the common methods of starting are:

- Full voltage or direct-on-line (DOL) starting: This is the simplest and most economical method of starting, where the machine is directly connected to the full voltage of the power supply. This method is suitable for small and medium-sized machines that have low starting currents and high starting torques. However, this method can cause high inrush currents, voltage dips, and mechanical stresses on the machine and the load.

- Reduced voltage or star-delta (Y-Δ) starting: This is a method of starting where the stator windings of a three-phase AC machine are initially connected in a star (Y) configuration and then switched to a delta (Δ) configuration after the machine reaches a certain speed. This method reduces the starting current and voltage by a factor of √3, but also reduces the starting torque by a factor of 3. This method is suitable for medium and large-sized machines that have high starting currents and low starting torques.

- Soft starting: This is a method of starting where the voltage and frequency of the power supply are gradually increased from zero to the rated values using a device called a soft starter. This method reduces the starting current and voltage, and provides a smooth acceleration of the machine and the load. This method is suitable for large and sensitive machines that require a gentle start.

- Variable frequency drive (VFD) starting: This is a method of starting where the voltage and frequency of the power supply are varied according to the speed and torque requirements of the machine and the load using a device called a variable frequency drive. This method provides the best control over the starting current, voltage, torque, and speed of the machine and the load. This method is suitable for applications that require variable speed and torque, such as fans, pumps, and conveyors.

# Three Phase Synchronous Machines

- A three phase synchronous machine is a type of electric machine that can operate as either a generator or a motor.
- A synchronous generator converts mechanical energy into three phase electrical energy, while a synchronous motor converts three phase electrical energy into mechanical energy.
- A synchronous machine consists of two main parts: a stator and a rotor.
- The stator is the stationary part of the machine that contains a three phase winding, which is connected to the external AC supply or load.
- The rotor is the rotating part of the machine that contains a direct current (DC) winding, which is excited by a DC source or an exciter.
- The DC winding on the rotor produces a magnetic field that rotates at the same speed as the rotor, called the synchronous speed.
- The synchronous speed is determined by the frequency of the AC supply and the number of poles on the rotor, according to the formula: Ns = 120f/P, where Ns is the synchronous speed in revolutions per minute (rpm), f is the frequency in hertz (Hz), and P is the number of poles.
- The rotor can be either cylindrical or salient-pole, depending on the shape and distribution of the magnetic poles.
- A cylindrical rotor has a smooth surface and uniform air gap, and is used for high-speed machines, such as turbo-generators.
- A salient-pole rotor has protruding poles and non-uniform air gap, and is used for low-speed machines, such as hydro-generators and single-phase motors.
- The interaction between the rotating magnetic field of the rotor and the rotating magnetic field of the stator produces a torque that causes the rotor to rotate at the synchronous speed, regardless of the load or input power.
- A synchronous machine is said to be in synchronism when the rotor and the stator fields are rotating at the same speed and direction.
- A synchronous machine can operate at different power factors, depending on the magnitude and direction of the rotor field with respect to the stator field.
- A synchronous machine can operate at unity power factor, leading power factor, or lagging power factor, by adjusting the DC excitation current on the rotor.
- A synchronous machine can also operate as a synchronous condenser, which is a device that can supply or absorb reactive power to or from the power system, by overexciting or underexciting the rotor field.



# Principle of operation of alternator and synchronous motor

- An **alternator** or **synchronous generator** is a device that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by producing alternating current (AC).
- A **synchronous motor** is a device that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy by rotating at a constant speed in synchronism with the AC supply frequency.
- Both devices work on the principle of **electromagnetic induction**, i.e., when the flux linking a conductor changes, an EMF is induced in the conductor .

## Alternator

- An alternator consists of a **stator** and a **rotor**. The stator is the stationary part that contains a set of coils called the **armature winding**. The rotor is the rotating part that contains a set of magnets or electromagnets called the **field winding**.
- The rotor is driven by a prime mover, such as a turbine, engine, or water wheel, and creates a **rotating magnetic field** that induces an EMF in the stator coils.
- The EMF generated in the stator coils depends on the **speed of rotation** of the rotor, the **number of poles** on the rotor, the **strength of the magnetic field** on the rotor, and the **number of turns** on the stator coils.
- The EMF generated in the stator coils is **alternating** in nature, i.e., it changes its direction and magnitude periodically. The frequency of the EMF is equal to the **product of the speed of rotation and the number of poles** divided by 120.

## Synchronous motor

- A synchronous motor consists of a **stator** and a **rotor**. The stator is the stationary part that contains a set of coils called the **stator winding**. The rotor is the rotating part that contains a set of magnets or electromagnets called the **rotor winding**.
- The stator winding is connected to a **three-phase AC supply** that creates a **rotating magnetic field** in the stator. The rotor winding is connected to a **DC supply** that creates a **constant magnetic field** in the rotor.
- The rotor is attracted by the stator magnetic field and tries to align itself with it. When the rotor reaches the **synchronous speed**, i.e., the speed at which the stator magnetic field rotates, the rotor locks in with the stator magnetic field and rotates at a **constant speed**.
- The speed of the synchronous motor depends on the **frequency of the AC supply** and the **number of poles** on the stator and the rotor. The speed of the synchronous motor is equal to the **product of the frequency and 120** divided by the **number of poles** .

# ELCB

ELCB stands for Earth Leakage Circuit Breaker. It is a safety device used in electrical installations with high earth impedance to prevent shock. It detects small stray voltages on the metal enclosures of electrical equipment, and interrupts the circuit if a dangerous voltage is detected   .

## Types of ELCB

There are two types of ELCBs:

- Voltage-operated ELCB: This type of ELCB monitors the voltage difference between the live conductor and the earth. If the voltage exceeds a preset value, the ELCB trips and disconnects the power supply. This type of ELCB is obsolete and has some disadvantages, such as being insensitive to balanced faults and being prone to nuisance tripping  .
- Current-operated ELCB: This type of ELCB monitors the current difference between the live and neutral conductors. If the current difference exceeds a preset value, the ELCB trips and disconnects the power supply. This type of ELCB is also known as Residual Current Device (RCD) or Residual Current Circuit Breaker (RCCB). This type of ELCB is more reliable and sensitive than the voltage-operated ELCB, and can detect both balanced and unbalanced faults   .

Some of the advantages of using an ELCB are:

- It can protect the people from electric shock, fire, and equipment damage caused by earth leakage  .
- It can detect very small leakage currents, as low as 5 mA, and trip within 25 ms .
- It can be used in both single-phase and three-phase systems .
- It can be combined with other protective devices, such as overcurrent circuit breakers and fuses, to provide multiple protection .

## Disadvantages of ELCB

Some of the disadvantages of using an ELCB are:

- It can be affected by external factors, such as lightning, moisture, and electromagnetic interference, which can cause false tripping or failure to trip .

# Fundamentals of Earthing and Lightning Protection

## Introduction

Earthing and lightning protection are important for everyone that uses electrical equipment and that includes the large majority of the world. Earthing, Lighting Arrestors and Surge Protection Devices are the leading devices that protect devices against current shock. Lightning protection grounding helps protect structures and equipment from direct strikes. Overhead ground wires and surge arresters, connected to ground, can limit dangerous system overvoltages to safe values.

## Objectives of Earthing and Lightning Protection

The main objectives of earthing and lightning protection are:

- To provide a low resistance path for fault currents to flow to the earth and clear the fault.
- To prevent electric shock to humans and animals by limiting the touch and step voltages to safe levels.
- To protect electrical and electronic equipment from damage due to lightning surges and transient overvoltages.
- To reduce electromagnetic interference and noise in sensitive circuits and devices.
- To stabilize the voltage of the electrical system with respect to the earth.

## Types of Earthing Systems

There are different types of earthing systems depending on the configuration of the source, the load, and the earth connection. Some common types are:

- TN system: The source neutral is connected to the earth and the load is directly connected to the source neutral. The exposed metal parts of the load are also connected to the source neutral or earth. There are three subtypes of TN system: TN-C, TN-S, and TN-C-S.
- TT system: The source neutral is connected to the earth and the load is connected to a separate earth electrode. The exposed metal parts of the load are also connected to the load earth electrode.
- IT system: The source neutral is isolated from the earth or connected through a high impedance. The load is connected to a separate earth electrode. The exposed metal parts of the load are also connected to the load earth electrode.

## Components of Earthing System

The main components of an earthing system are:

- Earth electrode: A conductor or a group of conductors that are buried in the soil and provide a low resistance contact with the earth. The common types of earth electrodes are rods, plates, pipes, and meshes.
- Earth conductor: A conductor that connects the earth electrode to the electrical equipment or system that needs to be earthed. The earth conductor should have adequate cross-sectional area, mechanical strength, and corrosion resistance.
- Earth clamp: A device that securely connects the earth conductor to the earth electrode or the equipment. The earth clamp should have good electrical contact, low resistance, and high durability.

## Principles of Lightning Protection

- Capture: A lightning protection system should have one or more air terminals or rods that are located at the highest points of the structure or equipment to be protected. The air terminals should be made of conductive material and have sharp points to attract and capture the lightning strike.
- Conduction: A lightning protection system should have one or more down conductors or cables that connect the air terminals to the grounding electrodes. The down conductors should have low resistance, high current carrying capacity, and minimal bends or joints.
- Dissipation: A lightning protection system should have one or more grounding electrodes or rods that are buried in the soil and provide a low impedance path for the lightning current to flow to the earth. The grounding electrodes should have large surface area, good contact with the soil, and adequate spacing from each other and other underground utilities.

# Types of Batteries

A battery is a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy. It consists of one or more cells, each containing a positive and a negative electrode, and an electrolyte that allows the flow of electric charge between the electrodes.

There are four main types of batteries:

- **Primary batteries**: These are non-rechargeable batteries that can only be used once. They have a limited shelf life and capacity. Examples of primary batteries are alkaline, zinc-carbon, and lithium batteries.
- **Secondary batteries**: These are rechargeable batteries that can be used multiple times by restoring their chemical energy through an external power source. They have a longer shelf life and capacity, but they also suffer from self-discharge and memory effect. Examples of secondary batteries are lead-acid, nickel-cadmium, nickel-metal hydride, and lithium-ion batteries.
- **Reserve batteries**: These are batteries that are activated only when needed, such as in emergency situations. They have a long storage life and high power density, but they are also expensive and hazardous. Examples of reserve batteries are thermal batteries, magnesium batteries, and silver-zinc batteries.
- **Fuel cells**: These are batteries that generate electricity by continuously converting the chemical energy of a fuel and an oxidant. They have a high efficiency and environmental friendliness, but they also require a constant supply of fuel and oxygen, and a complex system to manage them. Examples of fuel cells are hydrogen-oxygen, methanol, and ethanol fuel cells.
