

# FUNDAMENTALS OF ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING

Electrical engineering is the branch of engineering that deals with the study and application of electricity, electronics, and electromagnetism. Electrical engineers design, develop, test, and supervise the operation of electrical and electronic devices, systems, and networks.

Some of the topics that are covered in the fundamentals of electrical engineering are:

- **Network Analysis**: This topic involves the analysis of electrical circuits using various methods, such as Kirchhoff's laws, mesh analysis, nodal analysis, superposition, Thevenin's theorem, Norton's theorem, and maximum power transfer theorem. Network analysis helps to determine the voltage, current, power, and impedance in any branch or node of a circuit. 

- **Capacitance**: This topic deals with the property of a device or a system to store electric charge. A capacitor is a device that consists of two conductors separated by an insulator, and it can store charge when connected to a voltage source. The capacitance of a capacitor is defined as the ratio of the charge stored to the voltage applied, and it is measured in farads. Capacitors can be used for filtering, smoothing, timing, and energy storage applications. 

- **Electromagnetic Induction**: This topic explains the phenomenon of generating an electric current in a conductor when it is exposed to a changing magnetic field. The induced current is proportional to the rate of change of the magnetic flux, and it opposes the cause of the change. This is known as Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. Electromagnetic induction is the basis of many devices, such as transformers, generators, motors, and solenoids. 

- **Motors Circuits and Diodes**: This topic covers the working and applications of some common electrical and electronic devices, such as motors, relays, switches, diodes, and transistors. A motor is a device that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy, and it can be classified into direct current (DC) and alternating current (AC) motors. A relay is a device that uses an electromagnet to switch a circuit on or off. A switch is a device that can make or break a circuit. A diode is a device that allows current to flow in one direction only, and it can be used for rectification, clipping, clamping, and voltage regulation. A transistor is a device that can amplify or switch an electrical signal, and it can be used for amplification, switching, and logic operations. 

- **AC (Single Phase and Three Phase) Circuit Analysis**: This topic involves the analysis of electrical circuits that are powered by alternating current (AC) sources, such as sinusoidal, square, and triangular waves. AC circuits can have resistive, capacitive, inductive, or complex impedances, and they can be analyzed using phasor diagrams, complex numbers, and power factor. AC circuits can also be classified into single phase and three phase circuits, depending on the number of AC sources and their phase difference. Single phase circuits are simpler to analyze, while three phase circuits are more efficient and balanced. 

- **Steady-state and Phase Analysis of AC Networks**: This topic deals with the analysis of AC networks that have reached a steady-state condition, where the voltage and current do not change with time. Steady-state analysis helps to determine the effective or rms values of voltage and current, the average and reactive power, the power factor, and the impedance in any branch or node of a network. Phase analysis helps to determine the phase angle or the phase difference between the voltage and current in any branch or node of a network, and it can be done using phasor diagrams or complex numbers. 

- **Series and Parallel Circuits**: This topic covers the analysis of simple circuits that have only one or two types of components, such as resistors, capacitors, or inductors, connected in series or parallel. Series circuits have only one path for the current to flow, while parallel circuits have multiple paths for the current to flow. Series and parallel circuits can be analyzed using Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws, and equivalent resistance or impedance formulas.

## Unit 1 - DC Circuits

- A DC circuit is a circuit that consists of direct current (DC) sources, such as batteries or solar cells, and resistors, capacitors, inductors, switches, and other components that are connected by wires or conductors.
- The main characteristics of a DC circuit are:
  - The current flows in one direction only, from the positive terminal of the source to the negative terminal.
  - The voltage across any component is constant and equal to the product of the current and the resistance of the component (Ohm's law).
  - The total voltage across a series connection of components is equal to the sum of the voltages across each component (Kirchhoff's voltage law).
  - The total current in a parallel connection of components is equal to the sum of the currents in each branch (Kirchhoff's current law).
  - The power dissipated by any component is equal to the product of the voltage and the current across the component (Joule's law).
- The main types of DC circuits are:
  - Series circuits: A series circuit is a circuit in which all the components are connected end to end, forming a single path for the current. The current is the same in every component, and the total resistance is equal to the sum of the resistances of each component.
  - Parallel circuits: A parallel circuit is a circuit in which the components are connected across the same pair of terminals, forming multiple paths for the current. The voltage is the same across every component, and the total resistance is equal to the reciprocal of the sum of the reciprocals of the resistances of each component.
  - Series-parallel circuits: A series-parallel circuit is a circuit that combines series and parallel connections of components, forming a complex network of paths for the current. The current and voltage in each component depend on the configuration and the values of the other components.
- The main methods of analyzing DC circuits are:
  - Ohm's law: Ohm's law states that the voltage across a resistor is proportional to the current through it, and the constant of proportionality is the resistance. Ohm's law can be used to find the current, voltage, or resistance of any component in a DC circuit, given the other two quantities.
  - Kirchhoff's laws: Kirchhoff's laws are two rules that apply to any closed loop or junction in a DC circuit. Kirchhoff's voltage law states that the sum of the voltages around any closed loop is zero. Kirchhoff's current law states that the sum of the currents entering any junction is equal to the sum of the currents leaving it. Kirchhoff's laws can be used to find the unknown currents or voltages in a DC circuit, by writing a system of equations and solving them.
  - Voltage divider rule: The voltage divider rule is a formula that relates the voltage across a resistor in a series circuit to the total voltage and the total resistance. The voltage divider rule states that the voltage across a resistor is equal to the ratio of its resistance to the total resistance, multiplied by the total voltage. The voltage divider rule can be used to find the voltage across any resistor in a series circuit, given the total voltage and the total resistance.
  - Current divider rule: The current divider rule is a formula that relates the current through a resistor in a parallel circuit to the total current and the total resistance. The current divider rule states that the current through a resistor is equal to the ratio of the total resistance to its resistance, multiplied by the total current. The current divider rule can be used to find the current through any resistor in a parallel circuit, given the total current and the total resistance.
  - Equivalent resistance: The equivalent resistance of a DC circuit is the resistance that would produce the same current and voltage as the original circuit, if it were connected to the same source. The equivalent resistance can be used to simplify a complex circuit into a single resistor, and then apply Ohm's law or Kirchhoff's laws to find the unknown quantities. The equivalent resistance of a series circuit is equal to the sum of the resistances of each component. The equivalent resistance of a parallel circuit is equal to the reciprocal of the sum of the reciprocals of the resistances of each component. The equivalent resistance of a series-parallel circuit can be found by combining series and parallel resistances until a single resistor is obtained.



### Electrical circuit elements (R, L and C)

- Electrical circuit elements are components that can store, dissipate, or transfer electrical energy in a circuit.
- The most common circuit elements are resistors (R), inductors (L), and capacitors (C).
- Resistors are devices that oppose the flow of electric current and convert electrical energy into heat. The resistance (R) of a resistor is measured in ohms (Ω) and is given by the ratio of the voltage (V) across the resistor to the current (I) through it: R = V/I.
- Inductors are devices that store electrical energy in a magnetic field when current flows through them. The inductance (L) of an inductor is measured in henries (H) and is given by the ratio of the magnetic flux (Φ) linked with the inductor to the current (I) through it: L = Φ/I.
- Capacitors are devices that store electrical energy in an electric field when voltage is applied across them. The capacitance (C) of a capacitor is measured in farads (F) and is given by the ratio of the electric charge (Q) stored on the capacitor to the voltage (V) across it: C = Q/V.
- The behavior of these circuit elements depends on the type of current (direct or alternating) and the frequency of the current or voltage source.
- In direct current (DC) circuits, resistors obey Ohm's law (V = IR), inductors act as short circuits (zero resistance), and capacitors act as open circuits (infinite resistance).
- In alternating current (AC) circuits, the current and voltage vary sinusoidally with time and frequency. The impedance (Z) of a circuit element is the ratio of the phasor voltage (V) to the phasor current (I) and is a complex number that depends on the frequency (f) and the circuit element. The impedance of a resistor is Z_R = R, the impedance of an inductor is Z_L = jωL, and the impedance of a capacitor is Z_C = 1/jωC, where ω = 2πf and j is the imaginary unit.
- The impedance of a circuit element can also be expressed in terms of its magnitude and phase angle. The magnitude of the impedance is |Z| = √(R^2 + (ωL - 1/ωC)^2) and the phase angle is θ = tan^(-1)((ωL - 1/ωC)/R).
- The power (P) dissipated or delivered by a circuit element is the product of the voltage (V) and the current (I) and is measured in watts (W). The power can also be expressed in terms of the impedance (Z) and the current (I) as P = I^2 Z or in terms of the impedance (Z) and the voltage (V) as P = V^2 /Z. The power factor (pf) of a circuit element is the ratio of the real power (P) to the apparent power (S) and is given by pf = P/S = cos θ, where θ is the phase angle of the impedance. The power factor indicates how efficiently the circuit element converts electrical energy into useful work or heat. A power factor of 1 means that the circuit element is purely resistive and all the electrical energy is dissipated as heat. A power factor of 0 means that the circuit element is purely reactive and no electrical energy is dissipated or delivered. A power factor between 0 and 1 means that the circuit element is a combination of resistive and reactive components and some electrical energy is dissipated and some is stored.

### Concept of active and passive elements

- Active and passive elements are two types of electronic circuit elements that have different roles and characteristics.
- Active elements are capable of supplying energy to a circuit or providing amplification of a signal. They can control the direction and magnitude of current and voltage in a circuit. They have a function of gain, which means they can increase the power of a signal. Examples of active elements are transistors, diodes, operational amplifiers, generators, batteries, etc.   
- Passive elements are those that do not supply energy to a circuit, but can only receive, store, dissipate, or absorb energy. They do not control the current or voltage in a circuit, but can affect their values by their properties. They do not have a function of gain, which means they cannot amplify a signal. Examples of passive elements are resistors, capacitors, inductors, transformers, etc.   
- The main difference between active and passive elements is that active elements can deliver net power to a circuit, while passive elements can only deliver or absorb power at the expense of another source. 
- Active and passive elements are essential for designing and analyzing electronic circuits, as they determine the behavior and performance of the circuit. Active elements are used for current and voltage control, signal processing, amplification, switching, etc. Passive elements are used for energy storage, discharge, oscillation, filtering, coupling, etc.

### Concept of Linearity

- Linearity is a property of an element or a system that describes a linear relationship between cause and effect  .
- A linear element or system is one that obeys the principles of homogeneity and superposition  .
- Homogeneity means that if the input is scaled by a constant factor, the output is also scaled by the same factor .
- Superposition means that if the input is a sum of two or more signals, the output is also a sum of the corresponding outputs for each signal  .
- In electrical engineering, linearity is often applied to circuit elements, such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, and sources, as well as to circuit analysis techniques, such as Kirchhoff's laws, mesh analysis, nodal analysis, and Thevenin's theorem  .
- A linear circuit element is one that has a current-voltage characteristic that is a straight line, such as a resistor . A linear circuit is one that consists of only linear elements and independent sources .
- A linear system is one that has a linear input-output relationship, such as a voltage divider, an amplifier, or a filter . A linear system can be modeled by a linear equation or a transfer function .
- Linearity is a useful property because it simplifies the analysis and design of circuits and systems, and allows the use of superposition and other powerful tools  .
- However, not all elements and systems are linear, and some may exhibit nonlinear behavior under certain conditions, such as diodes, transistors, LEDs, and speakers . Nonlinear elements and systems require more complex methods of analysis and design, and may have different or unexpected responses to different inputs .

# Kirchhoff's laws for DC circuits

Kirchhoff's laws are two principles that govern the analysis of electric circuits. They are named after Gustav Kirchhoff, a German physicist who formulated them in 1845. The laws are:

- Kirchhoff's current law (KCL): The algebraic sum of the currents entering and leaving any node in a circuit is zero. This means that the total charge flowing into a node is equal to the total charge flowing out of it. A node is a point where two or more branches of a circuit meet. A branch is a path that connects two nodes and contains one or more circuit elements. KCL is also known as the junction rule or the point rule.

- Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): The algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed loop in a circuit is zero. This means that the total energy gained or lost by the charges in a loop is zero. A loop is a closed path that starts and ends at the same node and does not pass through any node more than once. KVL is also known as the loop rule or the mesh rule.

Kirchhoff's laws are based on the conservation of charge and energy in electric circuits. They are useful for finding the values of current, voltage, and resistance in DC circuits, as well as for performing mesh and node analysis. Some applications of Kirchhoff's laws are:

- Wheatstone bridge: A circuit that consists of four resistors and a galvanometer that is used to measure the unknown resistance of one of the resistors. By applying KCL and KVL, the unknown resistance can be found by balancing the bridge, that is, making the current through the galvanometer zero.

- Superposition theorem: A method that simplifies the analysis of circuits with multiple sources by considering the effect of each source separately and then adding them algebraically. By applying KCL and KVL, the current or voltage in any branch of the circuit can be found by superposing the contributions of each source.

- Thevenin's theorem: A method that simplifies the analysis of circuits with multiple resistors by replacing them with an equivalent circuit that consists of a single voltage source and a single resistor. By applying KCL and KVL, the equivalent voltage and resistance can be found by considering the open-circuit voltage and the short-circuit current of the original circuit.

Some limitations of Kirchhoff's laws are:

- They are only valid for linear circuits, that is, circuits that obey Ohm's law and have constant resistances. Nonlinear circuits, such as those with diodes, transistors, or capacitors, may not follow Kirchhoff's laws.

- They are only valid for lumped circuits, that is, circuits that can be divided into discrete elements that are connected by ideal wires. Distributed circuits, such as those with long transmission lines, antennas, or coils, may not follow Kirchhoff's laws because the wires have non-negligible resistance, inductance, and capacitance.

- They are only valid for steady-state circuits, that is, circuits that have reached a stable condition and do not change with time. Transient circuits, such as those with switches, relays, or motors, may not follow Kirchhoff's laws because the currents and voltages may vary with time.



### Mesh and Nodal Methods of Analysis

- Mesh and nodal methods of analysis are two systematic techniques for solving circuits that involve writing and solving a set of equations.
- In mesh analysis, the unknown quantities are the loop currents. A loop is a closed path that does not contain any other closed paths within it. A mesh is a loop that does not contain any other loops within it.
- In nodal analysis, the unknown quantities are the node voltages. A node is a point where two or more elements are connected. A reference node is a node that is assigned a voltage of zero volts.
- Both methods are based on applying Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) and Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) to the circuit. KCL states that the algebraic sum of currents entering a node is zero. KVL states that the algebraic sum of voltages around a loop is zero.
- The steps for mesh analysis are:
  - Identify all the meshes in the circuit and assign a current to each mesh in a clockwise direction.
  - Write KVL equations for each mesh, expressing the voltages in terms of the mesh currents.
  - Solve the system of equations for the mesh currents.
  - Use Ohm's law to find the voltages across the elements if needed.
- The steps for nodal analysis are:
  - Identify all the nodes in the circuit and assign a voltage to each node with respect to the reference node.
  - Write KCL equations for each node, expressing the currents in terms of the node voltages.
  - Solve the system of equations for the node voltages.
  - Use Ohm's law to find the currents through the elements if needed.
- Mesh and nodal analysis can be applied to any linear circuit, but they are more suited for certain types of circuits. Mesh analysis is more convenient for circuits with many loops and few nodes. Nodal analysis is more convenient for circuits with many nodes and few loops.
- Mesh and nodal analysis can also handle circuits with dependent sources, which are sources whose value depends on another voltage or current in the circuit. Dependent sources are represented by a diamond shape with a label indicating the type and value of the source. There are four types of dependent sources: voltage-controlled voltage source (VCVS), current-controlled voltage source (CCVS), voltage-controlled current source (VCCS), and current-controlled current source (CCCS).
- To include dependent sources in mesh analysis, the following steps are added:
  - Identify the controlling variable (voltage or current) and the controlled source (voltage or current) in the circuit.
  - Write an equation relating the controlled source to the controlling variable.
  - Substitute the equation into the KVL equations where the controlled source appears.
- To include dependent sources in nodal analysis, the following steps are added:
  - Identify the controlling variable (voltage or current) and the controlled source (voltage or current) in the circuit.
  - Write an equation relating the controlled source to the controlling variable.
  - If the controlling variable is a voltage, use it as a supernode, which is a combination of two or more nodes that have a known voltage difference between them.
  - If the controlling variable is a current, use it as a current source in the KCL equations where it appears.



## Unit 2 - Steady State Analysis of Single Phase AC Circuits

- In steady-state AC circuits, all voltages and currents measured across or through the elements are periodic and in the ideal case harmonic (i.e., sine or cosine) functions of time.
- These voltages and currents have the same frequency but different phases and amplitudes.
- The word steady-state means that the circuit frequency, phases of all voltages and currents, and amplitudes of all voltages and currents do not change over time. Transient effects are entirely excluded from our consideration.
- Similarly, the AC voltage and currents are called steady-state AC voltage and steady-state alternating current.
- Steady-state AC circuits can be analyzed using the following steps:
  - Represent the sinusoidal waveforms of voltage and current using phasors, which are complex numbers that encode the amplitude and phase of the sinusoids.
  - Apply Kirchhoff's laws and Ohm's law to the phasor domain, using complex arithmetic to solve for the unknown phasors.
  - Convert the phasor solutions back to the time domain, using inverse trigonometric functions to find the amplitude and phase of the sinusoids.
- Steady-state AC circuits can also be analyzed using the following concepts :
  - Impedance, which is the ratio of phasor voltage to phasor current for a given element. It is a complex number that represents the opposition to the flow of AC current. It depends on the frequency of the AC source and the type of element (resistor, capacitor, inductor, etc.).
  - Admittance, which is the reciprocal of impedance. It is a complex number that represents the ease of the flow of AC current. It also depends on the frequency of the AC source and the type of element.
  - Power, which is the rate of energy transfer in an AC circuit. It can be divided into three components: real power, reactive power, and apparent power. Real power is the average power delivered to the resistive part of the circuit. Reactive power is the power exchanged between the capacitive and inductive parts of the circuit. Apparent power is the product of the RMS values of voltage and current. The power factor is the ratio of real power to apparent power, and it indicates the efficiency of the AC circuit.



### Representation of Sinusoidal waveforms – Average and effective values

- A sinusoidal waveform is a periodic function that oscillates between positive and negative peak values with a constant frequency and amplitude.
- The general equation of a sinusoidal waveform is: `v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + θ)`, where `v(t)` is the instantaneous value, `Vm` is the peak value, `ω` is the angular frequency, `t` is the time, and `θ` is the phase angle.
- The average value of a sinusoidal waveform is the arithmetic mean of the positive half-cycle or the negative half-cycle. It is calculated by multiplying the peak value by the constant `0.637`.
- The average value of a sinusoidal waveform is also known as the DC equivalent value, because it represents the constant voltage that would deliver the same amount of energy to a resistive load as the sinusoidal voltage.
- The effective value of a sinusoidal waveform is the root mean square (RMS) value, which is the square root of the average of the squares of the instantaneous values over one cycle. It is calculated by multiplying the peak value by the constant `0.707`.
- The effective value of a sinusoidal waveform is also known as the AC equivalent value, because it represents the constant voltage that would produce the same heating effect in a resistive load as the sinusoidal voltage.
- The form factor of a sinusoidal waveform is the ratio of the effective value to the average value. For a pure sinusoidal waveform, the form factor is always `1.11`.
- The form factor indicates the shape of the waveform. The higher the form factor, the more peaked the waveform is. The lower the form factor, the more flat the waveform is.
- The peak factor of a sinusoidal waveform is the ratio of the peak value to the effective value. For a pure sinusoidal waveform, the peak factor is always `1.414`.
- The peak factor indicates the magnitude of the waveform. The higher the peak factor, the larger the peak value is. The lower the peak factor, the smaller the peak value is.
- The sinusoidal waveform is the most common and important waveform in AC circuits, because it is the natural shape of the voltage and current produced by generators and transformers.
- The sinusoidal waveform can also be represented by a phasor, which is a rotating vector with a fixed length and angle. The length of the phasor is equal to the peak value of the waveform, and the angle of the phasor is equal to the phase angle of the waveform.
- The phasor representation simplifies the analysis of AC circuits, because it allows the use of complex numbers and algebra instead of trigonometry and calculus. The phasor representation also shows the phase relationship between different sinusoidal waveforms in the same circuit.

### Analysis of single phase AC Circuits consisting R-L-C combination (Series and Parallel)

- A single phase AC circuit is a circuit that is powered by a single alternating voltage or current source.
- A R-L-C circuit is a circuit that contains a resistor (R), an inductor (L) and a capacitor (C) as its elements. These elements can be connected in series or parallel across the AC source.
- The behavior of a R-L-C circuit depends on the frequency of the AC source and the values of R, L and C. The circuit can act as a filter, an oscillator, a resonator or a damper depending on the frequency and the configuration of the elements.
- The analysis of a R-L-C circuit involves finding the impedance (Z) or the admittance (Y) of the circuit, which are complex quantities that represent the opposition or the ease of current flow in the circuit. The impedance or the admittance can be expressed in rectangular or polar form, and can be represented by a phasor diagram or an impedance/admittance triangle.
- The impedance or the admittance of a R-L-C circuit can be calculated by using the following formulas:

  - For a series R-L-C circuit, the impedance is given by:

    $$Z = R + j(X_L - X_C) = R + j\omega L - \frac{j}{\omega C}$$

    where $j$ is the imaginary unit, $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the AC source, $X_L$ is the inductive reactance and $X_C$ is the capacitive reactance.

  - For a parallel R-L-C circuit, the admittance is given by:

    $$Y = G + j(B_C - B_L) = G + j\omega C - \frac{j}{\omega L}$$

    where $G$ is the conductance, $B_C$ is the capacitive susceptance and $B_L$ is the inductive susceptance.

- The impedance or the admittance of a R-L-C circuit can be simplified by using the concept of resonance. Resonance occurs when the inductive reactance or susceptance equals the capacitive reactance or susceptance, which means that the circuit behaves as a pure resistance or a pure conductance. The resonant frequency ($f_0$) is given by:

  $$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

  At resonance, the impedance or the admittance of a R-L-C circuit is given by:

  - For a series R-L-C circuit, the impedance is:

    $$Z = R$$

### Apparent, active and reactive power

- Apparent power is the product of the RMS voltage and current in an AC circuit, without reference to the phase angle between them. It is measured in volt-amperes (VA) and symbolized by S. It represents the total power supplied to the circuit, but not all of it may be useful for doing work.  
- Active power is the component of the apparent power that is in phase with the voltage and does work in the circuit. It is measured in watts (W) and symbolized by P. It represents the average power transferred to the load, such as a resistor, a motor, or a lamp.    
- Reactive power is the component of the apparent power that is out of phase with the voltage and does not do work in the circuit. It is measured in volt-amperes reactive (VAR) and symbolized by Q. It represents the power that is stored and released by the reactive elements in the circuit, such as a capacitor or an inductor.    

- The relationship between apparent, active and reactive power can be expressed by the power triangle, as shown below:

power triangle

- The power triangle shows that the apparent power is the hypotenuse of the right triangle, and the active and reactive powers are the adjacent and opposite sides, respectively. The angle between the apparent and active powers is the power factor angle, which indicates how much the current lags or leads the voltage in the circuit.   
- The power triangle can be used to calculate the apparent, active and reactive powers using the following formulas:

  - S = Vrms * Irms
  - P = S * cos(θ) = Vrms * Irms * cos(θ)
  - Q = S * sin(θ) = Vrms * Irms * sin(θ)

  - S = P + jQ = Vrms * Irms * (cos(θ) + j sin(θ)) = Vrms * Irms * ∠θ

### Power factor

- Power factor is a dimensionless quantity that measures how effectively an AC circuit uses the supplied power.
- Power factor is defined as the ratio of real power (P) to apparent power (S), where real power is the average of the instantaneous product of voltage and current, and apparent power is the product of the RMS values of voltage and current.
- Power factor can also be expressed as the cosine of the angle (θ) between the voltage and current phasors in an AC circuit, where θ is the phase difference between the voltage and current waveforms.
- Power factor can range from 0 to 1, where 0 means no power is delivered to the load, and 1 means all the power is delivered to the load. A power factor of 1 is also called unity power factor, and it indicates that the voltage and current are in phase and the circuit is purely resistive.
- A power factor less than 1 means that the voltage and current are not in phase and the circuit has some reactive components, such as capacitors or inductors, that store and release energy periodically. A power factor less than 1 also means that the apparent power is greater than the real power, and the circuit draws more current than necessary to deliver the required power to the load. This can cause power losses, voltage drops, and overheating in the power system.
- Power factor can be improved by adding power factor correction devices, such as capacitors or synchronous motors, that cancel out the reactive power and bring the voltage and current in phase. This can reduce the apparent power and the current drawn by the circuit, and increase the efficiency and reliability of the power system.



### Concept of Resonance in series & parallel circuits

- Resonance is a condition that occurs in AC circuits when the reactive power of the capacitors and inductors become equal.
- Resonance can happen in circuits where capacitors and inductors are connected in series or in parallel.
- In series resonance, the circuit impedance is minimum and the current is maximum at the resonant frequency.
- In parallel resonance, the circuit impedance is maximum and the current is minimum at the resonant frequency.
- Series resonance circuit is also known as the acceptor circuit because it accepts the voltage at the resonant frequency.
- Parallel resonance circuit is also known as the rejector circuit because it rejects the current at the resonant frequency.
- The resonant frequency of a series or parallel circuit is given by the formula: f0 = 1 / (2π√LC), where L is the inductance and C is the capacitance.
- The quality factor or Q factor of a series or parallel circuit is a measure of how sharp the resonance is.
- The Q factor of a series circuit is given by: Qseries = XL / R, where XL is the inductive reactance and R is the resistance.
- The Q factor of a parallel circuit is given by: Qparallel = RT / XL, where RT is the total parallel resistance and XL is the inductive reactance.
- The Q factor of a series or parallel circuit is inversely proportional to the bandwidth, which is the range of frequencies around the resonant frequency where the circuit behaves as a resonator.
- The bandwidth of a series or parallel circuit is given by: B = f0 / Q, where f0 is the resonant frequency and Q is the quality factor.
- The presence of resistance in a series or parallel circuit can affect the resonant frequency and the Q factor, causing a phenomenon called antiresonance .
- Antiresonance is the tendency for added resistance to skew the point at which impedance reaches a maximum or minimum in an LC circuit .
- Antiresonance can be avoided by using high-quality components with low resistance and by tuning the circuit to the desired frequency .
- Resonance in series and parallel circuits has many applications in electrical engineering, such as filters, oscillators, transformers, antennas, and sensors.

### Magnetic circuits

- A magnetic circuit is a closed path to which a magnetic field, represented as lines of magnetic flux, is confined.
- A magnetic circuit is analogous to an electric circuit, but with magnetic quantities instead of electric quantities. The main difference is that in a magnetic circuit, nothing actually flows, whereas in an electric circuit, electric charge flows.
- A magnetic circuit consists of a structure composed for the most part of high permeability magnetic material, such as iron, which guides the magnetic flux along the desired path.
- The presence of high permeability material causes the magnetic flux to be concentrated in the paths defined by the structure, much as currents are confined to the conductors of an electric circuit.
- A magnetic circuit may have air gaps or other materials in the path, which increase the reluctance of the circuit and reduce the flux.
- Some examples of magnetic circuits are: horseshoe magnet with iron keeper (low-reluctance circuit), horseshoe magnet with no keeper (high-reluctance circuit), electric motor (variable-reluctance circuit), and transformer (multi-loop circuit) .
- The main quantities used to analyze a magnetic circuit are: magnetic flux (Φ), magnetic flux density (B), magnetic field intensity (H), magnetomotive force (MMF), and reluctance (R).
- The basic laws governing a magnetic circuit are: Ampere's circuital law, which relates the MMF and the H around a closed path, and Gauss's law for magnetism, which relates the flux and the B through a closed surface.

### Ideal and Practical Transformer

- A transformer is a device that transfers electrical energy from one circuit to another through mutual induction.
- An ideal transformer is a hypothetical transformer that has no energy losses and is 100% efficient .
- A practical transformer is a real transformer that has some energy losses and is less than 100% efficient .

#### Differences between Ideal and Practical Transformer

- The main difference between an ideal and practical transformer is that the former has an efficiency of 100% while the latter has an efficiency close to 100% (around 99%) .
- The efficiency of an ideal transformer does not depend on any parameter, while the efficiency of a practical transformer depends on the power factor and loading of the transformer.
- The resistance of the windings of an ideal transformer is zero (or negligibly small), while the resistance of the windings of a practical transformer is finite and causes copper losses .
- The magnetic circuit of an ideal transformer has infinite permeability and zero core loss, while the magnetic circuit of a practical transformer has finite permeability and causes hysteresis and eddy current losses .
- The leakage flux of an ideal transformer is zero, meaning that the whole flux of the magnetic circuit completely links both coils, while the leakage flux of a practical transformer is nonzero, meaning that some flux of the magnetic circuit does not link both coils and causes voltage drops .
- The voltage regulation of an ideal transformer is zero, meaning that the secondary voltage is equal to the primary voltage multiplied by the turns ratio, while the voltage regulation of a practical transformer is nonzero, meaning that the secondary voltage is less than the primary voltage multiplied by the turns ratio.
- The current drawn by an ideal transformer from the source is equal to the current delivered to the load multiplied by the turns ratio, while the current drawn by a practical transformer from the source is more than the current delivered to the load multiplied by the turns ratio due to the magnetizing current and the no-load current.

### Losses in Transformers

A transformer is a device that transfers electrical energy from one circuit to another by electromagnetic induction. However, some energy is always lost in the process of transformation due to various factors. The losses in transformers can be classified into four main types:

- **Copper loss** or **I2R loss**: This is the power loss due to the resistance of the transformer windings. It is proportional to the square of the current flowing through the windings and the resistance of the windings. Copper loss can be reduced by using thicker wires or better conductors for the windings. Copper loss is also called variable loss because it depends on the load of the transformer.
- **Core loss** or **iron loss**: This is the power loss due to the magnetization and demagnetization of the transformer core. It consists of two components: hysteresis loss and eddy current loss. Hysteresis loss is caused by the repeated reversal of the magnetic domains in the core material as the alternating current changes direction. Eddy current loss is caused by the induced currents in the core that oppose the main flux and generate heat. Core loss can be reduced by using a core material with low hysteresis and high resistivity, such as silicon steel or ferrite. Core loss is also called constant loss because it does not depend on the load of the transformer.
- **Stray loss**: This is the power loss due to the leakage of the magnetic flux from the transformer. Some of the flux generated by the primary winding does not link with the secondary winding and escapes to the surrounding air or other nearby objects. This reduces the efficiency and regulation of the transformer. Stray loss can be reduced by using a better design and construction of the transformer, such as using a closed core or a shell-type core, or using magnetic shields or shunts.
- **Dielectric loss**: This is the power loss due to the heating of the insulation material of the transformer. The insulation material, such as oil or paper, has some electrical conductivity and acts as a capacitor between the windings and the core. When an alternating voltage is applied, the insulation material undergoes polarization and depolarization, which causes heat generation and energy dissipation. Dielectric loss can be reduced by using a better quality and quantity of insulation material, or by cooling the transformer.

The total loss in a transformer can be calculated by adding the copper loss, the core loss, the stray loss, and the dielectric loss. The efficiency of a transformer can be calculated by dividing the output power by the input power and subtracting the total loss. The efficiency of a transformer is usually expressed as a percentage. The higher the efficiency, the lower the losses and the better the performance of the transformer.



### Regulation and Efficiency of Transformers

- Regulation of a transformer is the measure of how well the output voltage of the transformer remains constant under varying load conditions.
- The regulation of a transformer is defined as the percentage change in the secondary voltage from no-load to full-load at a constant primary voltage and frequency.
- The regulation of a transformer can be calculated by the formula:

  `Regulation (%) = [(VNL - VFL) / VFL] x 100`

  where VNL is the no-load secondary voltage and VFL is the full-load secondary voltage.

- The regulation of a transformer depends on the power factor, impedance, and load current of the transformer.
- A transformer with low regulation has better voltage regulation, meaning it can maintain a more constant output voltage under varying load conditions.
- A transformer with high regulation has poor voltage regulation, meaning it has a large drop in output voltage when the load increases.

- Efficiency of a transformer is the measure of how well the transformer converts the input power to the output power without losses.
- The efficiency of a transformer is defined as the ratio of the output power to the input power, expressed as a percentage.
- The efficiency of a transformer can be calculated by the formula:

  `Efficiency (%) = [(Pout / Pin) x 100]`

  where Pout is the output power and Pin is the input power of the transformer.

- The efficiency of a transformer depends on the losses in the transformer, which are mainly of two types: core losses and copper losses.
- Core losses are the losses due to the hysteresis and eddy currents in the magnetic core of the transformer. They are constant and independent of the load.
- Copper losses are the losses due to the resistance of the windings of the transformer. They are proportional to the square of the load current.
- The efficiency of a transformer can be improved by reducing the core losses and copper losses, by using better materials, design, and cooling methods.

## Unit 4 - Electrical machines

Electrical machines are devices that convert electrical energy into mechanical energy or vice versa. They are classified into two main categories: **generators** and **motors**.

- Motors are machines that convert electrical energy into mechanical energy. They use the principle of electromagnetism, which states that a current-carrying conductor experiences a force when placed in a magnetic field. Motors can be further divided into **AC motors** and **DC motors**.
  - AC motors operate on alternating current and use induction, synchronous, or universal methods to create a rotating magnetic field that interacts with the rotor. AC motors are used in fans, pumps, compressors, and other applications that require variable speed and torque.
  - DC motors operate on direct current and use permanent magnets, electromagnets, or both to create a constant magnetic field that interacts with the rotor. DC motors are used in toys, electric vehicles, robots, and other applications that require precise speed and position control.

### Principle and Construction of Electrical Machines

- Electrical machines are devices that convert mechanical energy to electrical energy and vice versa.
- The principle of operation of electrical machines is based on the interaction of magnetic fields and electric currents.
- The main types of electrical machines are DC machines, AC machines, and special purpose machines.
- DC machines are those that operate on direct current (DC) and have a commutator to change the direction of current in the armature coils .
- AC machines are those that operate on alternating current (AC) and have no commutator. They are further classified into synchronous machines and induction machines.
- Special purpose machines are those that are designed for specific applications or functions, such as stepper motors, servo motors, linear motors, etc.

#### Construction of DC Machines

- The main parts of a DC machine are yoke, poles, field coils, armature, commutator, and brushes .
- Yoke is the outer frame of the machine that supports and protects the other parts. It is usually made of cast iron or steel .
- Poles are the projections on the inner side of the yoke that carry the field coils. They are usually made of laminated iron or steel to reduce eddy current losses .
- Field coils are the windings that produce the magnetic field when current flows through them. They are usually connected in series or parallel to the DC supply .
- Armature is the rotating part of the machine that carries the armature coils. It is usually made of laminated iron or steel to reduce eddy current losses and has slots on its surface to accommodate the coils .
- Armature coils are the windings that generate the electromotive force (EMF) or torque when they cut the magnetic field. They are usually connected in series to form a closed loop .
- Commutator is a cylindrical device that consists of insulated segments of copper or brass. It is mounted on the shaft of the armature and rotates with it. It acts as a mechanical switch that reverses the direction of current in the armature coils every half cycle .
- Brushes are the stationary contacts that press against the commutator and transfer the current to or from the external circuit. They are usually made of carbon or graphite .

#### Working Principle of DC Machines

- The working principle of a DC machine is based on Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, which states that whenever a conductor cuts a magnetic field, an EMF is induced in it .
- In a DC generator, the armature coils are rotated by a prime mover (such as a turbine or an engine) in the magnetic field produced by the field coils. As the coils cut the field, an EMF is induced in them. The EMF is collected by the commutator and brushes and delivered to the external load .
- In a DC motor, the armature coils are connected to a DC supply through the commutator and brushes. As the current flows through the coils, a force is exerted on them by the magnetic field. The force causes the armature to rotate. The rotation of the armature is transferred to the load by a shaft .

### Types of Electrical Machines

Electrical machines are devices that convert electrical energy to mechanical energy or vice versa. They can be broadly classified into two types: static and dynamic.

- Static electrical machines are stationary devices that do not have any moving parts. They transfer electrical energy from one circuit to another by electromagnetic induction. The most common example of a static electrical machine is a transformer, which can step up or step down the voltage and current of an alternating current (AC) source. Transformers are used for power transmission, distribution, and conversion applications.   

- Dynamic electrical machines are rotating devices that have a rotor and a stator. They can either generate electrical energy from mechanical energy or use electrical energy to produce mechanical energy. The most common types of dynamic electrical machines are:

  - DC machines: These are machines that operate on direct current (DC) sources. They have a commutator and brushes that switch the direction of the current in the rotor coil. DC machines can be classified into DC generators and DC motors. DC generators convert mechanical energy to DC electrical energy, while DC motors convert DC electrical energy to mechanical energy. DC machines are used for applications that require variable speed and torque, such as electric vehicles, cranes, and elevators.  

  - Synchronous machines: These are machines that operate on AC sources and have a constant speed that is proportional to the frequency of the source. They have a rotor that is magnetized by either permanent magnets or electromagnets. Synchronous machines can be classified into synchronous generators and synchronous motors. Synchronous generators convert mechanical energy to AC electrical energy, while synchronous motors convert AC electrical energy to mechanical energy. Synchronous machines are used for applications that require constant speed and power factor, such as power plants, pumps, and compressors.  

  - Induction machines: These are machines that operate on AC sources and have a variable speed that depends on the load and the slip. They have a rotor that is induced by the magnetic field of the stator. Induction machines can be classified into induction generators and induction motors. Induction generators convert mechanical energy to AC electrical energy, while induction motors convert AC electrical energy to mechanical energy. Induction machines are used for applications that require high efficiency and low maintenance, such as fans, blowers, and conveyor belts.  

  - Permanent magnet machines: These are machines that have permanent magnets in the rotor that set up a magnetic field. They can operate on either DC or AC sources and have a high power density and efficiency. Permanent magnet machines can be classified into permanent magnet generators and permanent magnet motors. Permanent magnet generators convert mechanical energy to electrical energy, while permanent magnet motors convert electrical energy to mechanical energy. Permanent magnet machines are used for applications that require high performance and reliability, such as wind turbines, electric vehicles, and robotics.

### Slip-torque characteristics of induction motor

- The slip-torque characteristic of an induction motor is the relation between the torque produced by the motor and the slip of the rotor with respect to the synchronous speed.
- The slip of the rotor is defined as the difference between the synchronous speed and the actual speed of the rotor, expressed as a fraction of the synchronous speed.
- The slip-torque characteristic can be derived from the equivalent circuit of the induction motor, by equating the mechanical power output to the electrical power input to the rotor.
- The torque-slip characteristic curve can be divided roughly into three regions   :
  - Low slip region: This is the region where the slip is very small (less than 5%) and the rotor speed is near the synchronous speed. In this region, the torque is proportional to the slip and the rotor resistance is negligible compared to the rotor reactance. The torque is low and increases linearly with the slip.
  - Medium slip region: This is the region where the slip is moderate (between 5% and 15%) and the rotor speed is lower than the synchronous speed. In this region, the torque is proportional to the square of the slip and the rotor resistance is comparable to the rotor reactance. The torque is higher and reaches a maximum value at a certain slip, called the pull-out slip or the critical slip. This is the region where the motor operates normally under load.
  - High slip region: This is the region where the slip is high (greater than 15%) and the rotor speed is much lower than the synchronous speed. In this region, the torque is inversely proportional to the slip and the rotor resistance is dominant over the rotor reactance. The torque is lower and decreases rapidly with the slip. This is the region where the motor stalls or runs in reverse direction.

- The shape of the torque-slip characteristic curve depends on the rotor resistance, the rotor reactance, and the stator voltage. The higher the rotor resistance, the higher the maximum torque and the pull-out slip. The higher the rotor reactance, the lower the maximum torque and the pull-out slip. The higher the stator voltage, the higher the torque at any slip.
- The torque-slip characteristic curve can be modified by changing the rotor resistance, the rotor reactance, or the stator voltage. This can be done by using external resistors, slip rings, variable frequency drives, or soft starters.
- The torque-slip characteristic curve can be used to analyze the performance, efficiency, and stability of the induction motor under different operating conditions and loads.

- An alternator consists of a **stator** and a **rotor**. The stator is the stationary part that contains a set of **armature windings** connected to the external load. The rotor is the rotating part that contains a set of **field windings** connected to a DC source.
- The rotor produces a **rotating magnetic field** that induces an EMF in the armature windings of the stator. The frequency of the induced EMF depends on the speed of the rotor and the number of poles. The voltage of the induced EMF depends on the strength of the magnetic field and the number of turns of the armature windings.
- The direction of the induced EMF alternates as the rotor rotates, hence the output is AC. The output voltage can be controlled by varying the DC current in the field windings or by using a **voltage regulator**.

#### Synchronous motor

- A synchronous motor consists of a **stator** and a **rotor**. The stator is the stationary part that contains a set of **stator windings** connected to a three-phase AC supply. The rotor is the rotating part that contains a set of **rotor windings** connected to a DC source or a permanent magnet.
- The stator produces a **rotating magnetic field** that rotates at a constant speed determined by the supply frequency and the number of poles. The rotor produces a **constant magnetic field** that aligns with the rotating magnetic field of the stator. The torque exerted on the rotor by the stator makes the rotor rotate at the same speed as the stator field, hence the name synchronous motor.
- The speed of the synchronous motor is constant and independent of the load. The power factor of the synchronous motor can be adjusted by varying the DC current in the rotor windings or by using a **power factor controller**.
