# Kirchhoff's laws for DC circuits

Kirchhoff's laws are two principles that govern the analysis of electric circuits. They are named after Gustav Kirchhoff, a German physicist who formulated them in 1845. They are:

- Kirchhoff's current law (KCL): The algebraic sum of the currents entering and leaving any node in a circuit is zero. This means that the total charge flowing into a node is equal to the total charge flowing out of it. This law is based on the conservation of charge principle. 
- Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): The algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed loop in a circuit is zero. This means that the total energy gained or lost by the charges in a loop is zero. This law is based on the conservation of energy principle. 

Kirchhoff's laws can be used to find the values of current, voltage, and resistance in DC circuits. They can also be used to verify the validity of Ohm's law and to perform mesh and node analysis. Some applications of Kirchhoff's laws are:

- Wheatstone bridge: A circuit that consists of four resistors and a galvanometer that can be used to measure the unknown resistance of one of the resistors. By applying KCL and KVL, the condition for the balance of the bridge can be derived. 
- Superposition theorem: A method that states that the response (current or voltage) in any element of a linear circuit is equal to the algebraic sum of the responses caused by each independent source acting alone. By applying KCL and KVL, the responses due to each source can be calculated. 
- Thevenin's theorem: A method that states that any linear circuit with voltage sources and resistances can be replaced by an equivalent circuit consisting of a single voltage source and a single resistance connected in series. By applying KCL and KVL, the equivalent voltage and resistance can be determined. 
- Norton's theorem: A method that states that any linear circuit with current sources and resistances can be replaced by an equivalent circuit consisting of a single current source and a single resistance connected in parallel. By applying KCL and KVL, the equivalent current and resistance can be determined. 

Some limitations of Kirchhoff's laws are:

- They are only applicable to linear circuits, i.e., circuits that obey Ohm's law and the principle of superposition. They are not valid for nonlinear circuits, such as those with diodes, transistors, or capacitors. 
- They are only applicable to lumped circuits, i.e., circuits that can be divided into discrete elements with well-defined voltages and currents. They are not valid for distributed circuits, such as transmission lines or antennas, where the voltages and currents vary continuously along the circuit. 
- They are only applicable to steady-state circuits, i.e., circuits that have reached a constant or periodic behavior after a transient period. They are not valid for transient circuits, such as those with switching elements or time-varying sources, where the voltages and currents change rapidly over time.



### Mesh and nodal methods of analysis

- Mesh and nodal methods of analysis are two systematic techniques for solving circuits that involve writing and solving a set of equations.
- The main difference between mesh and nodal analysis is that mesh analysis uses Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) to calculate the currents in the loops of a circuit, while nodal analysis uses Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) to calculate the voltages at the nodes of a circuit.
- Both methods can be applied to any circuit that is planar, meaning that it can be drawn on a plane without any crossing branches.
- The steps for applying mesh and nodal analysis are as follows:

#### Mesh analysis
1. Identify all the meshes (closed loops) in the circuit and assign a mesh current to each one. The direction of the mesh current can be arbitrary, but it is usually chosen to be clockwise.
2. Write a KVL equation for each mesh, expressing the sum of the voltages around the loop in terms of the mesh currents and the known voltages. If there are any dependent sources in the circuit, write the controlling variable in terms of the mesh currents as well.
3. Solve the system of equations for the mesh currents using any method of linear algebra, such as substitution, elimination, or matrix inversion.
4. Use the mesh currents to find any other quantities of interest, such as branch currents, node voltages, or power dissipation.

#### Nodal analysis
1. Identify all the nodes (junctions) in the circuit and assign a node voltage to each one. Choose one node as the reference node (also called the ground) and assign it a voltage of zero. The reference node is usually the one that is connected to the most branches or the negative terminal of a voltage source.
2. Write a KCL equation for each node, expressing the sum of the currents entering and leaving the node in terms of the node voltages and the known currents. If there are any dependent sources in the circuit, write the controlling variable in terms of the node voltages as well.
3. Solve the system of equations for the node voltages using any method of linear algebra, such as substitution, elimination, or matrix inversion.
4. Use the node voltages to find any other quantities of interest, such as branch currents, mesh currents, or power dissipation.

- Mesh and nodal analysis are useful tools for analyzing circuits that have many components and sources, as they reduce the number of equations needed to solve the circuit. However, they may not be the most efficient methods for circuits that have only a few components or sources, or that have a lot of parallel or series connections, as simpler methods such as voltage divider, current divider, or source transformation may be more convenient.

### Three phase balanced circuits

- A three phase balanced circuit is a type of alternating current (AC) power system that consists of three voltage sources and three loads connected in either star (Y) or delta (Δ) configuration.
- In a balanced system, each of the three instantaneous voltages have equal amplitudes but are separated from the other voltages by a phase angle of 120°. The three voltages (or phases) are typically labeled a, b and c .
- The advantages of using a three phase balanced circuit over a single phase circuit are:
  - Higher power transfer capability: A three phase circuit can deliver three times the power of a single phase circuit with the same voltage and current ratings.
  - Higher efficiency: A three phase circuit can reduce the conductor size and the power losses by using less copper and iron in the transmission lines and transformers.
  - Higher reliability: A three phase circuit can maintain the power supply even if one of the phases fails or is disconnected.
- The analysis of a three phase balanced circuit can be simplified by using the following steps :
  - Choose a reference phase, usually phase a, and draw the corresponding single phase equivalent circuit.
  - Apply Kirchhoff's voltage and current laws to the single phase circuit and solve for the unknown variables.
  - Use the phase relationships and the star-delta transformations to find the voltages and currents in the other phases and the neutral wire.
  - Use the power factor, the apparent power, the real power and the reactive power to calculate the total power delivered or consumed by the circuit.
- The power factor of a three phase balanced circuit is defined as the ratio of the real power to the apparent power. It is a measure of how efficiently the circuit uses the supplied power. A power factor of 1 indicates that the circuit only consumes real power and no reactive power. A power factor of 0 indicates that the circuit only consumes reactive power and no real power. A power factor between 0 and 1 indicates that the circuit consumes both real and reactive power.
- The apparent power of a three phase balanced circuit is the product of the line voltage and the line current. It is measured in volt-amperes (VA) or kilovolt-amperes (kVA). The apparent power is the total power that the circuit draws from the source.
- The real power of a three phase balanced circuit is the product of the line voltage, the line current and the power factor. It is measured in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW). The real power is the useful power that the circuit delivers to the load.
- The reactive power of a three phase balanced circuit is the product of the line voltage, the line current and the sine of the phase angle between the voltage and the current. The reactive power is the power that the circuit exchanges with the source due to the presence of inductors and capacitors.

# Magnetic circuits

- A magnetic circuit is a closed path to which a magnetic field, represented as lines of magnetic flux, is confined .
- A magnetic circuit is usually made up of one or more loops containing a magnetic flux, which is generated by permanent magnets or electromagnets and confined to the path by magnetic cores consisting of ferromagnetic materials like iron.
- Some examples of magnetic circuits are: horseshoe magnet with iron keeper (low-reluctance circuit), horseshoe magnet with no keeper (high-reluctance circuit), electric motor (variable-reluctance circuit), and some types of pickup cartridge (variable-reluctance circuits).
- A magnetic circuit can be analyzed using the analogy of an electric circuit, where the magnetic flux is analogous to the electric current, the magnetic potential difference (or magnetomotive force) is analogous to the voltage, and the magnetic reluctance is analogous to the resistance .
- The basic equation for a magnetic circuit is:

  $$\Phi = \frac{F}{R}$$

  where $\Phi$ is the magnetic flux, $F$ is the magnetic potential difference, and $R$ is the magnetic reluctance .
- The magnetic potential difference $F$ is the product of the current $I$ and the number of turns $N$ of the coil that produces the magnetic field, i.e., $F = NI$ .
- The magnetic reluctance $R$ is the ratio of the magnetic potential difference $F$ to the magnetic flux $\Phi$, i.e., $R = \frac{F}{\Phi}$ .
- The magnetic reluctance $R$ depends on the length $l$, the cross-sectional area $A$, and the permeability $\mu$ of the magnetic material, i.e., $R = \frac{l}{\mu A}$ .
- The permeability $\mu$ is a measure of how easily a magnetic material can be magnetized. It is the product of the relative permeability $\mu_r$ and the permeability of free space $\mu_0$, i.e., $\mu = \mu_r \mu_0$ .
- The permeability of free space $\mu_0$ is a constant value of $4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ H/m. The relative permeability $\mu_r$ is a dimensionless quantity that varies for different materials. For air, $\mu_r = 1$. For ferromagnetic materials, $\mu_r$ is much greater than 1 .
- A magnetic circuit may have more than one loop or branch, in which case the total magnetic flux is the sum of the fluxes in each loop or branch, and the total magnetic reluctance is the equivalent reluctance of the parallel or series combination of the reluctances in each loop or branch .
- A magnetic circuit may also have air gaps or other materials in the path, which increase the magnetic reluctance and reduce the magnetic flux .
- A magnetic circuit can be used to model the behavior of devices such as transformers and relays, which use magnetic fields to transfer energy or control signals.

### Losses in Transformers

Transformers are devices that transfer electrical energy from one circuit to another by electromagnetic induction. They are used to increase or decrease the voltage and current levels of AC power systems. However, transformers are not ideal and they incur some losses during the energy conversion process. These losses reduce the efficiency and performance of the transformers. The main types of losses in transformers are:

- **Copper loss**: This is the power loss due to the resistance of the transformer windings. It is proportional to the square of the current flowing through the windings and the resistance of the windings. Copper loss can be reduced by using thicker wires or better conductors for the windings. Copper loss is also called ohmic loss, variable loss or resistive loss  .

- **Core loss**: This is the power loss due to the magnetization and demagnetization of the transformer core. It consists of two components: hysteresis loss and eddy current loss. Hysteresis loss is caused by the repeated reversal of the magnetic domains in the core material as the alternating magnetic flux changes direction. It depends on the type and quality of the core material, the frequency and the peak value of the magnetic flux. Eddy current loss is caused by the induced currents in the core due to the changing magnetic flux. It depends on the conductivity, thickness and shape of the core material, the frequency and the peak value of the magnetic flux. Core loss can be reduced by using low-loss core materials, laminating the core into thin sheets, and minimizing the air gaps in the core. Core loss is also called iron loss or constant loss   .

- **Stray loss**: This is the power loss due to the leakage of the magnetic flux from the transformer. It occurs when some of the magnetic flux does not link with both the primary and secondary windings, but instead escapes to the surrounding air or other nearby objects. It depends on the design and construction of the transformer, the load and the operating conditions. Stray loss can be reduced by using better insulation, shielding and cooling methods for the transformer. Stray loss is also called leakage loss  .

- **Dielectric loss**: This is the power loss due to the heating of the transformer insulation by the electric field. It occurs when the insulation material has some electrical conductivity or polarization, which causes it to dissipate some energy as heat when subjected to an alternating electric field. It depends on the type and quality of the insulation material, the frequency and the peak value of the electric field. Dielectric loss can be reduced by using low-loss insulation materials, minimizing the thickness and area of the insulation, and maintaining the insulation in a dry and clean condition. Dielectric loss is also called insulation loss .

The total loss in a transformer is the sum of the copper loss, the core loss, the stray loss and the dielectric loss. The efficiency of a transformer is the ratio of the output power to the input power, which is equal to one minus the ratio of the total loss to the input power. The efficiency of a transformer can be improved by minimizing the losses in the transformer. The losses in a transformer can be calculated by using various methods, such as the open-circuit test, the short-circuit test, the load test and the phasor diagram analysis  .



### Regulation and Efficiency of Transformers

- Regulation of a transformer is the measure of how well it maintains the output voltage when the load changes. It is defined as the percentage change in the secondary voltage from no-load to full-load at a constant primary voltage and frequency.
- Regulation of a transformer depends on its equivalent series impedance, which consists of the resistance and leakage reactance of the primary and secondary windings. A low equivalent series impedance means a low voltage drop across the transformer and a better regulation.

```

```

where Pout is the output power and Pin is the input power.

- The slip-torque characteristic of an induction motor is the relationship between the torque produced by the motor and the slip of the rotor with respect to the synchronous speed.
- The slip of the rotor is defined as the difference between the synchronous speed and the rotor speed, expressed as a fraction of the synchronous speed.
- The slip-torque characteristic can be derived from the equivalent circuit of the induction motor, by equating the mechanical power output to the electrical power input to the rotor.
- The slip-torque characteristic can be represented by a rectangular hyperbola, as shown in the figure below.

Slip-torque characteristic

- The slip-torque characteristic can be divided into three regions, depending on the value of the slip:

  - Low slip region: This is the region where the slip is very small, and the rotor speed is near the synchronous speed. In this region, the torque is proportional to the slip, and the rotor resistance is negligible compared to the rotor reactance. The torque is given by:

    $$T = \frac{3V^2_{ph}R_2}{sX^2_0}$$

    $$T = \frac{3V^2_{ph}R_2/s}{(R_2/s)^2 + X^2_0}$$

    The torque reaches its maximum value when $R_2/s = X_0$, which corresponds to the slip value of:

    $$s_{max} = \frac{R_2}{X_0}$$

    $$T_{max} = \frac{3V^2_{ph}}{2X_0}$$

    $$T = \frac{3V^2_{ph}s}{(R_2/s)^2 + X^2_0}$$

# ELCB

- ELCB stands for Earth Leakage Circuit Breaker   .
- It is a safety device used in electrical installations with high Earth impedance to prevent shock  .
- It detects small stray voltages on the metal enclosures of electrical equipment, and interrupts the circuit if a dangerous voltage is detected  .
- It is a specialised type of latching relay that has a building's incoming mains power connected through its switching contacts so that the ELCB disconnects the power when earth leakage is detected .
- It detects fault currents from live to the Earth (ground) wire within the installation it protects .

## Types of ELCB

- There are two types of ELCB: voltage-operated and current-operated  .
- Voltage-operated ELCB: It measures the voltage difference between the metal body of the equipment and the Earth wire. It trips when the voltage exceeds a preset value  .
- Current-operated ELCB: It measures the current difference between the live and neutral wires. It trips when the current difference exceeds a preset value  .
- Current-operated ELCB is more sensitive and reliable than voltage-operated ELCB  .

## Advantages of ELCB

- ELCB provides protection against electrical shock and fire hazards caused by earth leakage  .
- ELCB can detect very low levels of leakage current that may not be detected by other devices  .
- ELCB can isolate the faulty circuit quickly and prevent further damage  .
- ELCB can be easily reset after tripping without replacing any components  .

## Disadvantages of ELCB

- ELCB may not work properly if the Earth wire is broken or disconnected  .
- ELCB may cause nuisance tripping due to external factors such as lightning, static electricity, or capacitive coupling  .
- ELCB may not detect leakage currents that flow through other paths such as water pipes, metal structures, or human bodies  .
- ELCB may not be compatible with some types of equipment that generate harmonics or have high inrush currents  .

## Applications of ELCB

- ELCB is widely used in residential, commercial, and industrial electrical installations to provide safety against earth leakage  .
- ELCB is especially suitable for applications where the equipment is exposed to moisture, dust, or corrosion, such as bathrooms, kitchens, garages, workshops, etc  .
- ELCB is also recommended for applications where the equipment has a high risk of insulation failure, such as electric heaters, motors, transformers, etc  .

# ACB

### Fundamentals of earthing and lightning protection

- Earthing and lightning protection are important for everyone that uses electrical equipment and that includes the large majority of the world.
- Earthing is the process of connecting the metallic parts of an electrical system or appliance to the earth. This provides a low-resistance path for fault currents and protects the system and the users from electric shock.
- Lightning protection is the process of intercepting or diverting lightning and providing a certain path for conducting the surges safely to the ground by adequate down conductors to grounding electrodes. This helps prevent disastrous events like fires, injuries, and deaths .
- The basic principles of earthing and lightning protection are:
  - Provide a low-impedance path for fault currents and lightning currents to the earth.
  - Minimize the potential difference between the earthed parts and the surrounding soil.
  - Avoid dangerous touch and step voltages for humans and animals.
  - Reduce electromagnetic interference and noise in the electrical system.
  - Enhance the performance and reliability of the electrical system .
- The types and components of earthing and lightning protection systems are:
  - Earthing electrodes: These are metal rods, plates, or wires that are buried in the soil and connected to the earthed parts of the electrical system. They provide a physical contact with the earth and dissipate the fault currents and lightning currents into the soil .
  - Earthing conductors: These are metal wires or cables that connect the earthing electrodes to the earthed parts of the electrical system. They provide a low-resistance path for the fault currents and lightning currents to flow to the earth .
  - Earthing bonds: These are metal connections that join the metallic parts of the electrical system that are not directly connected to the earthing conductors. They ensure that all the metallic parts are at the same potential and prevent circulating currents and sparks .
  - Lightning rods: These are metal rods or wires that are installed on the top of buildings or structures that are exposed to lightning strikes. They intercept the lightning and divert it to the earthing system .
  - Lightning down conductors: These are metal wires or cables that connect the lightning rods to the earthing system. They provide a low-impedance path for the lightning currents to flow to the earth .
  - Lightning air terminals: These are metal rods or wires that are installed along the edges or corners of buildings or structures that are exposed to lightning strikes. They provide additional protection to the lightning rods by creating a zone of protection around them .
  - Surge arresters: These are devices that are installed on the electrical system to limit the overvoltages caused by lightning or switching surges. They divert the excess voltage to the earthing system and protect the electrical equipment from damage .
