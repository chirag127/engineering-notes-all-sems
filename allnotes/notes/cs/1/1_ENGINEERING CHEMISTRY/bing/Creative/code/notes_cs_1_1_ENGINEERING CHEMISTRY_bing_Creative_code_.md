# Stereochemistry

Stereochemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies the spatial arrangement of atoms and molecules in three-dimensional space. Stereochemistry is important for understanding the structure, properties, and reactions of organic and inorganic compounds.

## Stereoisomers

Stereoisomers are molecules that have the same molecular formula and the same connectivity of atoms, but differ in the orientation of their atoms in space. Stereoisomers can be classified into two main types: **configurational isomers** and **conformational isomers**.

### Configurational Isomers

Configurational isomers are stereoisomers that cannot be interconverted by rotation around single bonds. They can only be interchanged by breaking and reforming bonds. Configurational isomers can be further divided into two subtypes: **enantiomers** and **diastereomers**.

#### Enantiomers

Enantiomers are configurational isomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. They have the same physical and chemical properties, except for their interaction with plane-polarized light and other chiral molecules. Enantiomers have opposite configurations at all chiral centers in the molecule. A chiral center is an atom, usually carbon, that is bonded to four different groups. Enantiomers are also called **optical isomers** because they can rotate the plane of polarized light in opposite directions. The degree of rotation is called **optical activity**, and the direction of rotation is indicated by the prefixes **(+)** and **(-)**, or **d** and **l**, respectively. Enantiomers have the same **absolute configuration**, which is determined by the **Cahn-Ingold-Prelog (CIP) rules**. The CIP rules assign priorities to the groups attached to a chiral center based on their atomic number, mass, and connectivity. The absolute configuration is then denoted by the symbols **R** (from Latin *rectus*, meaning right) and **S** (from Latin *sinister*, meaning left), depending on whether the priority order of the groups is clockwise or counterclockwise when viewed from the lowest priority group.

#### Diastereomers

Diastereomers are configurational isomers that are not mirror images of each other. They have different physical and chemical properties, and may have different configurations at some or all chiral centers in the molecule. Diastereomers can be further classified into several subtypes, such as **cis-trans isomers**, **epimers**, **anomers**, and **meso compounds**.

##### Cis-trans Isomers

Cis-trans isomers are diastereomers that differ in the orientation of two groups around a double bond or a ring. The groups can be on the same side (**cis**) or on opposite sides (**trans**) of the bond or the ring. Cis-trans isomers have different physical properties, such as melting point, boiling point, and solubility, because of their different shapes and polarity. Cis-trans isomers can also be called **geometric isomers** or **E-Z isomers**. The E-Z notation is based on the CIP rules, and assigns the labels **E** (from German *entgegen*, meaning opposite) and **Z** (from German *zusammen*, meaning together) to the isomers depending on whether the two highest priority groups are on opposite or same sides of the bond or the ring.

##### Epimers

Epimers are diastereomers that differ in the configuration of only one chiral center in the molecule. Epimers are common in carbohydrates, such as glucose and galactose, which differ in the configuration of the carbon atom at position 4. Epimers have different biological activities and functions, such as blood type and hormone regulation.

##### Anomers

Anomers are epimers that differ in the configuration of the anomeric carbon atom in a cyclic sugar. The anomeric carbon atom is the one that was involved in the formation of the ring from the open-chain form of the sugar. Anomers can have either **alpha** or **beta** configuration, depending on whether the hydroxyl group attached to the anomeric carbon is on the same or opposite side of the ring as the reference group, usually the terminal carbon atom. Anomers can interconvert in aqueous solution, a process called **mutarotation**.

However, water can also cause various problems in these systems, such as corrosion, scaling, fouling, and microbiological growth. These problems can affect the efficiency, safety, and reliability of the equipment, and may require costly repairs or replacements. Therefore, water treatment and quality control are essential for the proper operation and maintenance of water-based systems.

Some of the common water and boiler troubles used in industry are:

- **Priming**: This is the phenomenon of water droplets being carried away with the steam from the boiler, resulting in wet and low-quality steam. Priming can be caused by high water levels, sudden changes in steam demand, improper boiler design, or contaminated feed water. Priming can lead to water hammer, erosion, and damage to the steam system .
- **Foaming**: This is the formation of a layer of froth or stable foam on the surface of the water in the boiler, which reduces the steam space and increases the water level. Foaming can be caused by high concentrations of dissolved solids, organic matter, or oil in the feed water, or by low boiler pressure or temperature. Foaming can result in carryover, corrosion, and reduced efficiency .
- **Scaling**: This is the deposition of hard and insoluble minerals, such as calcium carbonate, on the internal surfaces of the boiler and the heat transfer equipment. Scaling can be caused by high hardness, alkalinity, or temperature of the feed water, or by inadequate water treatment or blowdown. Scaling can reduce the heat transfer efficiency, increase the fuel consumption, and cause overheating and failure of the metal parts  .
- **Corrosion**: This is the deterioration of the metal surfaces of the boiler and the water system due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with the water or the dissolved gases, such as oxygen and carbon dioxide. Corrosion can be caused by low pH, high dissolved oxygen, high chloride, or low alkalinity of the feed water, or by improper water treatment or material selection. Corrosion can lead to leaks, cracks, pitting, and reduced strength and life span of the equipment   .
- **Fouling**: This is the accumulation of soft and sticky substances, such as organic matter, oil, grease, or microbiological growth, on the internal surfaces of the boiler and the water system. Fouling can be caused by contamination of the feed water, inadequate water treatment, or low water velocity. Fouling can reduce the heat transfer efficiency, increase the pressure drop, and promote corrosion and scaling  .
- **Microbiological growth**: This is the proliferation of microorganisms, such as bacteria, algae, fungi, or protozoa, in the water system, especially in the cooling towers and the condensate return lines. ### Atomic and Molecular Structure: Molecular orbitals of diatomic molecules, bond order

- Molecular orbitals (MOs) are the wave functions that describe the distribution of electrons in a molecule. They are formed by the linear combination of atomic orbitals (AOs) of the atoms that constitute the molecule.
- The number of MOs is equal to the number of AOs that are combined. The MOs can be classified into two types: bonding and antibonding. Bonding MOs have lower energy and higher electron density between the nuclei, while antibonding MOs have higher energy and lower electron density between the nuclei.
- The MOs can be arranged in order of increasing energy, forming the molecular orbital diagram. The electrons are filled in the MOs according to the Aufbau principle, Hund's rule, and Pauli exclusion principle. The number of electrons in the bonding MOs minus the number of electrons in the antibonding MOs is called the bond order. The bond order indicates the strength and stability of the bond between the atoms.
- For diatomic molecules, the MOs can be derived from the AOs of the same symmetry. For example, the s orbitals of two atoms can combine to form a sigma (σ) bonding MO and a sigma star (σ*) antibonding MO. The p orbitals of two atoms can combine to form a sigma (σ) bonding MO, a sigma star (σ*) antibonding MO, and two pi (π) bonding MOs and two pi star (π*) antibonding MOs. The relative energies of the MOs depend on the overlap and the electronegativity of the AOs.

# Magnetic Characteristics and Numerical Problems

Magnetic characteristics are the properties of a material that determine its behavior in a magnetic field. Some of the important magnetic characteristics are:

- Magnetic permeability: The ratio of the magnetic flux density (B) to the magnetic field intensity (H) in a material. It measures how easily a material can be magnetized by an external field. The magnetic permeability of free space (vacuum) is denoted by μ0 and has a value of 4π × 10^-7 H/m. The relative permeability (μr) of a material is the ratio of its permeability to the permeability of free space. The permeability of a material (μ) is the product of μ0 and μr.

- Magnetic susceptibility: The ratio of the magnetization (M) to the magnetic field intensity (H) in a material. It measures how much a material is magnetized by an external field. The magnetic susceptibility of a material (χ) is related to its relative permeability by the formula: χ = μr - 1.

- Magnetic hysteresis: The phenomenon of retaining some magnetization even after the external field is removed. It is caused by the alignment of the magnetic domains in a material. The magnetic hysteresis loop is a graph of the magnetization (M) versus the magnetic field intensity (H) for a material. It shows the history of the magnetization process. The area enclosed by the loop is the energy loss per unit volume due to hysteresis.

- Magnetic saturation: The condition when the magnetization (M) of a material reaches a maximum value and does not increase further with increasing magnetic field intensity (H). It is caused by the alignment of all the magnetic domains in a material. The magnetic saturation magnetization (Ms) is the maximum value of M for a material.

- Coercivity: The magnetic field intensity (H) required to reduce the magnetization (M) of a material to zero after it has been magnetized to saturation. It measures the resistance of a material to demagnetization. The higher the coercivity, the harder the material is to demagnetize.

- Remanence: The magnetization (M) of a material that remains after the external field is removed. It measures the residual magnetism of a material. The higher the remanence, the stronger the permanent magnet is.

### Green Chemistry: Introduction, 12 principles and importance of green Synthesis, Green

- Green chemistry is an area of chemistry and chemical engineering that aims to design products and processes that minimize or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances .
- Green chemistry applies across the life cycle of a chemical product, from its design, manufacture, use, and disposal.
- Green chemistry can also be measured by various metrics, such as mass, energy, toxicity, and environmental impact.
- Green chemistry is based on 12 principles that provide guidelines for greener practices. These principles are:

  1. Prevent waste: Design chemical syntheses to prevent waste, leaving no waste to treat or clean up.
  2. Maximize atom economy: Design syntheses so that the final product contains the maximum proportion of the starting materials. There should be few, if any, wasted atoms.
  3. Design less hazardous chemical syntheses: Design syntheses to use and generate substances with little or no toxicity to humans and the environment.
  4. Design safer chemicals and products: Design chemical products to be fully effective, yet have little or no toxicity.
  5. Use safer solvents and reaction conditions: Avoid using solvents, separation agents, or other auxiliary chemicals. If these chemicals are necessary, use innocuous chemicals.
  6. Increase energy efficiency: Run chemical reactions at ambient temperature and pressure whenever possible.
  7. Use renewable feedstocks: Use starting materials (also known as feedstocks) that are renewable rather than depleting. Renewable feedstocks are often made from agricultural products or are the wastes of other processes; depleting feedstocks are made from fossil fuels (petroleum, natural gas, or coal) or are mined.
  8. Avoid chemical derivatives: Avoid using blocking or protecting groups or any temporary modifications if possible. Derivatives use additional reagents and generate waste.
  9. Use catalysts, not stoichiometric reagents: Minimize waste by using catalytic reactions. Catalysts are used in small amounts and can carry out a single reaction many times. They are preferable to stoichiometric reagents, which are used in excess and work only once.
  10. Design chemicals and products to degrade after use: Design chemical products to break down to innocuous substances after use so that they do not accumulate in the environment.
  11. Analyze in real time to prevent pollution: Include in-process real-time monitoring and control during syntheses to minimize or eliminate the formation of byproducts.
  12. Minimize the potential for accidents: Design chemicals and their forms (solid, liquid, or gas) to minimize the potential for chemical accidents including explosions, fires, and releases to the environment.

- The importance of green chemistry lies in its benefits for human health, environmental quality, and economic growth. Some of the benefits are:

  - Reducing the use and generation of hazardous substances can prevent pollution, protect workers and consumers, and reduce the costs and liabilities associated with waste management and cleanup.
  - Improving the efficiency and selectivity of chemical reactions can save energy and resources, lower the production costs, and increase the competitiveness and profitability of chemical industries.
  - Developing safer and more effective products can meet the needs and demands of consumers, create new markets and opportunities, and enhance the reputation and image of chemical enterprises.
  - Innovating and adopting greener technologies can foster scientific creativity and discovery, stimulate the development of new skills and knowledge, and create new jobs and careers.

- Stereochemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies the spatial arrangement of atoms and molecules and their effects on the physical and chemical properties of substances.
- Optical isomerism is a type of stereoisomerism that occurs when a compound has the same molecular formula and connectivity but different spatial orientation of atoms, resulting in non-superimposable mirror images. These mirror images are called enantiomers or optical isomers.
- Optical isomers have the same physical and chemical properties in achiral environments, but they differ in their interaction with plane-polarized light and other chiral molecules. Optical isomers can rotate the plane of polarized light either to the right (dextrorotatory) or to the left (levorotatory), depending on their configuration.
- Optical isomerism usually occurs in compounds that have one or more chiral centers, which are atoms that are bonded to four different groups. However, optical isomerism can also occur in compounds without chiral centers, such as allenes, biphenyls, and spiranes. These compounds have a different type of chirality, called axial chirality, which arises from the restricted rotation around a bond or a ring.
- Geometrical isomerism is another type of stereoisomerism that occurs when a compound has the same molecular formula and connectivity but different spatial arrangement of atoms due to the presence of a double bond or a ring. These isomers are called geometrical isomers or cis-trans isomers.
- Geometrical isomers have different physical and chemical properties, such as melting point, boiling point, solubility, and reactivity. Geometrical isomerism is common in heteroleptic compounds, which are coordination compounds that have different ligands attached to the central metal atom.

### Electrochemistry and Batteries: Basic concepts of electrochemistry

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. Electrochemical reactions involve the transfer of electrons between substances, which can be harnessed to produce electricity or to drive chemical reactions.

Some basic concepts of electrochemistry are:

- **Electrochemical cell**: A device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy or vice versa. It consists of two electrodes (conductors) and an electrolyte (a solution or a solid that allows the flow of ions).
- **Electrode**: A conductor that is in contact with the electrolyte and allows the flow of electrons to or from the external circuit. The electrode where oxidation (loss of electrons) occurs is called the **anode**, and the electrode where reduction (gain of electrons) occurs is called the **cathode**.
- **Electrolyte**: A substance that contains ions that can move freely and carry electric charge. It can be a liquid, a solid, or a gel. The electrolyte completes the electric circuit by allowing the flow of ions between the electrodes.
- **Electrode potential**: The measure of the tendency of an electrode to lose or gain electrons. It depends on the nature of the electrode material and the concentration of the ions in the electrolyte. The electrode potential is measured relative to a standard reference electrode, such as the **standard hydrogen electrode**.
- **Cell potential**: The difference in electrode potentials between the two electrodes of an electrochemical cell. It is also called the **electromotive force (emf)** of the cell. It represents the maximum amount of work that can be obtained from the cell. The cell potential is positive for a spontaneous reaction and negative for a non-spontaneous reaction.
- **Galvanic cell**: An electrochemical cell that converts chemical energy into electrical energy by a spontaneous redox reaction. It is also called a **voltaic cell** or a **battery**. A battery can consist of one or more galvanic cells connected in series or parallel. Examples of galvanic cells are the **Daniell cell**, the **lead-acid battery**, and the **alkaline battery**.
- **Electrolytic cell**: An electrochemical cell that converts electrical energy into chemical energy by a non-spontaneous redox reaction. It requires an external source of electricity to drive the reaction. Examples of electrolytic cells are the **electroplating cell**, the **electrolysis of water**, and the **chlor-alkali process**.

# Lead Acid Battery

A lead acid battery is a type of rechargeable battery that was first invented in 1859 by French physicist Gaston Planté. It is the oldest type of rechargeable battery and is widely used in various applications, such as automobiles, uninterruptible power supplies, and electric grids .

The main components of a lead acid battery are:

- Two electrodes: a positive electrode made of lead oxide (PbO2) and a negative electrode made of porous or spongy lead (Pb).
- An electrolyte: a solution of sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and water (H2O) that conducts electricity between the electrodes.
- A separator: a material that prevents the electrodes from touching each other and causing a short circuit.
- A container: a plastic or metal case that holds the electrodes, electrolyte, and separator.

The basic principle of a lead acid battery is the reversible chemical reaction between the electrodes and the electrolyte. When the battery is charging, an external power source forces electrons to flow from the positive electrode to the negative electrode, converting lead oxide to lead sulfate (PbSO4) and lead to lead sulfate. This process consumes sulfuric acid and water, making the electrolyte more dilute. When the battery is discharging, the reverse process occurs, and electrons flow from the negative electrode to the positive electrode, converting lead sulfate back to lead oxide and lead. This process produces sulfuric acid and water, making the electrolyte more concentrated.

The voltage of a lead acid battery depends on the concentration of the electrolyte and the temperature. The nominal voltage of a single cell is about 2 volts, and a typical lead acid battery consists of six cells connected in series, giving a total voltage of 12 volts. The capacity of a lead acid battery is measured in ampere-hours (Ah), which indicates how much current the battery can deliver for a given time. The capacity depends on the size and design of the battery, as well as the discharge rate and temperature.

Lead acid batteries have some advantages and disadvantages compared to other types of batteries. Some of the advantages are:

- Low cost: lead acid batteries are relatively cheap to manufacture and recycle, making them affordable for many applications.
- High power: lead acid batteries can deliver high currents and power, making them suitable for starting engines and other high-demand applications.
- Long lifespan: lead acid batteries can last for several years if maintained properly and not overcharged or deeply discharged.

Some of the disadvantages are:

- Low energy density: lead acid batteries have a low amount of energy stored per unit of weight or volume, making them bulky and heavy compared to other types of batteries .
- Self-discharge: lead acid batteries lose some of their charge over time when not in use, requiring periodic recharging or maintenance.
- Environmental impact: lead acid batteries contain toxic materials, such as lead and sulfuric acid, that can cause environmental damage and health hazards if not disposed of properly .

- Corrosion can cause loss of material, loss of function, loss of aesthetics, and loss of safety.
- Corrosion can be classified into different types based on the mechanism, morphology, environment, or affected material. Some common types of corrosion are:
  - Uniform corrosion: The metal surface corrodes at a uniform rate over the entire exposed area. This type of corrosion is predictable and easy to measure.
  - Galvanic corrosion: Two dissimilar metals are in contact with each other and an electrolyte, creating a galvanic cell. The more active metal (anode) corrodes faster than the less active metal (cathode).
  - Pitting corrosion: The metal surface corrodes at localized spots, forming pits or holes. This type of corrosion is dangerous because it can lead to perforation and failure of the metal.
  - Crevice corrosion: The metal surface corrodes at narrow gaps or crevices, where the electrolyte is stagnant and oxygen is depleted. This type of corrosion is common in joints, bolts, rivets, and gaskets.
  - Intergranular corrosion: The metal surface corrodes along the grain boundaries, leaving the grains intact. This type of corrosion is caused by impurities, alloying elements, or heat treatment that affect the grain boundary structure.
  - Stress corrosion cracking: The metal surface cracks under the combined effect of tensile stress and a corrosive environment. This type of corrosion is influenced by the material, the stress level, and the environment.
  - Corrosion fatigue: The metal surface cracks under the combined effect of cyclic stress and a corrosive environment. This type of corrosion reduces the fatigue strength of the metal.
  - Erosion corrosion: The metal surface corrodes due to the mechanical abrasion of a fluid or a solid particle. This type of corrosion increases the corrosion rate and changes the corrosion morphology.
  - Microbiologically influenced corrosion: The metal surface corrodes due to the metabolic activity of microorganisms, such as bacteria, fungi, or algae. This type of corrosion can produce various forms of corrosion, such as pitting, crevice, intergranular, or stress corrosion cracking.
- The cause of corrosion is the thermodynamic tendency of metals to return to their natural state, such as oxides, sulfides, or carbonates. Metals are extracted from their ores by reducing them with energy. Corrosion is the reverse process of oxidation, where metals lose electrons and form ions. The driving force of corrosion is the difference in the chemical potential or the free energy of the metal and its environment.

  - Material selection: Choosing a material that is resistant to corrosion in a given environment, such as stainless steel, aluminum, or titanium.
  - Coating: Applying a protective layer on the metal surface that acts as a barrier or a sacrificial anode, such as paint, zinc, or chromium.
  - Cathodic protection: Applying an external electric current or a sacrificial anode to the metal surface that makes it the cathode of a galvanic cell, preventing it from corroding.
  - Inhibitors: Adding chemicals to the environment that reduce the corrosion rate by forming a protective film, scavenging oxygen, or inhibiting the corrosion reactions, such as phosphates, chromates, or amines.
  - Design: Designing the structure or the system to avoid crevices, stress concentration, galvanic coupling, or erosion, and to facilitate inspection, maintenance, and repair.

- Fill a 100 mL volumetric flask with the water sample and record the initial volume.

- Start adding the acid titrant slowly and stir the solution until the pink color disappears. This is the first endpoint, and it corresponds to the pH of 8.3. Record the volume of the acid used and the pH.
- Continue adding the acid titrant until the pH reaches 4.5. This is the second endpoint, and it corresponds to the total alkalinity (due to the presence of hydroxide, carbonate, and bicarbonate ions). Record the final volume of the acid used and the pH.

Alkalinity (mg/L as CaCO3) = (Volume of acid used x Normality of acid x 50,000) / Volume of water sample

### Polymers; Classification, Polymerization processes, Thermosetting and Thermoplastic

- Polymers are large molecules composed of repeating units called monomers, which are linked by covalent bonds.
- Polymers can be classified based on various factors, such as their source, structure, mode of synthesis, molecular forces, and thermal behavior.
- Based on the source, polymers can be natural (derived from plants or animals) or synthetic (made by chemical reactions).
- Based on the structure, polymers can be linear (consisting of a single chain of monomers), branched (having side chains attached to the main chain), or cross-linked (having covalent bonds between different chains).
- Based on the mode of synthesis, polymers can be addition (formed by adding monomers without losing any atoms) or condensation (formed by removing small molecules such as water or ammonia from monomers).
- Based on the molecular forces, polymers can be crystalline (having ordered and regular arrangement of chains) or amorphous (having random and irregular arrangement of chains).
- Based on the thermal behavior, polymers can be thermoplastic (softening on heating and hardening on cooling) or thermosetting (hardening on heating and not softening on cooling).
- Polymerization is the process of forming polymers from monomers. There are two main types of polymerization: chain (or free radical) and step (or condensation).
- Chain polymerization involves the initiation, propagation, and termination of a chain reaction, in which a free radical (an atom or molecule with an unpaired electron) attacks a monomer and adds it to the growing chain. The chain reaction continues until the free radicals are consumed or deactivated. Chain polymerization is used to produce most thermoplastic polymers, such as polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, and PVC.
- Step polymerization involves the repeated condensation of monomers with functional groups, such as carboxylic acids, alcohols, amines, and amides. The monomers react with each other to form larger molecules, releasing small molecules such as water or ammonia. Step polymerization is used to produce most thermosetting polymers, such as polyesters, polyamides, and epoxy resins.
- Thermosetting polymers have cross-linked molecular chains, which prevent them from flowing or melting when heated. They are rigid and brittle, and have high thermal stability and chemical resistance. They are usually formed by curing (heating or adding a catalyst) a mixture of monomers or prepolymers, which causes the cross-linking to occur. Thermosetting polymers cannot be recycled or reshaped. Examples of thermosetting polymers are bakelite, melamine, and vulcanized rubber.
- Thermoplastic polymers have linear or slightly branched molecular chains, which allow them to soften and flow when heated. They are flexible and ductile, and have lower thermal stability and chemical resistance. They are usually formed by cooling or solidifying a melt or a solution of polymers, which preserves the chain arrangement. Thermoplastic polymers can be recycled or reshaped by heating and cooling. Examples of thermoplastic polymers are nylon, polyethylene, and polycarbonate.

# Organometallic Compounds: General methods of preparation and applications

Organometallic compounds are compounds that contain at least one metal-carbon bond, where the carbon is part of an organic group. They are important in various fields of chemistry, such as organic synthesis, catalysis, medicine, and materials science .

## General methods of preparation

There are several methods to prepare organometallic compounds, depending on the type of metal and the organic group involved. Some of the common methods are:

- **Reaction of metal with organic halides**: This method is suitable for metals that are more electropositive than the halogen, such as lithium, magnesium, zinc, and cadmium. The reaction is usually carried out in an inert solvent, such as ether or tetrahydrofuran (THF). For example, the reaction of ethyl bromide with magnesium in ether produces ethylmagnesium bromide, a Grignard reagent:

`CH3CH2Br + Mg -> CH3CH2MgBr`

- **Reaction of metal with alkynes or alkenes**: This method is suitable for metals that can form pi bonds with carbon, such as transition metals. The reaction is usually carried out in the presence of a ligand, such as carbon monoxide, phosphine, or cyclopentadienyl. For example, the reaction of cyclopentadiene with titanium tetrachloride in the presence of sodium produces bis(cyclopentadienyl)titanium dichloride, a metallocene:

`2 C5H6 + TiCl4 + 2 Na -> (C5H5)2TiCl2 + 2 NaCl + 2 H2`

- **Reaction of metal with carbon monoxide**: This method is suitable for metals that can form carbonyl complexes, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt. The reaction is usually carried out under high pressure and temperature. For example, the reaction of iron with carbon monoxide produces iron pentacarbonyl, a volatile liquid:

`Fe + 5 CO -> Fe(CO)5`

## General applications

Organometallic compounds have a wide range of applications in various fields, such as:

- **Organic synthesis**: Organometallic compounds are often used as reagents, intermediates, or catalysts in organic synthesis, as they can perform various reactions, such as addition, substitution, elimination, oxidation, reduction, coupling, and polymerization. For example, Grignard reagents can react with aldehydes, ketones, esters, and nitriles to form alcohols, ketones, tertiary alcohols, and amines, respectively. Metallocenes can catalyze the polymerization of olefins, such as ethylene and propylene, to form polyethylene and polypropylene, respectively.

- **Medicine**: Organometallic compounds are often used as drugs, drug delivery systems, or biomimetic models in medicine, as they can interact with biological molecules, such as DNA, proteins, and enzymes. For example, cisplatin, a platinum complex, is used as an anticancer drug, as it can bind to DNA and inhibit its replication. Silicone rubbers, which contain silicon-carbon bonds, are used as implants or prosthetics in surgery, as they are biocompatible and durable.

- **Materials science**: Organometallic compounds are often used as precursors, modifiers, or components in materials science, as they can form various types of materials, such as metals, alloys, ceramics, polymers, and nanomaterials. For example, iron pentacarbonyl can be decomposed to form pure iron nanoparticles, which have magnetic and catalytic properties. Organometallic polymers, which contain metal-carbon bonds in the main chain or the side chain, can exhibit electrical, optical, or thermal properties, depending on the metal and the organic group.

# Green Chemistry

Green chemistry is the approach in chemical sciences that efficiently uses renewable raw materials, eliminating waste and avoiding the use of toxic and hazardous reagents and solvents in the manufacture and application of chemical products.

## Principles of Green Chemistry

There are twelve basic principles of green chemistry that can be grouped into “Reducing Risk” and “Minimizing the Environmental Footprint”.

### Reducing Risk

- **Prevention**: It is better to prevent waste than to treat or clean up waste after it has been created.
- **Atom Economy**: Synthetic methods should be designed to maximize the incorporation of all materials used in the process into the final product.
- **Less Hazardous Chemical Syntheses**: Wherever practicable, synthetic methods should be designed to use and generate substances that possess little or no toxicity to human health and the environment.
- **Designing Safer Chemicals**: Chemical products should be designed to affect their desired function while minimizing their toxicity.
- **Safer Solvents and Auxiliaries**: The use of auxiliary substances (e.g., solvents, separation agents, etc.) should be made unnecessary wherever possible and innocuous when used.
- **Design for Energy Efficiency**: Energy requirements of chemical processes should be recognized for their environmental and economic impacts and should be minimized. If possible, synthetic methods should be conducted at ambient temperature and pressure.

### Minimizing the Environmental Footprint

- **Use of Renewable Feedstocks**: A raw material or feedstock should be renewable rather than depleting whenever technically and economically practicable.
- **Reduce Derivatives**: Unnecessary derivatization (use of blocking groups, protection/ deprotection, temporary modification of physical/chemical processes) should be minimized or avoided if possible, because such steps require additional reagents and can generate waste.
- **Catalysis**: Catalytic reagents (as selective as possible) are superior to stoichiometric reagents.

# Stereochemistry

- **Chirality**: the property of a molecule that makes it non-superimposable on its mirror image.
