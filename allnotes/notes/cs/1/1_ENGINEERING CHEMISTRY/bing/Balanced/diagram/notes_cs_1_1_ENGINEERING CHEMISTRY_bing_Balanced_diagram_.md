# Advanced Materials

Advanced materials are materials that have novel properties or superior performance compared to conventional materials. They can be classified into different categories based on their structure, composition, or applications. Some examples of advanced materials are liquid crystals, nanomaterials, graphite, fullerenes, and green chemistry.

## Liquid Crystals

Liquid crystals are materials that have properties of both liquids and crystals. They can flow like liquids, but also have an ordered arrangement of molecules like crystals. Liquid crystals can be divided into different types based on the orientation and alignment of their molecules, such as nematic, smectic, cholesteric, and discotic. Liquid crystals have various applications in displays, sensors, optical devices, and thermometers.

## Nanomaterials

Nanomaterials are materials that have at least one dimension in the range of 1 to 100 nanometers. Nanomaterials can have unique physical, chemical, optical, electrical, and magnetic properties due to their small size and high surface area. Nanomaterials can be synthesized by different methods, such as top-down, bottom-up, self-assembly, and biological routes. Nanomaterials can be used in various fields, such as electronics, energy, medicine, catalysis, and environmental remediation.

## Graphite

Graphite is a form of carbon that consists of layers of hexagonal rings of carbon atoms. Graphite has a high electrical and thermal conductivity, a low coefficient of friction, and a high resistance to corrosion and oxidation. Graphite can be used as an electrode, a lubricant, a moderator in nuclear reactors, and a precursor for other carbon-based materials, such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, and fullerenes.

## Fullerenes

Fullerenes are a class of carbon-based nanomaterials that have a spherical, cylindrical, or ellipsoidal shape. The most common fullerene is C60, also known as buckminsterfullerene or buckyball, which has a soccer ball-like structure. Fullerenes have remarkable mechanical, electrical, optical, and chemical properties, such as high tensile strength, superconductivity, photoluminescence, and catalytic activity. Fullerenes can be used in various applications, such as drug delivery, solar cells, sensors, and nanocomposites.

## Green Chemistry

Green chemistry is a branch of chemistry that aims to design and produce chemical products and processes that minimize or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. Green chemistry follows 12 principles, such as prevention, atom economy, less hazardous synthesis, safer solvents, renewable feedstocks, and design for degradation. Green chemistry can benefit the environment, human health, and economic development by reducing waste, energy consumption, pollution, and toxicity.

- Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies the relationship between electricity and chemical reactions.
- Electrochemical reactions involve the transfer of electrons between substances, either in a spontaneous process that produces electrical energy (such as in a battery) or in a non-spontaneous process that consumes electrical energy (such as in electrolysis).
- Electrochemical cells are devices that convert chemical energy into electrical energy or vice versa. They consist of two electrodes (conductors) connected by an external circuit and immersed in an electrolyte (a solution or a solid that allows the flow of ions).
- The electrode where oxidation (loss of electrons) occurs is called the anode, and the electrode where reduction (gain of electrons) occurs is called the cathode. The direction of electron flow in the external circuit is from the anode to the cathode.
- The potential difference between the electrodes is called the cell potential or the electromotive force (emf) of the cell. It is measured in volts (V) and depends on the nature and concentration of the substances involved in the reaction, the temperature, and the pressure.
- The standard cell potential is the potential difference between the electrodes when the reactants and products are in their standard states (usually 1 M for solutions, 1 atm for gases, and pure solids or liquids). It is calculated by subtracting the standard reduction potential of the anode from the standard reduction potential of the cathode.
- The Nernst equation relates the cell potential to the concentration of the reactants and products. It can be used to calculate the equilibrium constant of an electrochemical reaction or the concentration of an unknown substance in a cell.
- Batteries are electrochemical cells or combinations of cells that provide a continuous source of electrical energy. They can be classified into primary batteries, which are not rechargeable and can only be used once, and secondary batteries, which are rechargeable and can be used multiple times.
- Some common examples of primary batteries are zinc-carbon batteries, alkaline batteries, lithium batteries, and button cells. Some common examples of secondary batteries are lead-acid batteries, nickel-cadmium batteries, nickel-metal hydride batteries, and lithium-ion batteries.
- The performance of a battery depends on several factors, such as the capacity (the amount of charge it can store), the energy density (the amount of energy it can deliver per unit mass or volume), the power density (the rate at which it can deliver energy), the shelf life (the time it can retain its charge when not in use), and the cycle life (the number of times it can be charged and discharged).
- Corrosion is the deterioration of a metal or an alloy due to its reaction with the environment. It is an electrochemical process that involves the formation of anodic and cathodic sites on the metal surface, where oxidation and reduction occur, respectively.
- The rate and extent of corrosion depend on several factors, such as the nature and composition of the metal, the nature and concentration of the corrosive agents, the temperature, the humidity, the presence of oxygen, and the presence of inhibitors or coatings.
- Some common types of corrosion are uniform corrosion, galvanic corrosion, pitting corrosion, crevice corrosion, intergranular corrosion, stress corrosion cracking, and corrosion fatigue.
- Corrosion can cause serious problems in various industries and applications, such as pipelines, bridges, buildings, vehicles, aircraft, ships, and biomedical implants. Therefore, it is important to prevent or control corrosion by using various methods, such as material selection, alloying, cathodic protection, anodic protection, corrosion inhibitors, coatings, and corrosion monitoring.



# Chemistry of Engineering Materials: Cement

Cement is a hydraulic binder, which means that it sets and hardens by reacting with water. It is used as a main component of mortar and concrete, which are mixtures of cement with sand, gravel, or other aggregates.

## Composition of Cement

The most common type of cement is Portland cement, which is made from four main compounds:

- Tricalcium silicate (3CaO · SiO2), which is responsible for the early strength development of cement.
- Dicalcium silicate (2CaO · SiO2), which contributes to the long-term strength and durability of cement.
- Tricalcium aluminate (3CaO · Al2O3), which reacts rapidly with water and causes heat generation and shrinkage of cement.
- Tetracalcium aluminoferrite (4CaO · Al2O3Fe2O3), which influences the color and resistance to sulfates of cement.

These compounds are formed by heating a mixture of limestone (calcium carbonate) and clay (alumino-silicates) in a rotary kiln at about 1450°C. The resulting clinker is then ground with a small amount of gypsum (calcium sulfate) to regulate the setting time of cement.

## Hydration of Cement

When cement is mixed with water, a complex chemical reaction called hydration takes place, which involves the formation of various products that fill the space between the cement particles and the aggregates. The main hydration products are:

- Calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H), which is a gel-like substance that provides strength and cohesion to the cement paste.
- Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2), which is a crystalline compound that can react with carbon dioxide in the air and form calcium carbonate, reducing the alkalinity of the cement paste.
- Calcium aluminate hydrate (C-A-H), which is a group of compounds that can form different phases depending on the temperature and the presence of sulfates and other ions in the water.

The hydration process is influenced by many factors, such as the water-cement ratio, the curing temperature and humidity, the type and amount of additives, and the age of the cement. The hydration process is usually divided into five stages:

- Initial stage: The cement particles start to dissolve in water and form a thin layer of hydration products around them. The mixture is still fluid and workable.
- Induction stage: The hydration rate slows down and the mixture becomes stiff and loses its workability. This is also called the dormant period or the initial setting time of cement.
- Acceleration stage: The hydration rate increases rapidly and the C-S-H and C-A-H start to form and interlock, creating a solid network. This is also called the final setting time of cement.
- Deceleration stage: The hydration rate decreases and the strength and durability of the cement paste continue to develop over time.
- Steady state stage: The hydration rate reaches a minimum and the cement paste reaches its final properties. This stage can last for years or even decades.

## Properties of Cement

The properties of cement are determined by its composition, hydration, and interaction with the aggregates and the environment. Some of the important properties of cement are:

- Fineness: The degree of grinding of the cement particles, which affects the rate of hydration, the water demand, and the strength of the cement paste.
- Setting time: The time required for the cement paste to lose its workability and reach a certain degree of hardness, which depends on the type and amount of gypsum and other additives.
- Soundness: The ability of the cement paste to retain its volume and shape without cracking or disintegrating, which depends on the stability of the hydration products and the resistance to sulfates and other harmful agents.
- Strength: The resistance of the cement paste to compressive, tensile, and flexural forces, which depends on the water-cement ratio, the curing conditions, and the type and amount of C-S-H and C-A-H.
- Durability: The ability of the cement paste to withstand the effects of weathering, chemical attack, abrasion, and corrosion, which depends on the permeability, porosity, and alkalinity of the cement paste and the type and amount of C-S-H and C-A-H.

# Water and Boiler Troubles Used in Industry

Water is a vital medium for many industrial and commercial processes, such as boilers, cooling towers, cooling systems and other closed circuit systems. However, water can also cause various problems in these systems, such as corrosion, scaling, fouling, and microbiological growth. These problems can affect the efficiency, safety, and reliability of the equipment, and may require costly repairs or replacements. Therefore, water treatment and quality control are essential for preventing and solving these problems.

## Boiler Troubles

A boiler is a device that converts water into steam by heating it. Steam is used for various purposes, such as power generation, heating, and industrial processes. However, boilers can encounter several problems that can impair their performance or cause damage to the system. Some of the common boiler troubles are:

- Priming: This is the phenomenon of water droplets being carried away with the steam, resulting in wet and low-quality steam. Priming can be caused by high water level, sudden changes in steam demand, improper boiler design, or impurities in the water. Priming can lead to water hammer, erosion, corrosion, and loss of heat transfer.
- Foaming: This is the formation of a layer of froth or stable foam on the surface of the water, which reduces the steam quality and increases the water level. Foaming can be caused by high concentration of dissolved solids, organic matter, or oil in the water, or by low water hardness or alkalinity. Foaming can result in priming, carryover, corrosion, and overheating.
- Scaling: This is the deposition of hard and insoluble substances, such as calcium carbonate, magnesium carbonate, or iron oxide, on the inner surfaces of the boiler tubes or walls. Scaling can be caused by high water hardness, high temperature, or low pH. Scaling can reduce the heat transfer, increase the fuel consumption, cause overheating, and damage the boiler metal.
- Corrosion: This is the deterioration of the boiler metal due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with the water, steam, or air. Corrosion can be caused by low pH, dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, chloride, sulfate, or other corrosive agents in the water or steam, or by galvanic action between different metals. Corrosion can weaken the boiler metal, cause leaks, cracks, or holes, and contaminate the steam.
- Carryover: This is the entrainment of water droplets or solids in the steam, which can be caused by priming, foaming, or high steam velocity. Carryover can affect the steam quality, cause corrosion, erosion, or deposition in the downstream equipment, and damage the turbine blades or valves.

## Boiler Water Treatment

Boiler water treatment is the process of removing or controlling the impurities and conditions that cause boiler troubles. Boiler water treatment can include:

- External treatment: This is the treatment of the feed water before it enters the boiler, such as softening, deaeration, filtration, or chemical addition. External treatment can prevent or reduce scaling, corrosion, and carryover.
- Internal treatment: This is the treatment of the water inside the boiler, such as pH adjustment, oxygen scavenging, sludge conditioning, or chemical addition. Internal treatment can prevent or reduce scaling, corrosion, foaming, and priming.
- Blowdown: This is the periodic or continuous removal of a portion of the boiler water, which contains high concentration of dissolved solids, impurities, or sludge. Blowdown can control the water quality, prevent scaling, and reduce corrosion.
- Monitoring and testing: This is the regular measurement and analysis of the boiler water and steam parameters, such as pH, hardness, alkalinity, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, chloride, sulfate, silica, iron, etc. Monitoring and testing can help to evaluate the effectiveness of the water treatment, detect any problems, and adjust the treatment accordingly.

# Polymer Composites

- A polymer composite is a multi-phase material in which reinforcing fillers are integrated with a polymer matrix, resulting in synergistic mechanical properties that cannot be achieved from either component alone.
- A polymer is a natural or synthetic substance composed of very large molecules, called macromolecules, that are multiples of simpler chemical units called monomers.
- A polymer composite can be classified according to the type of polymer matrix, the type of reinforcement, the shape and size of reinforcement, and the processing method.
- The most common types of polymer matrices are thermosets and thermoplastics. - The most common types of reinforcements are fibers, particles, and flakes. Fibers are long and slender materials that have high strength and stiffness along their length. - The most common shapes and sizes of reinforcements are continuous and discontinuous. Discontinuous reinforcements are short and broken fibers, particles, or flakes that are randomly distributed in the polymer matrix, providing isotropic properties and better processability.
- The most common processing methods are molding, extrusion, injection, and pultrusion. Molding is a process of shaping the composite by applying heat and pressure to a mold that contains the polymer and reinforcement. Extrusion is a process of forcing the composite through a die that has a desired cross-sectional shape. Injection is a process of injecting the molten composite into a mold cavity that has a desired shape. Pultrusion is a process of pulling the continuous fiber reinforcement through a resin bath and a heated die that has a desired cross-sectional shape.

# Magnetic Characteristics and Numerical Problems

## Magnetic Fields and Field Lines

- A magnetic field is a region of space where a magnetic force can be exerted on a moving charge or a magnet.
- A magnetic field can be represented by magnetic field lines, which are imaginary curves that show the direction and strength of the magnetic field at each point.
- The direction of the magnetic field at a point is given by the tangent to the field line at that point.
- The strength of the magnetic field at a point is proportional to the density of the field lines at that point. The closer the field lines are, the stronger the field is.
- Magnetic field lines have the following properties :
  - They originate from the north pole of a magnet and terminate at the south pole of a magnet.
  - They never cross or intersect each other.
  - They form closed loops around a current-carrying wire or a solenoid.
  - They are perpendicular to the surface of a magnetic material.

## Magnetic Circuits and Magnetic Materials

- A magnetic circuit is a closed path of magnetic flux, which is the measure of the quantity of magnetism in a region of space.
- A magnetic circuit can be composed of different magnetic materials, such as air, iron, steel, etc., which have different magnetic properties, such as permeability, reluctance, magnetization, etc.
- The permeability of a material is the measure of how easily it allows magnetic flux to pass through it. It is the ratio of the magnetic flux density (B) to the magnetic field intensity (H) in the material. # Nanomaterials, Carbon Nanotubes (CNT)

- Nanomaterials are materials that have at least one dimension in the nanometer range (1-100 nm).
- Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are a type of nanomaterial that consist of cylindrical sheets of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice.
- CNTs can be classified into single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) depending on the number of layers of carbon sheets.
- CNTs have remarkable properties such as high strength, thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, and optical activity.
- CNTs have potential applications in various fields such as electronics, nanomedicine, energy, and composite materials.

## Single-Walled Carbon Nanotubes (SWCNTs)

- SWCNTs are formed by rolling up a single sheet of graphene into a seamless cylinder.
- SWCNTs have diameters in the range of 0.4-2 nm and lengths up to several micrometers.
- SWCNTs can have different chiralities, which are determined by the direction and angle of rolling the graphene sheet. Chirality affects the electronic structure and optical properties of SWCNTs.
- SWCNTs can be either metallic or semiconducting depending on their chirality. Metallic SWCNTs have zero band gap and high electrical conductivity, while semiconducting SWCNTs have a finite band gap and tunable electrical conductivity.
- SWCNTs can exhibit quantum effects such as ballistic transport, Aharonov-Bohm effect, and Coulomb blockade due to their small size and low dimensionality.

## Multi-Walled Carbon Nanotubes (MWCNTs)

- MWCNTs are formed by stacking multiple layers of graphene sheets into concentric cylinders.
- MWCNTs have diameters in the range of 2-100 nm and lengths up to several millimeters.
- MWCNTs can have different structures, such as parallel, helical, or random, depending on the relative orientation of the graphene layers. Structure affects the mechanical and thermal properties of MWCNTs.
- MWCNTs have higher strength and stiffness than SWCNTs due to the interlayer interactions. MWCNTs can also withstand higher temperatures and pressures than SWCNTs.
- MWCNTs have lower electrical conductivity and optical activity than SWCNTs due to the scattering and interference of electrons and photons between the layers.

## Applications of CNTs

- CNTs have been used as nanoscale wires, transistors, sensors, electrodes, and interconnects in nanoelectronics and nanophotonics.
- CNTs have been used as drug delivery vehicles, biosensors, imaging agents, and tissue scaffolds in nanomedicine and biotechnology.
- CNTs have been used as catalysts, electrodes, and membranes in energy conversion and storage devices such as fuel cells, batteries, and supercapacitors.
- CNTs have been used as fillers, reinforcements, and coatings in composite materials to improve their mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties.

### Green Chemistry: Introduction, 12 principles and importance of green Synthesis, Green

Green chemistry is an area of chemistry and chemical engineering that aims to design products and processes that minimize or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances . Green chemistry applies across the life cycle of a chemical product, from its design, manufacture, use, and disposal. Green chemistry can also be measured by various metrics, such as mass, energy, toxicity, and environmental impact.

The 12 principles of green chemistry are a set of guidelines that help chemists and engineers to practice green chemistry. They are :

1. Prevention: It is better to prevent waste than to treat or clean up waste after it is formed.
2. Atom economy: Synthetic methods should be designed to maximize the incorporation of all materials used in the process into the final product.
3. Less hazardous chemical syntheses: Wherever practicable, synthetic methods should be designed to use and generate substances that possess little or no toxicity to human health and the environment.
4. Designing safer chemicals: Chemical products should be designed to affect their desired function while minimizing their toxicity.
5. Safer solvents and auxiliaries: The use of auxiliary substances (e.g., solvents, separation agents, etc.) should be made unnecessary wherever possible and innocuous when used.
6. Design for energy efficiency: Energy requirements of chemical processes should be recognized for their environmental and economic impacts and should be minimized. If possible, synthetic methods should be conducted at ambient temperature and pressure.
7. Use of renewable feedstocks: A raw material or feedstock should be renewable rather than depleting whenever technically and economically practicable.
8. Reduce derivatives: Unnecessary derivatization (use of blocking groups, protection/ deprotection, temporary modification of physical/chemical processes) should be minimized or avoided if possible, because such steps require additional reagents and can generate waste.
9. Catalysis: Catalytic reagents (as selective as possible) are superior to stoichiometric reagents.
10. Design for degradation: Chemical products should be designed so that at the end of their function they break down into innocuous degradation products and do not persist in the environment.
11. Real-time analysis for pollution prevention: Analytical methodologies need to be further developed to allow for real-time, in-process monitoring and control prior to the formation of hazardous substances.
12. Inherently safer chemistry for accident prevention: Substances and the form of a substance used in a chemical process should be chosen to minimize the potential for chemical accidents, including releases, explosions, and fires.

- The use of nanomaterials, such as nanoparticles, nanotubes, and nanosheets, to enhance the catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability of catalysts, as well as to enable new applications in sensing, imaging, and drug delivery.

# Isomerism and Chiral Drugs

- Isomers can be classified into two main types: constitutional isomers and stereoisomers.
- Constitutional isomers have different connectivity of atoms, while stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different orientation in space.
- Stereoisomers can be further divided into two subtypes: geometric isomers and optical isomers.
- Geometric isomers have different arrangement of atoms around a double bond or a ring, while optical isomers have different arrangement of atoms around a chiral center.
- A chiral center is an atom, usually carbon, that is bonded to four different groups. A chiral center can give rise to two mirror-image forms of the same molecule, called enantiomers.
- Enantiomers have identical physical and chemical properties, except for their interaction with plane-polarized light and other chiral molecules.
- A mixture of equal amounts of two enantiomers is called a racemic mixture or a racemate. A racemic mixture is optically inactive, meaning it does not rotate plane-polarized light.
- A chiral drug is a drug that contains one or more chiral centers. A chiral drug can exist as a single enantiomer or a racemic mixture, depending on the synthesis and purification methods.
- The stereoisomeric composition of a chiral drug can have significant implications for its pharmacological, toxicological, and clinical effects, as different enantiomers may have different interactions with biological targets, such as receptors, enzymes, and transporters.
- For example, ibuprofen, a common painkiller, is a chiral drug that is sold as a racemic mixture. However, only the S-enantiomer is responsible for the anti-inflammatory activity, while the R-enantiomer is inactive or even harmful.
- Therefore, it is important to know and control the stereoisomeric composition of chiral drugs, as it can affect their efficacy, safety, and quality. The FDA has issued guidelines for the development of new stereoisomeric drugs, which recommend the use of single enantiomers whenever possible, or the justification of the use of racemic mixtures.

### Electrochemistry and Batteries: Basic concepts of electrochemistry

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. Electrochemical reactions involve the transfer of electrons between two substances, called electrodes, that are immersed in a solution, called electrolyte, that can conduct electricity.

Electrochemical cells are devices that can convert chemical energy into electrical energy or vice versa. There are two main types of electrochemical cells: galvanic cells and electrolytic cells.

- Galvanic cells, also known as voltaic cells, are electrochemical cells in which spontaneous oxidation-reduction reactions produce electrical energy. In a galvanic cell, the oxidation reaction occurs at the anode (the negative electrode) and the reduction reaction occurs at the cathode (the positive electrode). The electrons flow from the anode to the cathode through an external circuit, which can power a device. The electrolyte allows the movement of ions to maintain charge balance in the cell.
- Electrolytic cells are electrochemical cells in which electrical energy is used to drive non-spontaneous oxidation-reduction reactions. In an electrolytic cell, the oxidation reaction occurs at the anode (the positive electrode) and the reduction reaction occurs at the cathode (the negative electrode). The electrons flow from the external power source to the cathode and from the anode to the power source. The electrolyte allows the movement of ions to complete the circuit.

Batteries are galvanic cells, or a series of cells, that produce an electric current. There are two basic types of batteries: primary and secondary.

- Primary batteries are “single use” and cannot be recharged. They are made of electrochemical cells that have irreversible reactions. Once the reactants are consumed, the battery stops working. Examples of primary batteries are alkaline batteries, zinc-carbon batteries, and lithium batteries.
- Secondary batteries are “rechargeable” and can be used multiple times. They are made of electrochemical cells that have reversible reactions. When the battery is connected to an external power source, the reactions are reversed and the battery is restored to its original state. Examples of secondary batteries are lead-acid batteries, nickel-cadmium batteries, and lithium-ion batteries.

The performance of a battery depends on several factors, such as the type of electrodes, the type of electrolyte, the temperature, and the rate of discharge. Some important properties of batteries are:

- Voltage: The potential difference between the two electrodes of a battery, measured in volts. It indicates how much electrical energy is available per unit charge. The voltage of a battery depends on the electrochemical reactions and the concentration of the electrolyte.
- Capacity: The amount of electric charge that a battery can deliver, measured in ampere-hours (Ah). It indicates how long a battery can operate at a given current. The capacity of a battery depends on the amount of reactants and the rate of discharge.
- Energy density: The amount of energy that a battery can store per unit mass or volume, measured in watt-hours per kilogram (Wh/kg) or watt-hours per liter (Wh/L). It indicates how compact and lightweight a battery is. The energy density of a battery depends on the type of electrodes and the type of electrolyte.
- Power density: The amount of power that a battery can deliver per unit mass or volume, measured in watts per kilogram (W/kg) or watts per liter (W/L). It indicates how fast a battery can deliver energy. The power density of a battery depends on the internal resistance and the rate of discharge.

Batteries have many applications in various fields, such as electronics, transportation, communication, medicine, and renewable energy. Batteries can provide portable and reliable power sources for devices and vehicles. Batteries can also store excess energy from renewable sources, such as solar and wind, and supply it when needed.



### Batteries; Classification and applications of Primary Cells (Dry Cell) and Secondary Cells

Batteries are devices that store chemical energy and convert it into electrical energy. They consist of one or more electrochemical cells that are connected in series or parallel. Each cell has a positive and a negative electrode, and an electrolyte that allows the flow of ions between them. When the electrodes are connected to an external circuit, a chemical reaction occurs that produces an electric current.

Batteries can be classified into two main types based on their ability to be recharged:

- **Primary cells** are non-rechargeable batteries that can only be used once. They are also called dry cells because they have a solid or paste-like electrolyte that prevents leakage. Primary cells are usually cheaper and have a higher energy density than secondary cells, but they have a limited shelf life and capacity. Primary cells are commonly used in household appliances, such as torches, clocks, cameras, and remote controls. Some examples of primary cells are:

  - **Zinc-carbon cells** have a zinc anode and a carbon rod cathode, surrounded by a paste of manganese dioxide and ammonium chloride. They have a nominal voltage of 1.5 V and a low current output. They are suitable for low-power devices, such as radios, toys, and flashlights.

  - **Alkaline cells** have a zinc anode and a manganese dioxide cathode, immersed in a potassium hydroxide electrolyte. They have a nominal voltage of 1.5 V and a higher current output than zinc-carbon cells. They are suitable for high-power devices, such as digital cameras, portable audio players, and electronic games.

  - **Lithium cells** have a lithium metal anode and a variety of cathode materials, such as manganese dioxide, thionyl chloride, or iodine. They have a nominal voltage of 3 V and a very high energy density. They are suitable for long-lasting devices, such as pacemakers, watches, and memory backup systems.

- **Secondary cells** are rechargeable batteries that can be used multiple times. They are also called storage cells because they can store electrical energy and release it when needed. Secondary cells are usually more expensive and have a lower energy density than primary cells, but they have a longer shelf life and capacity. Secondary cells are commonly used in vehicles, industrial applications, and portable devices. Some examples of secondary cells are:

  - **Lead-acid cells** have a lead anode and a lead dioxide cathode, immersed in a sulfuric acid electrolyte. They have a nominal voltage of 2 V and a high current output. They are suitable for starting, lighting, and ignition (SLI) systems in cars, motorcycles, and trucks.

  - **Nickel-cadmium cells** have a nickel oxide hydroxide cathode and a cadmium anode, immersed in a potassium hydroxide electrolyte. They have a nominal voltage of 1.2 V and a moderate current output. They are suitable for power tools, emergency lighting, and cordless phones.

  - **Nickel-metal hydride cells** have a nickel oxide hydroxide cathode and a metal hydride anode, immersed in a potassium hydroxide electrolyte. They have a nominal voltage of 1.2 V and a higher current output than nickel-cadmium cells. They are suitable for laptops, mobile phones, and hybrid electric vehicles.

  - **Lithium-ion cells** have a lithium cobalt oxide cathode and a graphite anode, immersed in a lithium salt electrolyte. They have a nominal voltage of 3.6 V and a very high energy density. They are suitable for smartphones, tablets, and electric vehicles.



# Lead Acid Battery

A lead acid battery is a type of rechargeable battery that was first invented in 1859 by French physicist Gaston Planté. It is the oldest type of rechargeable battery and has a relatively low energy density compared to modern batteries.

## Structure and Operation

A lead acid battery consists of two electrodes submerged in an electrolyte of sulfuric acid. The positive electrode is made of grains of metallic lead oxide, while the negative electrode is attached to a grid of metallic lead. The electrodes are separated by a porous separator that prevents short circuits.

When the battery is charged, an electric current is applied to the electrodes, causing the lead oxide to be reduced to lead and the lead to be oxidized to lead sulfate. The sulfuric acid is also consumed and water is produced. The chemical reactions are as follows:

Positive electrode: PbO2 + 4H+ + SO42- + 2e- → PbSO4 + 2H2O

Negative electrode: Pb + SO42- → PbSO4 + 2e-

Overall: PbO2 + Pb + 2H2SO4 → 2PbSO4 + 2H2O

When the battery is discharged, the reverse reactions occur, producing electricity and restoring the original state of the electrodes and the electrolyte.

## Types and Applications

Lead acid batteries are classified into two types: flooded and valve-regulated. Flooded batteries have excess electrolyte that covers the electrodes and allows gas to escape during charging and discharging. Valve-regulated batteries have a sealed case and a pressure relief valve that prevents gas from escaping and maintains a constant pressure inside the battery. Valve-regulated batteries are also known as sealed lead acid (SLA) or maintenance-free batteries.

Lead acid batteries are widely used in various applications, such as:

- Uninterrupted power modules, electric grid, and automotive applications, including all hybrid and lithium-ion battery-powered vehicles, as an independent 12-V supply to support starting, lighting, and ignition modules, as well as critical systems, under cold conditions and in the event of a high-voltage battery failure.
- Solar energy systems, as a low-cost and reliable option for storing excess electricity generated by photovoltaic panels.
- Backup power for telecommunication, emergency lighting, security, and medical equipment.

## Advantages and Disadvantages

Some of the advantages of lead acid batteries are:

- Low cost and easy availability
- High power output and good performance at low temperatures
- Long lifespan and durability if properly maintained
- High recyclability and environmental friendliness

Some of the disadvantages of lead acid batteries are:

- Low energy density and heavy weight
- High self-discharge rate and sulfation if left unused or undercharged
- Corrosion and leakage of electrolyte if overcharged or damaged
- Limited cycle life and capacity loss at high temperatures



### Corrosion: Introduction to corrosion, Types of corrosion, Cause of corrosion, Corrosion

- Corrosion is the deterioration of a material, usually a metal, due to a chemical or electrochemical reaction with its environment.
- Corrosion can cause loss of material, mechanical strength, functionality, and aesthetic value of the metal.
- Corrosion can also pose safety, health, and environmental hazards if not prevented or controlled.
- Corrosion can be classified into different types based on the mechanism, morphology, environment, or affected material. Some common types of corrosion are:
  - Uniform corrosion: The metal surface corrodes at a uniform rate over the entire exposed area. This type of corrosion is predictable and easy to measure.
  - Galvanic corrosion: Two dissimilar metals are in contact with each other and an electrolyte, creating a galvanic cell. The more active metal (anode) corrodes faster than the less active metal (cathode).
  - Pitting corrosion: The metal surface corrodes at localized spots, forming pits or holes. This type of corrosion is difficult to detect and can cause perforation of the metal.
  - Crevice corrosion: The metal surface corrodes at narrow gaps or crevices where stagnant solution accumulates. This type of corrosion is influenced by the geometry, size, and chemistry of the crevice.
  - Intergranular corrosion: The metal surface corrodes along the grain boundaries, leaving the grains intact. This type of corrosion is caused by the segregation of impurities or alloying elements at the grain boundaries.
  - Stress corrosion cracking: The metal surface cracks under the combined action of tensile stress and a corrosive environment. This type of corrosion is influenced by the stress level, material, and environment.
  - Corrosion fatigue: The metal surface cracks under the combined action of cyclic stress and a corrosive environment. This type of corrosion is influenced by the stress amplitude, frequency, material, and environment.
  - Erosion corrosion: The metal surface corrodes due to the mechanical abrasion of a fluid or solid particles. This type of corrosion is influenced by the velocity, direction, and hardness of the fluid or particles.
  - Microbiologically influenced corrosion: The metal surface corrodes due to the metabolic activity of microorganisms, such as bacteria, fungi, or algae. This type of corrosion is influenced by the type, concentration, and diversity of the microorganisms.
- The cause of corrosion is the thermodynamic tendency of metals to revert to their natural state, which is usually an oxide, hydroxide, or sulfide. Metals are extracted from their ores by applying energy, such as heat or electricity. When metals are exposed to a corrosive environment, they tend to release this energy and return to their lower energy state, which is more stable.
- Corrosion can be prevented or controlled by various methods, such as:
  - Material selection: Choosing a metal or alloy that is resistant to corrosion in a given environment, or using non-metallic materials, such as plastics or ceramics.
  - Coating: Applying a protective layer of paint, metal, or polymer on the metal surface to isolate it from the corrosive environment.
  - Cathodic protection: Applying an external electric current or a sacrificial anode to the metal surface to make it the cathode of a galvanic cell, thus preventing its corrosion.
  - Corrosion inhibitors: Adding chemicals to the corrosive environment to reduce the corrosion rate of the metal surface, either by forming a protective film, reducing the aggressiveness of the environment, or interfering with the corrosion reaction.
  - Design: Avoiding crevices, sharp corners, stress concentrations, and dissimilar metals in the metal structure to reduce the susceptibility to corrosion.
  - Maintenance: Inspecting, cleaning, repairing, and replacing the metal components to prevent or mitigate corrosion damage.

# Green Chemistry

Green chemistry is the approach in chemical sciences that efficiently uses renewable raw materials, eliminating waste and avoiding the use of toxic and hazardous reagents and solvents in the manufacture and application of chemical products.

## Principles of Green Chemistry

There are twelve basic principles of green chemistry that can be grouped into “Reducing Risk” and “Minimizing the Environmental Footprint”.

### Reducing Risk

- **Prevention**: It is better to prevent waste than to treat or clean up waste after it has been created.
- **Atom Economy**: Synthetic methods should be designed to maximize the incorporation of all materials used in the process into the final product.
- **Less Hazardous Chemical Syntheses**: Wherever practicable, synthetic methods should be designed to use and generate substances that possess little or no toxicity to human health and the environment.
- **Safer Chemicals**: Chemical products should be designed to affect their desired function while minimizing their toxicity.
- **Safer Solvents and Auxiliaries**: The use of auxiliary substances (e.g., solvents, separation agents, etc.) should be made unnecessary wherever possible and innocuous when used.
- **Design for Energy Efficiency**: Energy requirements of chemical processes should be recognized for their environmental and economic impacts and should be minimized. If possible, synthetic methods should be conducted at ambient temperature and pressure.

### Minimizing the Environmental Footprint

- **Use of Renewable Feedstocks**: A raw material or feedstock should be renewable rather than depleting whenever technically and economically practicable.
- **Reduce Derivatives**: Unnecessary derivatization (use of blocking groups, protection/ deprotection, temporary modification of physical/chemical processes) should be minimized or avoided if possible, because such steps require additional reagents and can generate waste.
- **Catalysis**: Catalytic reagents (as selective as possible) are superior to stoichiometric reagents.
- **Design for Degradation**: Chemical products should be designed so that at the end of their function they break down into innocuous degradation products and do not persist in the environment.
- **Real-time Analysis for Pollution Prevention**: Analytical methodologies need to be further developed to allow for real-time, in-process monitoring and control prior to the formation of hazardous substances.
- **Inherently Safer Chemistry for Accident Prevention**: Substances and the form of a substance used in a chemical process should be chosen to minimize the potential for chemical accidents, including releases, explosions, and fires.

## Examples of Green Chemistry

Some examples of green chemistry applications are:

- The use of enzymes as catalysts to perform selective reactions, such as the synthesis of antibiotics, vitamins, and flavors.
- The use of supercritical carbon dioxide as a solvent for dry cleaning, extraction, and polymerization, instead of using toxic and flammable organic solvents.
- The use of ionic liquids as solvents for organic synthesis, separation, and electrochemistry, instead of using volatile and hazardous organic solvents.
- The use of biodegradable polymers derived from renewable sources, such as starch, cellulose, and lactic acid, instead of using petroleum-based plastics.
- The use of microwave irradiation to accelerate chemical reactions, reducing the reaction time and energy consumption.

## Importance of Green Chemistry

Green chemistry is important for several reasons, such as:

- It reduces the environmental impact of chemical processes and products, by minimizing waste, pollution, and toxicity.
- It improves the safety and health of workers, consumers, and communities, by avoiding the use and generation of hazardous substances.
- It enhances the economic competitiveness and innovation of the chemical industry, by reducing the costs of raw materials, energy, waste management, and regulatory compliance.
- It promotes the sustainable development and use of natural resources, by using renewable feedstocks, energy-efficient processes, and biodegradable products.
- It fosters the education and awareness of the principles and practices of green chemistry among students, researchers, and professionals in the chemical sciences and related fields.

# Stereochemistry

Stereochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms and molecules and the effect of this on chemical reactions . Stereochemistry focuses on the relationships between stereoisomers, which are molecules that have the same molecular formula and sequence of bonded atoms, but differ in the orientation of their atoms in space.

Some of the main topics in stereochemistry are:

- **Chirality**: The property of a molecule that makes it non-superimposable on its mirror image. A molecule that has chirality is called a chiral molecule. A molecule that does not have chirality is called an achiral molecule. Chirality can arise from the presence of an asymmetric carbon atom, which is a carbon atom that is bonded to four different groups, or from other structural features such as helical or cyclic shapes .
- **Enantiomers**: A pair of stereoisomers that are mirror images of each other and non-superimposable. Enantiomers have the same physical and chemical properties, except for their interaction with plane-polarized light and other chiral molecules. Enantiomers are also called optical isomers, because they can rotate the plane of polarized light in opposite directions. The rotation of plane-polarized light by a chiral molecule is called optical activity .
- **Diastereomers**: A pair of stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other and non-superimposable. Diastereomers have different physical and chemical properties, such as melting point, boiling point, solubility, and reactivity. Diastereomers can be further classified into different types, such as cis-trans isomers, which differ in the arrangement of groups around a double bond or a ring, and conformational isomers, which differ in the rotation of groups around a single bond .
- **Stereochemical configuration**: The specification of the spatial arrangement of atoms and groups in a molecule. Stereochemical configuration can be represented by different notations, such as the R/S system, which assigns a priority to each group attached to a chiral center based on the atomic number and the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules, and the E/Z system, which assigns a priority to each group attached to a double bond based on the same rules. Stereochemical configuration can also be represented by different projections, such as the Fischer projection, which shows the molecule as a flat cross with horizontal lines representing bonds pointing towards the viewer and vertical lines representing bonds pointing away from the viewer, and the Newman projection, which shows the molecule as a circle with a dot representing the front carbon atom and a circle representing the back carbon atom, and the groups attached to them as lines radiating from the center .
- **Stereochemical reaction**: A chemical reaction that involves the formation, breaking, or rearrangement of bonds between atoms in a way that affects the stereochemistry of the molecules. Stereochemical reactions can be classified into different types, such as inversion, which is a reaction that reverses the configuration of a chiral center, retention, which is a reaction that preserves the configuration of a chiral center, racemization, which is a reaction that converts a pure enantiomer into a racemic mixture of equal amounts of both enantiomers, and resolution, which is a reaction that separates a racemic mixture into its individual enantiomers .
