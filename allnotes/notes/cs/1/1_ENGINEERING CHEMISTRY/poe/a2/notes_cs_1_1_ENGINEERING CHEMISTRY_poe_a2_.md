# Stereochemistry

Stereochemistry is the study of the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms in molecules and their manipulation. It is useful for identifying compounds and elements.

## Identifying Enantiomers
Enantiomers are stereoisomers that are mirror images of each other and non-superimposable. They have centers of chirality and rotate polarized light in opposite directions. To identify enantiomers:

1. Locate the chiral center(s): Carbons bonded to four different atoms/groups.
2. Draw mirror image arrangements: Switch positions of groups around chiral center.
3. Check for superimposability: If mirror images cannot be overlapped, the molecules are enantiomers.

## Identifying Diastereomers
Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images and have centers of chirality or double bonds. To identify diastereomers:

1. Locate multiple chiral centers and/or double bonds.
2. Arrange groups in 3D space in different ways.
3. Check if arrangements are not mirror images and cannot be superimposed. If so, they are diastereomers.

## Identifying Elements Using Stereochemistry
The stereochemistry of a molecule can be used to identify elements present. For example:

- Tetrahedral carbons: C
- Flat trigonal planar: sp2 hybridized C, N, O
- Octahedral: sp3d2 hybridized C, Si, N, P, S
- Square planar: d8 configuration transition metals like Pt, Pd

# Polymer Composites

## What are Polymer Composites?

- Polymer composites are materials that consist of a polymer matrix and reinforcement materials such as fibers, particles or flakes.
- The polymer matrix acts as a host material that transfers load to the reinforcement material.
- The reinforcement materials absorb stresses and improve the mechanical properties such as strength, stiffness, impact resistance, wear resistance, etc. of the polymer.

## Types of Polymer Composites

- Fiber-reinforced polymer composites: Fibers such as glass, carbon or aramid are embedded in a polymer matrix. The fibers take up stresses and enhance strength and stiffness.
- Particle-filled polymer composites: Reinforcing particles such as talc, mica or glass microspheres are dispersed in a polymer matrix. The particles enhance properties such as dimensional stability and wear resistance.
- Flake-filled polymer composites: Reinforcing flakes such as glass or mica are oriented in a polymer matrix. The flakes increase properties such as impact resistance and tensile strength in a particular direction.

## Applications of Polymer Composites

- Automotive components such as drive shafts, leaf springs, bumpers, etc.
- Aircraft components such as radomes, fairings, tail sections, etc.
- Boat and ship components such as hulls, masts, etc.
- Sports goods such as golf clubs, bike frames, etc.
- Pipes, tanks and structural components due to high strength-to-weight ratio.

### Atomic and Molecular Structure: Molecular orbital’s of diatomic molecules, Bond Order,

1. Molecular orbitals are formed by the combination of atomic orbitals of the constituent atoms in a molecule. For homonuclear diatomic molecules like H2, N2, etc. having equal energy atomic orbitals, the molecular orbitals are formed by the linear combination of atomic orbitals.

2. The molecular orbitals so formed are filled with electrons in the increasing order of their energy. The bonding molecular orbitals have lower energy and are more stable than the anti-bonding molecular orbitals which have higher energy.

3. The bond order is defined as the difference between the number of bonding electrons and the number of anti-bonding electrons. Bond order is directly proportional to the stability and strength of the bond. A bond order of one corresponds to a single bond, two to a double bond and three to a triple bond.

4. In hydrogen molecule, the 1s atomic orbitals of the two atoms combine to form two molecular orbitals - bonding (lower energy) and antibonding (higher energy). The bonding molecular orbital is filled with two electrons resulting in a bond order of 1 and a stable H2 molecule.

5. In nitrogen molecule, each nitrogen atom contributes five electrons. The molecular orbitals formed are filled as: bonding1s2, bonding2s2, antibonding2s2. This results in a bond order of 3 and a triple bond between the two nitrogen atoms.

# Magnetic characters and numerical problems

1. Magnetic field: The space around a magnet within which magnetic force can be detected is called magnetic field. The magnetic field is represented by magnetic field lines. These lines indicate the direction of magnetic force acting on a north pole of a small magnet placed in the field.

2. Magnetic field due to a bar magnet: The magnetic field due to a bar magnet is in the form of magnetic field lines emerging from its north pole and merging into its south pole. The field is strongest near the poles and weakens as we move away from the magnet.

3. Right-hand thumb rule: The right-hand thumb rule is used to find the direction of magnetic field around a current carrying conductor. According to the rule, if we grasp the conductor in our right hand such that the thumb points in the direction of current, then the direction in which the fingers encircle the conductor gives the direction of magnetic field.

4. Force on a current carrying conductor: When a current carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field, it experiences a force. The magnitude of force depends upon the strength of magnetic field (B), current (I) passing through the conductor and the length (l) of the conductor placed in the magnetic field. The direction of force can be determined by the Fleming's left-hand rule.

5. Numerical problems: Numerical problems based on magnetic field and forces on current carrying conductors include calculating the magnitude and direction of magnetic field at different points due to a given magnet/solenoid, calculating the force experienced by a conductor carrying current in a magnetic field, etc. Step-by-step solutions can be used to solve such numerical problems.

### Green Chemistry: Introduction, 12 principles and importance of green Synthesis

Green Chemistry refers to the design of chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate the use or generation of hazardous substances. It focuses on the development of innovative chemical technologies that prevent pollution and minimize adverse health and environmental impacts of chemicals.

The 12 Principles of Green Chemistry are:

1. Prevent waste: Design chemical syntheses to prevent waste, leaving no waste to treat or clean up.
2. Design safer chemicals and products: Design chemical products to be fully effective, yet have little or no toxicity.
3. Design less hazardous chemical syntheses: Design syntheses to use and generate substances that have little or no toxicity to humans and the environment.
4. Use renewable feedstocks: Use raw materials and feedstocks that are renewable rather than depleting. Renewable feedstocks are often made from agricultural products or are the wastes of other processes; depleting feedstocks are made from fossil fuels (petroleum, natural gas, or coal) or mined.
5. Use catalysts, not stoichiometric reagents: Minimize waste by using catalytic reactions. Catalysts are used in small amounts and can carry out a reaction many times. They are preferable to stoichiometric reagents, which are used in excess and discarded.
6. Avoid chemical derivatives: Avoid using blocking groups or protection/ deprotection in synthesis whenever possible. Derivatives use additional reagents and generate waste.
7. Maximize atom economy: Design syntheses to maximize the incorporation of all materials used in the process into the final product.
8. Use safer solvents and separation processes: Avoid using volatile organic compounds (VOCs) as solvents. Use or develop separation processes that recycle solvents and reagents.
9. Increase energy efficiency: Run chemical reactions at ambient temperature and pressure whenever possible. Minimize the need for auxiliary energy consumption.

### Batteries; Classification and applications of Primary Cells (Dry Cell) and Secondary Cells.

# Lead Acid battery

- Lead acid batteries consist of lead plates immersed in an electrolyte of sulfuric acid and water.
- When the battery is charging, the lead plates react with the sulfuric acid to form lead sulfate and electrons. The electrons flow out of the battery to power a device.
- When the battery is in use, the lead sulfate decomposes back into lead and sulfuric acid, and the electrons flow from the device back into the battery.
- The sulfuric acid is the conductive electrolyte that transports the electrons between the lead plates. But the acid is not used up in the process. It is regenerated during both charge and discharge cycles.
- The battery capacity depends on the amount of lead used in the plates. The more lead provided, the more electrons can be produced and the more energy can be stored.
- However, the lead plates can deteriorate over many charge-discharge cycles, resulting in battery degradation and eventual failure. The electrolyte can also break down over time due to high temperatures.
- To maximize battery lifespan, slow charge and discharge rates should be used and high temperatures should be avoided. Lead acid batteries should also be fully charged before long term storage.
- Due to the chemical reactions involved, lead acid batteries have relatively low energy densities compared to other rechargeable battery types. But they are inexpensive to manufacture and are very tolerant of abuse like overcharging or short circuiting. This makes them well suited to applications like car batteries where cost and safety are more important than energy density.

### Corrosion: Introduction to corrosion, Types of corrosion, Cause of corrosion, Corrosion

1. Introduction to Corrosion: Corrosion is the gradual destruction of materials by chemical reaction with their environment. Almost all metals can suffer from corrosion. Corrosion causes significant material damage or loss to materials. As metals corrode, the mechanical properties are degraded until the structure fails.

2. Types of Corrosion: The most common types of corrosion are:
- Uniform corrosion: Corrosion occurs uniformly across the surface of the metal. Rusting of iron is a common example of uniform corrosion.
- Galvanic corrosion: Two dissimilar metals in contact with each other undergo corrosion. The less noble metal acts as the anode and corrodes faster.
- Pitting corrosion: Corrosion occurs in the form of pits or holes in the metal surface. Pitting can penetrate deep into the metal and cause failure.
- Crevice corrosion: Corrosion occurs in confined spaces or crevices where the electrolyte is stagnant. Crevices create localized corrosion cells leading to rapid metal loss.
- Stress corrosion cracking: Corrosion combined with tensile stress leads to cracking of metals. Certain metals are susceptible to SCC in specific environments.

3. Cause of Corrosion: The three conditions required for corrosion are:
1. Presence of an anode (metal that corrodes): The anode in a corrosion cell is the metal that corrodes.
2. Presence of an electrolyte: An electrolyte is required to conduct electric current between the anode and cathode. Electrolytes can be acids, bases or salts in aqueous solutions or humid environments.
3. Presence of a cathode (metal that does not corrode): The cathode is the other metal surface that completes the circuit. It does not corrode and can be the same or a different metal as the anode.

4. Prevention of Corrosion: Corrosion can be prevented or mitigated using methods like:
- Material selection: Choose corrosion resistant metals or alloys.
- Coatings: Use protective coatings such as paints or other barrier layers.
- Cathodic protection: Supply external electric current to make the structure a cathode and reduce corrosion.
- Chemical addition: Use corrosion inhibitors or antioxidants that reduce the corrosion rate.
- Control environment: Control moisture, oxygen, acidity, and other factors that accelerate corrosion.

# Prevention and Control of Corrosion

1.  Using corrosion resistant materials: Materials like stainless steel, plastics, ceramics and anodized aluminum can be used in applications where corrosion resistance is important. These materials either have sufficient passivation or lack of reactivity to resist corrosion.

3.  Cathodic protection: This method is used to protect underground metallic structures like pipelines and storage tanks. It involves making the metal to be protected as a cathode of an electrochemical cell. Due to this, the metal gets polarized and the rate of corrosion is reduced. 4.  Chemical treatment: Certain chemicals can be used to create a protective film on metals to enhance their corrosion resistance. Some examples are chromate treatment for aluminum, phosphating of steel, etc. These chemicals convert the metal surface into a passive state.
5.  Design modifications: The corrosion rate can be reduced by making design changes like avoiding crevices where moisture and salts can accumulate, providing good ventilation and drainage, using threaded joints instead of welded joints, etc. These changes minimize corrosion by eliminating conditions that aid corrosion.
6.  Impressing current: By impressing an external current, the metal can be made a cathode and the corrosion rate can be reduced. # Values, Gross & Net calorific value, Determination of calorific value by Bomb Calorimeter,

### Gross calorific value:
- It is the total amount of heat released by complete combustion of unit mass of fuel.
- It includes the latent heat of vaporization of water formed during combustion.
- It is also known as higher heating value (HHV).

### Net calorific value:
- It is the amount of heat released by complete combustion of unit mass of fuel at constant pressure which is available for heating the system.
- It excludes the latent heat of vaporization of water formed during combustion.
- It is also known as lower heating value (LHV).

### Determination of calorific value by Bomb Calorimeter:
- A bomb calorimeter is used to determine calorific values of fuels.
- A known mass of fuel is burned in an oxygen atmosphere inside a strong, insulated steel container called a bomb.
- The initial and final temperatures of water surrounding the bomb are measured.
- Using the temperature change and heat capacity of the calorimeter, the amount of heat released during combustion, i.e calorific value can be determined.
- The oxygen consumption can also be determined in bomb calorimeter for calculation of stoichiometric amount of air required for combustion.

# Products and Corrosion

Corrosion is the gradual destruction of materials by chemical reaction with their environment. It is a major problem that affects a wide range of products and structures. There are several reasons why corrosion occurs and various methods to control or prevent corrosion.

## Reasons for Corrosion

1. Presence of moisture: Corrosion requires the presence of water or moisture to act as an electrolyte for the chemical reaction to occur. Dry environments do not support corrosion.
2. Presence of oxygen: The presence of oxygen is necessary for oxidation reactions that lead to corrosion of metals. Corrosion will not occur in environments devoid of oxygen like in vacuum or inert gas environments.
3. Presence of ions: The presence of ions like chloride, nitrate, carbonate, etc. can influence the corrosion process by affecting the pH level or conducting electricity.
4. Environmental pollutants: Environmental pollutants like acid rain, industrial fumes, etc. can react with materials and accelerate the corrosion process.
5. Microbial action: Micro-organisms like bacteria can act on materials and form corrosion. This type of corrosion is known as microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC).

## Determination of Calorific Values and Analysis of Coal

1. Gross Calorific Value (GCV): It is the amount of heat released by burning a unit quantity of fuel completely in oxygen at a constant pressure. It takes into account the latent heat of vaporization of water formed during combustion. It is determined using a bomb calorimeter. The coal sample is burnt in an oxygen atmosphere inside a strong, insulated metallic container (bomb) and the temperature rise is noted. Using the known heat capacity of the calorimeter, the GCV is calculated.
2. Proximate Analysis: It determines the amount of moisture, volatile matter and ash present in coal. This analysis gives an indication about the grade of coal and its suitability for different applications.
- Moisture: It is the amount of water present in coal. It is determined by heating the coal at 105-110°C to a constant weight.
- Volatile Matter: It is the amount of gaseous products driven out of coal at high temperatures in the absence of air. It determines the volatile combustible matter in coal. It is determined by heating coal at 900°C in the absence of air and collecting the volatile gases.
- Ash: It is the non-combustible residue left after burning coal. It is determined by burning coal completely and noting the residue left. Ash content determines the purity of coal.
3. Ultimate Analysis: It determines the elemental composition of coal i.e. the percentages of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulfur and oxygen present in coal. It gives information useful for calculating the heating value and for understanding coal's behavior during combustion. The various elements are determined by chemical analysis. Carbon and hydrogen are determined using combustion methods while nitrogen and sulfur are determined by decomposition and subsequent analysis of resulting compounds. The oxygen percentage is determined by difference.
