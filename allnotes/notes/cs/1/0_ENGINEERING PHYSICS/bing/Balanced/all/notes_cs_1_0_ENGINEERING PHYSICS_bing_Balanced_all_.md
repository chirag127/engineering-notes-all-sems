

## Unit 1 - Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the behavior of matter and energy at the smallest scales of the physical world. Quantum mechanics is based on the idea that physical quantities, such as the position and momentum of a particle, are not definite and deterministic, but rather probabilistic and uncertain. Quantum mechanics also introduces the concept of wave-particle duality, which states that all physical objects can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties, depending on how they are observed and measured.

Some of the main topics covered in this unit are:

- The historical development of quantum mechanics, including the experiments and theories that led to its formulation, such as the photoelectric effect, the Compton effect, the Bohr model of the atom, the de Broglie hypothesis, the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, and the Schrödinger equation.
- The mathematical tools and formalism of quantum mechanics, such as complex numbers, operators, eigenvalues and eigenvectors, Hilbert spaces, Dirac notation, and matrix mechanics.
- The physical interpretation and implications of quantum mechanics, such as the wave function, the Born rule, the superposition principle, the collapse of the wave function, the measurement problem, and the Copenhagen interpretation.
- The applications and extensions of quantum mechanics, such as the hydrogen atom, the harmonic oscillator, the tunneling effect, the spin of the electron, the Pauli exclusion principle, the Stern-Gerlach experiment, and the quantum entanglement.



# Inadequacy of classical mechanics

Classical mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of macroscopic objects under the influence of forces. It is based on Newton's laws of motion and the principle of conservation of energy and momentum. Classical mechanics can explain correctly the motion of celestial bodies, such as planets and stars, and microscopic terrestrial bodies, such as bullets and cars, that move with non-relativistic speeds (much less than the speed of light).

However, classical mechanics fails to explain certain phenomena observed in nature, especially at the atomic and subatomic scales, where the objects move with relativistic speeds (comparable to the speed of light) and exhibit both wave-like and particle-like behavior. Some of the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot account for are:

- The stability of atoms: According to classical mechanics, an electron orbiting a nucleus should emit electromagnetic radiation and lose energy, eventually falling into the nucleus. This would make the atom unstable and collapse. However, atoms are observed to be stable and emit radiation only when they undergo transitions between discrete energy levels.
- The spectra of black body radiation: A black body is an idealized object that absorbs and emits all frequencies of electromagnetic radiation. According to classical mechanics, the energy of the radiation should be proportional to the frequency, and the black body should emit an infinite amount of energy at high frequencies. This is known as the ultraviolet catastrophe. However, experiments show that the energy of the radiation is quantized, and the black body emits a finite amount of energy that peaks at a certain frequency that depends on the temperature.
- The photoelectric effect: The photoelectric effect is the phenomenon of electrons being ejected from a metal surface when it is exposed to light. According to classical mechanics, the energy of the ejected electrons should depend on the intensity of the light, and there should be a delay between the exposure and the emission. However, experiments show that the energy of the ejected electrons depends on the frequency of the light, and there is no delay between the exposure and the emission. Moreover, there is a minimum frequency of light, called the threshold frequency, below which no electrons are ejected, regardless of the intensity of the light.
- The Compton effect: The Compton effect is the phenomenon of X-rays being scattered by electrons and losing some of their energy and wavelength. According to classical mechanics, the scattering of X-rays by electrons should not change their energy or wavelength, as they are both electromagnetic waves. However, experiments show that the scattered X-rays have a lower energy and a longer wavelength than the incident X-rays, and the change in wavelength depends on the angle of scattering.
- The discrete spectra of atoms: The discrete spectra of atoms are the characteristic patterns of frequencies of electromagnetic radiation emitted or absorbed by atoms when they undergo transitions between different energy levels. According to classical mechanics, the energy levels of atoms should be continuous, and the spectra of atoms should be continuous as well. However, experiments show that the energy levels of atoms are discrete, and the spectra of atoms are discrete as well, consisting of distinct lines corresponding to specific frequencies.

These phenomena, and others, indicate that classical mechanics is inadequate to describe the behavior of matter and radiation at the atomic and subatomic scales, and that a new theory is needed to account for them. This new theory is quantum mechanics, which is based on the principles of wave-particle duality, uncertainty, and superposition. Quantum mechanics can explain all the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot, and also reduces to classical mechanics in the limit of large objects and low speeds. Quantum mechanics is the foundation of modern physics, and has many applications in chemistry, biology, nanotechnology, and information technology.

# Compton Effect

- The Compton effect is the phenomenon of **elastic scattering** of photons by electrons, resulting in a **change in the wavelength** of the photons .
- The Compton effect was discovered by **Arthur Compton** in 1923, who measured the scattering of X-rays by graphite and observed that the scattered X-rays had a longer wavelength than the incident ones .
- The Compton effect demonstrated that **light has both wave and particle properties**, and that photons can transfer energy and momentum to electrons, as predicted by the **quantum theory** of light .
- The Compton effect can be explained by considering a **collision** between a photon and an electron, in which both particles are **conserved** in energy and momentum .

# De-Broglie Concept of Matter Waves

- De-Broglie concept of matter waves is based on the idea that matter has a dual nature, i.e., it can exhibit both particle-like and wave-like properties depending on the situation .
- De-Broglie proposed this concept in 1924 in his PhD thesis, inspired by the photon theory of light by Einstein .
- According to De-Broglie, when matter is in motion, it shows wave-like properties such as interference and diffraction, and when it is at rest, it shows particle-like properties such as mass and momentum .
- De-Broglie derived a relation between the wavelength (λ) of the matter wave and the momentum (p) of the particle as follows :

  - λ = h/p
  - where h is the Planck's constant.

- De-Broglie's concept of matter waves was experimentally verified by Davisson and Germer in 1927, who observed the diffraction of electrons by a nickel crystal .
- De-Broglie's concept of matter waves has important applications in quantum mechanics, such as explaining the quantization of angular momentum in the hydrogen atom, the uncertainty principle, the wave-particle duality, and the Schrödinger equation .



# Davisson and Germer Experiment

- The Davisson and Germer experiment was a 1923-27 experiment by Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Western Electric (later Bell Labs), in which electrons, scattered by the surface of a crystal of nickel metal, displayed a diffraction pattern.
- The experiment confirmed the de Broglie hypothesis that matter has wave-like behavior and established the wave–particle duality hypothesis which was a fundamental step in quantum theory.
- The experiment was based on the assumption that waves reflected from two distinct atomic levels of a Ni crystal will have a fixed phase difference and will interfere either constructively or destructively, producing a diffraction pattern .
- The experiment consisted of a vacuum chamber with an electron gun that emitted a beam of electrons towards a nickel target. The target could be rotated to change the angle of incidence of the electrons. A Faraday cup was used to measure the intensity of the scattered electrons at different angles.
- The experiment was initially designed to measure the dependence of the scattering coefficient on the angle of incidence, but due to an accidental heating of the nickel target, the crystal structure of the target was altered and the scattering pattern changed.
- The new scattering pattern showed a peak at a certain angle, which corresponded to the Bragg condition for constructive interference of X-rays. This suggested that the electrons were behaving like waves and were diffracting from the crystal lattice of the nickel.
- The wavelength of the electrons was calculated from the Bragg equation and was found to be consistent with the de Broglie relation, which predicted the wavelength of matter waves as inversely proportional to their momentum .
- The experiment was repeated with different accelerating voltages for the electron beam and the peak angle was found to vary accordingly, confirming the wave nature of electrons and the de Broglie equation .



# Phase velocity and group velocity

- Phase velocity is the velocity at which the phase of a wave propagates in a medium. It is given by the ratio of the angular frequency to the wave number: v_p = ω/k .
- Group velocity is the velocity at which the envelope or shape of a wave packet propagates in a medium. It is given by the derivative of the angular frequency with respect to the wave number: v_g = dω/dk .
- Phase velocity and group velocity are related by Rayleigh's formula: v_g = v_p + kv_p' , where v_p' is the derivative of the phase velocity with respect to the wave number.
- In quantum mechanics, phase velocity and group velocity have different interpretations for the associated particle. The phase velocity is related to the total energy of the particle by the de Broglie relation: v_p = E/ℏk . The group velocity is related to the kinetic energy of the particle by the relation: v_g = ℏk/m , where m is the mass of the particle.
- For a free particle, the phase velocity and the group velocity are inversely proportional to each other: v_p = ℏ^2k^2/2mE and v_g = 2E/ℏk . Therefore, the group velocity is twice the phase velocity: v_g = 2v_p . This means that the particle moves with the group velocity, while the phase velocity is superluminal .



# Time-dependent and time-independent Schrodinger wave equations

- The Schrodinger wave equation is a partial differential equation that describes the quantum state of a physical system.
- The equation can be written in two forms: time-dependent and time-independent.
- The time-dependent Schrodinger equation (TDSE) applies to a system that evolves over time and is affected by the potential energy of the system. The TDSE has the following expression:

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t} = \hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r},t)
$$

where $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ is the wave function of the system, $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator, $i$ is the imaginary unit, and $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant.

- The time-independent Schrodinger equation (TISE) applies to a system that does not change over time and has a constant potential energy. The TISE can be derived from the TDSE by using the separation of variables method, assuming that the wave function can be written as a product of a spatial part and a temporal part:

$$
\psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \psi(\mathbf{r}) e^{-i\omega t}
$$

where $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the spatial part, $\omega$ is the angular frequency, and $e^{-i\omega t}$ is the temporal part.

- Plugging this ansatz in the TDSE, we get the TISE:

$$
\hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r}) = E \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

where $E$ is the energy eigenvalue of the system, and $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the eigenfunction of the Hamiltonian operator.

- The TISE is an eigenvalue problem that can be solved for the allowed energy levels and the corresponding wave functions of the system. These solutions are called stationary states, because they do not change over time.
- The TDSE can be solved for any initial state by using the superposition principle, which states that any state can be written as a linear combination of the stationary states:

$$
\psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \sum_n c_n \psi_n(\mathbf{r}) e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}
$$

where $c_n$ are the coefficients of the superposition, $\psi_n(\mathbf{r})$ are the eigenfunctions of the TISE, and $E_n$ are the energy eigenvalues of the TISE.



# Physical interpretation of wave function

- A wave function is a mathematical function that describes the quantum state of a particle or a system of particles.
- The wave function, at a particular time, contains all the information that anybody at that time can have about the particle .
- But the wave function itself has no physical interpretation. It is not measurable . It is complex and can be expressed as \Psi(x,y,z,t) = a + ib, where a and b are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit.
- However, the square of the absolute value of the wave function has a physical interpretation. It gives the probability density of finding the particle in a certain location at a given instant  .
- The probability density is proportional to |\Psi(x,y,z,t)|^2, where |\Psi(x,y,z,t)| is the magnitude or modulus of the wave function .
- The probability density can be used to calculate the probability of finding the particle in a certain region of space by integrating over that region .
- The wave function can also be used to determine the expectation values of physical observables, such as position, momentum, and kinetic energy, by applying the appropriate operators to the wave function and integrating over the whole space .
- The expectation value of an observable is the average value that would be obtained from many measurements of the same system in the same quantum state .
- The wave function must satisfy certain properties, such as normalization, continuity, and differentiability, to be physically meaningful and consistent with the principles of quantum mechanics .

## Unit 2 - Electromagnetic Field Theory

- Electromagnetic field theory is the study of the physical phenomena associated with electric and magnetic fields, and their interactions with matter and radiation.
- Electric fields are produced by electric charges, and magnetic fields are produced by electric currents and changing electric fields.
- Electric and magnetic fields can exert forces on charged particles, and can also induce electric currents and voltages in conductors and dielectrics.
- Electric and magnetic fields can also propagate through space as electromagnetic waves, which carry energy and momentum, and can interact with matter and other electromagnetic waves.
- The fundamental equations that describe the behavior of electric and magnetic fields are known as Maxwell's equations, which are derived from the laws of conservation of charge, energy, and momentum, and the experimental laws of Coulomb, Ampere, Faraday, and Gauss.
- Maxwell's equations can be written in different forms, such as differential, integral, or vector forms, depending on the choice of coordinate system and notation.
- Maxwell's equations can be solved for specific problems using various methods, such as boundary conditions, superposition principle, separation of variables, Green's functions, or numerical methods.
- Some important concepts and applications of electromagnetic field theory are:

  - Electrostatics: the study of electric fields and potentials in the absence of time-varying phenomena.
  - Magnetostatics: the study of magnetic fields and potentials in the absence of time-varying phenomena.
  - Electrodynamics: the study of electric and magnetic fields and potentials in the presence of time-varying phenomena, such as electric currents, changing electric fields, and electromagnetic waves.
  - Electromagnetic induction: the phenomenon of generating electric currents and voltages in conductors and dielectrics due to changing magnetic fields or moving conductors in magnetic fields.
  - Electromagnetic radiation: the phenomenon of emitting and absorbing electromagnetic waves by accelerating charges or changing currents.
  - Electromagnetic wave propagation: the study of how electromagnetic waves travel through different media, such as vacuum, air, or materials, and how they are affected by boundaries, interfaces, and obstacles.
  - Electromagnetic wave polarization: the property of electromagnetic waves that describes the orientation and shape of their electric and magnetic field vectors.
  - Electromagnetic wave interference: the phenomenon of superposing two or more electromagnetic waves to produce a resultant wave with a different amplitude and phase.
  - Electromagnetic wave diffraction: the phenomenon of bending and spreading of electromagnetic waves around edges and apertures.
  - Electromagnetic wave scattering: the phenomenon of deflecting and redistributing electromagnetic waves by obstacles or irregularities in the medium.
  - Electromagnetic wave reflection and refraction: the phenomena of changing the direction and speed of electromagnetic waves when they encounter a boundary between two different media.
  - Electromagnetic wave transmission and absorption: the phenomena of passing through and losing energy to a medium by electromagnetic waves.
  - Electromagnetic wave dispersion and attenuation: the phenomena of varying the speed and amplitude of electromagnetic waves with frequency and distance in a medium.
  - Electromagnetic wave modulation and demodulation: the processes of encoding and decoding information onto and from electromagnetic waves by varying their amplitude, frequency, phase, or polarization.
  - Electromagnetic wave antennas and radiation patterns: the devices and methods of generating and receiving electromagnetic waves, and the spatial distribution of their intensity and direction.



# Basic concept of Stoke’s theorem and Divergence theorem

- Stoke's theorem and Divergence theorem are two important theorems in vector calculus that relate the integrals of vector fields over different dimensions.
- Stoke's theorem (also known as Kelvin-Stoke's theorem) states that the line integral of a vector field around a closed curve is equal to the surface integral of the curl of the vector field over any surface bounded by the curve .
- Divergence theorem (also known as Gauss's theorem) states that the surface integral of a vector field over a closed surface is equal to the volume integral of the divergence of the vector field over any volume enclosed by the surface .
- Both theorems are generalizations of the fundamental theorem of calculus, which states that the integral of a function over an interval is equal to the difference of the function values at the endpoints of the interval .
- Both theorems can be used to simplify the calculation of integrals of vector fields over complicated domains by reducing the dimension of the integration .
- Both theorems have applications in physics, such as electromagnetism, fluid dynamics, and heat transfer .



# Basic laws of electricity and magnetism

Electricity and magnetism are two aspects of one common phenomenon: electromagnetism. The interaction between electric charges and magnetic fields gives rise to many physical phenomena and applications. Here are some of the basic laws of electricity and magnetism that describe how they work:

- **Coulomb's law**: This law states that the force of attraction or repulsion between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force acts along the line joining the charges and has the same magnitude for both charges. The mathematical expression of Coulomb's law is:

  $$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

  where $F$ is the force, $k$ is a constant, $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charges, and $r$ is the distance. The constant $k$ depends on the medium in which the charges are placed and the units used. In SI units, $k = \frac{1}{4 \pi \epsilon_0}$, where $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.

- **Gauss's law**: This law states that the net electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the total charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of the medium. The electric flux is a measure of the number of electric field lines passing through the surface. The mathematical expression of Gauss's law is:

  $$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$

  where $\Phi_E$ is the electric flux, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, $d\vec{A}$ is a differential area element of the surface, $Q$ is the total charge, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space. Gauss's law can be used to calculate the electric field due to symmetric charge distributions, such as a point charge, a line of charge, a plane of charge, or a sphere of charge.

- **Faraday's law of induction**: This law states that the electromotive force (emf) induced in a loop of wire is equal to the rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop. The magnetic flux is a measure of the number of magnetic field lines passing through the loop. The emf is the potential difference that drives the current in the loop. The mathematical expression of Faraday's law is:

  $$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

  where $\mathcal{E}$ is the emf, $\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux, and $t$ is the time. Faraday's law can be used to explain the working of devices such as transformers, generators, and motors.

- **Ampere's law**: This law states that the line integral of the magnetic field around any closed loop is equal to the product of the permeability of the medium and the total current passing through the loop. The line integral is a measure of the total magnetic field along the loop. The current can be either steady or changing. The mathematical expression of Ampere's law is:

  $$\oint_L \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I$$

  where $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field, $d\vec{l}$ is a differential length element of the loop, $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space, and $I$ is the total current. Ampere's law can be used to calculate the magnetic field due to symmetric current distributions, such as a straight wire, a solenoid, or a toroid.

These are some of the basic laws of electricity and magnetism that govern the electromagnetic field theory. They can be combined and generalized into a set of four equations known as Maxwell's equations, which form the basis of modern electromagnetism.



# Continuity equation for current density

- Current density is a measure of the amount of electric current flowing through a unit area of a conductor. It is denoted by the symbol J and has the unit of ampere per square meter (A/m^2).
- The formula for current density is given as, J = I / A, where I is the current flowing through the conductor in amperes and A is the cross-sectional area of the conductor in square meters.
- The continuity equation for current density is a mathematical expression that relates the change in charge density (ρ) in a region of space to the current density (J) flowing out of that region. It is based on the principle of conservation of charge, which states that the net charge in a closed system is constant.
- The differential form of the continuity equation is: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇ ⋅ J = 0, where t is the time, ρ is the charge density, and ∇ ⋅ J is the divergence of the current density. 
- The integral form of the continuity equation is: d/dt ∫V ρ d^3 r = - ∮S J ⋅ n d^2 r, where V is an arbitrary stationary volume limited by the closed surface S, n is the unit normal vector to the surface, and J ⋅ n is the dot product of the current density and the normal vector.
- The continuity equation implies that the charge density in a region decreases if the net current flows out of the region, and increases if the net current flows into the region. It also implies that the current density is zero at any point where the charge density is constant.



# Displacement current

- Displacement current is a phenomenon in electromagnetism that is analogous to an ordinary electric current, but occurs when the electric field changes with time .
- Displacement current density is the quantity ∂D/∂t, where D is the electric displacement field, and t is time.
- Displacement current density has the same units as electric current density (A/m^2^), and it is a source of the magnetic field just as actual current is.
- Displacement current was introduced by James Clerk Maxwell in his equations to explain the magnetic fields produced by changing electric fields, and to make the equations consistent with the law of conservation of charge .
- Displacement current plays a central role in the propagation of electromagnetic waves, such as light and radio waves, through empty space or dielectric materials .
- A traveling, varying magnetic field is everywhere associated with a periodically changing electric field that may be conceived in terms of a displacement current.
- Displacement current allows the electric and magnetic fields to be coupled when the electric flux is time-varying.



# Maxwell equations in integral and differential form

Maxwell equations are a set of four equations that describe the relationship between electric and magnetic fields, charges, and currents. They are the foundation of electromagnetic theory and have many applications in physics and engineering.

## Integral form

The integral form of Maxwell equations can be used to make statements about a region of charge or current. They are:

- Gauss's law: The electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the net electric charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space. Mathematically,

$$\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$

where $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field, $d\mathbf{A}$ is the differential area element, $Q$ is the net charge, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.

- Gauss's law for magnetism: The magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero. This implies that there are no magnetic monopoles. Mathematically,

$$\oint_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0$$

where $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field.

- Faraday's law of induction: The electromotive force (EMF) around any closed loop is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop. This explains how a changing magnetic field can induce an electric current. Mathematically,

$$\oint_C \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d}{dt} \int_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A}$$

# Maxwell equations in vacuum and in conducting medium

- Maxwell equations are a set of four partial differential equations that describe the behavior of electric and magnetic fields in vacuum and in matter.
- They are derived from the experimental laws of Gauss, Faraday, Ampere and Maxwell, and they are consistent with the principle of conservation of charge and energy.
- They can be written in two equivalent forms: the integral form and the differential form.
- The integral form relates the flux of electric and magnetic fields through a closed surface to the total charge and current enclosed by the surface.
- The differential form relates the divergence and curl of electric and magnetic fields at a point to the charge and current density at that point.
- In vacuum, the electric and magnetic fields are independent of the properties of the medium, and they satisfy the following equations:

  - Gauss's law for electricity: $$\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$
  - Gauss's law for magnetism: $$\oint_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0$$
  - Faraday's law of induction: $$\oint_C \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d}{dt} \int_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A}$$
  - Ampere-Maxwell law: $$\oint_C \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 \left( I + \epsilon_0 \frac{d}{dt} \int_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} \right)$$

  where $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are the electric and magnetic fields, $Q$ and $I$ are the total charge and current, $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ are the permittivity and permeability of vacuum, $S$ is a closed surface, and $C$ is a closed loop.

- In a conducting medium, the electric and magnetic fields are affected by the presence of free charges and currents, and they satisfy the following equations:

  - Gauss's law for electricity: $$\oint_S \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = Q_f$$
  - Gauss's law for magnetism: $$\oint_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0$$
  - Faraday's law of induction: $$\oint_C \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d}{dt} \int_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A}$$
  - Ampere-Maxwell law: $$\oint_C \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_f + \frac{d}{dt} \int_S \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{A}$$

  where $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are the electric displacement and magnetic field intensity, $Q_f$ and $I_f$ are the free charge and current, and the other symbols are the same as before.

- The electric displacement and magnetic field intensity are related to the electric and magnetic fields by the constitutive relations:

  - $$\mathbf{D} = \epsilon \mathbf{E}$$
  - $$\mathbf{H} = \frac{1}{\mu} \mathbf{B}$$

  where $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ are the permittivity and permeability of the medium, which may depend on the frequency, direction and position of the fields.

# Poynting vector and Poynting theorem

- The **Poynting vector** is a vector quantity that represents the directional energy flux (the energy transfer per unit area per unit time) of an electromagnetic field.
- The Poynting vector is defined as the cross product of the electric field vector **E** and the magnetic field vector **H** at any point in space, that is **P = E x H** .
- The Poynting vector is named after its discoverer, J.H. Poynting, who first derived it in 1884.
- The Poynting vector has the units of watts per square meter (W/m^2^) and is a measure of the power density of the electromagnetic field.
- The Poynting vector is used in conjunction with **Poynting's theorem**, which is the continuity equation expressing the conservation of electromagnetic energy.
- Poynting's theorem states that the net electromagnetic power flowing into a region of space may be either dissipated, or used to change the energy stored in electric and magnetic fields within that region.
- Poynting's theorem can be written as **∇ · P = - ∂u/∂t - J · E**, where **u** is the electromagnetic energy density, **J** is the electric current density, and **E** is the electric field.
- Poynting's theorem can be derived from Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force law, by multiplying the first equation by **H** and the second equation by **E**, and subtracting them.
- Poynting's theorem can also be interpreted as a statement of the conservation of momentum density carried by the electromagnetic field, by dividing the Poynting vector by **c^2^**, where **c** is the speed of light.

# Newton's Rings and its Applications

Newton's rings are a phenomenon of interference of light that occurs when a thin film of air is sandwiched between a spherical surface and a flat surface. The light reflected from the two surfaces interferes constructively or destructively depending on the thickness of the air film, creating a pattern of bright and dark rings. The phenomenon is named after Isaac Newton, who investigated it in 1666.

Some of the applications of Newton's rings are:

- Testing the uniformity of a polished surface by studying the interference pattern the surface makes when placed in contact with a perfectly flat glass surface. Any deviation from a perfect circle in the rings indicates a defect or irregularity in the surface.
- Measuring the thickness of thin films, such as oil, soap, or paint, by observing the change in the interference pattern when the film is placed between the spherical and flat surfaces. The thickness of the film can be calculated from the radius and the order of the rings using the formula:

$$t = \frac{r^2}{2R} - \frac{\lambda}{2}m$$

where $t$ is the thickness of the film, $r$ is the radius of the ring, $R$ is the radius of curvature of the spherical surface, $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light, and $m$ is the order of the ring.

- Determining the refractive index of liquids by immersing the spherical and flat surfaces in the liquid and observing the change in the interference pattern. The refractive index of the liquid can be calculated from the change in the radius of the rings using the formula:

$$\frac{1}{r_1^2} - \frac{1}{r_2^2} = \frac{2\mu}{R}$$

where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the radii of the rings before and after immersion, $\mu$ is the refractive index of the liquid, and $R$ is the radius of curvature of the spherical surface.

- Controlling the thickness of paint that is used on posters. Too much paint would exceed total weight requirements, and too little paint would not cover the poster adequately. By observing the interference pattern of the paint film, the optimal thickness can be achieved.

# Absent spectra

- Absent spectra are the missing orders of principal maxima in the diffraction pattern of a plane grating.
- They occur when the minima due to diffraction coincide with the maxima due to interference for a certain order of spectrum.
- The condition for absent spectra is given by the equation: n = N sin θ, where n is the order of spectrum, N is the number of lines per unit length of the grating, and θ is the angle of diffraction.
- The order of the first absent spectrum is equal to the number of lines per unit length of the grating.
- The order of the second absent spectrum is equal to twice the number of lines per unit length of the grating, and so on.
- Absent spectra reduce the resolving power of the grating, as they create gaps in the spectrum.
- Absent spectra can be avoided by using a blazed grating, which has a sawtooth-shaped profile that reflects more light in a particular direction.



# Diffraction Grating

- A diffraction grating is an optical component with a periodic structure that diffracts light into several beams travelling in different directions (i.e., different diffraction angles).
- A diffraction grating can be made of a glass plate or a mirror with a large number of equidistant parallel lines or grooves on its surface. It can also be made of a thin film of metal or plastic with a series of slits or holes.
- A diffraction grating produces a spectrum by diffraction and interference of light, ultraviolet radiation, or X-rays. The spectrum consists of bright and dark fringes that correspond to the constructive and destructive interference of the diffracted waves.
- The diffraction angle of each beam depends on the wavelength of the light and the spacing of the grating elements. The grating equation relates the diffraction angle, the wavelength, and the grating spacing as follows:

  `d sin θ = m λ`

  where `d` is the grating spacing, `θ` is the diffraction angle, `m` is the order of diffraction, and `λ` is the wavelength.

- A diffraction grating can be used for various applications, such as spectroscopy, wavelength measurement, laser tuning, optical communication, and holography. # Resolving Power

- Resolving power is the ability of an optical instrument to separate two adjacent points or objects from each other from a considerable distance.
- Resolving power depends on the wavelength of the light used and the aperture of the instrument.
- The smaller the wavelength and the larger the aperture, the higher the resolving power.
- Resolving power is also related to the diffraction limit, which is the minimum angular separation between two point sources that can be resolved by an instrument.
- According to the Rayleigh criterion, resolution is possible when the minimum angular separation is given by:

$$\theta = 1.22 \frac{\lambda}{D}$$

where $\theta$ is the angular separation, $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light, and $D$ is the diameter of the aperture.

- The resolving power is the reciprocal of the angular separation, i.e.,:

$$R = \frac{1}{\theta} = \frac{D}{1.22 \lambda}$$

- Different optical instruments have different formulas for resolving power, depending on their design and function.
- For a telescope, the resolving power is given by:

$$R = \frac{d}{1.22 \lambda}$$

where $d$ is the diameter of the objective lens.

- For a microscope, the resolving power is given by:

$$R = \frac{2 n \sin \theta}{\lambda}$$

where $n$ is the refractive index of the medium between the objective lens and the specimen, and $\theta$ is the half-angle of the cone of light entering the objective lens.

- For a prism, the resolving power is given by:

$$R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta \lambda}$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the spectral line, and $\Delta \lambda$ is the difference between the wavelengths of two adjacent spectral lines.

- For a grating, the resolving power is given by:

$$R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta \lambda} = N m$$

where $N$ is the number of slits in the grating, and $m$ is the order of diffraction.



# Rayleigh's Criterion of Resolution

- Rayleigh's criterion of resolution is a measure of the ability of an optical system to distinguish two closely spaced point sources of light, such as stars or microscope specimens.
- According to Rayleigh's criterion, two point sources are just resolvable when the center of the diffraction pattern of one source coincides with the first minimum of the diffraction pattern of the other source .
- The minimum angular separation θ between two point sources that can be resolved by an optical system with a circular aperture of diameter D is given by   :

    θ = 1.22 λ/D

    where λ is the wavelength of the light used.

- Rayleigh's criterion is based on the assumption that the human eye can detect the dip in intensity between the overlapping diffraction patterns of the two sources. However, this may not always be the case, especially for sources with different brightness or color.
- Rayleigh's criterion is also applicable to other types of apertures, such as rectangular or elliptical, by using the effective diameter of the aperture, which is the diameter of a circular aperture that produces the same diffraction pattern as the actual aperture.
- Rayleigh's criterion is useful for estimating the resolution limit of optical instruments, such as telescopes, microscopes, cameras, etc. However, it does not account for other factors that may affect the resolution, such as aberrations, atmospheric turbulence, noise, etc .



# Resolving power of grating

- Resolving power of grating is its ability to form two separate maxima of two wavelengths which are very close to each other.
- It is defined as the ratio of the wavelength of one of the spectral line to the difference in the wavelengths between them such that the two lines appear to be just resolved.
- Mathematically, resolving power of grating is given by:

    R.P. = λ/dλ

    where λ is the mean wavelength and dλ is the smallest difference between two wavelengths that can be resolved.

- Resolving power of grating depends on the following factors:

    - The order of diffraction (n): Higher the order, higher the resolving power.
    - The number of lines per unit length on the grating (N): Higher the N, higher the resolving power.
    - The total number of lines on the grating (N'): Higher the N', higher the resolving power.
    - The width of the grating (a): Higher the a, higher the resolving power.

- Resolving power of grating can be derived using the Rayleigh criterion, which states that two spectral lines are just resolved when the central maximum of one coincides with the first minimum of the other.
- The derivation is as follows:

    - Consider a grating with N' lines and width a, illuminated by a monochromatic light of wavelength λ. The angle of diffraction for the nth order maximum is given by:

        a sin θ = nλ

    - The first minimum of the diffraction pattern occurs when the path difference between the rays from the adjacent slits is equal to λ/2. The angle of diffraction for the first minimum is given by:

        a sin θ' = (n + 1/2)λ

    - If another monochromatic light of wavelength λ + dλ is incident on the same grating, the angle of diffraction for the nth order maximum is given by:

        a sin θ'' = n(λ + dλ)

    - According to the Rayleigh criterion, the two spectral lines are just resolved when θ' = θ''. This implies:

        a sin θ' = a sin θ''

        (n + 1/2)λ = n(λ + dλ)

        dλ = λ/2n

    - Therefore, the resolving power of grating is given by:

        R.P. = λ/dλ = 2n

    - However, this expression does not take into account the finite width of the grating. If the grating has N' lines, then the width of the grating is given by:

        a = N'/N

    - Substituting this value of a in the expression for θ, we get:

        N'/N sin θ = nλ

    - Multiplying both sides by N, we get:

        N' sin θ = nNλ

    - This expression shows that the product of the order of diffraction and the number of lines per unit length on the grating is constant for a given angle of diffraction. This implies that the resolving power of grating is also proportional to the total number of lines on the grating. Hence, we can write:

        R.P. = 2nN' = 2Nλ/a sin θ

    - This is the final expression for the resolving power of grating.

# Fibre Optics: Principle and construction of optical fiber

- Fibre optics is the science of transmitting data, voice, and images by the passage of light through thin, transparent fibers.
- Optical fibers are flexible, transparent fibers made by drawing glass or plastic to a diameter slightly thicker than that of a human hair.
- Optical fibers work on the principle of total internal reflection of light, which means that no part of the light signal gets refracted or transmitted to the second medium, but instead, all of the signal is retained inside the fiber .
- The condition for total internal reflection is that the light must travel from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium, and the angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle for the boundary.
- The construction of an optical fiber consists of three main parts :
  - The core, which is the innermost layer of the fiber, where the light is transmitted. The core has a high refractive index and a small diameter (typically 8 μm for single-mode fibers and 50-62.5 μm for multimode fibers).
  - The cladding, which is the outer layer of the fiber, surrounding the core. The cladding has a lower refractive index than the core and a larger diameter (typically 125 μm). The cladding acts as a boundary for the light and prevents it from escaping the core.
  - The protective coating, which is a layer of plastic or other material that covers the cladding and protects the fiber from damage and environmental factors. The coating has a diameter of about 250 μm and can be removed by stripping tools when the fiber is spliced or connected to other devices.



# Acceptance Angle in Fiber Optics

- The acceptance angle of an optical fiber is defined based on a purely geometrical consideration (ray optics): it is the maximum angle of a ray (against the fiber axis) hitting the fiber core which allows the incident light to be guided by the core .
- The acceptance angle depends on the refractive indices of the core, the cladding, and the surrounding medium (usually air). The larger the difference between the core and the cladding refractive indices, the larger the acceptance angle.
- The acceptance angle can be calculated using Snell's law and the condition for total internal reflection at the core-cladding interface. If n_f is the core refractive index, n_c is the cladding refractive index, and n_0 is the surrounding medium refractive index, then the acceptance angle \theta_a is given by:

\theta_a = \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{n_f}{n_0} \sqrt{1 - \left( \frac{n_c}{n_f} \right)^2} \right)

- The acceptance angle is related to the numerical aperture (NA) of the fiber, which is a measure of the light-gathering ability of the fiber. The numerical aperture is defined as:

NA = n_0 \sin \theta_a

# Step index and graded index fibers

## Introduction

Optical fibers are thin strands of glass or plastic that can transmit light signals over long distances. Optical fibers are used for various applications such as telecommunications, data transmission, sensing, and medical imaging. Optical fibers can be classified into two main types based on the variation of the refractive index in the core and the cladding: step index fibers and graded index fibers.

## Step index fibers

- A step index fiber has a core with a constant refractive index and a cladding with a lower refractive index. The refractive index changes abruptly at the core-cladding interface, forming a step-like profile.
- A step index fiber can be either single mode or multimode, depending on the core diameter and the wavelength of the light. A single mode fiber has a very thin core (about 10 microns) and can support only one mode of propagation. A multimode fiber has a thicker core (about 50 to 1000 microns) and can support multiple modes of propagation.
- A step index fiber has a high modal dispersion, which is the spreading of the light pulses due to the different path lengths and velocities of the modes. This limits the bandwidth and the distance of the step index fiber. The modal dispersion can be reduced by using a single mode fiber or by using a light source with a narrow spectral width.
- A step index fiber is cheaper and easier to manufacture than a graded index fiber, but it cannot offer higher bandwidth nor longer distances.

## Graded index fibers

- A graded index fiber has a core with a varying refractive index and a cladding with a lower refractive index. The refractive index decreases gradually from the center to the edge of the core, forming a parabolic profile.
- A graded index fiber is always multimode, but it has a lower modal dispersion than a step index fiber. This is because the varying refractive index causes the light rays to bend towards the center of the core, reducing the difference in path lengths and velocities of the modes. This increases the bandwidth and the distance of the graded index fiber.
- A graded index fiber offers superior transmission quality, higher-bandwidth, and longer distance than a step index fiber. However, a graded index fiber is more expensive and complex to manufacture than a step index fiber.
- A graded index fiber is preferred for modern communication applications, such as local area networks, fiber to the home, and data centers.

## Conclusion

- Step index fiber and graded index fiber are two types of multimode fibers
- Graded index fiber offers superior transmission quality, higher-bandwidth, and longer distance
- Higher modal dispersion limits the bandwidth of the step index fiber
- In modern communication applications, graded index fiber is preferred
- Step index fiber is cheaper but it cannot offer higher bandwidth nor longer distances

# Attenuation in Fiber Optics

- Attenuation is the reduction in intensity of the light beam (or signal) with respect to distance travelled through a transmission medium .
- Attenuation is measured in decibels (dB) and is calculated by the formula: Attenuation (dB) = 10 x Log10 (P in/P out) = 20xLog10 (V in/V out) where P is power and V is voltage.
- Attenuation limits the distance in which the signal can travel through optical fiber and affects the performance and reliability of the fiber optic network.
- Attenuation can be caused by intrinsic and extrinsic factors .
  - Intrinsic factors are inherent to the fiber material and include:
    - Absorption: the conversion of optical power into heat due to the interaction of light with the molecular structure of the fiber . Absorption depends on the wavelength of the light and the impurities in the fiber.
    - Scattering: the deflection of light from its original direction due to the microscopic variations in the refractive index of the fiber . Scattering depends on the wavelength of the light and the fiber diameter.
    - Dispersion: the spreading of light pulses over time due to the different velocities of different wavelengths or modes in the fiber . Dispersion causes distortion and interference of the signal and limits the bandwidth of the fiber.
  - Extrinsic factors are external to the fiber material and include:
    - Bending: the deviation of the fiber from its original shape due to mechanical stress or installation . Bending causes leakage of light from the core to the cladding or the air and increases the attenuation.
    - Splicing: the joining of two fiber segments by fusion or mechanical means . Splicing causes loss of light due to imperfect alignment, mismatch of fiber characteristics, or air gaps between the fibers.
    - Connectors: the devices that link the fiber to the source, receiver, or other fibers . Connectors cause loss of light due to reflection, misalignment, dirt, or damage on the connector surfaces.
- Attenuation can be reduced by using appropriate fiber types, wavelengths, sources, receivers, splices, connectors, and installation techniques .



# Dispersion

- Dispersion is the phenomenon of splitting of a beam of white light into its constituent colors when it passes through a transparent medium, such as a prism or a fiber.
- Dispersion occurs because different wavelengths of light have different refractive indices in a medium, and hence they travel at different speeds and bend at different angles.
- Dispersion can be classified into two types: intermodal dispersion and intramodal dispersion.
- Intermodal dispersion is the dispersion caused by the different propagation modes of light in a multimode fiber. It depends on the fiber geometry, core-cladding refractive index difference, and the launching conditions of light into the fiber.
- Intramodal dispersion is the dispersion caused by the different group velocities of light in a single mode fiber. It depends on the wavelength dependence of the refractive index of the fiber material and the waveguide structure.
- Intermodal dispersion can be reduced by using graded-index fibers, which have a parabolic variation of refractive index in the core, or by using single mode fibers, which have only one propagation mode.
- Intramodal dispersion can be reduced by using dispersion-shifted fibers, which have a zero dispersion wavelength near the operating wavelength, or by using dispersion-flattened fibers, which have a low and constant dispersion over a wide wavelength range.

# Spontaneous and stimulated emission of radiation

- Spontaneous emission is the process in which an atom or molecule in an excited state decays to a lower energy state and emits a photon of energy without the aid of external agency. This process is random and the emitted photons are incoherent, meaning they have different frequencies, phases, polarizations, and directions of propagation .
- Stimulated emission is the process in which an atom or molecule in an excited state is induced to emit a photon of energy by interacting with another photon of the same frequency. This process is non-random and the emitted photons are coherent, meaning they have the same frequency, phase, polarization, and direction of propagation as the incident photons  .
- Spontaneous emission and stimulated emission are the two main mechanisms of light emission in atoms and molecules. They are essential for the operation of lasers, which rely on stimulated emission to amplify light and produce coherent beams. Spontaneous emission is also important for fluorescence and phosphorescence, which are types of luminescence that occur when certain materials absorb and re-emit light.



# Population inversion

- Population inversion is a condition in which the number of atoms or molecules in an excited state is greater than the number in the ground state.
- Population inversion is necessary for the operation of a laser, as it allows stimulated emission to dominate over absorption.
- Population inversion can be achieved by various methods, such as optical pumping, electric discharge, chemical reaction, or nuclear reaction.
- Population inversion can be described by the Boltzmann distribution, which gives the relative population of atoms or molecules in different energy levels as a function of temperature.
- Population inversion can also be quantified by the inversion factor, which is the ratio of the population in the upper laser level to the population in the lower laser level.
- Population inversion can be maintained by a continuous or pulsed supply of energy to the system, which keeps the atoms or molecules in the excited state.

- The Einstein B coefficients are related to the absorption and stimulated emission of light, which are the processes of an atom or molecule in a lower energy state absorbing a photon and jumping to a higher energy state, or an atom or molecule in an excited state emitting a photon and returning to a lower energy state due to the presence of an external radiation field .
- The Einstein C coefficients are related to the collisional transitions of atoms or molecules, which are the processes of an atom or molecule changing its energy state due to the impact of another particle.
- The Einstein coefficients are fixed probabilities per time associated with each atom or molecule, and do not depend on the state of the gas of which the atoms or molecules are a part.
- The Einstein coefficients can be derived from the principle of detailed balance, which states that at thermodynamic equilibrium, the rate of transitions from one state to another must be equal to the rate of transitions from the latter state to the former .
- The Einstein coefficients can be used to calculate the absorption and emission coefficients of a gas, which are the measures of how much radiation is absorbed or emitted by the gas per unit length.
- The Einstein coefficients can also be used to calculate the radiative lifetime of an excited state, which is the average time that an atom or molecule spends in the excited state before emitting a photon.



# Principles of laser action

- Laser is an acronym for **Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation**.
- Laser action is the process of producing a coherent, monochromatic, and intense beam of light by stimulating atoms or molecules to emit photons of the same frequency, phase, and direction.
- The basic principles of laser action are :
  - **Spontaneous emission**: An atom or molecule in an excited state can decay to a lower energy state by emitting a photon of energy equal to the energy difference between the two states. This process is random and isotropic, meaning that the photon can be emitted in any direction and with any phase.
  - **Stimulated emission**: An atom or molecule in an excited state can also decay to a lower energy state by emitting a photon of energy equal to the energy difference between the two states, but only if it is stimulated by another photon of the same energy, frequency, and phase. This process is coherent and directional, meaning that the emitted photon has the same phase and direction as the stimulating photon. The result is an amplification of the stimulating photon by the emitted photon.
  - **Stimulated absorption**: An atom or molecule in a lower energy state can absorb a photon of energy equal to the energy difference between the two states and jump to a higher energy state. This process is the opposite of stimulated emission and reduces the intensity of the photon beam.
  - **Non-radiative decay**: An atom or molecule in an excited state can also decay to a lower energy state by transferring its excess energy to other atoms or molecules through collisions or vibrations. This process is non-radiative and does not involve the emission or absorption of photons. It reduces the population of the excited state and the efficiency of the laser action.
  - **Population inversion**: For laser action to occur, the number of atoms or molecules in the excited state must be greater than the number of atoms or molecules in the lower energy state. This condition is called population inversion and is achieved by pumping energy into the system through various methods, such as electrical discharge, optical pumping, chemical reaction, etc.
  - **Amplification of light**: When a photon beam passes through a medium with population inversion, it undergoes multiple cycles of stimulated emission and stimulated absorption, resulting in an exponential increase in the intensity of the beam. This process is called amplification of light and is the essence of laser action. The medium with population inversion is called the gain medium or the active medium of the laser.
  - **Optical resonator**: To enhance the amplification of light, the gain medium is placed between two mirrors that reflect the photon beam back and forth, creating an optical feedback loop. This arrangement is called an optical resonator or a laser cavity. One of the mirrors is partially transparent, allowing a fraction of the photon beam to escape as the laser output. The optical resonator also determines the wavelength, mode, and shape of the laser beam.

# Meissner Effect

- The Meissner effect is the expulsion of a magnetic field from the interior of a material that is in the process of becoming a superconductor .
- A superconductor is a material that loses its resistance to the flow of electrical currents when cooled below a certain temperature, called the transition temperature, usually close to absolute zero.
- The Meissner effect was discovered by the German physicists W. Meissner and R. Ochsenfeld in 1933 .
- The Meissner effect is a property of all superconductors .

- The Meissner effect occurs because the superconductor develops a surface current that generates a magnetic field opposite to the applied field, thus canceling it out inside the material .
- The Meissner effect is a consequence of the perfect diamagnetism of superconductors, which means they have a magnetic susceptibility of -1 .
- The Meissner effect is different from the ordinary diamagnetism of normal materials, which is a weak and linear response to an applied magnetic field .
- The Meissner effect is also different from the electromagnetic induction of normal conductors, which is a transient and non-uniform response to a changing magnetic field .
- The Meissner effect is a manifestation of the superconducting phase transition, which is a quantum phenomenon that involves the formation of Cooper pairs of electrons and the breaking of the symmetry of the normal state.



# Temperature dependence of critical field for superconductors

- The critical field of a superconductor is the maximum magnetic field that the material can withstand without losing its superconductivity.
- The critical field depends on the temperature of the superconductor, and generally increases as the temperature decreases.
- For type I superconductors, which are pure metals or alloys, the critical field can be approximated by the empirical formula:

$$H_c(T) = H_c(0) \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{T}{T_c} \right)^2 \right]$$

where $H_c(0)$ is the critical field at absolute zero, and $T_c$ is the critical temperature of the superconductor.

- For type II superconductors, which are complex materials with mixed or layered structures, the critical field is divided into two regions: the lower critical field $H_{c1}$ and the upper critical field $H_{c2}$.
- The lower critical field $H_{c1}$ is the field at which the superconductor starts to allow some magnetic flux to penetrate its interior, forming vortices of supercurrents around the flux lines. The superconductor is still in the Meissner state, where it expels most of the magnetic field.
- The upper critical field $H_{c2}$ is the field at which the superconductor becomes fully normal, losing its superconductivity. The superconductor is in the normal state, where it behaves like a normal conductor with resistance.
- The lower and upper critical fields of type II superconductors depend on the temperature and the material properties, such as the coherence length, the penetration depth, and the anisotropy of the superconducting layers.
- The lower critical field can be estimated by the formula:

$$H_{c1}(T) = \frac{\phi_0}{4\pi \lambda^2(T)} \ln \left( \frac{\lambda(T)}{\xi(T)} \right)$$

where $\phi_0$ is the magnetic flux quantum, $\lambda(T)$ is the penetration depth, and $\xi(T)$ is the coherence length of the superconductor.

- The upper critical field can be calculated by the formula:

$$H_{c2}(T) = \frac{\phi_0}{2\pi \xi^2(T)}$$

where $\xi(T)$ is the coherence length of the superconductor.

- The coherence length and the penetration depth are temperature-dependent quantities that characterize the spatial extent of the superconducting order parameter and the magnetic field penetration, respectively.
- The coherence length decreases as the temperature increases, approaching zero at the critical temperature. The penetration depth increases as the temperature increases, diverging at the critical temperature.
- Therefore, the lower and upper critical fields of type II superconductors also decrease as the temperature increases, approaching zero at the critical temperature.



# Persistent Current in Superconductors

- Persistent current is possible in superconductors because they have zero resistance and no energy dissipation  .
- Persistent current can be induced in a superconducting ring by placing it in a magnetic field above its critical temperature and then cooling it below its critical temperature. The magnetic flux through the ring is then trapped and the current circulates to maintain it .

The current flows through the weak link and creates a phase difference between the two ends of the ring. The phase difference determines the magnitude and direction of the persistent current .

- Persistent current has applications in superconducting magnets, quantum computing, and metrology .



# Type I and Type II Superconductors

- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and perfect diamagnetism below a certain critical temperature.
- Superconductors can be classified into two types based on their behavior in the presence of an external magnetic field: type I and type II.
- Type I superconductors have only one critical magnetic field, Hc, above which they lose their superconductivity and become normal conductors. They expel all the magnetic flux from their interior when the applied field is below Hc, a phenomenon known as the Meissner effect. They are usually pure metals, such as mercury, lead, tin, and aluminum.
- Type II superconductors have two critical magnetic fields, Hc1 and Hc2, between which they exhibit a mixed state of superconductivity and normal conductivity. They allow some magnetic flux to penetrate their interior in the form of quantized vortices, creating a non-uniform magnetic field. They are usually metal alloys or complex oxide ceramics, such as niobium, vanadium, technetium, and high-temperature superconductors.
- Type II superconductors can sustain much higher magnetic fields and currents than type I superconductors, making them more suitable for practical applications, such as magnets, generators, motors, and transmission lines.

# Quantum wires and quantum well

## Quantum well

- A quantum well is a two-dimensional (2D) structure or a thin layer of a lower bandgap semiconductor material sandwiched between two layers of a higher bandgap material.  
- In a quantum well, the motion of electrons and holes is confined in one direction (perpendicular to the layer), while they are free to move in the other two directions (parallel to the layer).  
- The confinement of carriers in one direction leads to the quantization of their energy levels, which means that they can only occupy discrete energy states.  
- The energy levels of a quantum well depend on the well width, the effective mass of the carriers, and the bandgap difference between the well and the barrier materials.  
- Quantum wells have many applications in optoelectronics, such as lasers, LEDs, photodetectors, and solar cells, because they can modify the optical properties of the semiconductor material, such as absorption, emission, and refractive index.  

## Quantum wire

- A quantum wire is a one-dimensional (1D) structure or a thin rod of a lower bandgap semiconductor material embedded in a higher bandgap matrix material.  
- In a quantum wire, the motion of electrons and holes is confined in two directions (perpendicular to the rod axis), while they are free to move in one direction (along the rod axis).  
- The confinement of carriers in two directions leads to the quantization of their energy levels, which means that they can only occupy discrete energy states.  
- The energy levels of a quantum wire depend on the wire diameter, the effective mass of the carriers, and the bandgap difference between the wire and the matrix materials.  
- Quantum wires have potential applications in nanoelectronics, optoelectronics, information technology, and biotechnology, because they can enhance the electrical and optical properties of the semiconductor material, such as conductivity, mobility, luminescence, and sensitivity.
