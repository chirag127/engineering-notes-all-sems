

# Unit 1 - Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is a branch of physics that deals with the behavior of matter and light on the atomic and subatomic scale. It differs from classical mechanics, which describes the motion of large objects that we can observe directly. Quantum mechanics reveals that the physical world is not deterministic, but probabilistic and uncertain. It also shows that some properties of particles, such as their position and momentum, cannot be measured simultaneously with arbitrary precision, due to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. Quantum mechanics also predicts that particles can behave like waves, and vice versa, as described by the wave-particle duality.

Some of the main topics in quantum mechanics are:

- The Schrödinger equation, which is the fundamental equation of motion for quantum systems.
- The Born rule, which gives the probability of finding a particle in a given state or location.
- The superposition principle, which states that a quantum system can exist in a linear combination of two or more states until an observation collapses it into one definite state.
- The entanglement phenomenon, which occurs when two or more quantum systems share a quantum state and influence each other, even when they are separated by large distances.
- The quantum tunneling effect, which allows a particle to pass through a potential barrier that it would not be able to overcome classically.
- The quantum harmonic oscillator, which is a model of a particle in a simple harmonic potential, such as a spring or a pendulum.
- The hydrogen atom, which is the simplest example of a quantum system with a bound state, where the electron orbits the nucleus due to the Coulomb attraction.
- The spin of a particle, which is a quantum property that gives it a magnetic moment and can have only discrete values.
- The Pauli exclusion principle, which states that two fermions (such as electrons) cannot occupy the same quantum state in the same system.
- The quantum numbers, which are labels that specify the state of a quantum system, such as its energy, angular momentum, spin, etc.



### Inadequacy of classical mechanics

Classical mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of macroscopic objects under the influence of forces. It is based on Newton's laws of motion and the principle of conservation of energy and momentum. Classical mechanics can explain correctly the motion of celestial bodies, such as planets and stars, and microscopic terrestrial bodies, such as bullets and balls, that move with non-relativistic speeds (much less than the speed of light).

However, classical mechanics has some limitations and failures when applied to the phenomena at the atomic and subatomic scales, where the objects move with relativistic speeds (comparable to the speed of light) and exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties. Some of the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot explain are:

- The stability of atoms: According to classical mechanics, an electron orbiting a nucleus should emit electromagnetic radiation and lose energy, causing it to spiral into the nucleus and collapse the atom. However, atoms are stable and do not emit radiation continuously. Classical mechanics cannot account for the discrete energy levels and the quantization of angular momentum of the electrons in atoms.
- The spectra of atoms: When atoms are excited by heat or electricity, they emit light of specific wavelengths, forming a discrete spectrum. Classical mechanics cannot explain why atoms emit only certain frequencies of light and not a continuous spectrum. Moreover, classical mechanics cannot explain the fine structure and the hyperfine structure of the atomic spectra, which are due to the relativistic effects and the interaction between the electron spin and the nuclear spin, respectively.
- The photoelectric effect: When light of a certain frequency or higher is shone on a metal surface, electrons are ejected from the metal. Classical mechanics cannot explain why the kinetic energy of the ejected electrons depends only on the frequency of the light and not on its intensity, and why there is a threshold frequency below which no electrons are emitted, regardless of the intensity of the light. These observations suggest that light behaves like a stream of particles, called photons, that have energy proportional to their frequency.
- The Compton effect: When X-rays are scattered by electrons, the wavelength of the scattered X-rays is longer than the wavelength of the incident X-rays. Classical mechanics cannot explain why the change in wavelength depends on the angle of scattering and not on the intensity of the X-rays, and why the energy and momentum of the X-rays are conserved in the scattering process. These observations imply that X-rays also behave like particles that collide with electrons.
- The blackbody radiation: A blackbody is an ideal object that absorbs and emits all frequencies of electromagnetic radiation. Classical mechanics predicts that the energy density of the blackbody radiation should increase with the frequency without any limit, leading to the ultraviolet catastrophe. However, experiments show that the energy density of the blackbody radiation reaches a maximum at a certain frequency and then decreases for higher frequencies. Classical mechanics cannot explain this deviation from the theoretical prediction.

These and other phenomena show that classical mechanics is inadequate to describe the nature of matter and radiation at the microscopic level. A new theory, called quantum mechanics, was developed in the early 20th century to explain these phenomena and to provide a more accurate and complete description of the physical reality. Quantum mechanics is based on the principles of uncertainty, superposition, and probability, and it uses mathematical tools such as complex numbers, matrices, and operators. Quantum mechanics reveals that the physical world is not deterministic, but probabilistic, and that the observer plays an essential role in the measurement process. Quantum mechanics also shows that matter and radiation have both wave-like and particle-like properties, and that these properties are complementary and cannot be observed simultaneously. Quantum mechanics is one of the most successful and fundamental theories of physics, and it has many applications in various fields of science and technology.



### Planck’s theory of black body radiation (qualitative)

- A black body is a hypothetical body that completely absorbs all radiant energy falling upon it, reaches some equilibrium temperature, and then reemits that energy as quickly as it absorbs it.
- The spectrum of the radiation emitted by a black body depends only on its temperature and not on its shape, size, or composition.
- The classical theory of electromagnetism failed to explain the observed spectrum of black body radiation, especially at high frequencies (short wavelengths), where it predicted an infinite amount of energy, known as the ultraviolet catastrophe.
- In 1900, German physicist Max Planck heuristically derived a formula for the observed spectrum by assuming that a hypothetical electrically charged oscillator in a cavity that contained black-body radiation could only change its energy in a minimal increment, E, that was proportional to the frequency of its associated electromagnetic wave.
- Planck's formula is given by:

```math
B(\nu, T) = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^{\frac{h\nu}{kT}} - 1}
```

where B is the spectral radiance, ν is the frequency, T is the temperature, h is the Planck constant, c is the speed of light, and k is the Boltzmann constant.

- Planck's formula implies that the energy of electromagnetic radiation is quantized, meaning that it can only take discrete values that are multiples of hν.
- Planck's theory of black body radiation is considered to be the birth of quantum mechanics, as it introduced the concept of energy quanta and paved the way for further developments by Albert Einstein, Niels Bohr, and others .

### de-Broglie concept of matter waves

- According to the de-Broglie concept of matter waves, the matter has **dual nature**. It means when the matter is moving it shows the **wave properties** (like interference, diffraction etc.) and when it is in the state of rest then it shows **particle properties**.
- This concept was inspired by the **photon theory** of light, which established that an electromagnetic wave can behave like a particle of light (photon) when interacting with matter.
- The de-Broglie hypothesis proposes that all matter exhibits wave-like properties and relates the observed wavelength of matter (λ) to its momentum (p) by the equation: λ = h/p, where h is the Planck's constant.
- The de-Broglie wavelength of matter is inversely proportional to its momentum, which means that the more massive or faster the particle is, the shorter its wavelength is. For macroscopic objects, the wavelength is negligible, but for microscopic particles, such as electrons, the wavelength can be significant and measurable.
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves was experimentally verified by the **Davisson-Germer experiment** in 1927, which showed that electrons can be diffracted by a crystal lattice, producing a diffraction pattern similar to that of X-rays.
- The de-Broglie concept of matter waves also provided a rationale for the **quantization of the electron's angular momentum** in the hydrogen atom, which was postulated in Bohr's quantum theory. De-Broglie postulated that electrons orbit the nucleus as **standing waves** (stationary waves), which are formed as a result of interference between two waves of equal frequency and amplitude travelling in opposite directions. The condition for a standing wave is that the circumference of the orbit must be an integer multiple of the wavelength, which leads to the quantization of the angular momentum.



### Davisson and Germer Experiment

- The Davisson and Germer experiment was a 1923-27 experiment by Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Western Electric (later Bell Labs), in which electrons, scattered by the surface of a crystal of nickel metal, displayed a diffraction pattern.
- The experiment confirmed the de Broglie hypothesis that matter has wave-like behavior and established the wave–particle duality hypothesis which was a fundamental step in quantum theory.
- The experiment was based on the assumption that waves reflected from two distinct atomic levels of a nickel crystal will have a fixed phase difference and will interfere either constructively or destructively, producing a diffraction pattern .
- The experiment consisted of a vacuum chamber with an electron gun that emitted a beam of electrons towards a nickel target. The target could be rotated to change the angle of incidence of the electrons. A Faraday cup was used to measure the intensity of the scattered electrons at different angles.
- The experiment was initially performed to measure the size of nickel atoms, but the results were inconsistent and irregular due to oxidation of the nickel surface. After heating the target to remove the oxide layer, the experimenters observed a sharp peak in the intensity of the scattered electrons at a certain angle, which was unexpected.
- The peak corresponded to the first-order maximum of a diffraction pattern, implying that the electrons were behaving like waves and were diffracted by the nickel atoms. The wavelength of the electrons was calculated using the Bragg's law and was found to be in agreement with the de Broglie relation.
- The experiment was repeated with different accelerating voltages for the electron beam and the peak angle was found to vary inversely with the square root of the voltage, as predicted by the de Broglie relation.
- The experiment was the first direct evidence of the wave nature of electrons and validated the de Broglie equation for the first time. It also supported the concept of matter waves, which was later developed by Erwin Schrödinger and Werner Heisenberg into the modern quantum mechanics .

### Time-dependent and time-independent Schrodinger wave equations

- The Schrodinger wave equation is a partial differential equation that describes the quantum state of a physical system.
- The quantum state is represented by a complex-valued wave function that depends on the spatial coordinates and time of the system.
- The Schrodinger wave equation can be written in two forms: time-dependent and time-independent.

#### Time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation

- The time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation (TDSE) depicts a system that evolves over time and is thus dependent on the physical state of that system.
- The TDSE has the following expression:

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t} = \hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r},t)
$$

- where $i$ is the imaginary unit, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ is the wave function, $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator, $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector, and $t$ is the time.
- The Hamiltonian operator is the total energy operator of the system, which consists of the kinetic and potential energy operators:

$$
\hat{H} = \hat{T} + \hat{V}
$$

- The TDSE can be derived from the equation that describes the motion of a wave in classical mechanics:

$$
\psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \exp\left[i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r} - \omega t)\right]
$$

- where $\mathbf{k}$ is the wave vector, and $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the wave.
- By applying the momentum and energy operators to the wave function, and equating them to the corresponding eigenvalues, we obtain the TDSE.

#### Time-independent Schrodinger wave equation

- The time-independent Schrodinger wave equation (TISE) describes a system that does not change over time and is thus independent of the physical state of that system.
- The TISE can be obtained from the TDSE by using the method of separation of variables, which assumes that the wave function can be written as a product of a space-only function and a time-only function:

$$
\psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \psi(\mathbf{r})\phi(t)
$$

- By substituting this into the TDSE, and dividing both sides by $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$, we obtain:

$$
i\hbar \frac{1}{\phi(t)} \frac{d\phi(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{\psi(\mathbf{r})} \hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

- Since the left-hand side depends only on time, and the right-hand side depends only on space, they must be equal to a constant, which we denote by $E$, the total energy of the system.
- This gives us two ordinary differential equations:

$$
i\hbar \frac{d\phi(t)}{dt} = E \phi(t)
$$

$$
\hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r}) = E \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

- The first equation can be easily solved to give:

$$
\phi(t) = \exp\left(-\frac{iEt}{\hbar}\right)
$$

- The second equation is the TISE, which has the following expression:

$$
\hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r}) = E \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

- The TISE is an eigenvalue equation, where the eigenvalues are the possible energy levels of the system, and the eigenfunctions are the corresponding stationary states or wave functions.
- The TISE can be solved for different types of potentials, such as the harmonic oscillator, the hydrogen atom, the infinite square well, etc.
- The solutions of the TISE are important as they form a complete basis for the Hilbert space of the system, and any state can be expressed as a linear combination of them.

### Physical interpretation of wave function

- A wave function is a mathematical function that describes the quantum state of a particle or a system of particles.
- The wave function, at a particular time, contains all the information that anybody at that time can have about the particle .
- But the wave function itself has no physical interpretation. It is not measurable . It is complex and can be expressed as \psi(x,y,z,t) = a + ib, where a and b are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit.
- However, the square of the absolute value of the wave function has a physical interpretation. It gives the probability density of finding the particle in a certain location at a given instant  . The probability density is proportional to |\psi(x,y,z,t)|^2.
- The wave function must satisfy some properties to be physically meaningful, such as being normalized, continuous, single-valued, and finite .
- The wave function can be used to determine probabilities and expectation values of physical observables, such as position, momentum, and kinetic energy .
- The wave function evolves in time according to the Schrödinger equation, which is a partial differential equation that relates the wave function to the potential energy of the system .
- The wave function is subject to the uncertainty principle, which states that there is a fundamental limit to the precision with which certain pairs of physical quantities, such as position and momentum, can be known simultaneously .
- The wave function is also subject to the measurement problem, which is the question of how the wave function collapses to a definite state when an observation is made. There are different interpretations of quantum mechanics that attempt to explain this phenomenon, such as the Copenhagen interpretation, the many-worlds interpretation, and the pilot-wave theory.

# Basic concept of Stoke’s theorem and Divergence theorem

- Stoke's theorem and Divergence theorem are two important theorems in vector calculus that relate different types of integrals over different types of regions.
- Stoke's theorem (also known as Kelvin-Stoke's theorem) states that the line integral of a vector field around a closed curve is equal to the surface integral of the curl of the vector field over any surface bounded by the curve.
- Divergence theorem (also known as Gauss's theorem) states that the surface integral of a vector field over a closed surface is equal to the volume integral of the divergence of the vector field over the region enclosed by the surface.
- Both theorems are generalizations of the fundamental theorem of calculus, which states that the integral of a function over an interval is equal to the difference of the values of its antiderivative at the endpoints of the interval.
- Both theorems can be used to simplify the calculation of integrals by transforming them into simpler forms, or to prove other results in vector calculus, such as the identities involving curl and divergence.



### Basic laws of electricity and magnetism

Electricity and magnetism are two aspects of the same phenomenon: electromagnetism. Electric charges and magnetic fields interact with each other in various ways, and these interactions are described by four basic laws:

- **Coulomb's law**: This law states that the electric force between two point charges is proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs and repulsive if they have the same sign. The mathematical expression of Coulomb's law is:

$$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

where $F$ is the force, $k$ is a constant, $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charges, and $r$ is the distance.

- **Gauss's law**: This law states that the electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the net charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space. The electric flux is a measure of how much electric field passes through a given area. The mathematical expression of Gauss's law is:

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$

where $\Phi_E$ is the electric flux, $S$ is the closed surface, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, $d\vec{A}$ is the differential area element, $Q$ is the net charge, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.

- **Ampere's law**: This law states that the magnetic flux through any closed loop is equal to the product of the permeability of free space and the net current enclosed by the loop. The magnetic flux is a measure of how much magnetic field passes through a given area. The mathematical expression of Ampere's law is:

$$\Phi_B = \oint_L \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I$$

where $\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux, $L$ is the closed loop, $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field, $d\vec{l}$ is the differential length element, $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space, and $I$ is the net current.

- **Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction**: This law states that the electromotive force (emf) induced in a loop of wire is equal to the negative rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop. The emf is a measure of how much potential difference is generated by a changing magnetic field. The mathematical expression of Faraday's law is:

$$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

where $\mathcal{E}$ is the emf, and $t$ is the time.

These four laws can be combined into a set of differential equations known as **Maxwell's equations**, which describe the behavior of electric and magnetic fields in any situation. Maxwell's equations also predict the existence of **electromagnetic waves**, which are oscillating electric and magnetic fields that propagate through space at the speed of light. Examples of electromagnetic waves include light, radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays.

This equation implies that the rate of change of total charge in a volume is equal to the net current leaving the volume through the surface.

Maxwell equations are a set of four partial differential equations that describe the relationship between electric and magnetic fields in space and time. They are:

- Gauss's law: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$, where $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field, $\rho$ is the charge density, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.
- Gauss's law for magnetism: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$, where $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field.
- Faraday's law of induction: $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$, where $t$ is the time.
- Ampere's law with Maxwell's correction: $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J} + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$, where $\mathbf{J}$ is the current density, and $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space.

In a vacuum, there is no charge or current, so $\rho = 0$ and $\mathbf{J} = 0$. Therefore, the Maxwell equations in vacuum are:

- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0$
- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$
- $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$
- $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$

These equations imply that electric and magnetic fields can propagate in vacuum as electromagnetic waves, with a constant speed given by $c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \epsilon_0}}$, which is the speed of light.

In a conducting medium, there is a non-zero charge and current, so $\rho \neq 0$ and $\mathbf{J} \neq 0$. Therefore, the Maxwell equations in a conducting medium are:

- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon}$, where $\epsilon$ is the permittivity of the medium.
- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$
- $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$
- $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu \mathbf{J} + \mu \epsilon \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$, where $\mu$ is the permeability of the medium.

These equations imply that electric and magnetic fields can propagate in a conducting medium as electromagnetic waves, with a speed that depends on the properties of the medium, such as its conductivity, permittivity, and permeability. The speed of electromagnetic waves in a conducting medium is generally lower than the speed of light in vacuum.

# Plane electromagnetic waves in conducting medium

- A plane electromagnetic wave is a wave that has constant amplitude and direction of electric and magnetic fields in any plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
- A conducting medium is a medium that has a finite conductivity (\uD835\uDF82), which means that it allows electric currents to flow through it when an electric field is applied.
- When a plane electromagnetic wave propagates in a conducting medium, it experiences attenuation and phase shift due to the presence of electric currents and charges in the medium.
- The electric and magnetic fields of a plane electromagnetic wave in a conducting medium can be written as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{E} &= \vec{E}_0 e^{-\alpha x} e^{i(\omega t - \beta x)} \\
\vec{B} &= \vec{B}_0 e^{-\alpha x} e^{i(\omega t - \beta x)}
\end{aligned}
$$

where \(\vec{E}_0\) and \(\vec{B}_0\) are the amplitudes of the electric and magnetic fields, \(\alpha\) is the attenuation constant, \(\beta\) is the phase constant, \(\omega\) is the angular frequency, and \(x\) is the distance along the direction of propagation.

- The attenuation constant \(\alpha\) measures the rate of decrease of the wave amplitude as it travels in the medium. It depends on the conductivity, permittivity, and frequency of the medium and the wave:

$$
\alpha = \frac{\sqrt{\pi f \mu \sigma}}{2}
$$

where \(f\) is the frequency, \(\mu\) is the permeability, and \(\sigma\) is the conductivity of the medium.

- The phase constant \(\beta\) measures the rate of change of the wave phase as it travels in the medium. It also depends on the conductivity, permittivity, and frequency of the medium and the wave:

$$
\beta = \frac{\omega}{c} \sqrt{1 - \frac{\sigma^2}{4 \epsilon^2 \omega^2}}
$$

where \(c\) is the speed of light in vacuum, and \(\epsilon\) is the permittivity of the medium.

- The ratio of the electric and magnetic fields in a plane electromagnetic wave in a conducting medium is given by the intrinsic impedance of the medium \(\eta\), which is a complex quantity that describes the resistance of the medium to the wave propagation:

$$
\eta = \frac{E}{B} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}} \left( 1 - i \frac{\sigma}{2 \epsilon \omega} \right)
$$

- The real part of \(\eta\) represents the ratio of the magnitudes of the electric and magnetic fields, while the imaginary part represents the phase difference between them. The phase difference indicates that the electric and magnetic fields are not in phase in a conducting medium, unlike in a vacuum or a lossless medium.
- The skin depth \(\delta\) is a parameter that defines the distance at which the wave amplitude decreases by a factor of \(e^{-1}\) or about 37% in the medium. It is inversely proportional to the attenuation constant \(\alpha\):

$$
\delta = \frac{1}{\alpha} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi f \mu \sigma}}
$$

- The skin depth indicates how far the electromagnetic wave can penetrate into the conducting medium before it is significantly attenuated. The skin depth is smaller at higher frequencies, which means that high frequency waves are more easily absorbed by the medium than low frequency waves.

### Resolving power

### Step index and graded index fibers

- Step index and graded index fibers are two types of multimode fibers that are used for optical communication.
- Multimode fibers allow multiple modes or rays of light to propagate through the fiber core, which can cause modal dispersion and limit the bandwidth and distance of the transmission.

- The main differences between step index and graded index fibers are:

  - Step index fiber has higher modal dispersion than graded index fiber, because different modes travel at different speeds and have different path lengths in the fiber.   - Step index fiber has shorter transmission distance than graded index fiber, because modal dispersion causes signal distortion and attenuation over long distances. Graded index fiber has longer transmission distance because it preserves the signal quality and strength over long distances.
  - Step index fiber is cheaper than graded index fiber, because it is easier to manufacture and has simpler structure. Graded index fiber is more expensive than step index fiber, because it requires more complex fabrication and has more sophisticated structure.

### Meissner effect

- The Meissner effect is the expulsion of a magnetic field from the interior of a material that is in the process of becoming a superconductor .
- A superconductor is a material that loses its resistance to the flow of electrical currents when cooled below a certain temperature, called the critical temperature .
- The Meissner effect was discovered by the German physicists W. Meissner and R. Ochsenfeld in 1933 .
- The Meissner effect is a property of all superconductors, and it is one of the defining characteristics of the superconducting state .
- The Meissner effect can be demonstrated by making a magnet levitate above a superconductor, as seen in the figure below:

- The Meissner effect occurs because the superconductor develops a surface current that generates a magnetic field opposite to the applied field, thus canceling it out inside the material .
- The Meissner effect is a consequence of the perfect diamagnetism of superconductors, which means that they have a magnetic susceptibility of -1 and a magnetic permeability of zero .
- The Meissner effect is different from the ordinary diamagnetism of normal materials, which is a weak and linear response to an applied magnetic field .
- The Meissner effect is also different from the Lenz's law, which states that an induced current in a conductor opposes the change in the magnetic flux that caused it .
- The Meissner effect is explained by the BCS theory of superconductivity, which states that the superconducting state is formed by the pairing of electrons into Cooper pairs, which have a lower energy than the individual electrons .
- The Meissner effect is a thermodynamic phenomenon, which means that it depends on the minimization of the free energy of the system .
- The Meissner effect is not absolute, as it can be destroyed by a magnetic field that exceeds a critical value, called the critical field .
- The Meissner effect is also influenced by the shape and size of the superconductor, as well as the presence of impurities and defects in the material .
- The Meissner effect has many applications in science and technology, such as magnetic levitation, magnetic resonance imaging, superconducting magnets, and quantum computing .



### Temperature dependence of critical field for superconductors

- The critical field depends on the temperature of the superconductor, and generally decreases as the temperature increases.
- The temperature dependence of the critical field can be described approximately by the empirical formula:

```math
B_c(T) = B_c(0) [1 - (T/T_c)^2]
```

where $B_c(0)$ is the critical field at absolute zero temperature, and $T_c$ is the critical temperature of the superconductor.

- The formula is valid for low-temperature superconductors (LTS), which have $T_c$ below 30 K, and for magnetic fields much smaller than the upper critical field $H_{c2}$, which is the maximum field that can penetrate the superconductor in the mixed state.

### Type I and Type II Superconductors

- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and perfect diamagnetism below a certain critical temperature.
- Superconductors can be classified into two types based on their response to an external magnetic field: type I and type II.
- Type I superconductors have only one critical magnetic field, Hc. When the applied magnetic field exceeds Hc, the superconductivity is destroyed and the material becomes normal.
- Type I superconductors are usually pure metals, such as mercury, lead, tin, aluminum, etc. They have a low critical temperature, typically below 10 K, and a low critical magnetic field, typically below 0.1 T.
- Type I superconductors exhibit the Meissner effect, which means they expel all the magnetic flux from their interior when they become superconducting. They have no mixed state or vortex state.
- Type II superconductors have two critical magnetic fields, Hc1 and Hc2. When the applied magnetic field is between Hc1 and Hc2, the superconductivity is not destroyed but weakened. The material enters a mixed state or vortex state, where some magnetic flux penetrates the superconductor in the form of quantized vortices.
- Type II superconductors are usually metal alloys or complex oxide ceramics. They have a high critical temperature, up to 138 K, and a high critical magnetic field, up to several tesla. They include all the high-temperature superconductors, such as YBCO, BSCCO, etc.
- Type II superconductors also exhibit the Meissner effect, but only below Hc1. Above Hc1, they allow some magnetic flux to enter, and above Hc2, they become normal. They have a more complex and rich phase diagram than type I superconductors.
