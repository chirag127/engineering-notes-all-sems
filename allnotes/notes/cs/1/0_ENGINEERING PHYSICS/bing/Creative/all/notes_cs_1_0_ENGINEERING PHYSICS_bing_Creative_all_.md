

# Unit 1 - Quantum Mechanics

- Quantum mechanics is a subfield of physics that describes the behavior of particles — atoms, electrons, photons and almost everything in the molecular and submolecular realm .
- Quantum mechanics attempts to describe and account for the properties of molecules and atoms and their constituents— electrons, protons, neutrons, and other more esoteric particles such as quarks and gluons.
- Quantum mechanics was developed during the first half of the 20th century, as a result of the failure of classical physics to explain certain phenomena, such as the blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, and the atomic spectra.
- Quantum mechanics is based on the principle of wave-particle duality, which states that every particle can also behave like a wave, and vice versa.
- Quantum mechanics introduces the concept of uncertainty, which means that certain physical quantities, such as the position and momentum of a particle, cannot be measured simultaneously with arbitrary precision.
- Quantum mechanics also implies the existence of superposition, which means that a quantum system can exist in a combination of two or more mutually exclusive states until an observation is made.
- Quantum mechanics is often extremely strange and counterintuitive, and challenges our common sense and classical logic.
- Quantum mechanics has many applications in various fields of science and technology, such as atomic physics, nuclear physics, condensed matter physics, quantum chemistry, quantum optics, quantum information, quantum cryptography, and quantum computing.



# Inadequacy of Classical Mechanics

Classical mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of macroscopic objects under the influence of forces. It is based on Newton's laws of motion and the principle of conservation of energy and momentum. Classical mechanics can explain correctly the motion of celestial bodies, such as planets and stars, and microscopic terrestrial bodies, such as bullets and balls, that move with non-relativistic speeds (much less than the speed of light).

However, classical mechanics fails to explain certain phenomena observed in nature, especially at the atomic and sub-atomic scales, where the objects move with relativistic speeds (comparable to the speed of light) and exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties. Some of the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot account for are:

- The stability of atoms: According to classical mechanics, an electron orbiting a nucleus should emit electromagnetic radiation and lose energy, eventually falling into the nucleus. This would make atoms unstable and collapse. However, atoms are observed to be stable and emit radiation only when they undergo transitions between discrete energy levels.
- The spectra of black body radiation: A black body is an idealized object that absorbs and emits all frequencies of electromagnetic radiation. According to classical mechanics, the energy of the radiation emitted by a black body should be proportional to the frequency and the number of modes of vibration. This leads to the prediction that the black body should emit infinite energy at high frequencies, which is known as the ultraviolet catastrophe. However, the observed spectrum of black body radiation shows that the energy emitted decreases at high frequencies and follows a different law, known as Planck's law.
- The photoelectric effect: The photoelectric effect is the phenomenon of emission of electrons from a metal surface when it is exposed to light. According to classical mechanics, the energy of the emitted electrons should depend on the intensity of the light and not on its frequency. However, the observed photoelectric effect shows that the energy of the emitted electrons depends on the frequency of the light and not on its intensity, and that there is a minimum frequency, known as the threshold frequency, below which no electrons are emitted, regardless of the intensity of the light.
- The Compton effect: The Compton effect is the phenomenon of scattering of X-rays by electrons. According to classical mechanics, the wavelength of the scattered X-rays should be the same as the wavelength of the incident X-rays. However, the observed Compton effect shows that the wavelength of the scattered X-rays is longer than the wavelength of the incident X-rays, and that the change in wavelength depends on the angle of scattering and the mass of the electron.
- The discrete spectra of atoms: The discrete spectra of atoms are the characteristic lines of radiation emitted or absorbed by atoms when they undergo transitions between different energy levels. According to classical mechanics, the energy levels of atoms should be continuous and the spectra of atoms should be continuous. However, the observed spectra of atoms show that the energy levels of atoms are discrete and the spectra of atoms are discrete, and that the frequencies of the spectral lines follow a simple formula, known as the Rydberg formula.

These phenomena, and many others, show that classical mechanics is inadequate to describe the behavior of matter and radiation at the atomic and sub-atomic scales, and that a new theory is needed to explain them. This new theory is quantum mechanics, which is based on the principles of uncertainty, superposition, and probability. Quantum mechanics introduces the concept of wave-particle duality, which states that matter and radiation can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties, depending on the situation. Quantum mechanics also introduces the concept of quantization, which states that certain physical quantities, such as energy, momentum, and angular momentum, can only take discrete values, rather than continuous values. Quantum mechanics can account for all the phenomena that classical mechanics cannot, and also reduces to classical mechanics in the limit of large objects and low speeds. Quantum mechanics is the most successful and accurate theory of physics to date, and it forms the basis of many fields of science and technology, such as atomic physics, nuclear physics, solid state physics, chemistry, and nanotechnology.

# Compton Effect

- The Compton effect was discovered by **Arthur Compton** in 1923, who measured the scattering of X-rays by graphite and observed that the scattered X-rays had a longer wavelength than the incident ones .

# De-Broglie Concept of Matter Waves

- De-Broglie concept of matter waves is based on the idea that matter has a dual nature, i.e., it can exhibit both particle-like and wave-like properties depending on the situation .
- De-Broglie proposed that when matter is in motion, it is associated with a wave of a certain wavelength, called the de-Broglie wavelength, given by the formula  :

  $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

  where $\lambda$ is the de-Broglie wavelength, $h$ is the Planck's constant, and $p$ is the momentum of the matter.
- De-Broglie wavelength is inversely proportional to the momentum of the matter, which means that the faster the matter moves, the shorter its wavelength becomes. Conversely, the slower the matter moves, the longer its wavelength becomes  .
- De-Broglie wavelength is also inversely proportional to the mass of the matter, which means that the heavier the matter is, the shorter its wavelength becomes. Conversely, the lighter the matter is, the longer its wavelength becomes  .
- De-Broglie concept of matter waves can explain the quantization of the angular momentum of the electron in the hydrogen atom, which was postulated by Bohr's quantum theory. According to de-Broglie, the electron in the hydrogen atom can be regarded as a standing wave that can only have certain wavelengths that fit into the circumference of the orbit .
- De-Broglie concept of matter waves can also explain the phenomenon of electron diffraction, which is the bending of electron beams when they pass through a narrow slit or a crystal lattice. This shows that electrons can behave like waves and interfere with each other .
- De-Broglie concept of matter waves is a generalization of the wave-particle duality of light, which was established by Einstein's photon theory and Compton's formula. According to de-Broglie, not only light but also matter can have both wave-like and particle-like properties  .



# Davisson and Germer Experiment

- The Davisson and Germer experiment was a 1923-27 experiment by Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Western Electric (later Bell Labs), in which electrons, scattered by the surface of a crystal of nickel metal, displayed a diffraction pattern.
- The experiment confirmed the de Broglie hypothesis that matter has wave-like behavior and established the wave–particle duality hypothesis which was a fundamental step in quantum theory.
- The experiment was based on the assumption that waves reflected from two distinct atomic levels of a Ni crystal will have a fixed phase difference and will interfere either constructively or destructively, producing a diffraction pattern .
- The experiment consisted of a vacuum chamber with an electron gun that emitted a beam of electrons towards a nickel target. The target could be rotated to change the angle of incidence of the electrons. A Faraday cup was used to measure the intensity of the scattered electrons at different angles.
- The experiment was initially performed to study the surface structure of nickel, but the results were unexpected and inconsistent. The experimenters found that the intensity of the scattered electrons varied with the angle and the voltage of the electron beam.
- The experiment was interrupted by an accident that damaged the vacuum chamber and the nickel target. The target was repaired by heating it to a high temperature, which changed its crystalline structure. When the experiment was resumed, the results showed a clear peak of intensity at a certain angle, which corresponded to the Bragg's law of X-ray diffraction.
- The experimenters realized that they had accidentally produced a diffraction pattern of electrons, which implied that electrons behaved like waves. They compared their results with the de Broglie equation, which relates the wavelength of matter waves to the momentum of the particles, and found a good agreement.
- The experiment was repeated with different materials and voltages, and the results confirmed the wave nature of electrons and the validity of the de Broglie equation.
- The experiment was a landmark in the development of quantum mechanics, as it provided the first direct evidence of the wave–particle duality of matter. It also stimulated further research on electron diffraction and the structure of matter.



# Phase velocity and group velocity

- Phase velocity and group velocity are two important and related concepts in wave mechanics. They arise in quantum mechanics in the time development of the state function for the continuous case, i.e. wave packets.
- The phase velocity of a wave is the rate at which the wave propagates in any medium. This is the velocity at which the phase of any one frequency component of the wave travels.
- The group velocity of a wave is the velocity with which the overall envelope shape of the wave's amplitudes—known as the modulation or envelope of the wave—propagates through space.
- The phase velocity and the group velocity are related through Rayleigh's formula:

$$v_g = v_p + \lambda \frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$$

- If the derivative term is zero, group velocity equals phase velocity. In this case, there is no dispersion.
- In quantum mechanics, the phase velocity and the group velocity have different interpretations for the associated particle. The phase velocity is related to the total energy of the particle, while the group velocity is related to the kinetic energy of the particle.
- Using the de Broglie postulates, we can show that the group velocity of a free particle is twice the phase velocity:

$$\lambda = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{p}$$
$$v_p = \frac{\omega}{\lambda} = \frac{E}{2\pi\hbar} \cdot \frac{p}{2\pi\hbar} = \frac{pE}{(2\pi\hbar)^2}$$
$$v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{d}{dk} \left( \frac{E}{\hbar} \right) = \frac{1}{\hbar} \frac{dE}{dk} = \frac{1}{\hbar} \frac{d}{dk} \left( \frac{p^2}{2m} \right) = \frac{p}{m}$$
$$v_g = \frac{p}{m} = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{\lambda} \cdot \frac{1}{m} = \frac{2}{\lambda} \cdot \frac{p}{2\pi\hbar} = 2v_p$$



# Time-dependent and time-independent Schrodinger wave equations

- The Schrodinger wave equation is a partial differential equation that describes the quantum state of a physical system.
- The quantum state is represented by a complex-valued wave function that depends on the spatial coordinates and time of the system.
- The Schrodinger wave equation can be written in two forms: time-dependent and time-independent.

## Time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation

- The time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation is the general form of the equation that applies to any quantum system that evolves over time.
- The time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation has the following expression:

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t} = \hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r},t)
$$

- Where $i$ is the imaginary unit, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ is the wave function, $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator, $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector, and $t$ is the time.
- The Hamiltonian operator is the total energy operator of the system, which consists of the kinetic and potential energy operators.
- The time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation is a linear equation, which means that any linear combination of solutions is also a solution.
- The time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation is also a unitary equation, which means that it preserves the normalization and probability of the wave function.

## Time-independent Schrodinger wave equation

- The time-independent Schrodinger wave equation is a special case of the time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation that applies to quantum systems that do not change over time, or are in a stationary state.
- A stationary state is a quantum state that has a definite energy and a wave function that oscillates in time with a fixed frequency.
- The time-independent Schrodinger wave equation can be obtained by using the separation of variables method on the time-dependent Schrodinger wave equation, assuming that the wave function can be written as a product of a spatial part and a temporal part:

$$
\psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \psi(\mathbf{r}) e^{-i\omega t}
$$

- Where $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the spatial part, $e^{-i\omega t}$ is the temporal part, and $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the wave function.
- Plugging this ansatz in the time-dependent Schrodinger equation, you get the so-called time-independent Schrodinger equation:

$$
\hat{H} \psi(\mathbf{r}) = E \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$

- Where $E$ is the energy of the stationary state, which is related to the angular frequency by $E = \hbar \omega$.
- The time-independent Schrodinger equation is an eigenvalue problem, so solving for $E$ and $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ will give you the energies for which a stationary state exists, as well as the wave functions of the said states.
- The time-independent Schrodinger equation is also a Hermitian equation, which means that it has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenfunctions.



# Physical interpretation of wave function

- A wave function is a mathematical function that describes the quantum state of a particle or a system of particles.
- The wave function, at a particular time, contains all the information that anybody at that time can have about the particle .
- But the wave function itself has no physical interpretation. It is not measurable . It is complex and can be expressed as \Psi(x, y, z, t) = a + ib, where a and b are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit.
- However, the square of the absolute value of the wave function has a physical interpretation. It gives the probability density of finding the particle in a certain location at a given instant  .
- The probability density is proportional to |\Psi(x, y, z, t)|^2, where | | denotes the modulus or the magnitude of the complex number. The probability density is a real and positive quantity that can be measured by repeated experiments .
- The wave function must satisfy some properties to be physically meaningful, such as normalization, continuity, differentiability, and boundary conditions .
- The wave function can also be used to determine the expectation values of physical observables, such as position, momentum, and kinetic energy. The expectation value is the average value of the observable over many measurements .
- The wave function is governed by the Schrödinger equation, which is a partial differential equation that relates the wave function to the potential energy and the total energy of the particle or the system .
- The wave function is not unique, but can be multiplied by any complex constant or phase factor without changing the physical predictions. This is known as the gauge symmetry of quantum mechanics .
- The physical meaning of the wave function is an important interpretive problem of quantum mechanics. There are different philosophical approaches to understand the nature and role of the wave function, such as the Copenhagen interpretation, the many-worlds interpretation, the pilot-wave theory, and the hidden variables theory.



# Particle in a one-dimensional box

- A particle in a one-dimensional box is a fundamental quantum mechanical approximation describing the translational motion of a single particle confined inside an infinitely deep well from which it cannot escape  .
- The simplest form of the particle in a box model considers a one-dimensional system. Here, the particle may only move backwards and forwards along a straight line with impenetrable barriers at either end. The walls of a one-dimensional box may be seen as regions of space with an infinitely large potential energy.
- The particle in a box model can be used to explain various phenomena, such as the absorption spectra of conjugated molecules, the electronic structure of quantum dots, and the quantum confinement of electrons in nanowires.
- The particle in a box model can be solved by applying the Schrödinger equation, which is a differential equation that relates the wavefunction of the particle to its energy and potential. # Unit 2 - Electromagnetic Field Theory

- Electromagnetic field theory is the study of the interaction between electric and magnetic fields, and the sources that produce them, such as electric charges and currents .
- Electromagnetic fields are described by Maxwell's equations, which are a set of four differential equations that relate the electric and magnetic fields to their sources and to each other  .

# Basic concept of Stoke’s theorem and Divergence theorem

- Stoke's theorem and Divergence theorem are two important theorems in vector calculus that relate different types of integrals over different types of regions.
- Stoke's theorem (also known as Kelvin-Stoke's theorem) relates a surface integral of the curl of a vector field to a line integral of the vector field along the boundary of the surface.
- Divergence theorem (also known as Gauss's theorem) relates a volume integral of the divergence of a vector field to a surface integral of the vector field over the boundary of the volume.
- Both theorems are generalizations of the fundamental theorem of calculus, which relates a definite integral of a function to the values of its antiderivative at the endpoints of the interval of integration.
- Both theorems can be used to simplify the calculation of integrals over complicated regions by transforming them into integrals over simpler regions.
- Both theorems have important applications in physics, such as electromagnetism, fluid dynamics, and heat transfer.

## Stoke's theorem

- The statement of Stoke's theorem is:

  $$\iint_S \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$

  where $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field, $S$ is an oriented surface, $\partial S$ is the boundary curve of $S$, and $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ is the curl of $\mathbf{F}$.
- The surface $S$ must be smooth, closed (i.e., without holes), and orientable (i.e., having two distinct sides). The orientation of $S$ determines the orientation of $\partial S$ by the right-hand rule: if the fingers of the right hand point along the surface normal, then the thumb points along the positive direction of the boundary curve.
- The curl of a vector field $\mathbf{F} = (P, Q, R)$ is defined as:

  $$\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left( \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right)$$

  where $\nabla = (\frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial}{\partial z})$ is the del operator.
- The curl of a vector field measures the tendency of the field to rotate around a point. It is a vector that is perpendicular to the plane of rotation and has a magnitude equal to the angular velocity of the rotation.
- The surface integral of the curl of a vector field over a surface $S$ measures the net circulation of the field around the boundary of $S$. It is a scalar that is positive if the circulation is in the same direction as the orientation of $\partial S$, and negative otherwise.
- The line integral of a vector field along a curve $C$ measures the work done by the field along the curve. It is a scalar that is positive if the field is in the same direction as the orientation of $C$, and negative otherwise.
- The physical interpretation of Stoke's theorem is that the work done by a vector field along a closed curve is equal to the net circulation of the field over any surface that has the curve as its boundary.

# Basic laws of electricity and magnetism

Electricity and magnetism are two aspects of the same phenomenon: electromagnetism. Electric charges and magnetic fields interact with each other in various ways, and these interactions are governed by four basic laws:

- **Coulomb's law**: This law states that the electric force between two point charges is proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs and repulsive if they have the same sign. The mathematical expression of Coulomb's law is:

$$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

where $F$ is the force, $k$ is a constant, $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charges, and $r$ is the distance.

- **Gauss's law**: This law states that the electric flux through any closed surface is equal to the net charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space. The electric flux is a measure of the number of electric field lines passing through the surface. The mathematical expression of Gauss's law is:

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$

where $\Phi_E$ is the electric flux, $\vec{E}$ is the electric field, $d\vec{A}$ is a differential area element of the surface, $Q$ is the net charge, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.

- **Ampere's law**: This law states that the magnetic flux through any closed loop is equal to the product of the permeability of free space and the net current passing through the loop. The magnetic flux is a measure of the number of magnetic field lines passing through the loop. The mathematical expression of Ampere's law is:

$$\oint_L \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I$$

where $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field, $d\vec{l}$ is a differential length element of the loop, $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space, and $I$ is the net current.

- **Faraday's law of induction**: This law states that the electromotive force (emf) induced in a loop of wire is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop. where $\mathcal{E}$ is the emf, and $\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux.

These four laws can be combined into a set of differential equations known as **Maxwell's equations**, which describe the behavior of electric and magnetic fields in any situation. Examples of electromagnetic waves include light, radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays.



# Continuity equation for current density

- Current density is a measure of the amount of electric current flowing through a unit area of a conductor. It is denoted by the symbol J and has the unit of ampere per square meter (A/m^2) .
- The formula for current density is given as, J = I / A, where I is the current flowing through the conductor in amperes and A is the cross-sectional area of the conductor in square meters .
- The continuity equation for current density is a mathematical expression that relates the change in charge density (ρ) in a region of space to the current density (J) flowing out of that region. It is based on the principle of conservation of charge, which states that the net charge in a closed system is constant .
- The differential form of the continuity equation is: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇ ⋅ J = 0, where t is the time, ρ is the charge density, and ∇ ⋅ J is the divergence of the current density  .
- The integral form of the continuity equation is: d/dt ∫V ρ d^3 r = - ∮S J ⋅ n d^2 r, where V is an arbitrary stationary volume limited by the closed surface S, n is the unit normal vector to the surface, and J ⋅ n is the dot product of the current density and the normal vector .
- The continuity equation implies that the charge density in a region can only change if there is a net current flowing out of the region. Conversely, if the charge density is constant, then the current density must have zero divergence, meaning that the current is conserved at any point in the region  .
- The continuity equation is analogous to the hydrodynamical continuity equation, which relates the change in mass density (ρ) in a fluid to the mass flux density (ρu), where u is the fluid velocity. The continuity equation for mass density is: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇ ⋅ (ρu) = 0  .
- The continuity equation is also related to the Kirchhoff's current law, which states that the algebraic sum of the currents entering and leaving a node in an electric circuit is zero. This law can be derived from the continuity equation by applying it to a small volume around the node and taking the limit as the volume shrinks to zero .

# Poynting vector and Poynting theorem

- The **Poynting vector** is a vector quantity that represents the directional energy flux (the energy transfer per unit area per unit time) of an electromagnetic field.
- The Poynting vector is defined as the cross product of the electric field vector **E** and the magnetic field vector **H**, that is, **S = E x H** .
- The Poynting vector is named after its discoverer, J.H. Poynting, who derived it in 1884.
- The Poynting vector has the units of power per unit area, or watts per square meter (W/m^2^).
- The Poynting vector is used throughout electromagnetics in conjunction with **Poynting's theorem**, the continuity equation expressing conservation of electromagnetic energy, to calculate the power flow in electromagnetic fields.

- **Poynting's theorem** states that the net electromagnetic power flowing into a region of space may be either dissipated, or used to change the energy stored in electric and magnetic fields within that region.
- Poynting's theorem can be derived from Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force law, and can be written in integral or differential form.
- The integral form of Poynting's theorem is:

  \[ \oint_S \mathbf{S} \cdot d\mathbf{a} = -\frac{d}{dt} \int_V \left( \frac{1}{2} \epsilon_0 E^2 + \frac{1}{2} \mu_0 H^2 \right) dV - \int_V \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} dV \]

  where **S** is the Poynting vector, **a** is the surface element vector, **V** is the volume enclosed by the surface **S**, **E** is the electric field, **H** is the magnetic field, **J** is the current density, and **ε0** and **μ0** are the permittivity and permeability of free space, respectively.

- The differential form of Poynting's theorem is:

  \[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{S} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{1}{2} \epsilon_0 E^2 + \frac{1}{2} \mu_0 H^2 \right) - \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} \]

  where **S**, **E**, **H**, **J**, **ε0**, and **μ0** are the same as above, and **∇** is the divergence operator.

- Poynting's theorem can be interpreted as follows: the net power crossing any closed surface is equal to the negative of the rate of change of the electromagnetic energy inside the surface, minus the power dissipated as heat or other forms of energy inside the surface.
- Poynting's theorem can be used to calculate the power radiated by an antenna, the power absorbed by a circuit element, the power transmitted by a waveguide, and the power reflected by a boundary, among other applications.

# Skin depth

Skin depth is a concept that describes how electromagnetic waves penetrate into a conducting material. It is defined as the distance over which the magnitude of the electric or magnetic field is reduced by a factor of 1/e (about 37%) of the value at the surface . Alternatively, it can be interpreted as the distance at which the power in the wave is reduced by a factor of (1/e)^2 (about 13.5%).

Skin depth depends on the frequency of the wave, the electrical conductivity of the material, and the magnetic permeability of the material. The higher the frequency, the lower the skin depth. The higher the conductivity or the permeability, the lower the skin depth. The skin depth can be calculated by the following formula :

$$\delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f \mu \sigma}}$$

where:

- $\delta$ is the skin depth
- $f$ is the frequency of the wave
- $\mu$ is the magnetic permeability of the material
- $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity of the material

Skin depth is important for understanding the behavior of electromagnetic waves in conductors, such as metals. For example, skin depth explains why high-frequency currents tend to flow on the surface of a conductor, rather than through its bulk. This phenomenon is known as the skin effect. Skin depth also affects the attenuation and reflection of electromagnetic waves at the interface of a conductor and a non-conductor, such as air. Skin depth can be used to design antennas, transmission lines, shielding, and eddy current testing devices.

# Interference in uniform and wedge shaped thin films

## Introduction

Interference is the phenomenon of superposition of two or more waves of the same frequency and phase, resulting in a new wave pattern. Interference can be constructive or destructive, depending on the relative phase difference between the waves. Interference can occur in any type of wave, such as sound, light, water, etc.

One of the common examples of interference of light waves is the thin film interference. A thin film is a layer of material with a thickness that is comparable to the wavelength of light. When light is incident on a thin film, some of it is reflected from the top surface and some of it is transmitted into the film. The transmitted light can then be reflected from the bottom surface of the film and emerge out of the film. The reflected light from the top and bottom surfaces can interfere with each other, producing a pattern of bright and dark fringes.

The thin film interference depends on the following factors:

- The thickness of the film
- The wavelength of light
- The angle of incidence of light
- The refractive indices of the film and the surrounding media

## Interference in uniform thin films

A uniform thin film is a film with a constant thickness. A common example of a uniform thin film is a soap bubble or an oil slick on water. When white light is incident on a uniform thin film, different wavelengths of light undergo different phase shifts upon reflection and transmission, resulting in different interference conditions. Therefore, the film appears to have different colors depending on the viewing angle and the film thickness.

The condition for constructive interference in a uniform thin film is given by:

$$2t = (m + \frac{1}{2})\lambda_n$$

where $t$ is the film thickness, $m$ is an integer, $\lambda_n$ is the wavelength of light in the film, and $n$ is the refractive index of the film.

The condition for destructive interference in a uniform thin film is given by:

$$2t = m\lambda_n$$

where $t$, $m$, $\lambda_n$, and $n$ are the same as above.

## Interference in wedge shaped thin films

A wedge shaped thin film is a film with a varying thickness, such as a thin air gap between two glass plates. When monochromatic light is incident on a wedge shaped thin film, the film thickness changes along the direction of the wedge, resulting in different path differences and phase shifts for the reflected light from the top and bottom surfaces. Therefore, the interference pattern consists of straight parallel fringes that are perpendicular to the edge of the wedge.

The condition for constructive interference in a wedge shaped thin film is given by:

$$t = (m + \frac{1}{2})\frac{\lambda}{2}$$

where $t$ is the film thickness at the point of observation, $m$ is an integer, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light in air.

The condition for destructive interference in a wedge shaped thin film is given by:

$$t = m\frac{\lambda}{2}$$

where $t$, $m$, and $\lambda$ are the same as above.

The fringe width, or the distance between two consecutive bright or dark fringes, is given by:

$$\beta = \frac{\lambda L}{2d}$$

where $L$ is the length of the wedge, and $d$ is the distance between the wedge and the screen.

- Controlling the thickness of paint that is used on posters. Too much paint would exceed total weight requirements, and too little paint would not cover the poster adequately. By observing the interference pattern of the paint film on a flat surface, the optimal thickness can be determined.
- Measuring the refractive index of liquids by placing a drop of liquid between the lens and the plate and observing the change in the radius of the rings. The refractive index can be calculated using the formula:

$$n=\frac{R}{t}\sqrt{\frac{r^2}{R^2}-\frac{t^2}{R^2}}$$

where $n$ is the refractive index, $R$ is the radius of curvature of the lens, $t$ is the thickness of the liquid film, and $r$ is the radius of the ring.

- Determining the wavelength of monochromatic light by measuring the diameter of the rings and using the formula:

$$D_{2n}^2-D_{2n-1}^2=4R\lambda$$

where $D_{2n}$ and $D_{2n-1}$ are the diameters of the $2n$th and $(2n-1)$th dark rings, $R$ is the radius of curvature of the lens, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light.



# Introduction to diffraction

- Diffraction is the process by which a beam of light or other system of waves is spread out as a result of passing through a narrow aperture or across an edge, typically accompanied by interference between the wave forms produced  .
- Diffraction occurs when the size of the obstacle or the slit is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength of the incident wave.
- Diffraction is a consequence of the wave nature of light and other electromagnetic radiation, as well as of sound waves and particles that exhibit wave-like behavior, such as electrons and neutrons.
- Diffraction can produce various patterns of light and dark or colored bands, depending on the shape and arrangement of the diffracting object, the wavelength of the incident wave, and the distance between the object and the screen where the pattern is observed.
- Diffraction can be classified into two types: Fresnel diffraction and Fraunhofer diffraction, depending on whether the source and the screen are close to or far from the diffracting object.
- Fresnel diffraction occurs when the source and the screen are at a finite distance from the diffracting object, and the wavefronts are spherical or cylindrical. Fresnel diffraction can be observed in the case of a single slit, a circular aperture, a straight edge, etc.
- Fraunhofer diffraction occurs when the source and the screen are at an infinite distance from the diffracting object, and the wavefronts are plane. Fraunhofer diffraction can be observed in the case of a double slit, a diffraction grating, a single slit with a lens, etc.
- Diffraction is an important phenomenon in many fields of science and engineering, such as optics, acoustics, crystallography, microscopy, spectroscopy, astronomy, etc. Diffraction can be used to study the structure and properties of matter, to create images and holograms, to measure wavelengths and distances, to analyze spectra and colors, etc.



# Fraunhofer Diffraction at Single Slit and Double Slit

- Fraunhofer diffraction is a type of diffraction that occurs when plane waves are incident on a diffracting object, and the diffraction pattern is viewed at a sufficiently long distance from the object (in the far-field region), or at the focal plane of a converging lens.
- Fraunhofer diffraction can be analyzed using the Huygens-Fresnel principle, which states that every point of the wavefront in the plane of the diffracting object acts as a secondary source and sends secondary waves in all directions .
- Fraunhofer diffraction can be used to study the shape and size of the diffracting object, such as a slit, a grating, a circular aperture, etc.

## Fraunhofer Diffraction at a Single Slit

- Let a parallel beam of monochromatic light of wavelength `λ` be incident on a slit of width `d` as shown in the figure below. The slit is perpendicular to the plane of the paper and is placed at the origin of the coordinate system.

Single slit diffraction

- According to the Huygens-Fresnel principle, every point of the slit acts as a secondary source and sends secondary waves in all directions. The secondary waves interfere with each other and form a diffraction pattern on a screen placed at a distance `D` from the slit, where `D` is much larger than `d` and `λ`.
- The intensity of the diffraction pattern at any point on the screen depends on the phase difference between the secondary waves reaching that point. The phase difference in turn depends on the path difference between the secondary waves.
- To find the path difference, we consider a point `P` on the screen at an angle `θ` from the central axis of the slit. We divide the slit into two equal halves, and consider two points `A` and `B` at the center of each half. The path difference between the secondary waves from `A` and `B` is given by `AB sin θ`, where `AB = d/2`.
- Similarly, we can divide each half of the slit into two equal parts, and consider two points `C` and `D` at the center of each part. The path difference between the secondary waves from `C` and `D` is given by `CD sin θ`, where `CD = d/4`.
- We can continue this process of dividing the slit into smaller and smaller parts, and find the path difference between the secondary waves from any two adjacent points on the slit. The path difference is always given by `ds sin θ`, where `ds` is the width of each part.
- The phase difference between the secondary waves from any two adjacent points on the slit is given by `φ = 2π/λ ds sin θ`. The phase difference between the secondary waves from the two ends of the slit is given by `Φ = 2π/λ d sin θ`.
- The amplitude of the resultant wave at `P` is given by the superposition of all the secondary waves from the slit. Using the principle of superposition, we can write the amplitude as `A = A0 sin(Φ/2) / (Φ/2)`, where `A0` is the amplitude of each secondary wave.
- The intensity of the resultant wave at `P` is proportional to the square of the amplitude, i.e., `I = I0 (sin(Φ/2) / (Φ/2))^2`, where `I0` is the intensity of each secondary wave.
- The intensity of the diffraction pattern at `P` depends on the value of `Φ`, which in turn depends on the angle `θ`. The intensity is maximum when `Φ` is zero or a multiple of `2π`, i.e., when `sin θ` is zero or a multiple of `λ/d`. The intensity is minimum when `Φ` is an odd multiple of `π`, i.e., when `sin θ` is an odd multiple of `λ/2d`.
- The central maximum of the diffraction pattern is the brightest and the widest, and the intensity and the width of the maxima decrease as we move away from the center. The minima are equally spaced



# Absent spectra

- Absent spectra are the missing orders of principal maxima in the diffraction pattern of a plane diffraction grating.
- They occur when the angle of diffraction θ is such that the minima due to diffraction component in the intensity distribution falls at the same positions of principal maxima due to interference component.
- The condition for absent spectra is given by:

$$
n\lambda = (N-1)d\sin\theta
$$

where n is the order of the absent spectrum, λ is the wavelength of light, N is the number of slits, d is the grating element, and θ is the angle of diffraction.

- The above equation implies that the absent spectra are more likely to occur for larger values of n, N, and λ.
- Absent spectra reduce the resolving power of a diffraction grating, which is the ability to distinguish between two closely spaced wavelengths.
- Absent spectra can be avoided by using a blazed grating, which is a grating with a sawtooth-shaped profile that reflects more light in a particular direction.



# Diffraction Grating

- A diffraction grating can be made of a glass plate or a mirror with a large number of equidistant parallel lines or grooves on its surface. It can also be made of a thin film of metal with a series of slits cut in it.
- A diffraction grating causes diffraction and interference of light, ultraviolet radiation, or X-rays, depending on the spacing of the lines or slits and the wavelength of the incident radiation.
- A diffraction grating can be used to produce a spectrum of colors by splitting white light into its component wavelengths. It can also be used to measure the wavelength of monochromatic light by using the formula:

  `d sin θ = m λ`

  where `d` is the grating spacing, `θ` is the diffraction angle, `m` is the order of diffraction, and `λ` is the wavelength of light.
- A diffraction grating can also be used to analyze the emission and absorption spectra of various elements, molecules, and compounds, by comparing the positions and intensities of the spectral lines. It can also be used to study the polarization and coherence of light.



# Spectra with grating

- A **grating** is an optical device that consists of a large number of equally spaced parallel slits or grooves on a transparent or reflective surface. It is used to produce a **spectrum** of light by diffracting the incident light into different directions according to the wavelength.
- A **spectrum** is the distribution of light intensity as a function of wavelength or frequency. It can be **continuous**, **emission** or **absorption** depending on the source of light and the medium through which it passes.
- A **diffraction grating spectrometer** is an instrument that uses a grating to analyze the spectrum of a light source. It consists of a **collimator**, a **grating**, an **adjustable telescope** and a **scale**. The collimator produces a parallel beam of light from the source, the grating diffracts the light into different orders of spectra, the telescope focuses on a particular order of spectrum and the scale measures the angular position of the spectral lines.
- The **grating equation** relates the wavelength of light, the angle of incidence, the angle of diffraction and the grating spacing. It is given by:

$$d(\sin i + \sin r) = n\lambda$$

where $d$ is the grating spacing, $i$ is the angle of incidence, $r$ is the angle of diffraction, $n$ is the order of spectrum and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light.

- The **resolving power** of a grating is a measure of its ability to separate two closely spaced spectral lines. It is given by:

$$R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta \lambda} = Nn$$

where $\lambda$ is the average wavelength of light, $\Delta \lambda$ is the minimum wavelength difference that can be resolved, $N$ is the total number of slits or grooves on the grating and $n$ is the order of spectrum.

- The **dispersion** of a grating is a measure of its ability to spread out different wavelengths of light. It is given by:

$$D = \frac{d\cos r}{n}$$

where $d$ is the grating spacing, $r$ is the angle of diffraction and $n$ is the order of spectrum.

- The **angular dispersion** of a grating is the rate of change of angle of diffraction with respect to wavelength. It is given by:

$$\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{n}{d\cos r}$$

where $d$ is the grating spacing, $r$ is the angle of diffraction, $n$ is the order of spectrum and $\theta$ is the angular position of the spectral line.

- The **linear dispersion** of a grating is the rate of change of linear position of the spectral line on the screen or detector with respect to wavelength. It is given by:

$$\frac{dx}{d\lambda} = f\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{fn}{d\cos r}$$

where $f$ is the focal length of the telescope, $x$ is the linear position of the spectral line and the other symbols have the same meaning as before.

- The **free spectral range** of a grating is the wavelength range between two successive orders of spectrum. It is given by:

$$\Delta \lambda = \frac{\lambda}{n}$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light and $n$ is the order of spectrum.

- The **blaze angle** of a grating is the angle of incidence that produces the maximum intensity in a particular order of spectrum. It depends on the shape and orientation of the slits or grooves on the grating surface. A **blazed grating** is a grating that has slits or grooves that are shaped like sawteeth or triangles to enhance the intensity of a desired order of spectrum.



# Dispersive Power

- Dispersive power is a measure of the ability of a transparent medium to separate different colors of light by refraction  .
- Dispersive power is denoted by the symbol \uD835\uDF14 and is defined as the ratio of the difference in refractive indices of the medium for two specified widely differing wavelengths divided by the refractive index at some specified intermediate wavelength minus 1  .
- Mathematically, dispersive power is given by the formula:

$$\uD835\uDF14 = \frac{\uD835\uDCD1_\uD835\uDCD9 - \uD835\uDCD1_\uD835\uDCD6}{\uD835\uDCD1_\uD835\uDCD7 - 1}$$

where \uD835\uDCD1_\uD835\uDCD9, \uD835\uDCD1_\uD835\uDCD7, and \uD835\uDCD1_\uD835\uDCD6 are the refractive indices of the medium for the violet, yellow, and red wavelengths of light, respectively.

- Dispersive power indicates how much a medium can disperse light of different wavelengths. The higher the value of \uD835\uDF14, the more the medium can spread out light into its constituent colors. The lower the value of \uD835\uDF14, the less the medium can spread out light into its constituent colors.
- Dispersive power is an important property of optical devices such as prisms and lenses, as it affects the quality and clarity of the images formed by them. For example, a prism with a high dispersive power can be used to produce a clear and colorful spectrum of light, while a lens with a low dispersive power can reduce the chromatic aberration or color distortion in the images formed by it.



# Resolving Power

- Resolving power is the ability of an optical instrument to separate two adjacent points or objects from each other from a considerable distance.
- Resolving power depends on the wavelength of the light used and the aperture of the instrument.
- The smaller the wavelength and the larger the aperture, the higher the resolving power.
- Resolving power is measured by the minimum angular separation between two points or objects that can be resolved by the instrument.
- Resolving power is the reciprocal of the angular separation, i.e., Resolving Power = 1/Δθ.
- Different optical instruments have different formulas for resolving power based on their design and function.
- For a telescope, the resolving power is given by :

  - Resolving Power = d/1.22λ
  - where d is the diameter of the objective lens, and λ is the wavelength of the light.
  - This formula is derived from the Rayleigh criterion, which states that two point sources are resolved when the central maximum of one diffraction pattern coincides with the first minimum of the other.

- For a microscope, the resolving power is given by :

  - Resolving Power = 2nsinθ/λ
  - where n is the refractive index of the medium between the objective lens and the specimen, θ is the half-angle of the cone of light entering the objective lens, and λ is the wavelength of the light.
  - This formula is also derived from the Rayleigh criterion, but it takes into account the effect of the medium and the numerical aperture of the lens.

- For a prism, the resolving power is given by:

  - Resolving Power = λ/dλ
  - where λ is the wavelength of the spectral line, and dλ is the difference between the wavelengths of two adjacent spectral lines.
  - This formula measures the ability of a prism to separate two spectral lines of different wavelengths in a spectrum.

- For a grating, the resolving power is given by:

  - Resolving Power = Nm
  - where N is the total number of slits in the grating, and m is the order of the spectrum.
  - This formula measures the ability of a grating to separate two spectral lines of different wavelengths in a spectrum.
  - Alternatively, the resolving power of a grating can be expressed as:

    - Resolving Power = λ/Δλ
    - where λ is the wavelength of the spectral line, and Δλ is the difference between the wavelengths of two adjacent spectral lines.
    - This formula is equivalent to the previous one, but it uses the relation between the wavelength and the angle of diffraction for a grating.

- Resolving power is an important concept in wave optics, as it determines the limit of resolution of optical instruments.
- Resolving power has various applications in astronomy, microscopy, spectroscopy, and other fields that use optical instruments.



# Rayleigh’s criterion of resolution

- Rayleigh’s criterion of resolution is a rule that defines the minimum angular separation between two point sources of light that can be resolved by an optical instrument such as a telescope or a microscope.
- The criterion is based on the diffraction patterns produced by the circular aperture of the instrument, which are called Airy disks. The Airy disk is the bright central spot surrounded by concentric rings of light and dark regions.
- The criterion states that two point sources are just resolvable when the center of the Airy disk of one source coincides with the first dark ring of the Airy disk of the other source. This is illustrated in the figure below.

Rayleigh's criterion

- The minimum angular separation θ that satisfies the criterion is given by the formula:

θ = 1.22 λ / D

where λ is the wavelength of light and D is the diameter of the aperture.

- The smaller the value of θ, the better the resolution of the instrument. The resolution also depends on the wavelength of light, with shorter wavelengths giving better resolution. The aperture size also affects the resolution, with larger apertures giving better resolution.



# Resolving Power of Grating

- Resolving power of grating is its ability to form two separate maxima of two wavelengths which are very close to each other.
- It is defined as the ratio of the wavelength of one of the spectral line to the difference in the wavelengths between them such that the two lines appear to be just resolved.
- Thus, resolving power of grating is given by R.P. = λ/dλ, where λ is the mean wavelength and dλ is the smallest difference between two wavelengths that can be resolved .
- Resolving power of grating depends on the order of diffraction, the number of lines per unit length of the grating, and the width of the grating .
- The expression for resolving power of grating can be derived as follows:

  - Consider a grating with N lines per unit length and a total width of b = N/a, where a is the distance between two adjacent lines.
  - Let λ and λ + dλ be two wavelengths that are just resolved in the nth order of diffraction.
  - The condition for constructive interference for these wavelengths is:

    - nλ = a(sinθ + sinα)
    - n(λ + dλ) = a(sinθ' + sinα)

  - where θ and θ' are the angles of diffraction, and α is the angle of incidence.
  - Subtracting these two equations and neglecting higher powers of dλ, we get:

    - ndλ = a(cosθ - cosθ')dθ

  - The resolving power of grating is the reciprocal of the smallest value of dλ that can be resolved, which occurs when dθ is the angular width of the diffraction peak.
  - The angular width of the diffraction peak can be approximated by the Rayleigh criterion, which states that two peaks are just resolved when the maximum of one peak coincides with the first minimum of the other peak .
  - The first minimum of the diffraction peak occurs when the path difference between the rays from the two ends of the grating is equal to λ, which gives:

    - λ = a(sinθ + sinα) - a(sinθ - sinα)
    - λ = 2asinα

  - Therefore, the angular width of the diffraction peak is:

    - dθ = λ/b

  - Substituting this value in the expression for ndλ, we get:

    - ndλ = a(cosθ - cosθ')λ/b

  - Dividing both sides by λ and rearranging, we get:

    - R.P. = λ/dλ = nN(cosθ - cosθ')/cosθ

  - This is the expression for the resolving power of grating   .

# Fibre Optics: Principle and construction of optical fiber

# Acceptance Angle in Fiber Optics

- The acceptance angle of an optical fiber is defined based on a purely geometrical consideration (ray optics): it is the maximum angle of a ray (against the fiber axis) hitting the fiber core which allows the incident light to be guided by the core .
- The acceptance angle depends on the refractive indices of the core, the cladding, and the surrounding medium (usually air). The larger the difference between the core and the cladding refractive indices, the larger the acceptance angle.
- The acceptance angle can be calculated using Snell's law and the condition for total internal reflection at the core-cladding interface. If n_f is the core refractive index, n_c is the cladding refractive index, and n_0 is the surrounding medium refractive index, then the acceptance angle \theta_a is given by:

\theta_a = \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{n_f}{n_0} \sqrt{1 - \left( \frac{n_c}{n_f} \right)^2} \right)

- The acceptance angle is related to the numerical aperture (NA) of the fiber, which is a measure of its light-gathering ability. The numerical aperture is defined as:

NA = n_0 \sin \theta_a

- The numerical aperture is usually given in datasheets for fiber optic cables instead of the acceptance angle. The numerical aperture can also be expressed in terms of the core and cladding refractive indices as:

NA = \sqrt{n_f^2 - n_c^2}

- The acceptance angle determines the cone of light that can be coupled into the fiber. The cone has a vertex at the fiber end and an opening angle of 2\theta_a. The cone of light is also called the acceptance cone of the fiber.
- The acceptance angle and the cone of light are important parameters for designing and optimizing fiber optic systems, as they affect the coupling efficiency, the bandwidth, and the signal quality of the transmitted light.



# Numerical Aperture of Optical Fiber

- Numerical aperture (NA) is a measure of the ability of an optical fiber to collect or confine the incident light ray inside it .
- It is among the most basic property of optical fiber and shows the efficiency with which light is collected inside the fiber in order to get propagated.
- Numerical aperture is defined as the sine of half of the angle of the fiber's light acceptance cone .
- The acceptance cone is the cone of light that can enter the fiber and be transmitted along it .
- The acceptance angle is the maximum angle at which the light can be incident on the fiber and still be transmitted along it .
- The numerical aperture depends on the refractive indices of the core and the cladding of the fiber, as well as the wavelength of the light .
- The numerical aperture can be calculated by the following formula :

NA = sin θ_a = √(n_1^2 - n_2^2)

where θ_a is the acceptance angle, n_1 is the refractive index of the core, and n_2 is the refractive index of the cladding.

- The numerical aperture determines the following characteristics of the optical fiber :

  - The amount of light that can be coupled into the fiber
  - The bandwidth or information-carrying capacity of the fiber
  - The modal dispersion or pulse spreading in the fiber
  - The resolution or image quality in fiber-optic imaging systems

- The numerical aperture is usually specified for a particular wavelength of light, as it varies slightly with the wavelength .
- The numerical aperture is usually higher for multimode fibers than for single-mode fibers, as multimode fibers can support more modes or paths of light propagation .
- The numerical aperture is usually lower for graded-index fibers than for step-index fibers, as graded-index fibers have a gradual change in refractive index from the core to the cladding, which reduces the acceptance angle .

# Step index and graded index fibers

## Introduction

Optical fibers are thin strands of glass or plastic that can transmit light signals over long distances. Optical fibers are used in various applications such as telecommunications, data transmission, sensing, and imaging. Optical fibers can be classified into different types based on their structure, mode of propagation, and refractive index profile.

## Step index fiber

- A step index fiber is a type of optical fiber that has a core with a constant refractive index and a cladding with a lower refractive index. The core and the cladding are separated by a sharp boundary, hence the name step index.
- A step index fiber can be either single mode or multimode, depending on the core diameter and the wavelength of the light. A single mode fiber has a core diameter that is comparable to the wavelength of the light, and it can support only one mode of propagation. A multimode fiber has a larger core diameter and can support multiple modes of propagation.
- A step index fiber has a high modal dispersion, which is the spreading of the light pulses due to the different path lengths and velocities of the modes. This limits the bandwidth and the distance of the step index fiber. The modal dispersion can be reduced by using a graded index fiber.

## Graded index fiber

- A graded index fiber is a type of optical fiber that has a core with a varying refractive index and a cladding with a lower refractive index. The refractive index of the core decreases gradually from the center to the edge, forming a parabolic profile. This causes the light rays to bend towards the center of the core, hence the name graded index.
- A graded index fiber is always multimode, as it can support multiple modes of propagation. However, the modes have similar path lengths and velocities, as the light rays are constantly refracted towards the center of the core. This reduces the modal dispersion and increases the bandwidth and the distance of the graded index fiber.
- A graded index fiber offers superior transmission quality, higher bandwidth, and longer distance than a step index fiber. In modern communication applications, graded index fiber is preferred over step index fiber.

## Conclusion

- Step index fiber and graded index fiber are two types of multimode fibers
- Graded index fiber offers superior transmission quality, higher bandwidth, and longer distance than step index fiber
- Higher modal dispersion limits the bandwidth and the distance of the step index fiber
- In modern communication applications, graded index fiber is preferred over step index fiber
- Step index fiber is cheaper but it cannot offer higher bandwidth nor longer distances

# Fiber Optic Communication Principle

- The core is the central region of the fiber where the light is confined and propagated.
- The cladding is the outer layer of the fiber that surrounds the core and has a lower refractive index than the core.
- The coating is the material that covers the cladding and protects the fiber from physical damage and moisture.
- The principle of fiber optic communication is based on total internal reflection, which is the phenomenon that occurs when light traveling in a medium with a higher refractive index reaches the boundary with a medium with a lower refractive index at an angle greater than the critical angle .
- When this happens, the light is reflected back into the higher refractive index medium and does not escape into the lower refractive index medium .
- In an optical fiber, the light travels in the core and is totally internally reflected at the core-cladding interface, thus staying within the fiber and reaching the other end .
- The advantages of fiber optic communication include:
  - Higher bandwidth and data transmission rate than copper wire or wireless systems .

  - Smaller size and weight than copper wire or wireless systems .

# Attenuation

- Attenuation is the reduction in intensity of the light beam (or signal) with respect to distance travelled through a transmission medium .
- Attenuation is measured in decibels (dB) and is calculated by the formula: Attenuation (dB) = 10 x Log 10 (P in/P out) = 20xLog 10 (V in/V out) , where P is the power and V is the voltage of the signal.
- Attenuation limits the distance in which the signal can travel through optical fiber and affects the performance and reliability of the fiber optic network .
- Attenuation can be caused by intrinsic and extrinsic factors  .
  - Intrinsic factors are inherent to the fiber optic material and include:
    - Absorption: the conversion of optical power into heat due to the interaction of light with the molecular structure of the fiber  . Absorption depends on the wavelength of the light and the impurities in the fiber .
    - Scattering: the deflection of light from its original direction due to the microscopic variations in the refractive index of the fiber  . Scattering depends on the wavelength of the light and the size of the variations .
    - Dispersion: the spreading of light pulses over time due to the different velocities of different wavelengths or modes in the fiber  . Dispersion causes distortion and interference of the signal and limits the bandwidth of the fiber .
  - Extrinsic factors are related to the external conditions and include:
    - Bending: the loss of light due to the curvature of the fiber that causes some light rays to escape from the core  . Bending can be macroscopic (visible) or microscopic (invisible) and depends on the radius of curvature and the numerical aperture of the fiber .
    - Splicing: the joining of two fiber segments that causes some light to reflect or refract at the interface  . Splicing can be fusion (melting) or mechanical (adhesive) and depends on the alignment and quality of the splice .
    - Connector: the coupling of two fiber ends that causes some light to reflect or refract at the interface  . Connector can be physical contact (PC) or expanded beam (EB) and depends on the cleanliness and quality of the connector .
    - Environmental: the variation of temperature, humidity, pressure, vibration, etc. that affects the physical properties of the fiber and causes changes in the attenuation  . Environmental factors can be controlled by proper installation and protection of the fiber .



# Dispersion in Fiber Optics

- Dispersion in an optical fiber is the spreading of light pulses when the wave travels through an optical fiber from an end to another.
- It results in some variation of the actually transmitted wave through an optical fiber.
- Due to the dispersion of light waves, various adverse effects are noticed on the signal being transmitted, such as distortion, attenuation, and noise.
- Dispersion distorts signals and limits the data rate of digital signals sent over fiber optic cable.
- Dispersion can be classified into three types: material dispersion, waveguide dispersion, and intermodal dispersion .

## Material Dispersion
- Material dispersion is caused by a change in fiber optic material’s refractive index with different wavelength.
- The higher the index, the slower the light travels.
- Material dispersion is also known as chromatic dispersion, as it depends on the color or frequency of light.
- Material dispersion can be reduced by using graded-index fibers, which have a varying refractive index in the core.

## Waveguide Dispersion
- Waveguide dispersion is due to the distribution of light between core and cladding.
- It depends on the geometry and refractive index profile of the fiber.
- Waveguide dispersion is also influenced by the mode of propagation of light, such as single-mode or multimode.
- Waveguide dispersion can be minimized by optimizing the fiber design and choosing the appropriate wavelength of light.

## Intermodal Dispersion
- Intermodal dispersion is caused by the different propagation speeds of different modes of light in a multimode fiber.
- It results in a broadening of the pulse as different modes arrive at different times at the receiver.
- Intermodal dispersion is also called modal dispersion or differential mode delay.
- Intermodal dispersion can be eliminated by using single-mode fibers, which only allow one mode of light to propagate.

# Spontaneous and stimulated emission of radiation

- Spontaneous emission is the process in which an atom or molecule in an excited state decays to a lower energy state and emits a photon of energy equal to the energy difference between the two states .
- Spontaneous emission occurs without any external influence and is random in direction, phase, and polarization  .
- Spontaneous emission is the source of natural light, such as the light from stars, fireflies, and fluorescent materials.
- Stimulated emission is the process in which an atom or molecule in an excited state is induced to emit a photon of the same energy, phase, polarization, and direction as an incident photon that has the same frequency as the energy difference between the two states  .
- Stimulated emission occurs when the incident photon interacts with the excited atom or molecule and causes it to drop to a lower energy state, releasing another photon that is identical to the incident photon  .
- Stimulated emission is the basis of laser operation, as it allows the amplification of light by creating a population inversion of atoms or molecules in the excited state .



# Population inversion

- Population inversion is the redistribution of atomic energy levels that takes place in a system so that laser or maser action can occur.
- Normally, a system of atoms is in thermal equilibrium and there are always more atoms in low energy states than in higher ones. This is described by the Boltzmann distribution.
- To achieve population inversion, a system of atoms must be excited by an external source of energy, such as an electric current or a flash of light, so that more atoms are in higher energy states than in lower ones.
- Population inversion is a necessary condition for all lasers, regardless of their power or wavelength, because all lasers operate on the same basic principle of a chain reaction.
- Excited atoms emit photons when they decay to lower energy states. These photons can stimulate other excited atoms to emit photons of the same frequency, phase, and direction, creating a coherent beam of light. This is the laser action.
- Ground-state atoms absorb photons and reduce the intensity of the beam. This is the loss mechanism.
- To maintain population inversion and laser action, the system must be continuously pumped by the external source of energy.



# Einstein’s Coefficients

- Einstein’s coefficients are mathematical quantities that describe the probability of absorption or emission of a photon by an atom or molecule.
- There are three Einstein coefficients: A, B12 and B21  .
- The Einstein A coefficient is related to the rate of spontaneous emission of light, which is the process of an atom or molecule in an excited state emitting a photon and returning to a lower energy state without any external influence .
- The Einstein B12 coefficient is related to the rate of absorption of light, which is the process of an atom or molecule in a lower energy state absorbing a photon and jumping to a higher energy state .
- The Einstein B21 coefficient is related to the rate of stimulated emission of light, which is the process of an atom or molecule in an excited state emitting a photon and returning to a lower energy state when stimulated by another photon of the same frequency .
- The Einstein coefficients are fixed probabilities per time associated with each atom, and do not depend on the state of the gas of which the atoms are a part.
- The Einstein coefficients are related to each other by the following equations  :

  - A/B21 = 8πhν3/c3, where h is the Planck constant, ν is the frequency of the photon, and c is the speed of light.
  - B12 = B21, which implies that the probability of absorption and stimulated emission are equal for the same transition.
  - A/B21 = (g1/g2)exp(-hν/kT), where g1 and g2 are the statistical weights of the lower and upper energy levels, k is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the temperature of the gas.

# Principles of laser action

- Laser is an acronym for **Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation**.
- Laser action is the process of producing a coherent, monochromatic, and intense beam of light by stimulating the atoms or molecules of a medium to emit photons in a synchronized manner.
- The basic principles of laser action are  :
  - **Spontaneous emission**: This is the natural process of an excited atom or molecule returning to a lower energy level and emitting a photon of a random wavelength, phase, and direction.
  - **Stimulated emission**: This is the process of an excited atom or molecule being stimulated by an incoming photon of the same wavelength and phase as the emitted photon, resulting in the emission of two identical photons in the same direction. This is the key mechanism for light amplification in a laser.
  - **Stimulated absorption**: This is the process of an atom or molecule in a lower energy level absorbing an incoming photon of the same wavelength and phase as the transition to a higher energy level, resulting in the excitation of the atom or molecule. This is the opposite of stimulated emission and reduces the light intensity in a laser.
  - **Non-radiative decay**: This is the process of an excited atom or molecule losing energy by other means than emitting a photon, such as collisions, vibrations, or heat. This is an undesirable process in a laser as it reduces the population of excited atoms or molecules available for stimulated emission.
  - **Population inversion**: This is the condition in which the number of atoms or molecules in a higher energy level is greater than the number in a lower energy level, resulting in a net gain of photons by stimulated emission. This is the essential requirement for laser action and is achieved by pumping energy into the medium.
  - **Amplification of light**: This is the process of increasing the intensity of light by passing it through a medium with population inversion, resulting in multiple cycles of stimulated emission and photon multiplication. This is the main function of a laser and is enhanced by using an optical resonator or cavity that reflects the light back and forth between two mirrors.

- The ruby crystal is usually rod-shaped and has a cylindrical cavity along its axis. The cavity is coated with highly reflective mirrors at both ends, forming an optical resonator .

# Superconductors: Temperature dependence of resistivity in superconducting materials

- Superconductivity is a phenomenon in certain metals and ceramics where the resistivity of the material drops to zero below a certain critical temperature known as Tc .
- The resistivity of materials depends on the temperature. The equation ρt = ρ0 [1 + α (T – T0) shows the relation between the temperature and the resistivity for any material .
- In the equation, ρ0 is the resistivity at a standard temperature, ρt is the resistivity at t 0 C, T0 is the reference temperature and α is the temperature coefficient of resistivity.
- For normal metals, α is positive, which means that the resistivity increases with increasing temperature. This is because the thermal vibrations of the atoms in the lattice increase, which scatter the electrons more and reduce their mobility.
- For superconductors, α is negative, which means that the resistivity decreases with increasing temperature until it reaches zero at Tc. This is because the electrons form pairs called Cooper pairs, which can move through the lattice without resistance due to quantum effects.
- The value of Tc depends on the type and structure of the superconducting material. For most industrial superconductors, Tc is below 12 K, which requires cooling with liquid helium. For some ceramic materials, such as cuprates, Tc can reach up to 135 K, which allows cooling with liquid nitrogen .
- The temperature dependence of resistivity in superconducting materials can be plotted as a curve that shows a sharp drop at Tc. The curve can be approximated by the equation ρt = ρn (1 - (T/Tc)^4), where ρn is the normal resistivity above Tc.



# Meissner effect

- The Meissner effect is the expulsion of a magnetic field from the interior of a material that is in the process of becoming a superconductor .
- A superconductor is a material that loses its resistance to the flow of electrical currents when cooled below a certain temperature, called the critical temperature .
- The Meissner effect was discovered by the German physicists W. Meissner and R. Ochsenfeld in 1933 .
- The Meissner effect is a property of all superconductors, and it is one of the defining characteristics of the superconducting state .
- The Meissner effect can be demonstrated by making a magnet levitate above a superconductor, as the superconductor repels the magnetic field of the magnet .
- The Meissner effect can be explained by the formation of superconducting currents on the surface of the material, which cancel out the applied magnetic field inside the material .
- The Meissner effect is a quantum phenomenon that involves the breaking of the symmetry of the electromagnetic field by the superconducting state.
- The Meissner effect is different from the diamagnetic effect, which is the tendency of a material to weakly oppose an applied magnetic field. The Meissner effect is a perfect diamagnetism, which means that the magnetic field is completely expelled from the superconductor.



# Temperature dependence of critical field for superconductors

- The critical field (Hc) of a superconductor is the maximum external magnetic field that can be applied to the material without destroying its superconducting state.
- The critical field depends on the temperature of the superconductor. As the temperature increases, the critical field decreases, until it reaches zero at the critical temperature (Tc) of the superconductor.
- The temperature dependence of the critical field can be described by the empirical formula:

$$H_c(T) = H_c(0) \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{T}{T_c} \right)^2 \right]$$

- where Hc(0) is the critical field at absolute zero, and Tc is the critical temperature of the superconductor.
- This formula is valid for ordinary (type I) superconductors, which have a single critical field and a single critical temperature. These superconductors are usually pure metals or metal alloys, such as mercury, lead, tin, niobium, etc.
- For some superconductors, especially those with high critical temperatures (high-Tc superconductors), the temperature dependence of the critical field is more complicated and may show anomalous behavior, such as negative curvature or multiband effects  .
- These superconductors are usually classified as type II superconductors, which have two critical fields: the lower critical field (Hc1) and the upper critical field (Hc2). Between these two fields, the superconductor allows partial penetration of magnetic flux in the form of vortices, creating a mixed state .
- The temperature dependence of the lower and upper critical fields of type II superconductors can be different from each other and from the formula for type I superconductors. They may depend on the material properties, such as the coherence length, the penetration depth, the anisotropy, the dimensionality, the impurity concentration, etc.
- Therefore, the temperature dependence of the critical field of superconductors is an important characteristic that can reveal the nature and mechanism of superconductivity in different materials.



# Persistent Current in Superconductors

- Persistent current is the electric current that flows indefinitely in a closed loop of superconducting material without any external power source.
- Persistent current is possible in superconductors because they have zero resistance and no energy dissipation  .

This is due to the self-inductance of the ring and the Meissner effect that expels the magnetic flux from the superconductor.

# Type I and Type II Superconductors

- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and perfect diamagnetism below a certain critical temperature.
- Superconductors can be classified into two types based on their response to an external magnetic field: type I and type II.
- Type I superconductors have only one critical magnetic field, Hc. When the applied magnetic field exceeds Hc, the superconductivity is destroyed and the material becomes normal.
- Type I superconductors are usually pure metals, such as mercury, lead, tin, aluminum, etc.
- Type I superconductors have a sharp transition from the superconducting state to the normal state, and they expel all the magnetic flux from their interior (Meissner effect).
- Type II superconductors have two critical magnetic fields, Hc1 and Hc2. When the applied magnetic field is between Hc1 and Hc2, the superconductivity is not completely destroyed, but the material allows some magnetic flux to penetrate into its interior (mixed state or vortex state).
- Type II superconductors are usually metal alloys or complex oxide ceramics, such as niobium, vanadium, technetium, yttrium barium copper oxide, etc.
- Type II superconductors have a gradual transition from the superconducting state to the normal state, and they can sustain much higher magnetic fields than type I superconductors.
- All high-temperature superconductors (those that have critical temperatures above 77 K) are type II superconductors.



# High temperature superconductors

- High temperature superconductors (HTS) are materials that behave as superconductors at temperatures above 77 K (−196.2 °C; −321.1 °F), the boiling point of liquid nitrogen.
- Superconductors are materials that have zero electrical resistance and can expel magnetic fields when cooled below a critical temperature (Tc).
- HTS have much higher Tc than conventional superconductors, which are mostly metallic alloys that require cooling with liquid helium to temperatures below 30 K (−243.2 °C; −405.7 °F).
- HTS have many potential applications in fields such as power transmission, energy storage, magnetic levitation, and quantum computing, due to their high efficiency and strong magnetic properties.

- The second class of HTS is the iron-based compounds, such as iron pnictides and iron chalcogenides, which have lower Tc but are easier to synthesize and manipulate.
- The mechanism of HTS is still not fully understood, but it is believed to involve the formation of Cooper pairs, which are pairs of electrons that are bound together by an attractive interaction mediated by lattice vibrations or other mechanisms .
- The Cooper pairs can move through the material without scattering, resulting in zero resistance and perfect diamagnetism (the expulsion of magnetic fields).
- HTS are usually characterized by their Tc, their critical current density (Jc), which is the maximum current they can carry without losing superconductivity, and their critical magnetic field (Hc), which is the maximum magnetic field they can withstand without losing superconductivity.
- HTS are also classified by their symmetry, which determines how the Cooper pairs are arranged in momentum space and how they respond to external perturbations. The symmetry can be either s-wave, d-wave, or p-wave, depending on the angular dependence of the pairing function.

# Properties and Applications of Superconductors

## Properties of Superconductors

- A superconductor is a material that has **zero electrical resistance** when cooled below a certain critical temperature. This means that an electric current can flow through it without any energy loss.
- A superconductor also exhibits the **Meissner effect**, which is the expulsion of magnetic fields from its interior when it becomes superconducting . This creates a perfect diamagnetism, meaning that a superconductor will repel any external magnetic field .
- A superconductor has a **critical magnetic field**, which is the maximum applied magnetic field at a given temperature that will allow it to remain superconducting. If the magnetic field exceeds this value, the superconductor will revert to a normal state.
- A superconductor has a **phase transition** at its critical temperature, which means that its properties change abruptly from a normal state to a superconducting state . This phase transition is accompanied by a change in the electronic structure and the lattice vibrations of the material .
- A superconductor has a **London moment**, which is a magnetic moment induced by a rotating supercurrent in a superconducting ring . This magnetic moment is proportional to the angular momentum of the supercurrent and is independent of the applied magnetic field .

## Applications of Superconductors

- Superconductors have a wide variety of applications in various fields, such as physics, engineering, medicine, transportation, computing, and power transmission  .
- Superconductors are used to create powerful **electromagnets** for devices such as MRI scanners, particle accelerators, fusion reactors, and magnetic levitation trains  . These electromagnets can generate very high magnetic fields with low power consumption and minimal heat generation  .
- Superconductors are used to transmit **electric power** for long distances with minimal losses and high efficiency  . Superconducting cables can carry more current than conventional wires and can reduce the need for transformers and substations  .
- Superconductors are used to enhance the performance of **electronic devices** such as computers, sensors, detectors, and communication systems  . Superconducting circuits can operate at very high speeds, low noise, and high sensitivity  .
- Superconductors are used to explore the **fundamental physics** of matter, such as quantum mechanics, superfluidity, and topological phases  . Superconducting materials can exhibit exotic phenomena such as Cooper pairs, Josephson effects, and Majorana fermions  .

# Basics concept of Quantum Dots

- Quantum dots are **nanoscale particles** of semiconducting material that can be embedded in cells or organisms for various experimental purposes, such as labeling proteins.
- Quantum dots have **optical and electronic properties** that differ from those of larger particles as a result of **quantum mechanics** . They are a central topic in **nanotechnology** and **materials science** .
- Quantum dots can be **defined** by lithographically patterned gate electrodes, or by etching on two-dimensional electron gases in semiconductor heterostructures. They can also be **synthesized** by chemical methods, such as colloidal synthesis.
- Quantum dots have **discrete energy levels** that depend on their size, shape, and composition. This means that they can **emit** or **absorb** light of specific wavelengths, depending on the energy difference between the levels .
- Quantum dots have many **applications** in fields such as **biomedical imaging**, **solar cells**, **quantum computing**, **light-emitting diodes**, and **lasers** . They can also be used to study **quantum phenomena**, such as **tunneling**, **entanglement**, and **superposition** .



# Quantum wires and Quantum well

## Quantum well

- A quantum well is a two-dimensional (2D) structure or a thin layer of a lower bandgap semiconductor material sandwiched between two layers of a higher bandgap material.  
- In a quantum well, the motion of electrons and holes is confined in one direction (perpendicular to the layer), while they are free to move in the other two directions (parallel to the layer).  
- The confinement of carriers in one direction leads to the quantization of their energy levels, forming discrete subbands.  
- The energy gap between the subbands depends on the well width and the material parameters.  
- Quantum wells have applications in optoelectronic devices, such as lasers, LEDs, photodetectors, and solar cells, due to their enhanced optical and electronic properties.   

## Quantum wire

- A quantum wire is a one-dimensional (1D) structure or a nanoscale filament of a lower bandgap semiconductor material embedded in a higher bandgap matrix material.  
- In a quantum wire, the motion of electrons and holes is confined in two directions (perpendicular to the wire axis), while they are free to move in one direction (along the wire axis).  
- The confinement of carriers in two directions leads to the quantization of their energy levels, forming discrete subbands.  
- The energy gap between the subbands depends on the wire diameter and the material parameters.  
- Quantum wires have applications in nanoelectronics, optoelectronics, information technology, and biotechnology, due to the quantum mechanical confinement provided by the small spatial extent of the structures.



# Fabrication of nano materials -TopDown approach (CVD) and Bottom-Up approach (Sol Gel)

- Nanomaterials are materials with at least one dimension in the nanoscale range (1-100 nm).
- Nanomaterials have unique physical, chemical, optical, and mechanical properties that depend on their size, shape, and structure.
- Nanomaterials can be fabricated by two general approaches: top-down and bottom-up.
- Top-down approach is where a process starts from a large piece and subsequently uses finer and finer tools for creating correspondingly smaller structures.
- Bottom-up approach is where smaller components of atomic or molecular dimensions self-assemble together, according to a natural physical principle or an externally applied driving force, to give rise to larger and more organized systems.
- Both approaches have advantages and disadvantages, depending on the type, quality, and application of the nanomaterials.

## Top-down approach (CVD)

- Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) is a top-down technique that involves the deposition of a thin film of a material on a substrate by exposing it to a gas or vapor containing the desired elements.
- CVD can be used to fabricate various types of nanomaterials, such as carbon nanotubes, graphene, nanowires, nanodots, etc.
- CVD can produce high-quality, uniform, and large-area nanomaterials with good control over the morphology, composition, and orientation.
- CVD requires high temperature, vacuum, and expensive equipment, and may generate toxic by-products and waste.
- CVD can be classified into different types, such as thermal CVD, plasma-enhanced CVD, laser-assisted CVD, etc., depending on the source of energy and the activation mechanism.

## Bottom-up approach (Sol Gel)

- Sol-gel processing is a bottom-up technique that involves the formation of a colloidal suspension (sol) of nanosized particles, followed by the gelation and drying of the sol to form a solid network (gel) of the particles.
- Sol-gel processing can be used to fabricate various types of nanomaterials, such as metal oxides, ceramics, composites, etc.
- Sol-gel processing can produce low-cost, versatile, and complex nanomaterials with good control over the size, shape, and distribution of the particles.
- Sol-gel processing requires low temperature, ambient pressure, and simple equipment, and can be easily scaled up and modified.
- Sol-gel processing may suffer from drawbacks, such as cracking, shrinkage, and contamination of the nanomaterials during the drying and calcination stages.



# Properties and Applications of Nanomaterials

Nanomaterials are materials that have at least one dimension in the range of 1 to 100 nanometers (nm). They exhibit novel physical and chemical properties that are different from those of bulk materials due to their high surface area to volume ratio, quantum confinement effects, and enhanced reactivity.

Some of the properties of nanomaterials are:

- Optical properties: Nanomaterials can absorb, emit, scatter, and manipulate light in various ways, depending on their size, shape, composition, and structure. They can be used to create optical detectors, sensors, lasers, displays, solar cells, and photonic devices. They can also be used for biomedical imaging, drug delivery, and photoelectrochemistry.
- Electrical properties: Nanomaterials can exhibit high conductivity, superconductivity, magnetoresistance, thermoelectricity, and piezoelectricity, depending on their structure and composition. They can be used to create nanoelectronics, nanowires, nanotransistors, nanogenerators, and nanosensors. They can also be used for energy storage, conversion, and harvesting.
- Magnetic properties: Nanomaterials can exhibit high magnetization, coercivity, and anisotropy, depending on their size, shape, and composition. They can be used to create nanomagnets, nanocomposites, magnetic nanoparticles, and spintronics. They can also be used for magnetic resonance imaging, drug delivery, and hyperthermia.
- Mechanical properties: Nanomaterials can exhibit high strength, stiffness, hardness, and toughness, depending on their structure and composition. They can be used to create nanofibers, nanotubes, nanosheets, and nanofilms. They can also be used for coating, lubrication, and adhesion applications. They can also be used to create mechanically stronger nanodevices for various purposes.
- Catalytic properties: Nanomaterials can exhibit high catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability, depending on their surface area, morphology, and composition. They can be used to create nanocatalysts, nanoreactors, and nanosensors. They can also be used for environmental remediation, chemical synthesis, and fuel cells.

Some of the applications of nanomaterials are:

- Nanomedicine: Nanomaterials can be used to diagnose, treat, and prevent diseases, as well as to enhance the performance and delivery of drugs, vaccines, and implants. They can also be used to engineer tissues and organs, and to modulate the immune system.
- Nanotechnology: Nanomaterials can be used to create nanoscale devices, systems, and structures that have novel functions and capabilities. They can also be used to manipulate matter at the atomic and molecular level, and to integrate different disciplines such as physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering.
- Nanomaterials science: Nanomaterials can be used to study the fundamental properties and phenomena of matter at the nanoscale, and to discover new materials and phenomena that are not possible at the bulk scale. They can also be used to develop new methods and tools for the synthesis, characterization, and manipulation of nanomaterials.
