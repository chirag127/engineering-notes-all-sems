# Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the process of combining two or more atomic nuclei into a single, heavier nucleus, releasing a large amount of energy in the process. Nuclear fusion is the source of energy for the Sun and other stars.

Some of the main points to know about nuclear fusion are:

- Nuclear fusion requires high temperatures and pressures to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between positively charged nuclei. The conditions for fusion are achieved in the cores of stars, where the temperature is about 15 million degrees Celsius and the pressure is about 250 billion atmospheres.
- The most common fusion reaction in stars is the proton-proton chain, which converts four hydrogen nuclei (protons) into one helium nucleus (alpha particle), releasing two positrons, two neutrinos, and gamma rays. The mass of the helium nucleus is slightly less than the mass of the four protons, and the difference is converted into energy according to Einstein's equation E = mc^2.
- Another fusion reaction that occurs in stars is the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen (CNO) cycle, which uses carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen as catalysts to fuse hydrogen into helium. The CNO cycle is dominant in stars that are more massive and hotter than the Sun.
- Nuclear fusion can also be achieved artificially in devices called fusion reactors, which use magnetic fields or lasers to confine and heat plasma (a state of matter consisting of ionized gas) to the required conditions for fusion. The most common fusion reaction in fusion reactors is the deuterium-tritium (D-T) reaction, which fuses one deuterium nucleus (an isotope of hydrogen with one proton and one neutron) and one tritium nucleus (an isotope of hydrogen with one proton and two neutrons) into one helium nucleus and one neutron, releasing a large amount of energy.
- Nuclear fusion has many potential advantages as a source of energy, such as being abundant, renewable, clean, and safe. However, there are also many challenges and difficulties in achieving and sustaining fusion, such as the high cost, complexity, and technical feasibility of fusion reactors, the environmental and health risks of tritium and neutron radiation, and the political and social issues of nuclear proliferation and security.

### Force moment and couple

- A moment of a force is defined as the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the point or axis to the line of action of the force. Mathematically, M = F x d, where M is the moment, F is the force, and d is the perpendicular distance.
- The moment of a force depends on the choice of the point or axis of rotation. It is said to be the moment about that point or axis. Different points or axes may give different moments for the same force.
- A couple is a system of two equal and opposite parallel forces that act on a body. A couple has no resultant force, but it has a resultant moment. It is also called a pure moment or a force couple.
- A couple can cause or prevent the rotation of a body, but it cannot cause any translation or linear motion of the body.
- The moment of a couple is independent of the choice of the point or axis of rotation. - A couple can be resolved into a single force by applying an equal and opposite force at any point on the line of action of one of the forces. This will create a force and a moment that are statically equivalent to the original couple. This process is called resolving a couple into a force and a moment.

- The principle of transmissibility is based on the assumption that the body is rigid, meaning that it does not deform under the action of forces.
- The principle of transmissibility allows us to simplify the analysis of concurrent force systems by replacing forces with equivalent forces that have the same magnitude and direction, but different points of application along the same line of action.
- The principle of transmissibility does not affect the moment of a force about a point that lies on the line of action of the force, since the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action is zero. ### Varignon's theorem

- Varignon's theorem is a theorem of French mathematician Pierre Varignon (1654–1722), published in 1687 in his book Projet d'une nouvelle mécanique.
- The theorem states that the torque of a resultant of two concurrent forces about any point is equal to the algebraic sum of the torques of its components about the same point .
- Mathematically, the theorem can be expressed as:

$$\vec{M}_O = \vec{M}_{O_1} + \vec{M}_{O_2}$$

where $\vec{M}_O$ is the moment of the resultant force $\vec{R}$ about point O, $\vec{M}_{O_1}$ is the moment of force $\vec{F}_1$ about point O, and $\vec{M}_{O_2}$ is the moment of force $\vec{F}_2$ about point O.

- The theorem can be extended to any number of concurrent forces, as follows:

$$\vec{M}_O = \sum_{i=1}^n \vec{M}_{O_i}$$

where $\vec{M}_O$ is the moment of the resultant force $\vec{R}$ about point O, and $\vec{M}_{O_i}$ is the moment of force $\vec{F}_i$ about point O.

- The theorem can be proved using the cross product of vectors, as shown below:

$$\vec{M}_O = \vec{r}_O \times \vec{R}$$

where $\vec{r}_O$ is the position vector of point O, and $\vec{R}$ is the resultant force.

$$\vec{R} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2$$

Substituting $\vec{R}$ in the above equation, we get:

$$\vec{M}_O = \vec{r}_O \times (\vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2)$$

Using the distributive property of the cross product, we get:

$$\vec{M}_O = \vec{r}_O \times \vec{F}_1 + \vec{r}_O \times \vec{F}_2$$

Using the definition of moment, we get:

$$\vec{M}_O = \vec{M}_{O_1} + \vec{M}_{O_2}$$

which proves the theorem for two concurrent forces.

- The proof can be generalized for any number of concurrent forces using induction.

- Varignon's theorem is extremely important in calculating the centroid and centre of mass and the centre of pressure of hydrostatic force on the dam wall and submerged object.
- Varignon's theorem is also useful in finding the equivalent force-couple system for a given system of forces.

$$
\begin{aligned}

\sum F_{y} &=0 \\
\sum M_{z} &=0
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\sum \mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0} \\
\sum \mathbf{M}=\mathbf{0}
$$

- Hookes’ law can be used to describe the elastic behavior of many materials and structures, such as springs, rubber bands, wires, beams, columns, etc. Hookes’ law can also be applied to other types of deformation, such as torsion, bending, and shear.

  - A plastic region, where the stress increases with increasing strain but the material undergoes permanent deformation. The material exhibits plasticity or viscoplasticity.
  - A strain hardening region, where the stress increases at a faster rate than the strain due to the increase in dislocation density and the resistance to plastic deformation. The material exhibits strain hardening or work hardening.
  - An ultimate point, where the stress reaches a maximum value and the material can no longer sustain the load. The ultimate strength is the stress at the ultimate point.
  - A necking region, where the cross-sectional area of the material decreases due to the localized plastic deformation. The stress decreases with increasing strain due to the decrease in load-bearing capacity. The material exhibits necking or instability.
  - A fracture point, where the material breaks or ruptures. The fracture strength is the stress at the fracture point.

- The stress-strain diagram for a brittle material typically has the following features:

  - A linear elastic region, where the stress is proportional to the strain and the material obeys Hooke's law. The slope of this region is the modulus of elasticity or Young's modulus, which measures the stiffness of the material.
  - A fracture point, where the material breaks or ruptures with little or no plastic deformation. The fracture strength is the stress at the fracture point.

### Factor of Safety

- The factor of safety (FoS) is a measure of how much stronger a system is than it needs to be for an intended load. It is the ratio of the ultimate strength of a system to the actual applied load or the maximum permissible load .
- The factor of safety is important for structures and systems that need to withstand various types of loads, such as mechanical, thermal, electrical, or environmental. It is a way of ensuring reliability, durability, and safety of the design .
- The factor of safety can be calculated using detailed analysis, testing, or empirical formulas. It depends on various factors, such as the type of material, the geometry of the system, the loading conditions, the failure criteria, the safety standards, and the level of uncertainty .
- The factor of safety can be expressed as:

    FoS = S / L

    where S is the ultimate strength of the system and L is the applied load or the maximum permissible load.

- The factor of safety can also be expressed as:

    FoS = Ld / La

    where Ld is the design load and La is the actual load.

- The factor of safety can have different values depending on the type of system and the design criteria. For example, a factor of safety of 1.5 means that the system can withstand 50% more load than the intended load, while a factor of safety of 2 means that the system can withstand twice the intended load .
- The factor of safety can be classified into two types: static and dynamic. Static factor of safety is used for systems that are subjected to constant or slowly varying loads, while dynamic factor of safety is used for systems that are subjected to rapidly varying or impact loads .
- The factor of safety can also be classified into two categories: partial and global. Partial factor of safety is used for individual components or parameters of a system, while global factor of safety is used for the whole system or structure .

### IC Engine

- An IC engine (internal combustion engine) is a type of heat engine that converts the heat energy released during combustion of fuel into mechanical work  .
- The combustion takes place within the engine cylinder, which is an integral part of the working fluid flow circuit.
- The working fluid is usually a mixture of air and fuel, which is compressed, ignited, and expanded in the cylinder, pushing the piston and producing power.
- The IC engine can use liquid and gaseous fuels for combustion, such as gasoline, diesel, natural gas, propane, biodiesel, or ethanol .
- The IC engine can be classified into two main types based on the cycle of operation: spark ignition (SI) engine and compression ignition (CI) engine .
  - In an SI engine, the fuel-air mixture is ignited by a spark plug at the end of the compression stroke. The SI engine is also known as a gasoline or petrol engine.
  - In a CI engine, the air is compressed to a high pressure and temperature, and then the fuel is injected directly into the cylinder. The fuel-air mixture ignites spontaneously due to the high temperature. The CI engine is also known as a diesel engine.
- The IC engine can also be classified into two main types based on the number of strokes: four-stroke engine and two-stroke engine .
  - In a four-stroke engine, the piston completes four strokes (intake, compression, power, and exhaust) in two revolutions of the crankshaft. The four-stroke engine is more efficient and cleaner than the two-stroke engine, but it is also more complex and heavier.
  - In a two-stroke engine, the piston completes two strokes (compression and power) in one revolution of the crankshaft. The two-stroke engine is simpler and lighter than the four-stroke engine, but it is also less efficient and more polluting.
- The IC engine can also be classified into different types based on the arrangement of cylinders: inline, V, W, radial, opposed, or rotary .
  - In an inline engine, the cylinders are arranged in a single row along the crankshaft. The inline engine is simple and compact, but it has a limit on the number of cylinders.
  - In a V engine, the cylinders are arranged in two rows at an angle to the crankshaft. The V engine can accommodate more cylinders than the inline engine, but it is also more complex and bulky.
  - In a W engine, the cylinders are arranged in three rows at an angle to the crankshaft. The W engine can accommodate even more cylinders than the V engine, but it is also very complex and rare.
  - In a radial engine, the cylinders are arranged in a circle around the crankshaft. The radial engine is compact and powerful, but it has a poor cooling and balance.
  - In an opposed engine, the cylinders are arranged in two rows opposite to each other along the crankshaft. The opposed engine has a good balance and cooling, but it is also wide and flat.
  - In a rotary engine, the cylinders are arranged in a ring around a triangular rotor that rotates inside a housing. The rotary engine is also known as a Wankel engine. The rotary engine has a high power-to-weight ratio and smooth operation, but it also has a low efficiency and high emissions.



### Basic definition of engine and components

- An engine is a device that converts one or more forms of energy into mechanical energy that performs useful work.
- There are different types of engines, such as steam engines, internal combustion engines, electric motors, etc.
- The most common type of engine used in automobiles is the internal combustion engine, which burns fuel (such as gasoline or diesel) inside a cylinder to produce power.
- The essential parts of an internal combustion engine include the block, cylinder head, valves, pistons, and piston rings.
  - The block is the main structure of the engine that contains the cylinders and the crankcase.
  - The cylinder head is the part that covers the top of the cylinders and houses the valves and the spark plugs (in gasoline engines).
  - The valves are the devices that control the flow of air and fuel mixture into the cylinders and the exhaust gases out of the cylinders.
  - The pistons are the cylindrical parts that move up and down inside the cylinders and transmit the force of the combustion to the crankshaft.
  - The piston rings are the metal rings that seal the gap between the pistons and the cylinder walls and prevent the leakage of gas and oil.
- Other important parts of an internal combustion engine include the crankshaft, connecting rods, camshaft, timing belt or chain, oil pump, water pump, fuel pump, fuel injectors, spark plugs, distributor, alternator, starter, etc .
  - The crankshaft is the part that converts the reciprocating motion of the pistons into rotary motion and delivers the power to the transmission.
  - The connecting rods are the rods that connect the pistons to the crankshaft and transfer the force from the pistons to the crankshaft.
  - The camshaft is the part that operates the valves by means of lobes or cams that push or pull the valve stems.
  - The timing belt or chain is the part that synchronizes the movement of the camshaft and the crankshaft and ensures the proper timing of the valve opening and closing and the ignition of the fuel.
  - The oil pump is the part that circulates the oil throughout the engine and lubricates the moving parts.
  - The water pump is the part that circulates the coolant (water and antifreeze) throughout the engine and the radiator and cools the engine.
  - The fuel pump is the part that delivers the fuel from the fuel tank to the fuel injectors or the carburetor.
  - The fuel injectors are the parts that spray the fuel into the intake manifold or the cylinders in a precise amount and timing.
  - The spark plugs are the parts that ignite the fuel-air mixture in the cylinders by creating a spark at the right moment.
  - The distributor is the part that distributes the high-voltage current from the ignition coil to the spark plugs in the correct order.
  - The alternator is the part that generates electricity from the engine's rotation and charges the battery and powers the electrical system.
  - The starter is the part that rotates the engine's crankshaft by means of an electric motor and a gear when the ignition key is turned.
- The internal combustion engine works by following four basic steps: intake, compression, power, and exhaust. These steps are also known as the four-stroke cycle .
  - Intake: The intake valve opens and the piston moves down, creating a vacuum that draws the air and fuel mixture into the cylinder.
  - Compression: The intake valve closes and the piston moves up, compressing the air and fuel mixture in the cylinder.
  - Power: The spark plug fires and ignites the compressed air and fuel mixture, creating a high-pressure gas that pushes the piston down and rotates the crankshaft.
  - Exhaust: The exhaust valve opens and the piston moves up, expelling the burned gas out of the cylinder.



### Construction and Working of Two stroke and four stroke SI & CI engine

- SI stands for spark ignition and CI stands for compression ignition. These are two types of internal combustion engines that use different methods to ignite the fuel-air mixture in the cylinder.
- A two stroke engine completes one cycle of operation in two strokes of the piston or one revolution of the crankshaft. A four stroke engine completes one cycle of operation in four strokes of the piston or two revolutions of the crankshaft.
- The four strokes of a four stroke engine are intake, compression, power and exhaust. The two strokes of a two stroke engine are compression and power (or expansion).
- In a four stroke SI engine, the intake stroke draws the fuel-air mixture into the cylinder through the intake valve. The compression stroke compresses the mixture and the spark plug ignites it at the end of the stroke. The power stroke produces work by expanding the hot gases and pushing the piston down. The exhaust stroke expels the burnt gases through the exhaust valve.
- In a four stroke CI engine, the intake stroke draws only air into the cylinder through the intake valve. The compression stroke compresses the air to a high pressure and temperature. The fuel is injected into the cylinder near the end of the stroke and ignites spontaneously due to the heat of the compressed air. The power stroke produces work by expanding the hot gases and pushing the piston down. The exhaust stroke expels the burnt gases through the exhaust valve.
- In a two stroke SI engine, the compression stroke compresses the fuel-air mixture in the cylinder and the spark plug ignites it at the end of the stroke. The power stroke produces work by expanding the hot gases and pushing the piston down. At the same time, the fuel-air mixture is drawn into the crankcase through the inlet port. The exhaust stroke expels the burnt gases through the exhaust port and transfers the fresh mixture from the crankcase to the cylinder through the transfer port.
- In a two stroke CI engine, the compression stroke compresses only air in the cylinder and the fuel is injected into the cylinder near the end of the stroke and ignites spontaneously due to the heat of the compressed air. The power stroke produces work by expanding the hot gases and pushing the piston down. At the same time, the air is drawn into the crankcase through the inlet port. The exhaust stroke expels the burnt gases through the exhaust port and transfers the fresh air from the crankcase to the cylinder through the transfer port.
- Two stroke engines have simpler valve systems and fewer moving parts, and are more power dense than four stroke engines. However, since they require oil to be mixed with the fuel, and some of the fuel mixture escapes unused, they pollute more than a comparable four stroke engine .
- Four stroke engines have more complex valve systems and more moving parts, and are less power dense than two stroke engines. However, they have better fuel efficiency and lower emissions than two stroke engines .

### Scavenging Process

Scavenging is the process of replacing the exhaust gas in a cylinder of an internal combustion engine with the fresh air/fuel mixture (or fresh air, in the case of direct-injection engines) for the next cycle. Scavenging is necessary for the engine's proper fuel combustion and better power output.

Some of the points to note about scavenging are:

- Scavenging occurs in the ic engine during the overlapping of valves (Opens at the same time) in which burnt gases are released out of the cylinder with the entering of fresh charge inside the engine at the same time.
- Scavenging is more important for two-stroke engines than for four-stroke engines, because in two-stroke engines, the intake and exhaust ports are open simultaneously, and there is no dedicated exhaust stroke.
- The efficiency of scavenging depends on the design of the cylinder, the piston, the ports, the valves, and the exhaust system.
- The three types of scavenging used in two-stroke ic engines are as follows:
  - Uniflow scavenging: The fresh charge enters from one end of the cylinder and the exhaust gas exits from the other end. This is the most efficient type of scavenging, but it requires a poppet valve or a piston valve for the exhaust port.
  - Cross-flow scavenging: The fresh charge enters from one side of the cylinder and the exhaust gas exits from the opposite side. This is the simplest type of scavenging, but it causes a lot of mixing of the fresh charge and the exhaust gas, leading to poor performance and emissions.
  - Loop or reverse scavenging: The fresh charge enters from the bottom of the cylinder and the exhaust gas exits from the top. This type of scavenging creates a loop of flow inside the cylinder, which helps to push out the exhaust gas, but it also wastes some of the fresh charge.

## Unit 3 - Introduction to Refrigeration and Air-Conditioning

- Refrigeration and air-conditioning are the processes of maintaining a desired temperature and humidity level in an enclosed space by removing or adding heat from or to that space.
- Refrigeration and air-conditioning use the **refrigeration cycle**, which is a series of thermodynamic processes that transfer heat from a low-temperature region to a high-temperature region using a **refrigerant** as the working fluid.
- The refrigeration cycle consists of four main components: **compressor**, **condenser**, **expansion device**, and **evaporator**.
- The refrigeration cycle can be explained as follows :
  - The refrigerant enters the compressor as a low-pressure, low-temperature vapor and is compressed to a high-pressure, high-temperature vapor.
  - The refrigerant leaves the compressor and flows through the condenser, where it releases heat to the surrounding air or water and condenses to a high-pressure, high-temperature liquid.
  - The refrigerant passes through the expansion device, which reduces its pressure and temperature and causes some of it to flash to a vapor. This process is called **throttling** or **expansion with flashing**.
  - The refrigerant enters the evaporator as a low-pressure, low-temperature liquid-vapor mixture and absorbs heat from the space to be cooled, such as a refrigerator or a room. The refrigerant evaporates to a low-pressure, low-temperature vapor and returns to the compressor to complete the cycle.
- The performance of a refrigeration or air-conditioning system is measured by the **coefficient of performance (COP)**, which is the ratio of the useful heat transfer to the work input. The COP can be expressed as:

  - COP = QL / W for refrigeration, where QL is the heat removed from the low-temperature region and W is the work done by the compressor.
  - COP = QH / W for air-conditioning, where QH is the heat delivered to the high-temperature region and W is the work done by the compressor.
- The properties of air that are relevant for air-conditioning are its **specific heat capacity (Cp)**, which is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of air by one degree Celsius, and its **humidity ratio (w)**, which is the mass of water vapor per unit mass of dry air. The Cp of air is about 1.005 kJ/kg.K and the w of air varies depending on the temperature and the relative humidity.
- Air-conditioning systems can be classified into two types: **all-air systems** and **air-water systems**. All-air systems use only air as the medium for heat transfer and distribution, while air-water systems use a combination of air and water for heat transfer and distribution.



### Refrigeration

Some of the uses of refrigeration are:

- To provide thermal comfort for humans and animals by cooling the air in buildings, vehicles and other spaces.

- To improve the performance and efficiency of some devices, such as electronic components, power plants, etc.

There are two main types of refrigeration systems:

- Vapor compression refrigeration system: This system uses a refrigerant, which is a fluid that changes its phase from liquid to vapor and back, to absorb and release heat. The refrigerant is compressed, condensed, expanded and evaporated in a closed cycle, while exchanging heat with the surroundings. This system is the most common and widely used type of refrigeration system .
- Vapor absorption refrigeration system: This system also uses a refrigerant, but instead of compressing it mechanically, it uses a secondary fluid, called an absorbent, to absorb and release the refrigerant. The refrigerant and the absorbent form a solution, which is separated by heat and pressure. This system is less efficient and more complex than the vapor compression system, but it can use low-grade heat sources, such as solar energy, waste heat, etc.

Refrigerants are the fluids that are used in refrigeration systems to carry heat from one place to another. - Blended refrigerants: These are mixtures of two or more refrigerants, which are designed to have desirable properties, such as low GWP and ODP, high efficiency, etc. They may have different compositions, such as azeotropic, zeotropic or near-azeotropic, depending on their behavior during phase change.

### Refrigerating effect

- Refrigerating effect is the amount of heat removed from a substance or a space to lower its temperature or to maintain it below the surrounding temperature.
- Refrigerating effect is measured in units of energy, such as joules (J), kilojoules (kJ), or kilowatt-hours (kWh).
- Refrigerating effect can be calculated by multiplying the mass of the substance or the volume of the space by the specific heat capacity and the temperature difference.
- Refrigerating effect can also be expressed as the rate of heat removal, which is measured in units of power, such as watts (W), kilowatts (kW), or tons of refrigeration (TR).
- One ton of refrigeration is defined as the refrigerating effect of melting one ton of ice at 0°C in 24 hours, which is equivalent to 211 kJ/min or 3.517 kW.
- Refrigerating effect is an important parameter in the design and performance analysis of refrigeration and air-conditioning systems, as it indicates the cooling capacity and the energy efficiency of the system.



### Ton of Refrigeration

- A ton of refrigeration (TR or TOR), also called a refrigeration ton (RT), is a unit of power used in some countries (especially in North America) to describe the heat-extraction capacity of refrigeration and air conditioning equipment .
- It is defined as the rate of heat transfer that results in the freezing or melting of 1 short ton (2,000 lb; 907 kg) of pure ice at 0 °C (32 °F) in 24 hours .
- A ton of refrigeration is equivalent to 211 kJ/min or 200 Btu/min .

### Coefficient of performance

- The coefficient of performance (COP) is a measure of the efficiency of a refrigeration or air-conditioning system.
- It is defined as the ratio of the cooling effect (or heat removed) to the work input (or energy consumed) by the system.
- Mathematically, COP = Q/W, where Q is the cooling effect and W is the work input.
- The COP depends on the operating conditions of the system, such as the temperatures of the refrigerant at different points in the cycle, the type of refrigerant used, and the design of the components.
- The higher the COP, the more efficient the system is. A COP of 1 means that the system is operating at the theoretical limit of efficiency, where the cooling effect is equal to the work input. A COP of less than 1 means that the system is consuming more energy than it is removing heat.
- The COP can also be expressed in terms of the heating effect (or heat delivered) by a heat pump system, which is the opposite of a refrigeration system. In this case, COP = Q/W, where Q is the heating effect and W is the work input.

### Newtonian and Non-Newtonian fluid

- A fluid is a substance that can flow and take the shape of the container that holds it.

  - Shear-thinning fluids: These are fluids whose viscosity decreases with increasing shear stress. They are also called pseudoplastic fluids. Examples are ketchup, paint, and shampoo.

### Pascal’s Law and Continuity Equation

Pascal's law is a principle of fluid mechanics that states that a pressure applied to a fluid in a closed container is transmitted equally to every point of the fluid and the walls of the container. This means that if a force F is applied to a piston with area A, the pressure P exerted by the fluid is given by:

P = F / A

This pressure is the same at any point in the fluid, regardless of the shape or size of the container. Pascal's law can be used to explain how hydraulic systems work, such as brakes, lifts, and presses.

The continuity equation is another principle of fluid mechanics that states that the mass flow rate of a fluid is constant in a pipe or channel under steady-state conditions. This means that the product of the cross-sectional area A and the velocity v of the fluid is the same at any point in the pipe or channel. The continuity equation can be written as:

A1v1 = A2v2

where A1 and v1 are the area and velocity of the fluid at one point, and A2 and v2 are the area and velocity of the fluid at another point. The continuity equation implies that the fluid accelerates when the cross-sectional area decreases, and decelerates when the cross-sectional area increases. The continuity equation can be used to analyze the flow of fluids in pipes, nozzles, and turbines.

Pascal's law and the continuity equation are two important concepts in fluid mechanics that describe the behavior of fluids under pressure and flow. They can be applied to various engineering problems and applications involving fluids.

- Systematic error is the error that is constant and predictable in a measurement. It can be caused by factors such as faulty instruments, incorrect calibration, improper methods, or personal bias. Systematic error can be reduced by correcting the source of error or applying a suitable correction factor.
- Random error is the error that is unpredictable and varies in a measurement. It can be caused by factors such as environmental disturbances, fluctuations in the instrument, or human errors. Random error can be reduced by increasing the number of measurements and applying statistical methods such as mean, standard deviation, or confidence interval.
- The accuracy of a measurement is the degree of closeness of the measured value to the true value. Accuracy can be expressed as a percentage or as an absolute error. Absolute error is the difference between the measured value and the true value. Percentage error is the ratio of the absolute error to the true value, multiplied by 100.
- The precision of a measurement is the degree of repeatability or consistency of the measured values. Precision can be expressed as a range or as a standard deviation. Range is the difference between the maximum and minimum measured values. Standard deviation is the measure of how much the measured values deviate from the mean value.



### Calibration

- Calibration is the process of comparing the measurement values delivered by a device under test (DUT) with those of a calibration standard of known accuracy .
- Calibration is done to determine the error or verify the accuracy of the DUT's unknown value.
- Calibration can also be defined as the operation that establishes a relation between the quantity values with measurement uncertainties provided by measurement standards and the corresponding indications with associated measurement uncertainties of the calibrated instrument or secondary standard.
- Calibration is essential for ensuring the quality and reliability of measurements in various fields of science, engineering, and technology.
- Calibration can be classified into two types: static calibration and dynamic calibration.
  - Static calibration is the comparison of the DUT with the standard under fixed or steady conditions.
  - Dynamic calibration is the comparison of the DUT with the standard under varying or transient conditions.
- Calibration can be performed in different ways, such as:
  - Direct calibration: the DUT and the standard measure the same quantity directly.
  - Indirect calibration: the DUT and the standard measure different quantities that are related by a known function.
  - Transfer calibration: the DUT is compared with an intermediate device that has been previously calibrated with the standard.
  - Self-calibration: the DUT is calibrated by using its own internal reference or by applying a known input.
- Calibration can be expressed in different terms, such as:
  - Calibration factor: the ratio of the indication of the standard to the indication of the DUT.
  - Calibration curve: the graphical representation of the relation between the indications of the DUT and the standard.
  - Calibration equation: the mathematical expression of the relation between the indications of the DUT and the standard.
  - Calibration uncertainty: the measure of the doubt or error associated with the calibration result.
- Calibration can be affected by various factors, such as:
  - Environmental conditions: the temperature, humidity, pressure, vibration, etc. that can influence the performance of the DUT and the standard.
  - Measurement errors: the systematic or random errors that can occur during the measurement process.
  - Drift: the gradual change in the characteristics or performance of the DUT or the standard over time.
  - Hysteresis: the difference in the indications of the DUT or the standard when the same quantity is measured in increasing or decreasing order.
  - Nonlinearity: the deviation of the calibration curve from a straight line.
- Calibration can be verified by using different methods, such as:
  - Repeatability: the ability of the DUT or the standard to give the same indication when the same quantity is measured repeatedly under the same conditions.
  - Reproducibility: the ability of the DUT or the standard to give the same indication when the same quantity is measured by different operators or in different locations.
  - Traceability: the ability of the DUT or the standard to be related to a higher-level standard or reference through an unbroken chain of comparisons with known uncertainties.
  - Accreditation: the formal recognition of the competence and credibility of the calibration laboratory or service provider by an authorized body.



### Measurements of Pressure (Bourdon Tube Pressure and U-Tube Manometer)

- Pressure is the force exerted by a fluid per unit area on a surface.
- Pressure can be measured by various devices that use the principle of balancing the fluid force with a known force, such as gravity, spring, or air.
- Two common devices for measuring pressure are the Bourdon tube pressure gauge and the U-tube manometer.

#### Bourdon Tube Pressure Gauge

- A Bourdon tube pressure gauge consists of a hollow metal tube that is bent into a circular or elliptical shape and has a flattened cross-section.
- One end of the tube is fixed and connected to the pressure source, while the other end is free and attached to a pointer that moves over a calibrated scale.
- When the pressure inside the tube increases, the tube tends to straighten due to the internal force, causing the free end to move and rotate the pointer.
- The pointer indicates the pressure difference between the inside and the outside of the tube, which is proportional to the amount of deflection of the tube.
- The Bourdon tube pressure gauge can measure both positive and negative pressures, and can be calibrated for different units of pressure, such as psi, bar, kPa, etc.
- The Bourdon tube pressure gauge is widely used in various applications because of its high precision, easy reading, and durability.

#### U-Tube Manometer

- A U-tube manometer consists of a U-shaped tube that is partially filled with a liquid, such as mercury, water, or oil.
- One end of the tube is open to the atmosphere, while the other end is connected to the pressure source.
- When the pressure inside the tube is different from the atmospheric pressure, the liquid level in the tube will rise or fall, creating a height difference between the two arms of the tube.
- The height difference, or the pressure head, is proportional to the pressure difference between the inside and the outside of the tube.
- The U-tube manometer can measure both positive and negative pressures, and can be calibrated for different units of pressure, such as mmHg, cmH2O, inH2O, etc.
- The U-tube manometer is simple, inexpensive, and accurate, but it requires a large vertical space and a stable liquid level.

# Temperature (Thermocouple and Optical Pyrometer)

- Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules in a substance.
- Temperature measurement is important for many industrial processes, such as metallurgy, chemical engineering, and manufacturing.
- There are different methods of measuring temperature, depending on the range, accuracy, and application required.
- Two common methods of temperature measurement are thermocouples and optical pyrometers.

## Thermocouple

- A thermocouple is a device that consists of two different metals joined at one end, forming a junction.
- When the junction is heated or cooled, a voltage is generated across the two metals, proportional to the temperature difference between the junction and the other end.
- The voltage can be measured by a voltmeter or a thermometer, and calibrated to give the temperature of the junction.
- Thermocouples are widely used for measuring temperatures from -200°C to 2000°C, with high accuracy and fast response time.
- Thermocouples are also cheap, rugged, and easy to install and use.
- However, thermocouples have some disadvantages, such as:
  - They require a reference temperature at the other end, which may be affected by ambient conditions or errors in measurement.
  - They are susceptible to corrosion, oxidation, and contamination, which may alter their characteristics and reduce their accuracy.
  - They have a nonlinear relationship between voltage and temperature, which requires complex calibration and compensation.
  - They have a low voltage output, which may be affected by noise and interference.

## Optical Pyrometer

- An optical pyrometer is a device that measures the temperature of a hot object by comparing its brightness or color to a known standard.
- The principle of an optical pyrometer is based on the fact that all objects emit electromagnetic radiation according to their temperature, and the wavelength and intensity of the radiation depend on the temperature.
- An optical pyrometer consists of an optical system, a detector, and a display or indicator.
- The optical system focuses the radiation from the target object onto the detector, which converts it into an electrical signal.
- The display or indicator shows the temperature of the target object, based on the calibration of the detector and the optical system.
- Optical pyrometers are used for measuring high temperatures, from 500°C to 3000°C, without contact or interference with the target object.
- Optical pyrometers have some advantages, such as:
  - They are fast, accurate, and reliable, as they do not depend on the physical properties or conditions of the target object.
  - They are safe, as they do not require contact or proximity to the target object, which may be hazardous or inaccessible.
  - They are simple, as they do not require complex calibration or compensation, and can be easily adjusted for different emissivities or wavelengths.
- However, optical pyrometers also have some disadvantages, such as:
  - They are affected by the emissivity of the target object, which is the ratio of the actual radiation emitted by the object to the ideal radiation emitted by a black body at the same temperature. The emissivity varies with the material, surface condition, and wavelength of the radiation, and may be unknown or variable.
  - They are affected by the ambient radiation, which may interfere with the radiation from the target object, and cause errors or fluctuations in the measurement.
  - They are affected by the optical system, which may introduce errors or distortions due to the lens, filter, or mirror used, or due to dust, smoke, or moisture in the air.

### Mass flow rate (Venturi Meter and Orifice Meter)

- Mass flow rate is the amount of mass of a fluid passing through a cross-sectional area per unit time. It is denoted by m dot and has the unit of kg/s.
- Mass flow rate can be measured using different devices, such as Venturi Meter and Orifice Meter, which are based on the principle of Bernoulli's equation.
- Bernoulli's equation states that the sum of the pressure, kinetic, and potential energies of a fluid along a streamline is constant. Mathematically, it can be written as:

`P + 1/2 rho v^2 + rho g h = constant`

where P is the pressure, rho is the density, v is the velocity, g is the gravitational acceleration, and h is the height of the fluid.

- Venturi Meter and Orifice Meter are devices that create a constriction in the flow of a fluid, which causes a change in the pressure and velocity of the fluid. By measuring the pressure difference between two points, the mass flow rate can be calculated using the continuity equation and Bernoulli's equation.
- The continuity equation states that the mass flow rate of a fluid is constant throughout a pipe. Mathematically, it can be written as:

`m dot = rho A v = constant`

where A is the cross-sectional area of the pipe.

- Venturi Meter is a device that consists of a converging section, a throat, and a diverging section. The converging section reduces the cross-sectional area of the pipe, which increases the velocity and decreases the pressure of the fluid. The throat is the narrowest part of the device, where the velocity is the highest and the pressure is the lowest. The diverging section increases the cross-sectional area of the pipe, which decreases the velocity and increases the pressure of the fluid. The pressure difference between the inlet and the throat can be used to calculate the mass flow rate using the following formula:

`m dot = A1 A2 sqrt(2 rho (P1 - P2) / (A1^2 - A2^2))`

where A1 and A2 are the cross-sectional areas of the inlet and the throat, and P1 and P2 are the pressures at the inlet and the throat.

- Orifice Meter is a device that consists of a thin plate with a hole in the center, which is inserted in the pipe. The hole creates a constriction in the flow of the fluid, which increases the velocity and decreases the pressure of the fluid. The pressure difference between the upstream and the downstream of the hole can be used to calculate the mass flow rate using the following formula:

`m dot = C A sqrt(2 rho (P1 - P2))`

where C is the discharge coefficient, which accounts for the losses due to friction and turbulence, A is the cross-sectional area of the hole, and P1 and P2 are the pressures at the upstream and the downstream of the hole.

### Strain and Strain Gauges

- Strain is the measure of the deformation or change in dimensions of a body due to an applied force or stress.
- Strain is defined as the ratio of the change in length to the original length of the body.
- Strain can be positive (tensile) or negative (compressive) depending on the direction of the applied force.
- Strain gauges are devices that can measure strain by converting it into a change in electrical resistance.
- Strain gauges are made of thin metallic wires or foils that are attached to the surface of the body under stress.
- When the body deforms, the strain gauge also deforms and changes its resistance according to the strain.
- The change in resistance can be measured by a Wheatstone bridge circuit and calibrated to give the value of strain.
- Strain gauges can be classified into two types: bonded and unbonded strain gauges.

### Bonded Strain Gauges

- Bonded strain gauges are strain gauges that are glued or cemented to the surface of the body whose strain is being measured.
- Bonded strain gauges are in direct contact with the body and transfer the entire strain to the strain gauge.
- Bonded strain gauges are more sensitive, accurate, and reliable than unbonded strain gauges.
- Bonded strain gauges can be further classified into two types: metallic and semiconductor strain gauges.
- Metallic strain gauges are made of metals such as constantan, nichrome, platinum, or copper-nickel alloys that have a high gauge factor (ratio of relative change in resistance to relative change in length).
- Semiconductor strain gauges are made of materials such as silicon or germanium that have a very high gauge factor (up to 100 times higher than metallic strain gauges) and can measure very small strains.
- However, semiconductor strain gauges are also more sensitive to temperature changes and require temperature compensation.

### Unbonded Strain Gauges

- Unbonded strain gauges are strain gauges that are not attached to the surface of the body whose strain is being measured.
- Unbonded strain gauges are suspended between two frames that are connected to the body by flexible wires or rods.
- When the body deforms, the frames also deform and change the tension in the strain gauge wire.
- Unbonded strain gauges are less sensitive, accurate, and reliable than bonded strain gauges.
- Unbonded strain gauges are also more prone to errors due to friction, hysteresis, and creep.
- Unbonded strain gauges are mainly used for measuring large strains or dynamic strains that vary rapidly with time.

### Introduction to Mechatronic Systems

- A mechatronic system is a system that integrates mechanical, electrical, and computer engineering components to perform a desired function.
- Mechatronic systems can be found in various applications, such as robotics, automotive, aerospace, biomedical, manufacturing, and smart devices.
- Mechatronic systems are designed using a multidisciplinary approach that considers the interactions and interdependencies among the subsystems and the environment.
- Mechatronic systems can be classified into three types based on the level of integration: discrete, continuous, and hybrid.
  - Discrete mechatronic systems consist of separate mechanical and electrical components that are connected by wires, cables, or connectors. Examples are electric motors, sensors, and actuators.
  - Continuous mechatronic systems have mechanical and electrical components that are physically integrated into a single structure. Examples are piezoelectric materials, shape memory alloys, and microelectromechanical systems (MEMS).
  - Hybrid mechatronic systems combine discrete and continuous components to achieve a higher level of functionality and performance. Examples are smart structures, adaptive optics, and haptic devices.
- Mechatronic systems are characterized by the following features:
  - Sensing: the ability to measure physical quantities, such as position, velocity, force, temperature, pressure, etc.
  - Actuation: the ability to generate physical outputs, such as motion, force, torque, heat, light, etc.
  - Control: the ability to regulate the behavior of the system according to a desired objective, such as stability, accuracy, speed, efficiency, etc.
  - Communication: the ability to exchange information among the components of the system or with external devices, such as computers, networks, or humans.
  - Intelligence: the ability to process information, make decisions, and learn from experience, using algorithms, software, or artificial intelligence techniques.
- Mechatronic systems are designed using a systematic process that involves the following steps:
  - Problem definition: identifying the needs, requirements, and specifications of the system.
  - Conceptual design: generating and evaluating alternative solutions based on the principles of engineering, physics, and mathematics.
  - Detailed design: selecting and optimizing the components, materials, and parameters of the system based on the criteria of functionality, performance, cost, reliability, etc.
  - Implementation: building, testing, and debugging the system using hardware, software, and tools.
  - Operation and maintenance: using, monitoring, and updating the system according to the feedback, data, and user feedback.
