6. **Manufacturing**: Manufacturing is the process of converting raw materials into finished products. It involves the design and operation of machines and processes for the production of goods.

7. **Control Systems**: Control systems are used to regulate the behavior of machines and processes. They involve the use of sensors, actuators, and control algorithms to achieve a desired outcome.

### Stress-Strain Diagram for Ductile and Brittle Materials

A stress-strain diagram is a graphical representation of the relationship between the stress and strain of a material. It is used to determine the mechanical properties of a material, such as its strength, ductility, and toughness.

#### Ductile Materials
- Ductile materials are materials that can undergo large plastic deformation before failure.
- The stress-strain diagram for a ductile material typically has a characteristic shape, with an initial linear region where the material behaves elastically, followed by a region of plastic deformation where the material yields and deforms permanently.
- The slope of the initial linear region is the modulus of elasticity, or Young's modulus, of the material.
- The point at which the material begins to yield is called the yield point, and the stress at this point is called the yield strength.
- Beyond the yield point, the material continues to deform plastically until it reaches its ultimate strength, which is the maximum stress the material can withstand before failure.
- After reaching its ultimate strength, the material begins to neck and the stress decreases until the material fractures.

#### Brittle Materials
- Brittle materials are materials that fracture with little or no plastic deformation.
- The stress-strain diagram for a brittle material typically has a linear region where the material behaves elastically, followed by a sudden fracture at the ultimate strength of the material.
- The slope of the linear region is the modulus of elasticity, or Young's modulus, of the material.
- Brittle materials do not have a well-defined yield point, and the ultimate strength is often used as a measure of the material's strength.




### Factor of Safety

Factor of safety (FOS) is a term used in engineering to describe the structural capacity of a system beyond the expected loads or actual loads. It is a measure of the load-carrying capacity of a structure or component beyond its intended use.

The factor of safety is calculated by dividing the ultimate strength of the material by the allowable stress. The ultimate strength is the maximum stress that a material can withstand before failure, while the allowable stress is the maximum stress that a material can safely withstand.

A higher factor of safety indicates a higher margin of safety and a lower likelihood of failure. However, a higher factor of safety also means that the structure or component is heavier and more expensive than it needs to be.

Factors that can affect the factor of safety include the type of material used, the manufacturing process, the environment in which the structure or component will be used, and the level of uncertainty in the design and analysis.

In general, the factor of safety is chosen based on the level of risk associated with the structure or component. For example, a higher factor of safety may be used for structures or components that are critical to the safety of people or the environment, or for structures or components that are difficult or expensive to repair or replace.

It is important to note that the factor of safety is not a guarantee of safety. It is a design tool used to help ensure that structures and components are able to withstand the loads and stresses they are likely to encounter during their intended use. However, it is still possible for a structure or component to fail, even if it has been designed with a high factor of safety. Therefore, regular inspection and maintenance are important to ensure the continued safety and reliability of structures and components.

### Scavenging Process

Scavenging is the process that occurs in an internal combustion engine during the overlapping of valves (when they open at the same time) in which burnt gases are released out of the cylinder with the entering of fresh charge inside the engine at the same time. There are three types of scavenging used in two-stroke IC engines:
1. Uniflow scavenging
2. Cross-flow scavenging
3. Loop or reverse scavenging.

If scavenging is incomplete, the remaining exhaust gases can cause improper combustion for the next cycle, leading to reduced power output.

#### Newtonian and Non-Newtonian Fluids

- A **Newtonian fluid** is a fluid whose viscosity remains constant, regardless of the amount of shear stress applied to it. This means that the fluid flows at a constant rate, regardless of the force applied to it. Examples of Newtonian fluids include water, air, and honey.

- A **Non-Newtonian fluid**, on the other hand, is a fluid whose viscosity changes with the amount of shear stress applied to it. This means that the fluid's flow rate changes depending on the force applied to it. Examples of Non-Newtonian fluids include ketchup, blood, and quicksand.

- The main difference between Newtonian and Non-Newtonian fluids is the way they respond to shear stress. Newtonian fluids have a linear relationship between shear stress and shear rate, while Non-Newtonian fluids have a non-linear relationship.

- Non-Newtonian fluids can be further classified into different types, based on their behavior under shear stress. These include shear-thinning fluids, shear-thickening fluids, and Bingham plastics.

- Shear-thinning fluids, also known as pseudoplastics, decrease in viscosity as the shear rate increases. This means that they flow more easily when a force is applied to them. Examples of shear-thinning fluids include paint and blood.

- Shear-thickening fluids, also known as dilatants, increase in viscosity as the shear rate increases. This means that they become more difficult to flow when a force is applied to them. Examples of shear-thickening fluids include cornstarch and water mixtures.

- Bingham plastics, on the other hand, require a certain amount of force to be applied before they start to flow. Once this force is reached, they behave like a Newtonian fluid. Examples of Bingham plastics include toothpaste and mayonnaise.

- In summary, Newtonian fluids have a constant viscosity, while Non-Newtonian fluids have a variable viscosity that changes with the amount of shear stress applied to them. Non-Newtonian fluids can be further classified into different types based on their behavior under shear stress.



### Pascal’s Law and Continuity Equation

Pascal's Law states that pressure exerted on a fluid in a closed container is transmitted equally and undiminished to every point in the fluid and to the walls of the container. ### Mass Flow Rate (Venturi Meter and Orifice Meter)

A Venturi meter is a device that measures the flow rate of a fluid through a pipe. It consists of a short, constricted section of pipe called a throat, which is placed between two wider sections of the pipe. As the fluid flows through the throat, its velocity increases, causing a decrease in pressure. The pressure difference between the wider sections of the pipe and the throat is measured, and this difference is used to calculate the flow rate of the fluid.

#### Orifice Meter

An orifice meter is another device used to measure the flow rate of a fluid through a pipe. It consists of a flat orifice plate with a small hole in the center, which is placed in the pipe. As the fluid flows through the hole, its velocity increases, causing a decrease in pressure. The pressure difference between the upstream and downstream sides of the orifice plate is measured, and this difference is used to calculate the flow rate of the fluid.

Both Venturi meters and orifice meters are based on the principle of conservation of energy, which states that the total energy of a fluid remains constant as it flows through a pipe. The increase in velocity of the fluid as it flows through the constricted section of the pipe (the throat or the orifice) is accompanied by a decrease in pressure, and this pressure difference is used to calculate the flow rate of the fluid.

These devices are commonly used in various industries, including oil and gas, water treatment, and chemical processing, to measure the flow rate of fluids in pipes.
