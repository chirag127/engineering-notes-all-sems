## Reading
Reading is the process of extracting meaning from written texts. Reading involves decoding symbols, recognizing words, comprehending sentences and paragraphs, and interpreting the author's purpose and perspective. Reading also requires critical thinking skills, such as evaluating the credibility, relevance and accuracy of the information, comparing and contrasting different viewpoints, and synthesizing and applying the knowledge to new situations. Reading is important for expanding one's vocabulary, enhancing one's general knowledge, and developing one's analytical and creative abilities.

Some tips and strategies for improving reading skills are:

- Set a clear purpose and goal for reading. For example, are you reading for pleasure, for information, for analysis, or for evaluation?
- Choose texts that match your interest, level and purpose. For example, if you are reading for pleasure, you can choose texts that are entertaining, engaging and easy to understand. If you are reading for information, you can choose texts that are factual, relevant and concise. If you are reading for analysis, you can choose texts that are complex, challenging and argumentative. If you are reading for evaluation, you can choose texts that are controversial, persuasive and debatable.
- Use different reading strategies depending on your purpose and text type. For example, if you are reading for pleasure, you can use skimming and scanning techniques to get a general idea of the text and identify the main points. If you are reading for information, you can use previewing and predicting techniques to activate your prior knowledge and anticipate what the text will be about. If you are reading for analysis, you can use annotating and summarizing techniques to highlight and organize the key information and ideas. If you are reading for evaluation, you can use questioning and critiquing techniques to challenge and assess the author's claims and evidence.
- Monitor your comprehension and adjust your reading speed and strategy accordingly. For example, if you are reading for pleasure, you can read at a comfortable pace and enjoy the text. If you are reading for information, you can read at a moderate pace and focus on the main facts and details. If you are reading for analysis, you can read at a slow pace and pay attention to the structure, language and style of the text. If you are reading for evaluation, you can read at a variable pace and compare and contrast different perspectives and arguments.
- Review and reflect on what you have read and how it relates to your purpose and goal. For example, if you are reading for pleasure, you can share your opinions and feelings about the text with others. If you are reading for information, you can summarize and paraphrase the main points and facts in your own words. If you are reading for analysis, you can synthesize and interpret the meaning and implications of the text. If you are reading for evaluation, you can formulate and justify your own position and perspective on the text.

- The objective of this topic is to help students improve their writing skills by learning and applying the rules of English grammar.
- Writing is a form of communication that requires clarity, accuracy, and coherence. Writing errors can affect the meaning, credibility, and effectiveness of the message.
- Grammar is the system of rules that governs how words and sentences are structured, used, and punctuated in a language. Grammar helps to ensure that the writer's intended meaning is conveyed to the reader.
- To use the correct and error-free writing, students need to be well versed in the following aspects of English grammar:

  - Parts of speech: the categories of words based on their function and meaning, such as nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions, and interjections.
  - Sentence structure: the way words are arranged to form complete and meaningful sentences, such as subject, predicate, object, complement, modifier, clause, and phrase.
  - Verb tenses: the forms of verbs that indicate the time and aspect of the action or state, such as present, past, future, simple, continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous.
  - Subject-verb agreement: the rule that the verb must agree in number and person with the subject of the sentence, such as singular or plural, first, second, or third person.
  - Pronoun-antecedent agreement: the rule that the pronoun must agree in number, gender, and person with the noun or noun phrase that it refers to, such as he, she, it, they, etc.
  - Modifiers: the words or phrases that add detail or description to another word or phrase, such as adjectives, adverbs, prepositional phrases, participial phrases, etc.
  - Parallelism: the principle of using the same grammatical form or structure for words, phrases, or clauses that are coordinated or compared, such as nouns with nouns, verbs with verbs, etc.
  - Punctuation: the marks that separate and clarify the elements of a sentence, such as commas, periods, semicolons, colons, apostrophes, quotation marks, etc.

- To train students to use the correct and error-free writing by being well versed in rules of English grammar, the following strategies can be used:

  - Provide clear and concise explanations and examples of the grammar rules and concepts.
  - Use a variety of exercises and activities to practice and reinforce the grammar skills, such as fill in the blanks, multiple choice, matching, error correction, rewriting, etc.
  - Give feedback and correction on the students' writing assignments and point out the grammar errors and how to fix them.
  - Encourage the students to read and analyze different types of texts and genres and observe how grammar is used in different contexts and purposes.
  - Motivate the students to use online resources and tools, such as dictionaries, grammar checkers, and websites, to learn more about grammar and improve their writing.

Some of the basic rules of English grammar and how to apply them in writing are:

- Parallel structure: Words, phrases, or clauses that are coordinated or compared must have the same form and function. For example, "She likes reading, writing, and painting." (reading, writing, and painting are gerunds) "She likes to read, to write, and to paint." (to read, to write, and to paint are infinitives)
