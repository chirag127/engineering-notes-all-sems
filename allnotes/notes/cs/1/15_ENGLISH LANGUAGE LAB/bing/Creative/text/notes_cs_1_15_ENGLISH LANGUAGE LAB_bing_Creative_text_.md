  - Accent: The distinctive way of pronouncing a language, especially one associated with a particular country, region or social class.
  - Intonation: The rise and fall of the voice in speaking, especially as this affects the meaning and expression of sentences.
  - Stress: The emphasis given to a word or syllable in speaking, either by pronouncing it louder, higher or longer than others, or by changing the pitch or tone of the voice.
  - Rhythm: The pattern of sounds and silences in speech, created by the alternation of stressed and unstressed syllables and pauses.
  - Fluency: The ability to speak or write a language easily and smoothly, without hesitation, repetition or errors.
  - Coherence: The quality of being logical and consistent in speech or writing, and of forming a unified whole.
  - Cohesion: The use of words, phrases and grammatical devices to connect and relate sentences and paragraphs in speech or writing.
  - Paraphrasing: The act of expressing the meaning of something using different words, especially to achieve greater clarity or brevity.
  - Summarizing: The act of giving a brief and concise account of the main points of something, omitting details and examples.
  - Presentation skills: The ability to deliver a speech or a report to an audience effectively and confidently, using appropriate language, voice, body language and visual aids.

- Part B: This part consists of written communication skills and focuses on the following aspects:

  - Vocabulary: The set of words that a person knows or uses in a language, or that are available in a language or subject.
  - Grammar: The system and structure of a language, including the rules of word formation, word order, sentence structure and punctuation.
  - Spelling: The way of writing a word using the correct letters in the correct order.
  - Punctuation: The marks, such as commas, periods and question marks, that are used in writing to separate sentences and clauses, and to indicate pauses, emphasis and intonation.
  - Style: The way of writing or speaking that is characteristic of a particular person, group, period or genre, and that reflects their choices of words, sentence structures, tone and register.
  - Format: The way of arranging and presenting written or printed material, such as margins, fonts, headings, paragraphs and lists, according to a set of conventions or standards.
  - Citation: The act of acknowledging the source of information, ideas or words that are not one's own, by providing the author's name, date of publication and page number, or a link to the online source, in a specific style, such as APA, MLA or Chicago.
  - Plagiarism: The act of copying or using someone else's work or ideas without giving proper credit, and presenting them as one's own, either intentionally or unintentionally.
  - Editing: The act of reviewing and correcting a written or printed text, by checking for errors, inconsistencies, clarity, accuracy and completeness, and making changes or suggestions for improvement.
  - Proofreading: The act of reading a written or printed text carefully and attentively, to find and mark any errors or mistakes, such as spelling, grammar, punctuation and formatting, before the final publication or submission.

### 1. Group Discussion: Practical based on Accurate and Current Grammatical Patterns.

- A group discussion is a form of oral communication where a group of participants exchange their views on a given topic in a structured and interactive manner.
- A group discussion can have various purposes, such as to assess the participants' knowledge, skills, attitudes, opinions, or personality traits; to generate ideas, solutions, or recommendations; to stimulate critical thinking, creativity, or teamwork; or to enhance learning, awareness, or communication skills.
- A group discussion requires the participants to use accurate and current grammatical patterns to express their ideas clearly, logically, and persuasively. Grammatical patterns are the combinations of words, phrases, clauses, and sentences that follow the rules and conventions of a language.
- Some of the grammatical patterns that are important for a group discussion are:

  - Subject-verb agreement: The subject and the verb of a sentence must agree in number and person. For example, "He speaks English well." (singular subject and verb) or "They speak English well." (plural subject and verb).
  - Tense and aspect: The tense and aspect of a verb indicate the time and duration of an action or state. For example, "She is speaking now." (present continuous tense and aspect) or "She spoke yesterday." (past simple tense and aspect).
  - Voice: The voice of a verb shows whether the subject is the doer or the receiver of the action. For example, "He wrote the report." (active voice) or "The report was written by him." (passive voice).
  - Mood and modality: The mood and modality of a verb express the attitude, possibility, obligation, or permission of the speaker or writer. For example, "She can speak three languages." (indicative mood and modal verb of ability) or "She must speak three languages." (indicative mood and modal verb of necessity).
  - Sentence types: There are four main types of sentences: declarative, interrogative, imperative, and exclamatory. For example, "This is a good idea." (declarative sentence) or "Is this a good idea?" (interrogative sentence).
  - Sentence structures: There are three main types of sentence structures: simple, compound, and complex. For example, "She likes coffee." (simple sentence) or "She likes coffee and he likes tea." (compound sentence) or "She likes coffee because it keeps her awake." (complex sentence).
  - Punctuation: Punctuation marks are the symbols that help to organize and clarify the meaning of written texts. For example, "She said, 'I like coffee.'" (quotation marks) or "She likes coffee, but he likes tea." (comma).
  - Capitalization: Capitalization is the use of uppercase letters for certain words, such as proper nouns, titles, or the first word of a sentence. For example, "She is from London." (capitalization of proper noun) or "She is a doctor." (capitalization of title) or "She likes coffee." (capitalization of first word of sentence).

- To practice accurate and current grammatical patterns for a group discussion, the participants can:

  - Review the rules and examples of the grammatical patterns mentioned above.
  - Identify and correct any grammatical errors in their own or others' speech or writing.
  - Use a variety of grammatical patterns to express their ideas in different ways and avoid repetition or monotony.
  - Listen to or read authentic texts that use accurate and current grammatical patterns and analyze how they convey meaning and tone.
  - Engage in group discussions on various topics and give and receive feedback on their grammatical accuracy and effectiveness.

### 6. Theme Presentation/ Keynote Presentation based on correct methodologies of argumentation

A theme presentation or a keynote presentation is a type of speech that aims to introduce, explain, or persuade the audience on a specific topic or theme. It is usually delivered by an expert, a leader, or a prominent figure in the field of the topic. A theme presentation or a keynote presentation should follow the correct methodologies of argumentation, which are:

- **Claim**: A claim is a statement that expresses the main idea or the point of view of the speaker. It should be clear, concise, and debatable. For example, "Nuclear energy is the best solution for the global energy crisis."
- **Evidence**: Evidence is the information that supports the claim. It should be relevant, reliable, and sufficient. For example, "According to the World Nuclear Association, nuclear energy provides 10% of the world's electricity and emits 20 times less carbon dioxide than coal."
- **Warrant**: A warrant is the logical connection that explains how the evidence supports the claim. It should be valid, sound, and consistent. For example, "Nuclear energy is the best solution for the global energy crisis because it provides a large amount of electricity with minimal environmental impact."
- **Counterclaim**: A counterclaim is a statement that challenges or opposes the claim. It should acknowledge the opposing views and show the weaknesses or limitations of the claim. For example, "However, some people argue that nuclear energy is too risky and expensive to be the best solution for the global energy crisis."
- **Rebuttal**: A rebuttal is a response that refutes or undermines the counterclaim. It should address the specific points of the counterclaim and show why the claim is still stronger or more convincing. For example, "Nevertheless, the risks and costs of nuclear energy can be reduced by improving the safety standards, the waste management, and the technology of the nuclear reactors."

A theme presentation or a keynote presentation should follow a clear and logical structure that includes:

- **Introduction**: The introduction should capture the attention of the audience, establish the credibility of the speaker, and state the main claim or the thesis statement of the presentation.
- **Body**: The body should develop the main claim or the thesis statement by presenting the evidence, the warrant, the counterclaim, and the rebuttal for each sub-claim or supporting point. The body should also use transitions, signposts, and summaries to guide the audience through the presentation.
- **Conclusion**: The conclusion should restate the main claim or the thesis statement, summarize the main points, and provide a call to action or a final remark that leaves a lasting impression on the audience.

A theme presentation or a keynote presentation should also use effective delivery techniques, such as:

- **Verbal**: The speaker should use clear and appropriate language, vary the tone, volume, and pace of the voice, and avoid filler words, jargon, and slang.
- **Non-verbal**: The speaker should use eye contact, facial expressions, gestures, and posture to convey confidence, enthusiasm, and rapport with the audience.
- **Visual**: The speaker should use visual aids, such as slides, charts, graphs, images, or videos, to enhance the presentation and illustrate the main points. The visual aids should be simple, relevant, and consistent with the verbal message.
