## List of experiments

An experiment is a scientific procedure that is carried out to test a hypothesis, observe a phenomenon, or measure an effect. Experiments can be classified into different types based on their purpose, design, and outcome. Here are some common types of experiments:

- **Controlled experiment**: An experiment in which only one variable is changed at a time, while all other variables are kept constant. The variable that is changed is called the independent variable, and the variable that is measured is called the dependent variable. The purpose of a controlled experiment is to isolate the effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable. For example, a controlled experiment to test the effect of fertilizer on plant growth would involve growing plants with different amounts of fertilizer, while keeping other factors such as water, light, and soil constant.

- **Randomized experiment**: An experiment in which the subjects or units are randomly assigned to different groups or treatments. The purpose of a randomized experiment is to reduce the bias and confounding factors that may affect the results. For example, a randomized experiment to test the effect of a new drug on blood pressure would involve randomly assigning patients to either receive the drug or a placebo, and measuring their blood pressure before and after the treatment.

- **Natural experiment**: An experiment in which the independent variable is not manipulated by the experimenter, but rather by a natural phenomenon or event. The purpose of a natural experiment is to study the causal relationship between the independent variable and the dependent variable in a real-world setting. For example, a natural experiment to study the effect of smoking on lung cancer would involve comparing the incidence of lung cancer among smokers and non-smokers who were exposed to the same environmental factors.

- **Quasi-experiment**: An experiment in which the subjects or units are not randomly assigned to different groups or treatments, but rather selected based on some criteria or pre-existing condition. The purpose of a quasi-experiment is to approximate the results of a randomized experiment when randomization is not possible or ethical. For example, a quasi-experiment to study the effect of a training program on employee performance would involve comparing the performance of employees who voluntarily enrolled in the program and those who did not.

- **Field experiment**: An experiment that is conducted in a natural or realistic setting, rather than in a laboratory or controlled environment. The purpose of a field experiment is to test the validity and generalizability of a hypothesis or theory in a more complex and dynamic context. For example, a field experiment to test the effect of social norms on littering behavior would involve observing how people behave in different public places with different levels of cleanliness and social cues.

- **Laboratory experiment**: An experiment that is conducted in a controlled and artificial setting, such as a laboratory or a simulation. The purpose of a laboratory experiment is to test the reliability and precision of a hypothesis or theory in a simplified and isolated context. For example, a laboratory experiment to test the effect of temperature on chemical reactions would involve manipulating and measuring the temperature and the reaction rate in a controlled environment.

- Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL) states that the algebraic sum of the currents entering a node (or a closed boundary) is zero. This means that the total charge entering a node is equal to the total charge leaving it.
- Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL) states that the algebraic sum of the voltages around a loop (or a closed path) is zero. This means that the total energy gained by the charges in a loop is equal to the total energy lost by them.

##### 1. Kirchhoff‟s laws

Kirchhoff's laws are a set of laws that quantify how current flows through a circuit and how voltage varies around a loop in a circuit. They are used to govern the conservation of charge and energy in standard electrical circuits . They were first described in 1845 by German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff.

There are two Kirchhoff's laws:

- Kirchhoff's current law (KCL): This law, also called Kirchhoff's first law, or Kirchhoff's junction rule, states that, for any node (junction) in an electrical circuit, the sum of currents flowing into that node is equal to the sum of currents flowing out of that node; or equivalently: The algebraic sum of currents in a network of conductors meeting at a point is zero . This law is based on the principle of conservation of charge, which implies that charge cannot be created or destroyed at any node.

- Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): This law, also called Kirchhoff's second law, or Kirchhoff's loop rule, states that, for any closed loop in an electrical circuit, the sum of voltages across each element of the loop is equal to zero; or equivalently: The algebraic sum of the products of the currents and the resistances in each of the conductors in any closed loop in a network plus the algebraic sum of the electromotive forces in that loop is zero . This law is based on the principle of conservation of energy, which implies that energy cannot be created or destroyed in any loop.

- Thevenin's theorem can be used to make circuit analysis simpler and to study a circuit's initial-condition and steady-state response.

##### 3. RLC series resonance

- RLC series resonance is a phenomenon that occurs in a circuit that contains a resistor (R), an inductor (L), and a capacitor (C) connected in series.
- When the circuit is driven by an alternating voltage source with a frequency that matches the natural frequency of the circuit, the circuit exhibits maximum current and minimum impedance.
- The natural frequency of the circuit is given by the formula:

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

- where f0 is the natural frequency in hertz, L is the inductance in henries, and C is the capacitance in farads.
- At resonance, the reactance of the inductor and the capacitor cancel each other out, and the circuit behaves like a pure resistor with a resistance equal to R.
- The current in the circuit is in phase with the voltage source, and the voltage across each component is proportional to its impedance.
- The voltage across the resistor is equal to the source voltage, the voltage across the inductor leads the current by 90 degrees, and the voltage across the capacitor lags the current by 90 degrees.
- The phasor diagram of the circuit at resonance is shown below:


- The total impedance of the circuit at resonance is given by the formula:

$$Z = R$$

- where Z is the impedance in ohms, and R is the resistance in ohms.
- The quality factor (Q) of the circuit is a measure of how sharp the resonance is, and is given by the formula:

$$Q = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$$

- where Q is the quality factor, R is the resistance in ohms, L is the inductance in henries, and C is the capacitance in farads.
- A higher Q means a narrower bandwidth and a higher peak current at resonance.
- The bandwidth (B) of the circuit is the range of frequencies around the resonance frequency where the current is at least 70.7% of the peak current, and is given by the formula:

$$B = \frac{f_0}{Q}$$

- where B is the bandwidth in hertz, f0 is the natural frequency in hertz, and Q is the quality factor.
- The power factor (PF) of the circuit is the ratio of the real power to the apparent power, and is given by the formula:

$$PF = \frac{R}{Z}$$

- where PF is the power factor, R is the resistance in ohms, and Z is the impedance in ohms.
- At resonance, the power factor is equal to 1, which means that the circuit is purely resistive and there is no reactive power.

##### 4. Measurement of power in 3- phase circuit by two wattmeter method and determination of its power factor for star as well as delta connected load.

- The two wattmeter method is a technique for measuring the power in a three-phase circuit using two wattmeters .
- The two wattmeters are connected to any two of the three supply lines of the three-phase circuit, and the third line is common to both wattmeters .
- The power measured by each wattmeter is the product of the voltage and current in the corresponding line, and the total power is the sum of the two wattmeter readings .
- The power factor of the load can be calculated from the two wattmeter readings using the following formula :

    `cos φ = (P1 + P2) / (√3 VL IL)`

    where φ is the phase angle between the voltage and current, P1 and P2 are the wattmeter readings, VL is the line voltage, and IL is the line current.

- The two wattmeter method can be applied to both star and delta connected loads, but the formula for the power factor is different for each case.
- For a star connected load, the line voltage is equal to the phase voltage, and the line current is equal to the phase current times √3. Therefore, the power factor formula becomes:

    `cos φ = (P1 + P2) / (3 VP IP)`

    where VP and IP are the phase voltage and current, respectively.

- For a delta connected load, the line voltage is equal to the phase voltage times √3, and the line current is equal to the phase current. Therefore, the power factor formula becomes:

    `cos φ = (P1 + P2) / (VP IP)`

- The two wattmeter method is useful for measuring the power and power factor of balanced or unbalanced three-phase loads .
- The two wattmeter method has some advantages and disadvantages over the other methods of power measurement in three-phase circuits .

    - It requires only two wattmeters, which reduces the cost and complexity of the measurement.

##### 6. To observe the B-H loop of a ferromagnetic material in CRO.

- A B-H loop is a graphical representation of the relationship between the magnetic flux density (B) and the magnetic field intensity (H) of a ferromagnetic material.
- A ferromagnetic material is one that can be magnetized by an external magnetic field and retain some magnetization even after the field is removed.
- A CRO (cathode ray oscilloscope) is an electronic device that can display the variation of an electrical signal on a screen.
- To observe the B-H loop of a ferromagnetic material in CRO, the following steps are followed:

  1. A ferromagnetic material, such as an iron rod, is wound with a primary coil and a secondary coil. The primary coil is connected to an AC source and a rheostat, while the secondary coil is connected to the CRO.
  2. The AC source provides a sinusoidal current to the primary coil, which creates a varying magnetic field around the ferromagnetic material. This induces an emf in the secondary coil, which is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux.
  3. The CRO is set to display the voltage across the secondary coil (V) on the vertical axis and the current through the primary coil (I) on the horizontal axis. The CRO is also set to trigger on the zero crossing of the current waveform.
  4. As the current in the primary coil increases from zero to its positive peak, the magnetic field intensity (H) also increases and the magnetic flux density (B) follows a nonlinear curve. This is the initial magnetization curve of the ferromagnetic material.
  5. As the current in the primary coil decreases from its positive peak to zero, the magnetic field intensity (H) also decreases, but the magnetic flux density (B) does not return to zero. This is because the ferromagnetic material has some residual magnetization or remanence. This is the first quadrant of the B-H loop.
  6. As the current in the primary coil changes its polarity and increases from zero to its negative peak, the magnetic field intensity (H) also changes its direction and increases in the opposite direction. The magnetic flux density (B) also changes its direction, but not as much as the magnetic field intensity (H). This is because the ferromagnetic material has some coercivity or resistance to demagnetization. This is the second quadrant of the B-H loop.
  7. As the current in the primary coil decreases from its negative peak to zero, the magnetic field intensity (H) also decreases and the magnetic flux density (B) follows a similar curve as in the first quadrant, but in the opposite direction. This is the third quadrant of the B-H loop.
  8. As the current in the primary coil changes its polarity again and increases from zero to its positive peak, the magnetic field intensity (H) also changes its direction and increases in the original direction. The magnetic flux density (B) also changes its direction, but not as much as the magnetic field intensity (H). This is because the ferromagnetic material has some hysteresis or memory of its previous magnetization. This is the fourth quadrant of the B-H loop.
  9. As the current in the primary coil completes one cycle, the B-H loop is also completed on the CRO screen. The area enclosed by the B-H loop represents the energy loss per cycle due to hysteresis in the ferromagnetic material.

- The B-H loop of a ferromagnetic material in CRO can be used to study the magnetic properties of the material, such as its saturation, remanence, coercivity, and hysteresis. It can also be used to compare different ferromagnetic materials and their suitability for different applications.

    `η = (Pout / Pin) x 100%`

    - Core losses are the losses due to the magnetic properties of the core material. They consist of hysteresis losses and eddy current losses. Hysteresis losses are caused by the repeated magnetization and demagnetization of the core as the alternating current changes direction. Eddy current losses are caused by the induced currents that circulate within the core due to the changing magnetic flux. Core losses are constant and independent of the load.
    - Copper losses are the losses due to the resistance of the windings. They consist of primary copper losses and secondary copper losses. Primary copper losses are the power dissipated in the primary winding due to the primary current. Secondary copper losses are the power dissipated in the secondary winding due to the secondary current. Copper losses are proportional to the square of the load current.

- The efficiency of a single phase transformer can be maximized by minimizing the core losses and the copper losses. This can be achieved by using high quality core material, reducing the thickness of the core laminations, increasing the cross-sectional area of the windings, and reducing the length of the windings.
