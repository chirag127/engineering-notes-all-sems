### Dimensioning

- Dimensioning is the process of adding measurements and other information to a drawing to specify the size, shape, location, and features of an object.
- Dimensioning is essential for communicating the design intent and ensuring the accuracy and functionality of the product.
- Dimensioning follows certain rules and conventions that are widely accepted and standardized by various organizations, such as ANSI, ISO, ASME, etc.
- Dimensioning consists of four elements: dimension lines, extension lines, dimension values, and dimension symbols.

  - Dimension lines are thin, solid lines that indicate the direction and extent of a dimension. They are terminated by arrowheads or tick marks at both ends.
  - Extension lines are thin, solid lines that extend from the outline of the object to the dimension line. They indicate the points or features that are being dimensioned. They should not touch the object or cross the dimension lines.
  - Dimension values are numerical values that indicate the size or distance of a dimension. They are usually placed above or below the dimension line, and aligned with it. They may be accompanied by units, prefixes, suffixes, or tolerances.
  - Dimension symbols are graphical symbols that provide additional information about a dimension, such as the type, method, or reference of measurement. They are usually placed before, after, or above the dimension value.

- Dimensioning should follow the principles of clarity, completeness, consistency, and economy.

  - Clarity means that the dimensions should be easy to read and understand, without ambiguity or confusion. The dimensions should be placed in the most appropriate view, with adequate spacing and orientation. The dimensions should not be repeated, unless necessary for clarity.
  - Completeness means that the dimensions should provide all the information needed to manufacture and inspect the product, without leaving any gaps or assumptions. The dimensions should cover all the features and details of the object, and indicate the tolerances and specifications required.
  - Consistency means that the dimensions should follow the same rules and conventions throughout the drawing, and conform to the standards and practices of the industry or field. The dimensions should use the same units, symbols, formats, and styles, and avoid any contradictions or conflicts.
  - Economy means that the dimensions should be as few and simple as possible, without compromising the clarity and completeness. The dimensions should avoid unnecessary or redundant information, and use the most efficient and effective methods of dimensioning.

### Transformation of Projections

- A projection is a method of representing a three-dimensional (3D) object on a two-dimensional (2D) plane, such as a sheet of paper or a computer screen.
- A projection is obtained by drawing lines of sight from an observer's eye to the points on the object and marking the points where these lines intersect the projection plane.
- A projection can be classified into two types: parallel and perspective, depending on the distance and orientation of the observer from the object.
- A parallel projection is a projection where the lines of sight are assumed to be parallel, and the projection plane is perpendicular to a principal axis of the object. Parallel projections are commonly used in engineering and technical drawings, as they preserve the true shape and size of the object.
- A perspective projection is a projection where the lines of sight converge at a single point, called the center of projection or the vanishing point. Perspective projections are more realistic and natural, as they mimic how the human eye perceives the 3D world.
- A transformation of projections is a process of changing the orientation or position of the projection plane or the object, resulting in a different view of the object. Transformation of projections can be used to show different aspects of the object, such as the front, top, side, or isometric views.
- A transformation of projections can be performed by applying a matrix multiplication to the coordinates of the object, using a specific projection matrix that depends on the type and parameters of the projection.
- A projection matrix is a 4x4 matrix that maps the homogeneous coordinates of a 3D point to the homogeneous coordinates of a 2D point on the projection plane.
- A projection matrix can be derived by applying the principles of similar triangles, using the ratios of the corresponding sides of the triangles formed by the lines of sight and the projection plane.
- A projection matrix can be decomposed into three components: a scaling matrix, a shear matrix, and a perspective matrix, each representing a different aspect of the projection.
- A scaling matrix is a matrix that scales the coordinates of the object by a factor, depending on the distance of the object from the projection plane.
- A shear matrix is a matrix that shifts the coordinates of the object by an amount, depending on the angle of the projection plane with respect to the principal axes of the object.
- A perspective matrix is a matrix that divides the coordinates of the object by a factor, depending on the distance of the object from the center of projection.
- A transformation of projections can be classified into four types: orthographic, oblique, axonometric, and perspective, depending on the type and parameters of the projection.
- An orthographic projection is a parallel projection where the projection plane is perpendicular to one of the principal axes of the object, and the lines of sight are parallel to the other two axes. Orthographic projections are simple and easy to construct, as they do not involve any scaling, shearing, or perspective effects.
- An oblique projection is a parallel projection where the projection plane is perpendicular to one of the principal axes of the object, but the lines of sight are not parallel to the other two axes. Oblique projections are more expressive and dynamic than orthographic projections, as they show more sides of the object, but they also distort the shape and size of the object.
- An axonometric projection is a parallel projection where the projection plane is not perpendicular to any of the principal axes of the object, and the lines of sight are equally inclined to all three axes. Axonometric projections are more realistic and balanced than oblique projections, as they show the object in a 3D perspective, but they also preserve the proportions and angles of the object.
- A perspective projection is a projection where the lines of sight converge at a single point, called the center of projection or the vanishing point. Perspective projections are the most natural and realistic way of representing a 3D object, as they mimic how the human eye perceives the 3D world, but they also introduce foreshortening and distortion effects.

### Geometry and topology of engineered components

- Geometry is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties, measurements, and relationships of points, lines, angles, surfaces, and solids in space.
- Topology is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties of shapes that are preserved under continuous deformations, such as stretching, twisting, bending, and shrinking, but not tearing or gluing.
- Geometry and topology are closely related, as many concepts and results in geometry can be generalized and abstracted to topological spaces, such as continuity, convergence, compactness, connectedness, and dimension.
- Geometry and topology are also important tools for studying and designing engineered components, such as mechanical parts, electrical circuits, optical devices, and nanomaterials, as they can capture the essential features and constraints of these components, such as shape, size, symmetry, rigidity, flexibility, and functionality.
- Some examples of geometry and topology applications in engineering are:

  - Computer-aided design (CAD), which uses geometric modeling and simulation to create, modify, analyze, and optimize the design of engineered components, such as curves, surfaces, solids, and assemblies, using various representations, such as parametric, implicit, spline, and subdivision.
  - Computer-aided manufacturing (CAM), which uses geometric and topological information to plan, control, and monitor the fabrication of engineered components, such as machining, 3D printing, and laser cutting, using various techniques, such as tool path generation, geometric tolerancing, and quality inspection.
  - Computer graphics, which uses geometry and topology to generate, manipulate, and render realistic and stylized images and animations of engineered components, such as lighting, shading, texturing, mapping, and ray tracing, using various models, such as polygonal, volumetric, and point-based.
  - Geometric and topological data analysis (GDA and TDA), which use geometry and topology to extract, visualize, and interpret meaningful features and patterns from complex and high-dimensional data sets, such as point clouds, graphs, networks, and images, using various methods, such as persistent homology, mapper, and shape descriptors.



### Creation of engineering models and their presentation in standard 2D blueprint form

- Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing specify the shape, form, profile, orientation, and position of features using geometric symbols, datums, and modifiers, and the allowable variations using tolerance zones.
- 2D blueprints consist of multiple views, such as front, top, side, isometric, section, detail, or auxiliary views, that show different aspects of the engineering model.
- 2D blueprints also include title blocks, borders, scales, notes, legends, and other information that provide additional details about the engineering model.
