- A header diagram is a graphical representation of the structure and layout of a web page or a document.
- A header diagram shows the hierarchy of the elements in the page or document, such as headings, subheadings, paragraphs, images, links, etc.
- A header diagram can help you to organize your content, improve readability, and enhance accessibility.
- A header diagram can also help you to identify the main topic, the subtopics, and the supporting details of your page or document.

## How to create a header diagram

- To create a header diagram, you need to use a tool that can generate a graphical outline of your page or document, such as Microsoft Word, Google Docs, or an online tool like [this one](https://www.writemaps.com/).
- You need to identify the main heading of your page or document, which is usually the title or the name of the topic. This is the highest level of your header diagram, and it should be at the top of the page.
- You need to identify the subheadings of your page or document, which are the main sections or categories of your topic. These are the second level of your header diagram, and they should be below the main heading, indented to the right.
- You need to identify the paragraphs, images, links, or other elements that belong to each subheading. These are the third level of your header diagram, and they should be below the subheadings, indented further to the right.
- You can repeat this process for any lower levels of your header diagram, such as sub-subheadings, sub-paragraphs, etc., as long as they are relevant and necessary for your page or document.

### Perspective Projection

- Perspective projection is a type of projection that shows a three-dimensional object as it appears to the human eye from a certain viewpoint.
- Perspective projection is used to create realistic and artistic drawings of objects and scenes, but it is not suitable for engineering drawings that require accurate dimensions and shapes.

- Perspective projection can be classified into three types: **one-point perspective**, **two-point perspective**, and **three-point perspective**, depending on the number of vanishing points used.

- Two-point perspective is used when the object is rotated at an angle to the viewer, and two sets of parallel lines (usually the width and the depth) converge at two vanishing points on the horizon line.
- Three-point perspective is used when the object is tilted up or down, and three sets of parallel lines (the width, the depth, and the height) converge at three vanishing points, two on the horizon line and one above or below it.
- To draw a perspective projection of an object, the following steps are followed:

  - Locate the **station point**, which is the position of the viewer's eye, and draw a line perpendicular to the horizon line to represent the **picture plane**, which is the imaginary plane where the object is projected.
  - Determine the number and position of the **vanishing points** on the horizon line according to the type of perspective projection.
  - Draw the **projectors**, which are the lines that connect the corners and edges of the object to the vanishing points or the station point.

- An affine transformation is a transformation that preserves the parallelism and ratio of distances between points, but not the angles or lengths. Examples of affine transformations are translation, rotation, scaling, and shearing.
- A projective transformation is a transformation that preserves the straightness of lines and the incidence of points on lines, but not the parallelism or ratio of distances. Examples of projective transformations are perspective projection and reflection.
- A perspective transformation is a transformation that preserves the incidence of points on lines and the cross-ratio of four collinear points, but not the straightness of lines or the ratio of distances. Examples of perspective transformations are oblique projection and fisheye projection.
