### Principles of Engineering Graphics and their significance for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction to Engineering Drawing and Orthographic Projections in the subject of ENGINEERING GRAPHICS & DESIGN LAB.

1. Principle of Orthogonality: All the projecting lines in an orthographic projection are perpendicular to the plane of projection. This results in views that are undistorted and have true shapes and sizes.
2. Principle of Projection: The projections of an object on more than one planes can be obtained by projecting the object onto those planes. This forms the basis of obtaining multiple views of an object in orthographic projection.
3. Principle of Views: To completely represent an object, a minimum of 3 views, namely front view, top view and side view (or right side view) are necessary to convey all the details and features of the object. Additional views may be required for complex objects.
4. Principle of Sectioning: An object can be imagined to be cut by one or more planes to expose the internal details, especially for complex objects. The shapes exposed as a result of cutting are then projected onto the planes of projection to obtain the sectional views. This helps in understanding the internal details, features and constructions of an object.

The above principles form the basis for representing objects pictorially through orthographic projections. An understanding of these principles is essential to be able to draw orthographic projections of any given object and to interpret the drawings. ### Dimensioning for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction to Engineering Drawing and Orthographic Projections

1.  Dimensions are measurements of linear, angular, or circular features on the drawing. They are used to specify sizes, shapes, locations, and positions of features.
2.  Dimensions are accompanied by dimension lines, dimension arrows, and dimension text.
    - Dimension line: thin continuous line that connects the dimension text to the object being dimensioned.
    - Dimension arrow: arrowhead at each end of the dimension line.
    - Dimension text: numbers and units specifying the measurement.
3.  Dimensions are placed parallel to the object being dimensioned where possible. Dimensions for circular/arc features are placed tangent to the arc.
4.  Accurate dimensioning requires consistent practices including:
    - Clearly indicating what feature each dimension refers to.
    - Placing dimensions and text to be readable from the bottom right.
    - Ensuring all dimensions are visible and not obscured.
    - Keeping dimension text aligned parallel/perpendicular to the dimension line where appropriate.
    - Using consistent styles for lines and text.
5.  Different types of dimensions include:
    - Linear: measurements of straight linear distances between two points/features.
    - Diameter: dimensions of circular features.
    - Radius: radius of circular features.
    - Angular: measurements of angles between two lines/surfaces.

### Orthographic Projection

- Orthographic projection is a method of representing three-dimensional objects in two dimensions. It projects an object onto a drawing surface (paper) by drawing parallel lines from the object to the drawing surface.
- The parallel lines are perpendicular to the drawing surface. This results in a unidirectional view of the object.
- To fully represent a 3D object, multiple orthographic projections are used. Typically, front, top and side views are used.
- Each view shows what the object would look like if it was sliced at those specific orientations. This gives the drafter key information needed to recreate the 3D object.
- Objects are projected independently along the vertical and horizontal planes. This means overlapping views can be shown without distortion.
- Hidden lines are used to show features of an object that are not visible in a particular view. Hidden lines are typically dashed or dotted lines.
- Section views can be used to show internal features. This involves theoretically cutting the object with a cutting plane and displaying what is visible.
- Advantages - Precise, versatile, no distortion or perspective effects. Useful for design and manufacturing.
- Disadvantages - Can be difficult to visualise 3D form from 2D projections. Hidden lines may be unclear.

### Projection of lines inclined to one plane and both planes

1. Lines inclined to one plane:
- When a line is inclined to only one plane, it will intersect that plane in a point and make an acute angle with that plane.
- The line will be projected as a shorter line and its true length will not be shown in the view.
- The ray of projection will pass through the end point of the line which is nearer to the plane.

2. Lines inclined to both planes:
- When a line is inclined to both the planes, it will intersect both the planes in two points and will make acute angles with both the planes.
- In this case, both the projections of the line will be foreshortened. The ray of projection will pass through the end point which is nearer to the corresponding plane.
- The true length of the line will not be shown in either of the views.

### Pyramids

1. A pyramid is a polyhedron formed by connecting a polygonal base and a point, called the apex.
2. The slant height of a pyramid is the distance between the apex and the base.
3. The height of a pyramid is the length of a line segment from the apex perpendicular to the base.
4. The lateral surface of a pyramid consists of triangles with a common base. The area of the lateral surface is equal to (1/2)bh, where b is the length of the base and h is the slant height.
5. The volume of a pyramid is (1/3)Ah, where A is the area of the base and h is the height of the pyramid.
6. Types of pyramids:
- Triangular pyramid: Base is a triangle, apex is a point.
- Square pyramid: Base is a square, apex is a point.
- Pentagonal pyramid: Base is a pentagon, apex is a point.

7. To draw the projections of a pyramid:
- Draw the plan and front view of the base.
- Project the apex onto the vertical and horizontal planes.
- Join the projections of the apex to the corresponding corners of the base.
- The lines joining the apex to the base represent the slant heights of the faces of the pyramid.

### Isometric scales for the notes of the Unit 4 - Isometric Projection in the subject of ENGINEERING GRAPHICS & DESIGN LAB.

1. Introduction
- Isometric projection is a way of representing three-dimensional objects in two dimensions. It produces three-dimensional pictorial effect.

2. Isometric scales
- The isometric scales are the scale ratios in which the measurements along the three axes are drawn to represent the object in isometric projection.
- The isometric scales are obtained by dividing the actual measurement of the object along each axis by the corresponding isometric measurement.
- If a, b and c are the actual measurements along X, Y and Z axes respectively and A, B and C are the isometric measurements, then the isometric scales are:

Scale along X-axis (Sx) = A/a
Scale along Y-axis (Sy) = B/b
Scale along Z-axis (Sz) = C/c

3. Conventions and applications
- The isometric scales are generally taken as 1:2:1 or 1:√3:1. 1:2:1 isometric scale is easy to construct but 1:√3:1 provides less distortion.
- Isometric projection is useful to represent the designs of 3D objects such as machines, structures etc. as it gives an 3D effect with all the three dimensions shown in a single view.
- It is a quick way of visualizing the spatial relationships between the parts of an object. The measurements can be directly taken from the isometric view and converted to actual measurements using the isometric scales.

1. CAD refers to Computer-Aided Design. It is the use of computer systems to assist in the creation, modification, analysis, or optimization of a design.
2. CAD software is used to increase the productivity of the designer, improve the quality of design, improve communications through documentation, and to create a database for manufacturing.
3. Some of the common CAD software tools are AutoCAD, SolidWorks, Fusion 360, FreeCAD, etc.
4. The basic steps involved in CAD are:

-   Learn the CAD software and its features
-   Set up the drawing template with units, limits, and grids
-   Draw the 2D sketches of the parts using basic shapes like lines, circles, arcs, etc.
-   Use editing tools like trim, extend, fillet, chamfer, etc. to modify the shapes
-   Apply constraints to create relationships between shapes and define dimensions
-   Convert 2D sketches to 3D models using extrude, revolve or other 3D tools
-   Assemble multiple 3D parts into a complete 3D model of the design
-   Create drawings, sections and render the 3D model
-   Analyze and optimize the design by conducting simulations and finite element analyses

5. The key benefits of CAD are:

-   Increased productivity due to the speed and precision of the software
-   Improved quality of designs through the use of parametric 3D modeling
-   Easier documentation through automatically generated drawings
-   Digital database of the design can be used for manufacturing and analysis
-   Design alternatives can be easily evaluated to arrive at an optimal solution

### Dim style for the notes of the Unit 5 - Introduction to Computer Aided Design in the subject of ENGINEERING GRAPHICS & DESIGN LAB

1.  Dim style refers to the style of dimensions used in the technical drawings. There are various styles of dimensions used as per the requirements and standards.
2.  The most commonly used dim styles are:
    - Aligned dimensions: The dimensions are aligned parallel to the dimension line.
    - Angular dimensions: Used to specify angles between two lines.
    - Chain dimensions: A series of dimensions connected one after the other to specify the overall size of an object.
    - Radius dimensions: Used to specify the radius of arcs and circles.
    - Diameter dimensions: Used to specify the diameter of arcs and circles.
3.  While creating dimensions, the following parameters need to be specified:
    - Arrowhead style: The style of arrowheads to be used (tick, arrow, etc.)
    - Dimension line: The line connecting the dimension text and the object.
    - Extension line: The line extending from the object to which the dimension line is attached.
    - Dimension text: The numeric value of the measurement.
    - Units: The units to be used for the dimensions (mm, cm, m, etc.)
4.  The dim styles can be applied as per the requirement to maintain uniformity and neatness in the technical drawings as per the drafting standards. The dim styles can be modified and custom dim styles can also be created as per the requirements.

### Conversion of Isometric Views to Orthographic Views and Vice-Versa in AutoCAD

1. To convert an Isometric view to an Orthographic view:
- Select the isometric drawing
- Go to Home tab -> View panel -> Select 'Orthographic' from the View Configuration drop-down
- The drawing will be converted to Orthographic view

2. To convert an Orthographic view to an Isometric view:
- Select the orthographic drawing
- Go to Home tab -> View panel -> Select 'Isometric' from the View Configuration drop-down
- The drawing will be converted to Isometric view

3. Some points to note:
- Hidden lines will not be shown in Isometric view
- Circles will appear as ellipses in Isometric view due to 30° viewing angle
- Dimensioning and hatching may get distorted in Isometric view
- It is advisable to create auxiliary views/sections if more details are required

This covers the steps to convert between Isometric and Orthographic views in AutoCAD. This will be useful in visualizing the 3D design in 2D space and understanding the object thoroughly.

### Geometry and topology of engineered components

- Points: Zero-dimensional entity that indicates a location in space.
- Lines: One-dimensional entity defined by two points. Can be straight, curved, etc.
- Planes: Two-dimensional entity defined by three non-collinear points or a line and a point not on the line.
- Angles: Measurement of rotation between two lines, planes or vectors. Can be acute, obtuse, right, etc.
- Intersections: Meeting of two or more entities (lines, planes, etc.) resulting in common points, lines or planes.
- Parallelism: Two or more entities that do not intersect and remain equidistant at all points.
- Perpendicularity: Intersection of two entities at right angles (90 degrees).
- Curves: One-dimensional entities defined by mathematical functions. Can be circular, parabolic, etc.
- Surfaces: Two-dimensional entities defined by curves, lines or functions. Can be planar, cylindrical, spherical, etc.
- Solids: Three-dimensional entities bounded by surfaces. Can be primitives (cube, sphere, etc.) or complex shapes.
- Topology: Study of properties preserved under bending, twisting and stretching transformations. Includes concepts like connectivity, holes, inside/outside, etc.

### Creation of Engineering Models and their Presentation in Standard 2D Blueprint Form

1. Engineering drawings are technical documents that visually communicate the design, specifications, and requirements of a product. They provide a comprehensive set of instructions that detail the requirements for producing a product.
2. Engineering drawings typically consist of technical sketches, orthographic projections, parts lists, notes, and other information necessary to manufacture a product.
3. Orthographic projections are a method of representing three-dimensional objects in two dimensions. They consist of planar projections of the front, top, and side of an object.
4. The standard orthographic views consist of the front view, top view, and side view. Additional views may be included as needed to fully define the object.
5. Views are typically arranged on the drawing in a standard configuration or "drawing view". The front view is typically at the top, the top view is to the right of the front view, and the side view is below the front view.
6. Each view contains specific details to define the 3D shape of the object, including visible lines, hidden lines, center lines, dimensions, notes, and callouts. These components must be drawn to strict conventions to create an accurate and easy to interpret engineering drawing.
7. Notes and callouts provide additional information not easily conveyed in the views, including materials, finishes, tolerances, and other specifications. They use standardized abbreviations and symbols to communicate this information.
