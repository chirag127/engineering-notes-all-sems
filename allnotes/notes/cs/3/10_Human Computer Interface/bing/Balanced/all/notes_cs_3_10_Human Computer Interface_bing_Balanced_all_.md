# Importance of 8 good design principles for human computer interface

Human computer interface (HCI) is the field of study that focuses on the interaction between humans and computers. HCI aims to design interfaces that are easy to use, efficient, and satisfying for the users. One of the most influential sources of HCI design principles is Shneiderman's eight golden rules of interface design  , which are:

- **Strive for consistency** by utilizing familiar icons, colors, menu hierarchy, call-to-actions, and user flows when designing similar situations and sequence of actions. Consistency reduces the cognitive load and confusion for the users, and helps them learn and remember the interface faster.
- **Enable frequent users to use shortcuts** such as keyboard commands, abbreviations, function keys, hidden commands, etc. Shortcuts allow users to perform tasks more quickly and efficiently, and enhance their sense of control and mastery over the interface.
- **Offer informative feedback** for every user action, such as visual, auditory, or tactile cues, progress indicators, confirmation messages, error messages, etc. Feedback informs the users about the state and outcome of their actions, and reassures them that the system is responsive and reliable.
- **Design dialogs to yield closure** by organizing the interface into meaningful and coherent groups of actions, such as steps, stages, phases, etc. Dialogs should have a clear beginning, middle, and end, and provide feedback when a group of actions is completed. Closure helps the users understand the structure and logic of the interface, and gives them a sense of accomplishment and satisfaction.
- **Prevent errors** by designing the interface to avoid or minimize the possibility of user mistakes, such as incorrect inputs, invalid commands, unintended actions, etc. Errors can be prevented by using constraints, warnings, confirmations, undo functions, etc. Preventing errors reduces the frustration and dissatisfaction of the users, and improves the usability and reliability of the interface.
- **Permit easy reversal of actions** by allowing the users to undo or redo their actions, such as using the back button, the cancel button, the undo command, the history function, etc. Reversal of actions enables the users to recover from errors, explore different options, and change their minds without penalty. Reversal of actions increases the confidence and comfort of the users, and encourages them to experiment and learn from the interface.
- **Support internal locus of control** by designing the interface to give the users the initiative and the freedom to perform their tasks, such as using direct manipulation, providing multiple ways to accomplish a goal, allowing customization, etc. Internal locus of control empowers the users to feel that they are in charge of the interface, and not the other way around. Internal locus of control enhances the motivation and engagement of the users, and fosters a positive attitude towards the interface.
- **Reduce short-term memory load** by designing the interface to minimize the amount of information that the users have to remember and process, such as using simple and natural language, providing visual cues, chunking information, etc. Reducing short-term memory load eases the mental effort and strain for the users, and improves their performance and satisfaction.

These eight principles are not exhaustive or absolute, but they provide a useful and practical framework for designing effective and user-friendly interfaces. By following these principles, HCI designers can create interfaces that meet the needs and expectations of the users, and enhance their experience and interaction with the computers.

# A brief history of screen design

- Screen design is the process of creating visual interfaces for digital devices, such as computers, smartphones, and tablets.
- Screen design has evolved over time, influenced by technological developments, user needs, and aesthetic trends.
- Some of the major milestones in the history of screen design are:

  - The first screens were cathode ray tube (CRT) displays, which were attached to computers in the 1950s and 1960s. They could only display monochrome text and simple graphics, and had low resolution and contrast.
  - In the 1970s, screens became more interactive, allowing users to enter commands and data using keyboards and light pens. However, the screens were still cluttered with many fields, menus, and buttons, and lacked visual hierarchy and consistency.
  - In the 1980s, graphical user interfaces (GUIs) emerged, which used icons, windows, menus, and pointers to make screens more intuitive and user-friendly. The first GUI was developed by Xerox PARC, and later popularized by Apple Macintosh and Microsoft Windows.
  - In the 1990s, screens became more colorful, multimedia-rich, and dynamic, thanks to the advancement of display technologies, such as liquid crystal display (LCD), and the emergence of the World Wide Web, which enabled the creation and sharing of web pages.
  - In the 2000s, screens became more diverse, as different types of devices, such as mobile phones, PDAs, and game consoles, required different screen sizes, resolutions, and orientations. Screen design also became more responsive, adaptive, and personalized, to cater to the varying needs and preferences of users.
  - In the 2010s, screens became more immersive, interactive, and intelligent, as new technologies, such as touchscreens, gesture recognition, voice control, augmented reality, and artificial intelligence, enabled new ways of interacting with screens and the digital world.
  - In the 2020s, screens are expected to become more ubiquitous, seamless, and ambient, as they integrate with the physical environment and the human body, and provide context-aware and anticipatory services and experiences.



# The graphical user interface for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction in the subject of Human Computer Interface

- A graphical user interface (GUI) is a type of user interface that allows users to interact with a computer system through graphical elements such as icons, menus, windows, buttons, etc.
- A GUI is designed to make the user experience more intuitive, efficient, and satisfying, by reducing the need for memorizing commands or typing text.
- A GUI is composed of two main components: the input devices and the output devices.
  - Input devices are the hardware or software components that allow the user to provide information or commands to the system, such as a mouse, a keyboard, a touchscreen, a microphone, etc.
  - Output devices are the hardware or software components that display the information or feedback from the system to the user, such as a monitor, a speaker, a printer, etc.
- A GUI is also composed of several graphical elements that are arranged in a logical and consistent way, following some design principles and guidelines. Some of the common graphical elements are:
  - Icons: small pictorial representations of objects, actions, or concepts, that can be clicked or tapped to perform a function or access a resource.
  - Menus: lists of options or commands that can be selected by the user, either by clicking, tapping, or hovering over them.
  - Windows: rectangular areas that display information or content, and can be moved, resized, minimized, maximized, or closed by the user.
  - Buttons: graphical elements that can be clicked or tapped to trigger an action or a command, such as submitting a form, playing a video, etc.
  - Text boxes: graphical elements that allow the user to enter or edit text, such as a search box, a username field, a password field, etc.
  - Check boxes: graphical elements that allow the user to select or deselect one or more options from a list, by clicking or tapping on them.
  - Radio buttons: graphical elements that allow the user to select one option from a list, by clicking or tapping on them.
  - Sliders: graphical elements that allow the user to adjust a value or a parameter, by dragging or tapping on a handle along a scale.
  - Drop-down lists: graphical elements that allow the user to choose one option from a list, by clicking or tapping on a button that reveals the list.
  - Toolbars: rows or columns of icons or buttons that provide quick access to frequently used functions or commands, such as formatting, copying, pasting, etc.
  - Status bars: horizontal or vertical areas that display information or feedback about the current state or progress of the system or the application, such as the time, the battery level, the network connection, etc.
- A GUI is influenced by several factors, such as the user's needs, preferences, and expectations, the system's capabilities and limitations, the application's goals and functions, the context of use, the design standards and conventions, etc.
- A GUI is evaluated by several criteria, such as the usability, the aesthetics, the accessibility, the functionality, the consistency, the learnability, the efficiency, the satisfaction, etc.

# The Concept of Direct Manipulation

- Direct manipulation is an approach to interfaces which involves continuous representation of objects of interest together with rapid, reversible, and incremental actions and feedback.
- Direct manipulation is a style of human-computer interaction (HCI) design which features a natural representation of task objects and actions promoting the notion of people performing a task themselves (directly) not through an intermediary like a computer.
- Direct manipulation is one of the central concepts of graphical user interfaces (GUIs) and is sometimes equated with "what you see is what you get" (WYSIWYG).
- Some examples of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - Dragging and dropping files or icons on a desktop
  - Resizing windows or images by dragging their corners or edges
  - Rotating or zooming a map or a photo by pinching or spreading fingers on a touchscreen
  - Drawing or writing on a canvas or a document with a stylus or a finger
- Some benefits of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - They reduce the cognitive load and the need for memorization by providing visual cues and feedback
  - They increase the sense of control and engagement by allowing users to directly manipulate the objects of interest
  - They enhance the learnability and usability by providing intuitive and consistent interactions
  - They support exploratory and creative tasks by allowing users to experiment and undo their actions
- Some challenges of direct manipulation interfaces are:
  - They may require more screen space and graphical resources than other interfaces
  - They may not support complex or abstract tasks that require more than simple actions or feedback
  - They may not be accessible or suitable for users with different abilities or preferences
  - They may not be compatible or consistent with other interfaces or platforms

# Principles of user interface for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introduction in the subject of Human Computer Interface

User interface (UI) is the part of a software system that allows users to interact with it and provides feedback on their actions. UI design is the process of creating user interfaces that are effective, efficient, and satisfying for the users. UI design is based on some principles that guide the design decisions and help achieve the desired goals. Some of the common principles of UI design are:

- **Clarity**: UI should be clear and understandable, using simple and consistent language, icons, and labels. UI should avoid ambiguity, confusion, and unnecessary complexity. UI should communicate the purpose, functionality, and state of the system to the users.
- **Consistency**: UI should be consistent across the system, using the same design elements, patterns, and rules. UI should follow the established conventions and standards of the platform, domain, and genre. UI should maintain the user's expectations and mental model of the system. 
- **Accessibility**: UI should be accessible and inclusive, catering to the diverse needs and preferences of the users. UI should support different modes of interaction, such as keyboard, mouse, touch, voice, etc. UI should provide alternative ways of presenting and receiving information, such as text, audio, visual, etc. UI should adhere to the accessibility guidelines and best practices.
- **Feedback**: UI should provide feedback to the users, informing them of the results of their actions, the status of the system, and the progress of the tasks. UI should use appropriate feedback mechanisms, such as text, sound, color, animation, etc. UI should provide feedback in a timely, accurate, and meaningful manner. 
- **Familiarity**: UI should be familiar and intuitive, using common metaphors, symbols, and analogies that the users can relate to. UI should leverage the user's prior knowledge and experience of the real world, other systems, and similar contexts. UI should avoid jargon, technical terms, and unfamiliar concepts.
- **Design Standards**: UI should follow the design standards and guidelines that are relevant and applicable to the system, such as the platform-specific guidelines, the domain-specific regulations, and the genre-specific conventions. UI should also follow the general principles and best practices of UI design, such as the golden rules, the heuristics, and the patterns.  
- **Structure & Hierarchy**: UI should have a clear and logical structure and hierarchy, organizing the information and functionality in a meaningful and useful way. UI should use appropriate layout, grouping, alignment, and spacing to create visual order and harmony. UI should use contrast, size, color, and typography to create visual emphasis and differentiation.  
- **Simplicity**: UI should be simple and minimal, using only the essential elements and information that are necessary and relevant for the users. UI should avoid clutter, noise, and redundancy. UI should reduce the cognitive load and the mental effort required by the users.  
- **Usability**: UI should be usable and user-friendly, enabling the users to achieve their goals and tasks efficiently, effectively, and satisfactorily. UI should support the user's needs, preferences, and abilities. UI should prevent, detect, and recover from errors. UI should provide help and guidance when needed.  
- **Aesthetics**: UI should be aesthetically pleasing and appealing, using appropriate colors, shapes, images, and fonts that suit the mood, tone, and style of the system. UI should create a positive and engaging user experience. UI should balance the form and the function, the beauty and the usability.   

These are some of the basic principles of UI design that can help create user interfaces that are clear, consistent, accessible, feedback-oriented, familiar, standard-compliant, structured, simple, usable, and aesthetic. However, these principles are not absolute or definitive, and they may vary depending on the context, the purpose, and the audience of the system. Therefore, UI design should also involve user research, testing, and evaluation to ensure that the UI meets the user's needs and expectations.



## Unit 2 - Design process

The design process is a systematic and creative approach to solving problems and developing solutions. It involves identifying the needs and requirements of the users, generating and evaluating ideas, prototyping and testing, and implementing and communicating the final outcome.

The design process can be divided into five main stages:

- **Empathize**: This stage involves understanding the problem and the context from the perspective of the users and stakeholders. It requires conducting research, such as interviews, surveys, observations, and data analysis, to gather information about the needs, goals, challenges, and expectations of the users and stakeholders. Empathizing helps to define the problem statement and the scope of the project.

- **Define**: This stage involves synthesizing the information gathered from the empathize stage and defining the core problem and the user needs. It requires creating a clear and concise problem statement that summarizes the challenge and the desired outcome. It also requires defining the criteria and constraints for the solution, such as the budget, timeline, resources, and specifications.

- **Ideate**: This stage involves generating and exploring a wide range of possible solutions to the problem. It requires using various brainstorming techniques, such as mind mapping, sketching, SCAMPER, and 6-3-5 method, to generate as many ideas as possible without judging or filtering them. It also requires selecting and refining the most promising ideas based on the criteria and constraints.

- **Prototype**: This stage involves creating and testing low-fidelity models or representations of the selected ideas. It requires using various prototyping methods, such as paper prototyping, wireframing, mockups, and storyboarding, to create simple and inexpensive prototypes that can demonstrate the key features and functions of the solution. It also requires testing the prototypes with the users and stakeholders to gather feedback and evaluate the usability, feasibility, and desirability of the solution.

- **Test**: This stage involves creating and testing high-fidelity prototypes or final products of the selected solution. It requires using various testing methods, such as usability testing, user acceptance testing, beta testing, and A/B testing, to test the solution with the users and stakeholders in real or simulated environments. It also requires collecting and analyzing the data and feedback from the testing and making improvements and refinements to the solution based on the results.

The design process is not linear or fixed, but iterative and flexible. It allows for going back and forth between the stages, revisiting and revising the problem statement, the ideas, the prototypes, and the tests, as new information and insights emerge. The design process is also collaborative and multidisciplinary, involving the participation and contribution of various stakeholders, such as the users, the clients, the designers, the developers, the testers, and the experts. The design process aims to create solutions that are user-centered, innovative, and effective.

# Importance of 8 Human Characteristics for Human Computer Interface

Human characteristics are the traits and abilities that influence how humans perceive, learn, remember, and perform tasks. Understanding human characteristics is essential for HCI because it helps designers and developers to create systems that match the needs, preferences, and expectations of the users.

The following are eight human characteristics that are important for HCI:

- **Perception**: Perception is the process of acquiring, interpreting, and organizing sensory information. Perception affects how users perceive the visual, auditory, tactile, and other aspects of a system. For example, perception influences how users notice and interpret the colors, shapes, sounds, and feedback of a system. HCI should consider the perception of the users and design systems that are clear, consistent, and compatible with their expectations.
- **Attention**: Attention is the ability to focus on specific stimuli and ignore irrelevant ones. Attention affects how users allocate their cognitive resources and prioritize their tasks. For example, attention influences how users scan, search, and filter the information presented by a system. HCI should consider the attention of the users and design systems that are simple, concise, and relevant to their goals.
- **Memory**: Memory is the ability to store and retrieve information over time. Memory affects how users learn, recall, and apply the knowledge and skills required to use a system. For example, memory influences how users remember the commands, functions, and features of a system. HCI should consider the memory of the users and design systems that are easy to learn, recall, and use.
- **Learning**: Learning is the process of acquiring and modifying knowledge and skills through experience, feedback, and instruction. Learning affects how users adapt to new or changing systems and improve their performance over time. For example, learning influences how users discover, explore, and master the capabilities of a system. HCI should consider the learning of the users and design systems that are discoverable, exploratory, and supportive of their learning needs.
- **Problem-solving**: Problem-solving is the ability to identify, analyze, and solve problems using logical and creative thinking. Problem-solving affects how users approach and overcome the challenges and difficulties they encounter when using a system. For example, problem-solving influences how users troubleshoot, debug, and recover from errors and failures of a system. HCI should consider the problem-solving of the users and design systems that are reliable, robust, and resilient to errors and failures.
- **Emotion**: Emotion is the affective state that arises from the appraisal of a situation or an event. Emotion affects how users feel and react to a system and its outcomes. For example, emotion influences how users experience satisfaction, frustration, joy, or anger when using a system. HCI should consider the emotion of the users and design systems that are pleasurable, engaging, and respectful of their feelings.
- **Motivation**: Motivation is the drive that initiates, directs, and sustains behavior toward a goal. Motivation affects how users choose and persist in using a system and its features. For example, motivation influences how users are attracted, interested, and committed to a system. HCI should consider the motivation of the users and design systems that are appealing, meaningful, and rewarding to them.
- **Communication**: Communication is the exchange of information, ideas, and emotions between two or more parties. # Information Retrieval on Web

- Information retrieval (IR) is the process of finding and accessing relevant information from a large collection of documents, such as the web.
- IR involves matching user queries, which are formal statements of information needs, to the best information sources that satisfy those needs.
- IR models are ways of integrating various sources of evidence about documents, such as the links, the structure, the content, the quality, etc., to rank them according to their relevance to the query.
- IR models can be classified into three main categories: Boolean, vector space, and probabilistic.
  - Boolean models use logical operators (AND, OR, NOT) to combine terms in a query and return documents that satisfy the exact Boolean expression.
  - Vector space models represent documents and queries as vectors of term weights in a high-dimensional space and measure their similarity using cosine or other metrics.
  - Probabilistic models estimate the probability of relevance of a document given a query based on some assumptions and statistical methods.
- IR on the web poses some unique challenges and opportunities, such as:
  - The web is a dynamic, heterogeneous, and unstructured collection of documents that requires efficient and scalable indexing and retrieval techniques.
  - The web contains rich information sources, such as images, videos, social media, etc., that require multimodal and personalized IR approaches.
  - The web enables users to interact with the information in various ways, such as searching, browsing, clicking, etc., that provide feedback and signals for IR systems to learn and improve.
  - The web allows users to express their information needs in different forms, such as keywords, natural language questions, voice commands, etc., that require advanced natural language processing and understanding techniques.

# Interaction Devices

Interaction devices are the hardware components that allow users to communicate with computers and other digital systems. They enable users to input data, commands, and feedback, as well as to receive output from the system. Interaction devices can be classified into different categories based on their functions, characteristics, and modalities.

Some of the common categories of interaction devices are:

- **Keyboard and mouse**: These are the most widely used interaction devices for desktop and laptop computers. They allow users to enter text, numbers, symbols, and commands using physical keys and buttons, as well as to move a cursor and select items on the screen using a pointing device. Keyboard and mouse are examples of discrete and direct interaction devices, as they require discrete actions (such as pressing a key or clicking a button) and provide direct feedback (such as a character appearing on the screen or a menu opening).

- **Touchscreen**: This is a type of interaction device that allows users to interact with a system by touching the surface of the screen with their fingers or a stylus. Touchscreens can be found on smartphones, tablets, kiosks, and other devices. They enable users to perform gestures, such as tapping, swiping, pinching, and zooming, to manipulate the interface and the content. Touchscreens are examples of continuous and indirect interaction devices, as they allow continuous input (such as dragging a finger across the screen) and provide indirect feedback (such as a visual change on the screen or a vibration).

- **Voice**: This is a type of interaction device that allows users to interact with a system by speaking natural language commands or queries. Voice interaction can be used to control smart devices, such as speakers, lights, and thermostats, as well as to access information and services, such as web search, navigation, and booking. Voice interaction is an example of a natural and multimodal interaction device, as it uses natural language as the input and output modality, and can be combined with other modalities, such as touch or gesture.

- **Gesture**: This is a type of interaction device that allows users to interact with a system by performing body movements, such as hand gestures, facial expressions, and eye movements. Gesture interaction can be used to control games, virtual reality, and augmented reality applications, as well as to communicate with other users or agents. Gesture interaction is an example of a natural and multimodal interaction device, as it uses body movements as the input and output modality, and can be combined with other modalities, such as voice or touch.

- **Biometric**: This is a type of interaction device that allows users to interact with a system by using their physiological or behavioral characteristics, such as fingerprints, face, iris, voice, or gait. Biometric interaction can be used to authenticate users, personalize services, and monitor health and wellness. Biometric interaction is an example of a passive and implicit interaction device, as it does not require active input from the user and can infer the user's identity, preferences, and state.

# Pointing Devices

- A pointing device is a human interface device that allows a user to input spatial (i.e., continuous and multi-dimensional) data to a computer.
- Pointing devices are used to control graphical user interfaces (GUIs) and computer-aided design (CAD) systems.
- There are several types of pointing devices used in human-computer interaction, including:
  - Computer mouse: A hand-held device that moves a cursor on the screen by detecting its motion on a surface.
  - Trackball: A stationary device that has a ball that can be rotated by the user to move the cursor.
  - Touchpad: A flat surface that senses the position and movement of the user's finger and translates it to cursor movement.
  - Stylus: A pen-like device that can be used to draw or write on a touch-sensitive screen.
  - Joystick: A lever that can be tilted in different directions to control the movement of an object on the screen.
  - Graphic tablet: A flat surface that can detect the position and pressure of a stylus or a finger and translate it to cursor movement or drawing.
- Pointing devices inform functionality by providing feedback to the user about the state and actions of the interface.
- Pointing devices can have different properties, such as:
  - Resolution: The smallest unit of movement that can be detected by the device.
  - Accuracy: The degree to which the device matches the user's intended movement.
  - Speed: The rate at which the device moves the cursor on the screen.
  - Responsiveness: The delay between the user's movement and the device's reaction.
  - Ergonomics: The comfort and ease of use of the device for the user.
- Pointing devices can have different advantages and disadvantages, depending on the context and the user's preferences.
  - For example, a mouse is easy to use and precise, but it requires a flat surface and a cable or a battery.
  - A touchpad is convenient and portable, but it can be less accurate and sensitive than a mouse.
  - A stylus is good for drawing and writing, but it can be lost or damaged easily.
  - A joystick is good for gaming and simulation, but it can be tiring and cumbersome to use.
  - A graphic tablet is good for artistic and creative work, but it can be expensive and require additional software.
- Pointing devices can have different modes of operation, such as:
  - Absolute mode: The device maps the position of the cursor to a fixed point on the screen, regardless of the device's location.
  - Relative mode: The device moves the cursor relative to its previous position, depending on the device's motion.
  - Hybrid mode: The device combines absolute and relative modes, depending on the user's input or the application's context.
- Pointing devices can have different types of buttons, wheels, or gestures that can perform additional functions, such as:
  - Selecting: The user can click or tap on an object to select it or activate it.
  - Dragging: The user can hold down a button or a finger and move the device to drag an object on the screen.

  - Malfunction: The device can stop working or behave erratically due to hardware or software errors.

# Speech Recognition, Digitization, and Generation

- Speech recognition is the process of converting spoken audio into text or commands that can be understood by a computer system .
- Speech digitization is the process of converting speech signals into digital data that can be stored, transmitted, or manipulated by a computer system.
- Speech generation is the process of converting text or commands into synthetic or natural speech that can be output by a computer system .

## Speech Recognition

- Speech recognition can be classified into two types: discrete-word recognition and continuous-speech recognition .
- Discrete-word recognition is the recognition of isolated words that are spoken with pauses between them. It is simpler and more accurate than continuous-speech recognition, but it is less natural and convenient for the user .
- Continuous-speech recognition is the recognition of fluent speech that is spoken without pauses between words. It is more natural and convenient for the user, but it is more complex and prone to errors than discrete-word recognition .
- Speech recognition systems typically consist of the following components:
  - Speech input: the device or method that captures the speech signal from the user, such as a microphone or a telephone.
  - Feature extraction: the process that transforms the speech signal into a sequence of feature vectors that represent the acoustic characteristics of the speech, such as the frequency, energy, and pitch.
  - Decoder: the component that uses acoustic models, pronunciation dictionaries, and language models to search for the most likely word sequence that matches the feature vectors.
  - Word output: the component that outputs the recognized word sequence to the user or the application.

## Speech Digitization

- Speech digitization can be performed by using different methods, such as pulse code modulation (PCM), adaptive differential pulse code modulation (ADPCM), or linear predictive coding (LPC).
- PCM is the simplest and most common method of speech digitization. It involves sampling the speech signal at a fixed rate and quantizing each sample into a fixed number of bits.
- ADPCM is a method of speech digitization that reduces the bit rate by encoding the difference between the current sample and the predicted sample based on the previous samples.
- LPC is a method of speech digitization that reduces the bit rate by encoding the parameters of a linear prediction filter that models the speech signal as a source-filter system.

## Speech Generation

- Speech generation can be classified into two types: speech synthesis and speech concatenation .
- Speech synthesis is the generation of speech from text or commands by using rules or models that describe the pronunciation, intonation, and prosody of the speech .
- Speech concatenation is the generation of speech from text or commands by using pre-recorded speech segments that are concatenated together to form the speech output .
- Speech generation systems typically consist of the following components :
  - Text input: the device or method that receives the text or commands from the user or the application, such as a keyboard or a voice command.
  - Text analysis: the process that analyzes the text or commands and converts them into a phonetic or prosodic representation that can be used for speech generation, such as a phonetic transcription or a pitch contour.
  - Speech output: the device or method that outputs the generated speech to the user or the application, such as a speaker or a headphone.
