### State in Ancient India

- The state in ancient India was a social organization with political power that ensured peace, order and happiness.
- The origin of the state in ancient India is debated, but three main theories are: social contract theory, divine origin theory and organic theory.
- Social contract theory: The state was formed by a voluntary agreement among individuals who gave up some of their rights and freedoms to a ruler or a group of rulers in exchange for protection and welfare.
- Divine origin theory: The state was created by God or gods who appointed a king or a dynasty to rule over the people with divine sanction and authority.
- Organic theory: The state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the growth and development of human society, which evolved from family to clan to tribe to state.
- The types of states in ancient India were mainly monarchy, republic and oligarchy.
- Monarchy: The most common and prevalent type of state, where a king or a queen had absolute or limited power over the territory and the people. The king was the source of law, justice, religion and administration.
- Republic: A rare and alternative type of state, where the people or their representatives had a say in the governance and decision-making of the state. The republics were usually formed by clans, tribes or guilds who elected their leaders or councils.
- Oligarchy: A type of state where a few powerful individuals or groups dominated the state and its affairs. The oligarchs were usually aristocrats, nobles, warriors or merchants who had wealth, influence and military strength.

### Evolutionary Theory

- Evolutionary theory is based on the assumption that societies gradually change from simple beginnings into more complex forms.
- Early sociologists, such as Auguste Comte, believed that human societies evolve in a unilinear way, that is, in one line of development. According to them, social change meant progress toward something better.
- This evolutionary view of social change was influenced by Charles Darwin's theory of organic evolution, which explains how species adapt to their environment through natural selection.
- Some sociologists, such as Herbert Spencer, applied this theory to human society and argued that societies must have evolved from the simple and primitive to the complex and advanced, such as the Western society. This idea is known as social Darwinism, which holds that "the survival of the fittest" explains and justifies differences in wealth and success among societies and people.
- However, social Darwinism has been criticized for being ethnocentric, deterministic, and ignoring the role of human agency and culture in social change.
- Other sociologists, such as Lewis Henry Morgan and Edward Burnett Tylor, proposed a different evolutionary model of social change, which divided human societies into three stages: savagery, barbarism, and civilization. They used criteria such as technology, religion, and family to classify societies into these stages.
- However, this model has also been criticized for being ethnocentric, simplistic, and ignoring the diversity and complexity of human societies.
- Modern sociologists have rejected the idea of unilinear evolution and have adopted a more pluralistic and relativistic approach to social change. They recognize that societies can change in different directions and at different rates, and that social change is influenced by various factors, such as environment, culture, conflict, and innovation  .
- Modern sociologists also use evolutionary theory to explain specific aspects of social change, such as social movements, social stratification, social institutions, and social behavior .

### Force Theory of State Origin

- The force theory of state origin is a theory that explains the emergence of the state as a result of the conquest and domination of one group of people over another by the use of physical force  .
- According to this theory, the state originated due to the need for security, order and protection from external threats, as well as the desire for power, glory and wealth by the conquerors  .
- The theory assumes that the state is a product of violence, coercion and subjugation, and that the state is based on the principle of might is right  .
- The theory also implies that the state is a natural and inevitable phenomenon, and that the state is the highest form of human organization.
- Some examples of the force theory of state origin are the ancient empires of Egypt, Babylon, Assyria, Persia, Greece and Rome, as well as the modern colonial empires of Britain, France, Spain and Portugal  .
- The force theory of state origin has been criticized for being simplistic, unrealistic, ethnocentric and ahistorical. It ignores the role of consent, cooperation, culture, religion, law and morality in the formation and development of the state   .
- The force theory of state origin also fails to account for the diversity and complexity of the state, as well as the variations and changes in the state over time and space   .

### Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

- State formation is the process of emergence and development of a centralized political organization that exercises authority and control over a territory and population.
- State formation in ancient India was influenced by various factors, such as geography, ecology, economy, society, culture, religion, and ideology.
- According to some scholars, state formation in ancient India can be divided into four stages: the tribal stage, the janapada stage, the mahajanapada stage, and the imperial stage.

#### The Tribal Stage

- The tribal stage refers to the period before the emergence of the first states in India, from the early Vedic period (c. 1500-1000 BCE) to the later Vedic period (c. 1000-600 BCE).
- In this stage, the basic unit of political organization was the tribe (jana), which consisted of a group of clans (kula) or families (gotra) that shared a common ancestry, territory, and culture.
- The tribe was led by a chief (raja), who was elected by the tribal assembly (sabha) or council of elders (samiti). The chief was assisted by a priest (purohita), a military commander (senani), and a treasurer (bhandagarika).
- The tribe was also divided into three classes or varnas: the Brahmins (priests and scholars), the Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), and the Vaishyas (merchants and farmers). The fourth class, the Shudras (servants and laborers), was added later.
- The tribe had a loose and flexible political structure, based on kinship, loyalty, and consensus. The tribe was often engaged in wars with other tribes for land, cattle, and resources.
- The tribe also practiced rituals and sacrifices to appease the gods and ancestors, and followed the sacred laws (dharma) and customs (rta) of the Vedic religion.

#### The Janapada Stage

- The janapada stage refers to the period when the first states or territorial kingdoms emerged in India, from the late Vedic period (c. 600-500 BCE) to the early Buddhist period (c. 500-400 BCE).
- In this stage, the unit of political organization was the janapada, which literally means "the land of the people". The janapada was a larger and more stable entity than the tribe, and had a defined territory, population, and administration.
- The janapada was ruled by a king (raja), who was either a hereditary monarch or an elected leader. The king was supported by a council of ministers (mantri), a bureaucracy (amatya), and a standing army (sena).
- The janapada was also divided into four classes or varnas: the Brahmins, the Kshatriyas, the Vaishyas, and the Shudras. The class system became more rigid and hierarchical, and the Brahmins gained more power and prestige as the custodians of the Vedic religion and culture.
- The janapada had a more complex and centralized political structure, based on law, taxation, and coercion. The janapada was often involved in alliances and conflicts with other janapadas for supremacy and expansion.
- The janapada also developed a more diverse and sophisticated culture, with the growth of urbanization, trade, industry, art, literature, and philosophy. The janapada also witnessed the rise of new religious movements, such as Buddhism and Jainism, which challenged the authority and orthodoxy of the Vedic religion.

### The Seven Limbs of the State

- The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) is a theory of statecraft given by Kautilya in his treatise Arthashastra  .
- The theory explains the essential components of a state and their interrelations.
- The seven limbs of the state are:

  - King: The king is the supreme authority and the head of the state. He is responsible for the peace, justice and stability of the state. He is independent and has the final say in all matters .
  - Amatya: The amatya are the bureaucrats or ministers who assist the king in administration and policy-making. They are selected based on their merit, loyalty and competence. They are accountable to the king and the people .
  - Janapada: The janapada is the territory or the land of the state. It includes the natural resources, the population, the culture and the economy of the state. It is the source of revenue and security for the state .

### Society in Ancient India

- The society in ancient India was based on the concept of **varna**, which was a four-fold division of people into four classes: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants and laborers).  This system was derived from the ancient Vedic scriptures and was influenced by the Indo-Aryan culture.
- The society in ancient India was also organized according to the **ashramas**, which were four stages of life that each individual was expected to follow: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (retired), and Sannyasa (renunciant).  These stages were meant to guide the spiritual and moral development of the person and to fulfill their social and religious duties.
- The society in ancient India was marked by the performance of **sanskaras**, which were rites of passage or sacraments that marked the important events in one's life, such as birth, initiation, marriage, death, etc. These ceremonies were conducted by the Brahmins and involved the use of mantras, rituals, and offerings.  They were believed to purify the person and to ensure their well-being in this life and the next.
- The society in ancient India was centered around the **family**, which was the basic unit of social organization. The family was usually patriarchal, meaning that the father or the eldest male was the head of the household and had authority over the other members. The family was also patrilineal, meaning that the inheritance and the lineage were traced through the male line. The family was also extended, meaning that it included not only the parents and the children, but also the grandparents, uncles, aunts, cousins, etc. 
- The society in ancient India was regulated by the institution of **marriage**, which was considered a sacred and lifelong bond between a man and a woman. The marriage was usually arranged by the parents or the elders of the families, based on the compatibility of the horoscopes, the social status, the caste, and the profession of the bride and the groom. The marriage was also accompanied by a dowry, which was a gift of money or property given by the bride's family to the groom's family. The marriage was solemnized by the performance of various rituals, such as the kanyadan (giving away of the bride), the saptapadi (seven steps around the fire), and the mangalsutra (tying of the auspicious thread). 
- The society in ancient India was characterized by the **position of women**, which varied according to the time period, the region, the caste, and the class. In general, women in ancient India enjoyed a high status and respect in the early Vedic period, when they could participate in religious ceremonies, receive education, and choose their own husbands. However, in the later periods, such as the post-Vedic, the Mauryan, the Gupta, and the medieval periods, women's status declined and they faced various restrictions and oppressions, such as the practice of sati (self-immolation of the widow on the husband's pyre), the prohibition of widow remarriage, the imposition of purdah (veil), and the prevalence of child marriage and polygamy.



### Purusārtha

- Artha means "wealth", "prosperity", or "economic values". It is the pursuit of material well-being and security that enables one to fulfill one's dharma and kāma. 
- Kāma means "desire", "pleasure", or "love". It is the enjoyment of sensual and aesthetic experiences that enrich one's life and happiness. 
- Moksha means "liberation", "freedom", or "salvation". It is the ultimate goal of human life, which is to attain release from the cycle of birth and death (samsāra) and union with the supreme reality (Brahman). 
- The purusārthas are the inherent values of the universe and the blueprint for human fulfillment. They offer a way for evaluating one's life and making good decisions. They also help one to create a satisfyingly balanced, meaningful life at the deepest and most holistic level.
- The purusārthas are not mutually exclusive or sequential, but interdependent and complementary. They are to be pursued in harmony and moderation, without neglecting or violating any of them. - The purusārthas are derived from the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, and elaborated by various schools of thought and traditions within Hinduism. They reflect the diversity and complexity of Hindu thought and practice, as well as the universal quest for human happiness and transcendence.

### Evolution of script and languages in India

- India has a rich and diverse linguistic history, with over 1,600 languages and dialects spoken in the subcontinent.
- The earliest evidence of writing in India is the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC) script, which dates back to around 2500 BCE. The IVC script is still undeciphered and its relation to other scripts is unclear .
- The next major script in India is Brahmi, which emerged around the 3rd century BCE and is considered the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts. Brahmi was used to write various languages, such as Prakrit, Sanskrit, Pali, and Tamil  .
- Brahmi evolved into different regional variants over time, such as Kharosthi, Gupta, Nagari, Siddham, and Grantha. These variants further developed into cursive styles that formed the basis of modern Indian scripts, such as Devanagari, Bengali, Gujarati, Gurmukhi, Kannada, Malayalam, Oriya, Tamil, Telugu, and Tibetan  .
- The evolution of Brahmi into so many scripts was influenced by various factors, such as cultural, religious, political, and geographical diversity, as well as the need for faster and easier writing methods.
- India also has scripts that are not derived from Brahmi, such as Perso-Arabic, which was introduced by the Muslim invaders and is used to write languages like Urdu, Kashmiri, and Sindhi. Another example is the Latin script, which was brought by the European colonizers and is used to write languages like Konkani, English, and Hindi .

### Harappan Script

- Harappan script is the name given to the symbols used by the Indus Valley Civilisation, which flourished in the northwest of India and Pakistan from about 2500 to 1900 BCE.
- Harappan script is also known as Indus script, because most of the inscriptions containing these symbols have been found in the Indus river valley.
- Harappan script is not yet deciphered, meaning that the meaning and the language behind the symbols are still unknown  .
- Harappan script is composed of about 400 signs, which vary in shape, size and orientation . Some signs may represent words or concepts, while others may represent sounds or syllables. This type of writing system is called logo-syllabic.
- Harappan script was mainly used for short inscriptions on seals, pottery, copper tablets and other objects  . The longest inscription has only 26 signs. The inscriptions are usually written from right to left, but sometimes also from left to right or boustrophedon (alternating direction) .
- Harappan script reflects the culture and society of the Indus Valley Civilisation, which was highly urbanised, organised and sophisticated  . The script may have been used for trade, administration, religion, communication or education  .
- Harappan script is one of the oldest known scripts of India and the world, and it is a source of mystery and fascination for scholars and enthusiasts   . Many attempts have been made to decipher it, but none have been widely accepted or proven . Some of the proposed languages behind the script are Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, Sumerian, Munda and Elamite  .

# Sangama Literature

Sangama literature is the term used to refer to the ancient Tamil literature that was composed in the region of Tamilakam, which covers the present-day states of Tamil Nadu, Kerala, parts of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Sri Lanka. The word Sangama means a confluence or an assembly, and it denotes the gatherings of Tamil poets and scholars who patronized by various kings and chieftains and produced literary works of high quality and originality.

The Sangama literature is divided into three periods, according to the tradition of the Tamil scholars. The first Sangama lasted for 4,400 years and was presided over by the sage Agastya at Then Madurai, a mythical city that was destroyed by a great flood. The second Sangama lasted for 3,700 years and was presided over by the sage Tolkappiyar at Kapalapuram, another mythical city that was also submerged by the sea. The third Sangama lasted for 1,850 years and was presided over by the poet Nakkirar at Madurai, the capital of the Pandya kingdom. The works of the third Sangama are the only ones that have survived and are available today.

The Sangama literature consists of two main genres: akam and puram. Akam poems deal with the theme of love, both in its various aspects and stages, and in relation to the five landscapes or tinai, which are the mountain, the forest, the pasture, the seashore and the desert. Each landscape has its own flora, fauna, seasons, deities, moods and types of lovers. The akam poems are highly lyrical, rich in imagery and symbolism, and express the emotions and sentiments of the lovers in a refined and subtle manner.

Puram poems deal with the theme of heroism, both in war and in peace, and in relation to the four types of kingship or velir, which are the righteous, the generous, the valiant and the victorious. The puram poems praise the kings and their deeds, their ancestors and their allies, their enemies and their battles, their wealth and their generosity, their virtues and their vices. The puram poems are also historical, political, social and religious, and reflect the culture and values of the ancient Tamils.

The Sangama literature comprises of eight anthologies of akam and puram poems, called Ettuthokai, and ten long poems, called Pattuppattu, which are also divided into akam and puram. The most famous of these are the Kuruntokai, a collection of 400 akam poems, the Purananuru, a collection of 400 puram poems, and the Silappatikaram, an epic poem that narrates the story of a chaste woman named Kannagi and her husband Kovalan, who is falsely accused of stealing a queen's anklet and executed by the king.

The Sangama literature is considered to be one of the oldest and finest examples of Indian literature, and has influenced many later works in Tamil and other languages. The Sangama literature is also a valuable source of information about the history, geography, society, religion, art, science and philosophy of the ancient Tamils. The Sangama literature is a testimony to the creativity, wisdom and culture of the Tamil people.

Indian astronomers adopted the zodiac signs, the seven planets, and the concept of epicycles from the Greeks .
- Indian astronomy flourished during the classical period, from the 5th to the 11th centuries CE, with the contributions of many scholars such as Aryabhata, Varahamihira, Brahmagupta, Bhaskara, Lalla, and Sripati. They developed mathematical methods for calculating planetary positions, eclipses, and the duration of the day and night. They also proposed heliocentric models, elliptical orbits, and the rotation of the earth on its axis .
- Indian astronomy continued to develop during the medieval period, from the 12th to the 18th centuries CE, with the works of Bhaskara II, Madhava, Nilakantha, Jyesthadeva, Munisvara, and others. They refined the methods of trigonometry, algebra, and calculus for astronomical computations. They also made observations of comets, supernovae, and lunar and solar eclipses .
- Indian astronomy was influenced by Islamic astronomy from the 13th century onwards, as a result of the cultural exchange between the Muslim and Hindu kingdoms. Indian astronomers adopted the use of the astrolabe, the sine quadrant, and the Arabic numerals from the Muslims. They also translated Arabic texts into Sanskrit and wrote commentaries on them .

Here are some points to note about the history and features of Indian cinema:

- Indian cinema began in the late 19th century, when the first public screening of a movie by the Lumiere brothers took place in Bombay in 1896. The first Indian filmmaker was Dadasaheb Phalke, who is considered the father of Indian cinema. He made the first full-length feature film, Raja Harishchandra, in 1913. It was a silent film based on a Hindu mythological story.
- The advent of sound in cinema revolutionized the industry and gave birth to the talkies. The first Indian talkie was Alam Ara, directed by Ardeshir Irani, in 1931. It was a musical drama that introduced songs and dialogues to the Indian audience. The talkies also led to the growth of regional cinema, as filmmakers started to make films in various languages, such as Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Marathi, and Kannada, to cater to the diverse linguistic and cultural preferences of the Indian people .
- The post-independence period saw the emergence of a new wave of Indian cinema, influenced by the social and political changes in the country. Filmmakers such as Satyajit Ray, Ritwik Ghatak, Mrinal Sen, Guru Dutt, Bimal Roy, and Raj Kapoor explored realistic and humanistic themes, such as poverty, oppression, corruption, and alienation, in their films. They also experimented with cinematic techniques, such as montage, flashbacks, and symbolism, to create a distinctive aesthetic and narrative style .
- The 1950s and 1960s also witnessed the rise of Bollywood, the Hindi film industry based in Bombay (now Mumbai). Bollywood is the largest and most popular sector of Indian cinema, producing films that appeal to a mass audience with a mix of genres, such as romance, comedy, action, drama, and musical. Bollywood films are known for their lavish sets, costumes, songs, dances, and star power. Some of the iconic actors and actresses of Bollywood are Dilip Kumar, Dev Anand, Rajesh Khanna, Amitabh Bachchan, Nargis, Madhubala, Waheeda Rehman, and Hema Malini .
- Indian cinema has also evolved with the changing times and tastes of the audience. The 1970s and 1980s saw the emergence of parallel cinema, which was a movement of independent and alternative filmmakers who challenged the mainstream conventions and addressed social issues, such as gender, caste, and class, in their films. Some of the notable directors of parallel cinema are Shyam Benegal, Govind Nihalani, Adoor Gopalakrishnan, and Mani Kaul. The 1990s and 2000s saw the globalization and diversification of Indian cinema, as filmmakers explored new genres, such as thriller, horror, sci-fi, and animation, and reached out to international markets and audiences. Some of the successful films of this period are Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge, Lagaan, 3 Idiots, Dangal, and Baahubali .
- Indian cinema is a global enterprise and its films have wide viewership and fanbase throughout South Asia and have spread internationally. Modern film releases are dubbed into many languages, forming a Pan-India films movement. Indians overseas account for 12% of revenue for the industry. Indian cinema has also influenced and been influenced by other film industries, such as Hollywood, Chinese cinema, and Iranian cinema. Indian cinema has also received recognition and awards at various international film festivals, such as Cannes, Berlin, Venice, and Oscar .
