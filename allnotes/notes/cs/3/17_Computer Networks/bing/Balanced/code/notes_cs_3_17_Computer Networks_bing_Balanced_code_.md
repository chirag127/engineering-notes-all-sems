### What is artificial intelligence (AI)?

- Artificial intelligence (AI) is the study and design of intelligent agents that can perceive, learn, reason, and act in complex environments.
- AI can also be defined as the science and engineering of making machines that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as vision, language, decision making, and creativity.
- AI is a broad and interdisciplinary field that draws from computer science, mathematics, logic, psychology, neuroscience, linguistics, philosophy, and other disciplines.
- AI can be classified into different types, such as weak AI, strong AI, narrow AI, general AI, and super AI, depending on the level and scope of intelligence exhibited by the system.

### What are the main goals and applications of AI?

- The main goals of AI are to understand, model, and replicate human intelligence and behavior, and to create systems that can enhance human capabilities and improve human lives.
- The main applications of AI are diverse and span across various domains, such as health care, education, entertainment, business, security, transportation, and social media.
- Some examples of AI applications are:

  - Speech recognition and natural language processing: enabling machines to understand and generate natural language, such as Siri, Alexa, and Google Assistant.
  - Computer vision and image processing: enabling machines to perceive and analyze visual information, such as face recognition, object detection, and self-driving cars.
  - Machine learning and data mining: enabling machines to learn from data and discover patterns, such as recommendation systems, spam filtering, and fraud detection.
  - Knowledge representation and reasoning: enabling machines to store and manipulate knowledge and perform logical inference, such as expert systems, ontologies, and semantic web.
  - Planning and decision making: enabling machines to generate and execute plans and actions, such as chess, robotics, and scheduling.
  - Artificial neural networks and deep learning: enabling machines to learn complex and abstract features and functions, such as image generation, natural language generation, and reinforcement learning.

### What are the main challenges and limitations of AI?

- The main challenges and limitations of AI are related to the complexity, uncertainty, and diversity of the real world, and the ethical and social issues that arise from the use and impact of AI systems.
- Some examples of AI challenges and limitations are:

  - The knowledge acquisition problem: how to obtain and represent the vast and dynamic knowledge required for intelligent behavior.
  - The common sense problem: how to equip machines with the basic and intuitive knowledge and reasoning that humans possess.
  - The explainability problem: how to make machines transparent and accountable for their decisions and actions, and how to communicate them to humans.
  - The generalization problem: how to make machines adaptable and robust to new and unseen situations and tasks.
  - The evaluation problem: how to measure and compare the performance and intelligence of different AI systems and methods.
  - The ethical problem: how to ensure that AI systems are aligned with human values and principles, and do not cause harm or injustice to humans or other beings.
  - The social problem: how to regulate and govern the development and deployment of AI systems, and how to mitigate their potential negative effects on society, such as unemployment, inequality, and bias.

### What are the main components and methods of AI?

- The main components and methods of AI are the tools and techniques that enable the design and implementation of intelligent systems and agents.
- The main components and methods of AI are:

  - Search: a process of finding a solution or an optimal action from a large and complex space of possibilities, such as breadth-first search, depth-first search, heuristic search, and adversarial search.
  - Logic: a formal system of symbols and rules that can be used to represent and manipulate knowledge and reasoning, such as propositional logic, first-order logic, and fuzzy logic.
  - Probability: a mathematical framework that can be used to model and reason about uncertainty and randomness, such as Bayes' theorem, Bayesian networks, and Markov models.
  - Optimization: a process of finding the best or most efficient solution or action from a set of alternatives, such as linear programming, gradient descent, and genetic algorithms.
  - Learning: a process of acquiring and improving knowledge and skills from data and experience, such as supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning.
  - Neural networks: a computational model that can be used to learn complex and nonlinear functions and features from data, such as perceptrons



### Goals and applications of networks

A network is a system of two or more devices (e.g. computers, printers, scanners, etc.) that are connected by some communication medium (e.g. cables, wireless, etc.) in order to share resources, exchange data, or enable electronic communication  .

Some of the main goals of networks are:

- **Resource sharing**: This means to make all programs, data, and equipment available to anyone on the network regardless of the physical location of the resource and the user. For example, a group of office workers can share a common printer, fax, modem, scanner, etc. Resource sharing also enables cost reduction, as fewer devices need to be purchased and maintained.
- **High reliability**: This means to provide alternative sources of supply or backup in case of failure or damage of any component of the network. For example, if one server goes down, another server can take over its tasks. High reliability also improves performance, as the workload can be distributed among multiple devices.
- **Scalability**: This means to allow the network to grow or shrink according to the changing needs and demands of the users and applications. For example, a network can add or remove devices, increase or decrease bandwidth, or change the network topology without affecting the functionality of the network.
- **Security**: This means to protect the network and its resources from unauthorized access, modification, or destruction. For example, a network can use encryption, authentication, firewalls, or antivirus software to prevent cyberattacks or data breaches.

Some of the main applications of networks are :

- **The internet**: This is the global network of networks that connects billions of devices and enables various online services and activities, such as web browsing, email, online search, social media, online shopping, online gaming, etc.
- **Online search**: This is the process of finding information or resources on the internet using keywords or queries. Online search engines, such as Google or Bing, use algorithms and databases to provide relevant and accurate results to the users.
- **Email**: This is the electronic transmission of messages and attachments between users over the internet. Email protocols, such as SMTP, POP3, or IMAP, define the rules and formats for sending and receiving emails.
- **Audio and video sharing**: This is the online distribution and consumption of multimedia content, such as music, podcasts, movies, TV shows, etc. Audio and video sharing platforms, such as YouTube or Spotify, allow users to upload, stream, download, or share their content with others.
- **Online commerce**: This is the buying and selling of goods and services over the internet. Online commerce websites, such as Amazon or eBay, allow users to browse, compare, order, pay, or review products or services online.
- **Live-streaming**: This is the real-time transmission and reception of audio or video content over the internet. Live-streaming applications, such as Zoom or Twitch, allow users to broadcast or watch live events, such as webinars, concerts, games, etc.
- **Social networks**: This is the online creation and maintenance of personal or professional relationships and communities. Social network platforms, such as Facebook or LinkedIn, allow users to create profiles, post updates, send messages, join groups, or follow others.



### Categories of networks

A computer network is a system of interconnected devices that can communicate and share data, resources, and applications. Computer networks can be classified based on several criteria, such as the transmission medium, the network size, the topology, and organizational intent. Based on a geographical scale, the different types of networks are:

- **Personal area network (PAN)**: A PAN is the smallest and simplest type of network. PANs connect devices within the range of an individual and are no larger than about 10 meters (m). Examples of PANs are wireless headphones, Bluetooth speakers, smartwatches, and smartphones.
- **Local area network (LAN)**: A LAN is a network that connects devices within a limited area, such as a home, an office, or a building. LANs are usually owned and managed by a single organization or individual. LANs can use wired or wireless technologies, such as Ethernet, Wi-Fi, or Bluetooth. LANs enable fast and secure data transfer, resource sharing, and communication among the devices .
- **Wireless local area network (WLAN)**: A WLAN is a type of LAN that uses wireless technologies, such as Wi-Fi, to connect devices. WLANs provide mobility, flexibility, and scalability to the users. WLANs can be standalone or connected to a wired LAN. WLANs are subject to interference, security risks, and performance issues due to the wireless medium.
- **Campus area network (CAN)**: A CAN is a network that connects devices within a campus, such as a university, a school, or a business park. A CAN can span several buildings and can use wired or wireless technologies. A CAN is usually owned and managed by the campus administration. A CAN can provide high-speed data transfer, internet access, and network services to the campus users.
- **Metropolitan area network (MAN)**: A MAN is a network that connects devices within a metropolitan area, such as a city or a town. A MAN can span several kilometers and can use wired or wireless technologies, such as fiber-optic cables, microwave links, or cellular networks. A MAN is usually owned and operated by a public or private entity, such as a local government, a cable TV provider, or a telecom company. A MAN can provide broadband access, voice and video services, and public safety communications to the metropolitan users .
- **Wide area network (WAN)**: A WAN is a network that connects devices across a large geographical area, such as a country or a continent. A WAN can span thousands of kilometers and can use wired or wireless technologies, such as copper wires, satellite links, or cellular networks. A WAN is usually owned and operated by multiple entities, such as internet service providers, telecom companies, or governments. A WAN can provide global access, data transmission, and network services to the wide area users .
- **Virtual private network (VPN)**: A VPN is a network that creates a secure and encrypted connection between devices over a public network, such as the internet. A VPN can use various protocols, such as IPsec, SSL, or PPTP, to establish the connection. A VPN can provide privacy, security, and anonymity to the users. A VPN can also enable remote access, bypass geo-restrictions, and enhance network performance .

### Organization of the Internet

The Internet is the world's largest computer network. It is a global system of interconnected computing devices that can communicate and exchange data using common protocols and standards.

The organization of the Internet can be understood at different levels, such as:

- **Computer and network hardware**: This level deals with the physical devices and components that make up the Internet, such as routers, switches, cables, servers, and end-user devices. These devices are organized in different ways to form networks of various sizes and types, such as local area networks (LANs), wide area networks (WANs), and metropolitan area networks (MANs).
- **Network topology**: This level describes the shape and structure of a network, or how the devices are connected to each other. There are a number of topologies but the most common are bus, ring, star, and mesh. A bus network topology is when every network node is directly connected to a main cable. In a ring topology, nodes are connected in a loop, so each device has exactly two neighbors. In a star network topology, all nodes are connected to a single, central hub and each node is indirectly connected through that hub. In a mesh network topology, nodes are connected to each other in a web-like pattern, so there are multiple paths between any two nodes.
- **Internet access and Internet service providers (ISPs)**: This level deals with how users and devices connect to the Internet and what services they can access. ISPs are companies that provide a service for users to connect to the Internet and, in doing so, are able to control and regulate how people use the Internet. ISPs can offer different types of Internet access, such as dial-up, broadband, wireless, satellite, or fiber-optic.
- **Navigation within the Internet**: This level deals with how users and devices find and access information and resources on the Internet. This involves the use of protocols and standards, such as the Internet Protocol (IP), the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), the Domain Name System (DNS), the Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP), and the Uniform Resource Locator (URL). These protocols and standards enable the identification, addressing, routing, and delivery of data packets across the Internet.

The organization of the Internet is not static or fixed, but rather dynamic and evolving. The Internet is constantly changing and growing, as new devices, networks, protocols, and services are added or modified. The Internet is also influenced by various factors, such as technical, economic, social, political, and legal issues. Therefore, the organization of the Internet is not controlled or governed by a single entity or authority, but rather by a complex and diverse network of organizations and stakeholders, such as:

- **Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE)**: This is an organization that develops and publishes standards for various fields of engineering, including computer networks. IEEE standards cover topics such as Ethernet, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and IEEE 802.11.
- **Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF)**: This is an organization that develops and publishes standards for the Internet, such as IP, TCP, DNS, HTTP, and many others. IETF standards are called Requests for Comments (RFCs) and are developed by working groups of volunteers.
- **Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN)**: This is an organization that manages and coordinates the domain name system, the IP address space, and other identifiers for the Internet. ICANN oversees the allocation and registration of domain names and IP addresses, and ensures the security and stability of the Internet.
- **World Wide Web Consortium (W3C)**: This is an organization that develops and publishes standards for the World Wide Web, such as HTML, CSS, XML, and many others. W3C standards aim to ensure the interoperability and accessibility of web content and applications.
- **Internet Society (ISOC)**: This is an organization that promotes the development and use of the Internet for the benefit of society. ISOC supports the education, research, policy, and advocacy of the Internet, and fosters the collaboration and cooperation of the Internet community.

### Network structure and architecture

- Network structure and architecture refers to the way network devices and services are structured to serve the connectivity needs of client devices .
- Network devices typically include switches, routers, hubs, gateways, firewalls, etc.
- Network services typically include DHCP, DNS, FTP, HTTP, SMTP, etc.
- Network structure and architecture also describes how the network devices are connected and the rules that govern data transfer between them   .
- The medium of data transmission, such as wired or wireless, also constitutes the network structure and architecture .
- There are many ways to approach network architecture design, which depend on the purpose and size of the network  .
- The main types of network architecture are peer-to-peer (P2P) and client/server .
- In a P2P network, each node can act as both a client and a server, and there is no central authority or server.
- In a client/server network, there is a dedicated server that provides services to multiple clients, and the clients depend on the server for resources and functionality.
- Some advantages of P2P networks are lower cost, easy setup, and distributed workload.
- Some disadvantages of P2P networks are lack of security, reliability, and scalability.
- Some advantages of client/server networks are centralized management, security, and performance.
- Some disadvantages of client/server networks are higher cost, complexity, and single point of failure.



# Layering Principles in Computer Networks

Layering is a design principle that divides a complex system into smaller and simpler components, called layers, that can be reused and modified independently. Layering allows for abstraction, modularity, and interoperability of different network technologies and protocols.

Some of the benefits of layering are:

- It reduces the complexity of the system by hiding the details of lower layers from higher layers.
- It enables the reuse of common functions and protocols across different layers and applications.
- It facilitates the development and testing of each layer separately, without affecting the other layers.
- It allows for the evolution and innovation of each layer independently, as long as the interfaces between layers are maintained.
- It enables the interoperability of different network devices and systems that use different technologies and protocols at different layers.

One of the most widely used layering models in computer networks is the ISO/OSI model, which stands for International Organization for Standardization/Open Systems Interconnection. The ISO/OSI model defines seven layers of network functionality, from the physical layer to the application layer. Each layer performs a specific function and communicates with the adjacent layers through well-defined interfaces.

The seven layers of the ISO/OSI model are:

- Physical layer: This layer is responsible for the transmission and reception of raw bits over a physical medium, such as a cable, a wireless channel, or an optical fiber. It defines the characteristics of the physical medium, such as the voltage levels, the modulation schemes, the connectors, and the encoding methods. It also handles the synchronization, multiplexing, and error detection of the bit stream.
- Data link layer: This layer is responsible for the reliable and efficient transfer of data frames between two nodes on the same network segment, such as a LAN or a WAN. It defines the format and structure of the data frames, the addressing scheme, the flow control, the error control, and the medium access control mechanisms. It also handles the framing, delimiting, and acknowledgement of the data frames.
- Network layer: This layer is responsible for the routing and forwarding of data packets between two nodes on different network segments, across one or more intermediate nodes, such as routers or switches. It defines the addressing scheme, the routing protocols, the congestion control, and the quality of service mechanisms. It also handles the segmentation, reassembly, and fragmentation of the data packets.
- Transport layer: This layer is responsible for the end-to-end delivery of data segments between two processes on different nodes, across one or more networks. It defines the connection-oriented or connectionless communication modes, the port numbers, the reliability, the flow control, and the error control mechanisms. It also handles the segmentation, reassembly, and acknowledgement of the data segments.
- Session layer: This layer is responsible for the establishment, management, and termination of sessions between two processes on different nodes. It defines the session identifiers, the synchronization, the dialogue control, and the security mechanisms. It also handles the authentication, authorization, and encryption of the sessions.
- Presentation layer: This layer is responsible for the representation, transformation, and compression of data between two processes on different nodes. It defines the syntax and semantics of the data, the encoding and decoding methods, and the compression and decompression algorithms. It also handles the translation, encryption, and decryption of the data.
- Application layer: This layer is responsible for the provision and support of application-specific services and protocols between two processes on different nodes. It defines the application protocols, the message formats, and the service primitives. It also handles the user interface, the data access, and the network management functions.

### Services for the notes of the Unit 1 - Introductory Concepts in the subject of Computer Networks

- A service is a set of functions or capabilities that a network provides to its users or applications.
- A service can be classified as either connection-oriented or connectionless, depending on whether it requires the establishment of a connection before data transfer or not.
- A service can also be classified as either reliable or unreliable, depending on whether it guarantees the delivery of data or not.
- Some examples of services are:
  - File transfer service: allows users to transfer files from one host to another.
  - Email service: allows users to send and receive messages.
  - Web service: allows users to access web pages and other resources on the internet.
  - Streaming service: allows users to receive continuous media data, such as audio or video.
  - Remote login service: allows users to access a remote host and execute commands.
  - Voice over IP service: allows users to make voice calls over the internet.
- A service is provided by a layer of the network architecture, which is a logical grouping of functions and protocols that operate at a certain level of abstraction.
- A network architecture can be divided into several layers, such as the application layer, the transport layer, the network layer, the data link layer, and the physical layer.
- Each layer provides a service to the layer above it, and uses the service of the layer below it.
- Each layer has a set of protocols that define the rules and formats for communication within the layer and with the adjacent layers.
- A protocol is a set of rules that govern the exchange of messages between two or more entities.
- A message is a unit of data that is transmitted from one entity to another.
- A message can be divided into several parts, such as the header, the payload, and the trailer.
- The header contains information that is needed by the protocol, such as the source and destination addresses, the type of message, and the sequence number.
- The payload contains the actual data that is being transferred, such as the file, the email, or the web page.
- The trailer contains information that is used for error detection and correction, such as the checksum or the cyclic redundancy check.
- A message can also be called a packet, a frame, or a bit, depending on the layer that is handling it.



### Protocols and Standards in Computer Networks

- A **protocol** is a set of rules that defines how devices communicate and exchange data in a network. A protocol specifies the format, syntax, semantics, and synchronization of the data packets that are sent and received by the devices  .
- A **standard** is a document that describes the specifications, characteristics, and requirements of a protocol. A standard ensures that different devices from different vendors can interoperate and comply with the same protocol.
- Protocols and standards are important for computer networks because they enable different devices to communicate with each other, regardless of their internal processes, structure, or design  .
- Some examples of protocols and standards in computer networks are:
  - **Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol (TCP/IP)**: This is the most widely used protocol suite in the internet. It consists of four layers: application, transport, internet, and network interface. TCP/IP defines how data is divided, addressed, routed, transmitted, and reassembled across the network .
  - **Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP)**: This is the protocol that governs the communication between web browsers and web servers. HTTP defines how requests and responses are formatted and exchanged over the internet. HTTP is based on the client-server model, where the client initiates a request and the server responds with the requested resource .
  - **Simple Mail Transfer Protocol (SMTP)**: This is the protocol that enables the sending and receiving of email messages over the internet. SMTP defines how email messages are composed, addressed, transferred, and delivered between mail servers and mail clients .
  - **Domain Name System (DNS)**: This is the protocol that translates domain names (such as www.google.com) into IP addresses (such as 172.217.14.206) that are used by the network devices to locate and access the web resources. DNS operates as a distributed database that maps domain names to IP addresses and provides other information about the domains .
  - **Ethernet**: This is the most common standard for local area networks (LANs). Ethernet defines the physical and data link layers of the network, such as the cables, connectors, frames, and media access control (MAC) addresses. Ethernet enables the devices in the same LAN to communicate with each other using a shared medium .
  - **Wi-Fi**: This is the standard for wireless LANs. Wi-Fi defines the physical and data link layers of the network, such as the radio waves, frequencies, channels, and access points. Wi-Fi enables the devices in the same LAN to communicate with each other without wires .
  - **Bluetooth**: This is the standard for personal area networks (PANs). Bluetooth defines the physical and data link layers of the network, such as the radio waves, frequencies, channels, and devices. Bluetooth enables the devices in the same PAN to communicate with each other over short distances .



### The OSI reference model

The OSI reference model is a conceptual framework that describes how information from a software application in one computer moves through a physical medium to the software application in another computer. It was developed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) in the late 1970s and early 1980s to facilitate interoperability and standardization of network protocols and devices .

The OSI reference model consists of seven layers, each of which performs a specific function in the communication process. The layers are:

- **Layer 1: Physical layer**. This layer defines the physical characteristics of the transmission medium, such as the voltage, frequency, modulation, and encoding of the signals. It also deals with the mechanical and electrical aspects of the connectors, cables, and devices. The physical layer is responsible for transmitting and receiving raw bits of data over the network  .
- **Layer 2: Data link layer**. This layer provides reliable and error-free transmission of data frames between two nodes on the same network segment. It also handles the addressing, framing, and flow control of the data. The data link layer is divided into two sublayers: the logical link control (LLC) sublayer, which provides common services to the upper layers, and the media access control (MAC) sublayer, which controls how the nodes access the shared medium  .
- **Layer 3: Network layer**. This layer provides the logical addressing and routing of data packets across different networks. It also handles the fragmentation, reassembly, and congestion control of the packets. The network layer is responsible for establishing and maintaining the end-to-end connection between the source and destination nodes  .
- **Layer 4: Transport layer**. This layer provides the reliable and error-free delivery of data segments between two end points. It also handles the segmentation, reassembly, and flow control of the data. The transport layer is responsible for ensuring that the data is delivered in the correct order and without duplication or loss. The transport layer can provide either connection-oriented or connectionless services to the upper layers  .
- **Layer 5: Session layer**. This layer provides the management and coordination of the communication session between two applications. It also handles the establishment, maintenance, and termination of the session. The session layer is responsible for synchronizing the data exchange and controlling the dialog between the applications  .
- **Layer 6: Presentation layer**. This layer provides the translation, encryption, compression, and formatting of the data for the application layer. It also handles the syntax and semantics of the data. The presentation layer is responsible for ensuring that the data is compatible and understandable by the applications  .
- **Layer 7: Application layer**. This layer provides the interface and services for the user applications to access the network. It also handles the authentication, authorization, and accounting of the users and applications. The application layer is responsible for providing high-level functions such as email, web browsing, file transfer, and remote access  .

The OSI reference model is a useful tool for understanding and designing network systems and protocols. It also helps to compare and contrast different network technologies and architectures. However, it is important to note that the OSI reference model is not a strict specification or implementation of network communication, but rather a general guideline and reference point .



# TCP/IP protocol suite

- TCP/IP stands for Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol and is a suite of communication protocols used to interconnect network devices on the internet .
- TCP/IP is also used as a communications protocol in a private computer network (an intranet or extranet).
- TCP/IP is commonly known as the Internet protocol suite, which is a framework for organizing the set of communication protocols used in the Internet and similar computer networks according to functional criteria.
- TCP/IP consists of four layers: the application layer, the transport layer, the internet layer, and the link layer.
- The application layer provides the interface for the user applications to communicate with the network, such as web browsers, email clients, FTP clients, etc. Some of the protocols in this layer are HTTP, SMTP, FTP, DNS, etc.
- The transport layer provides the end-to-end data transmission services between the hosts, such as reliability, flow control, congestion control, error detection, etc. Some of the protocols in this layer are TCP, UDP, SCTP, etc.
- The internet layer provides the routing and addressing functions for the packets across the network, such as IP addresses, subnet masks, gateways, etc. Some of the protocols in this layer are IP, ICMP, ARP, etc.
- The link layer provides the physical and logical connection between the network devices, such as Ethernet, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, etc. Some of the protocols in this layer are MAC, LLC, PPP, etc.
- TCP/IP is the most widely used protocol suite in the world and is the basis of the Internet  .



### Network devices and components

Network devices and components are the physical or logical entities that enable communication and data transfer among different devices in a network. They can be classified into two main categories: inter-network devices and intra-network devices.

- Inter-network devices are the ones that are mounted within the device, such as a network interface card (NIC) or an RJ45 connector. They allow the device to connect to the network media, such as a cable or a wireless signal.
- Intra-network devices are the ones that are internal components of the network, such as switches, routers, hubs, repeaters, gateways, bridges, and servers. They perform different functions in the network, such as forwarding, routing, filtering, amplifying, translating, or providing services to the connected devices.

Some of the basic network devices and components are:

- Sender: The device that initiates the communication or data transfer in the network, such as a computer, a smartphone, or a printer.
- Receiver: The device that receives the communication or data transfer in the network, such as a computer, a smartphone, or a printer.
- End Point: The device that is either the source or the destination of the communication or data transfer in the network, such as a sender or a receiver.
- Network Interface Card (NIC): The hardware component that allows a device to connect to the network media and communicate with other devices in the network. It has a unique address called a MAC address that identifies the device in the network.
- Network Media: The physical or wireless medium that carries the communication or data transfer signals in the network, such as a twisted pair cable, a coaxial cable, a fiber optic cable, or a radio wave.
- Connectors: The hardware components that connect the network media to the network devices, such as an RJ45 connector, a BNC connector, or an ST connector.
- Switch: The network device that forwards the communication or data transfer signals to the appropriate destination device based on the MAC address. It creates a separate collision domain for each connected device, which reduces network congestion and improves network performance.
- Router: The network device that forwards the communication or data transfer signals to the appropriate destination network based on the IP address. It creates a separate broadcast domain for each connected network, which reduces network traffic and improves network security.
- Hub: The network device that forwards the communication or data transfer signals to all the connected devices in the network. It creates a single collision domain and a single broadcast domain for all the connected devices, which increases network congestion and reduces network performance.
- Repeater: The network device that amplifies the communication or data transfer signals in the network. It extends the range of the network media and prevents signal degradation due to attenuation or interference.
- Gateway: The network device that translates the communication or data transfer signals between different network protocols, such as TCP/IP, IPX/SPX, or AppleTalk. It enables communication and data transfer among different types of networks, such as LAN, WAN, or MAN.
- Bridge: The network device that filters the communication or data transfer signals based on the MAC address. It divides a large network into smaller segments, which reduces network congestion and improves network performance.
- Server: The network device that provides services to the connected devices in the network, such as file sharing, web hosting, email, or printing. It has a high processing power, memory, and storage capacity to handle multiple requests from the network devices.



## Unit 2 - Physical Layer

The physical layer is the first and lowest layer of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model of computer networking. It is responsible for sending bits from one device to another along the network using electrical, mechanical, or procedural interfaces.

Some of the main functions of the physical layer are:

- It defines the physical characteristics of the transmission medium, such as the type of cable, connectors, voltage levels, etc.

# Network topology design

Network topology design is the process of planning and arranging the physical, virtual, and logical layout of a network to optimize its performance, security, and reliability. Network topology design involves choosing the best type of network topology for a given scenario, as well as mapping the locations of nodes, devices, and connections on a network diagram .

Some of the factors that influence network topology design are:

- The size and scope of the network
- The bandwidth and latency requirements of the network
- The cost and availability of network resources
- The security and redundancy needs of the network
- The scalability and flexibility of the network

## Types of network topology

There are two major categories of network topology: physical and logical.

- Physical topology refers to the actual shape and arrangement of the network components, such as cables, switches, routers, and servers. Physical topology determines how data travels from one node to another, and how the network can be expanded or modified.
- Logical topology refers to the way data is transmitted and processed on the network, regardless of the physical layout. Logical topology defines the protocols, rules, and algorithms that govern the network communication and functionality.

There are several common types of network topology, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Some of the most widely used network topologies are:

- Bus topology: A linear network where all nodes are connected to a single cable or backbone. This topology is simple and easy to install, but it has low bandwidth, high collision rate, and poor fault tolerance.
- Ring topology: A circular network where each node is connected to two other nodes, forming a closed loop. This topology has high bandwidth, low collision rate, and easy data transmission, but it has poor scalability, difficult troubleshooting, and single point of failure.
- Star topology: A network where all nodes are connected to a central hub or switch. This topology has high bandwidth, low collision rate, and easy fault detection, but it has high cost, high dependency on the central device, and limited cable length.
- Tree topology: A hierarchical network where nodes are arranged in a tree-like structure, with a root node at the top and branches at the lower levels. This topology has high scalability, easy management, and clear data flow, but it has high cost, high maintenance, and single point of failure at the root node.
- Mesh topology: A network where each node is connected to every other node, forming a complete or partial mesh. This topology has high bandwidth, high fault tolerance, and high reliability, but it has high cost, high complexity, and high overhead.

## Network topology design process

The network topology design process consists of several steps, such as :

- Analyzing the network requirements and objectives, such as the number of users, the types of applications, the expected traffic, and the budget constraints.
- Choosing the best type of network topology that meets the network requirements and objectives, as well as the available network resources and technologies.
- Creating a network diagram that shows the physical and logical layout of the network components, such as the nodes, devices, connections, and protocols.
- Testing and evaluating the network performance, security, and reliability, using various tools and methods, such as simulation, modeling, monitoring, and analysis.
- Implementing and maintaining the network topology, using best practices and standards, such as documentation, configuration, backup, and troubleshooting.

# Network performance and transmission impairments

- Network performance is the measure of how well a network can deliver data and services to its users.
- Network performance can be affected by various factors, such as bandwidth, latency, jitter, packet loss, throughput, availability, reliability, and security.
- Transmission impairments are the damage or harm caused to the signal during the signal transmission. They can degrade the quality and integrity of the data and affect the network performance.
- There are various kinds of transmission impairments in the network, such as noise, attenuation, distortion, and interference.

## Noise
- Noise is any unwanted or random signal that interferes with the original signal and reduces its signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).
- There are several types of noise, such as induced noise, crosstalk noise, thermal noise, and impulse noise, which may corrupt the signal.
- Induced noise comes from sources such as motors and appliances. These devices act as sending antenna and transmission medium act as receiving antenna.
- Crosstalk noise is the interference caused by signals from adjacent wires or channels in the same transmission medium.
- Thermal noise is the random fluctuation of electrons in the transmission medium due to thermal agitation. It depends on the temperature and resistance of the medium.
- Impulse noise is a sudden and short-duration spike in the signal, caused by external events such as lightning, power surges, or switching circuits.

## Attenuation
- Attenuation is the loss of signal strength or power as it travels through the transmission medium. It is measured in decibels (dB).
- Attenuation depends on the distance, frequency, and characteristics of the transmission medium. The longer the distance, the higher the frequency, and the more resistive or absorptive the medium, the more attenuation occurs.
- Attenuation can be compensated by using amplifiers or repeaters, which boost the signal strength at regular intervals along the transmission path.

## Distortion
- Distortion is the alteration or change in the shape or form of the signal as it travels through the transmission medium. It can affect the amplitude, phase, or frequency of the signal.
- Distortion can be caused by various factors, such as non-linearities in the transmission medium or devices, multipath propagation, or dispersion.
- Non-linearities can result in harmonic distortion, intermodulation distortion, or clipping of the signal. They occur when the transmission medium or devices do not respond linearly to the input signal, creating new frequencies or altering the existing ones.
- Multipath propagation is the phenomenon where the signal travels along different paths to reach the destination, due to reflection, refraction, or scattering of the signal by obstacles or atmospheric conditions. This can cause delay distortion, phase distortion, or fading of the signal.
- Dispersion is the spreading or separation of the signal components based on their frequency or wavelength. It can occur in both wired and wireless transmission media. It can cause frequency distortion or inter-symbol interference (ISI) of the signal.

## Interference
- Interference is the unwanted or undesired signal that affects the original signal and reduces its signal-to-interference ratio (SIR).
- There are two types of interference, namely co-channel interference and adjacent channel interference.
- Co-channel interference is the interference caused by signals from the same frequency or channel in the same transmission medium. It can occur due to frequency reuse, multiple access, or overlapping channels.
- Adjacent channel interference is the interference caused by signals from nearby frequencies or channels in the same transmission medium. It can occur due to imperfect filtering, channel spacing, or bandwidth allocation.



### Switching techniques and multiplexing

Switching techniques and multiplexing are two important concepts in computer networks that enable efficient and reliable data transmission over shared communication links.

#### Switching techniques

Switching techniques are methods of connecting multiple devices in a network and allowing them to communicate with each other by sending and receiving data packets. There are three main types of switching techniques: circuit switching, message switching, and packet switching.

- Circuit switching: In circuit switching, two nodes communicate with each other over a dedicated communication path. The path is established before the data transmission and remains active until the communication is terminated. Circuit switching is suitable for real-time applications that require constant and uninterrupted data flow, such as voice and video calls. However, circuit switching is inefficient in terms of bandwidth utilization, as the dedicated path cannot be used by other nodes even if there is no data to send or receive. 
- Message switching: In message switching, the whole message is treated as a data unit and is stored and forwarded by intermediate nodes until it reaches the destination. Message switching does not require a dedicated path, and the intermediate nodes can buffer the messages until the next node is available. Message switching is suitable for applications that do not require real-time communication, such as email and file transfer. However, message switching has some drawbacks, such as variable delay, high storage requirement, and possible loss or duplication of messages. 
- Packet switching: The packet switching technique is derived from message switching, where the message is broken down into smaller chunks called packets. Each packet has a header that contains the source and destination addresses, the sequence number, and other information. The packets are then transmitted independently over the network and are reassembled at the destination. Packet switching is more efficient than circuit switching and message switching, as it allows multiple nodes to share the same communication link and reduces the delay and storage requirement. However, packet switching also has some challenges, such as packet loss, packet reordering, packet duplication, and congestion control. 

#### Multiplexing

Multiplexing is a technique of combining multiple signals into one signal over a shared medium. Multiplexing allows the efficient utilization of the bandwidth and the transmission capacity of the medium. The modulated signals are then combined and transmitted over the medium. FDM is suitable for analog signals, such as radio and television broadcasts. However, FDM has some limitations, such as interference, crosstalk, and synchronization. 
- Time division multiplexing (TDM): In TDM, the time of the medium is divided into several time slots, and each signal is assigned a different time slot. The signals are then transmitted over the medium in a sequential manner. TDM is suitable for digital signals, such as telephone and computer networks. However, TDM has some drawbacks, such as synchronization, overhead, and wastage of bandwidth. 
- Wavelength division multiplexing (WDM): In WDM, the wavelength of the medium is divided into several channels, and each signal is modulated by a different wavelength of light. The modulated signals are then combined and transmitted over the medium, such as an optical fiber. WDM is suitable for high-speed and long-distance communication, such as internet backbone and submarine cables. However, WDM has some challenges, such as attenuation, dispersion, and amplification.

The link layer is the lowest layer in the Internet protocol suite, the networking architecture of the Internet. - Media access control: The link layer coordinates the access of multiple hosts to a shared medium, such as a wireless channel or a bus network. - Link management: The link layer establishes, maintains, and terminates the links between hosts, and handles the link state changes, such as link failure, link recovery, and link quality. ### Error Detection and Correction in Link Layer

- Error control in link layer is the process of detecting and correcting data frames that have been corrupted or lost during transmission  .
- Error detection is the process of identifying errors in the received data frames by using some techniques such as parity checks, checksums, or cyclic redundancy checks (CRC) .
- Error correction is the process of recovering the original data frames from the corrupted or lost ones by using some techniques such as retransmission, forward error correction (FEC), or hybrid schemes .
- Error control in link layer can be classified into two types: automatic repeat request (ARQ) and forward error correction (FEC) .
- ARQ is a technique that uses error detection and retransmission to correct errors. The sender sends a data frame and waits for an acknowledgment (ACK) from the receiver. If the receiver detects an error in the received frame, it discards the frame and sends a negative acknowledgment (NAK) to the sender. The sender then retransmits the frame until it receives an ACK or a timeout occurs .
- FEC is a technique that uses error detection and correction codes to correct errors. The sender encodes the data frame with some redundant bits that can be used by the receiver to detect and correct errors without requesting retransmission. The receiver decodes the received frame and corrects the errors if possible. If the errors are too many or too severe, the receiver discards the frame and may request retransmission .
- Hybrid schemes are techniques that combine ARQ and FEC to achieve better performance and reliability. For example, the sender may use FEC to correct some errors and ARQ to correct the remaining errors. Alternatively, the sender may use ARQ to correct errors in some frames and FEC to correct errors in other frames .

  - Stop-and-wait: The sender sends one frame and waits for an acknowledgment before sending the next frame .

  - TCP: The sender and the receiver negotiate a window size based on the available buffer space and the network congestion. The sender adjusts the window size dynamically according to the feedback from the receiver and the network .
  - UDP: The sender does not perform any flow control and relies on the application layer to handle the data rate and the reliability .

### Sliding Window Protocols

Sliding window protocols are data link layer protocols for reliable and sequential delivery of data frames. They are also used in the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) for flow control and congestion control. The sliding window technique allows the sender to send multiple frames at a time before receiving any acknowledgment from the receiver. The receiver uses the sequence number of each frame to detect any missing or duplicate frames and to reorder them if necessary. The sender and the receiver maintain a window of frames that indicates which frames are expected to be sent or received next. The window size is determined by the available buffer space and the bandwidth-delay product of the channel. The window slides forward as new frames are sent or received, and the sender can adjust the window size dynamically to adapt to the network conditions.

There are two main types of sliding window protocols: go-back-N and selective repeat. In go-back-N, the sender can send up to N frames without waiting for acknowledgments, where N is the window size. The receiver only sends a cumulative acknowledgment for the last correctly received frame in sequence. If the sender does not receive an acknowledgment for a frame within a timeout period, it assumes that the frame or the acknowledgment was lost, and it retransmits all the frames from that point onward. This protocol is simple but inefficient, as it may retransmit frames that the receiver has already received.

In selective repeat, the sender can send up to N frames without waiting for acknowledgments, where N is the window size. The receiver sends a selective acknowledgment for each correctly received frame, regardless of the sequence. If the sender does not receive an acknowledgment for a frame within a timeout period, it only retransmits that frame. This protocol is more efficient but more complex, as it requires the receiver to buffer out-of-order frames and the sender to keep track of individual acknowledgments.

### Channel allocation for the notes of the Unit 4 - Medium Access Control and Local Area Networks in the subject of Computer Networks

Channel allocation is a process in which a single channel is divided and allotted to multiple users in order to carry user specific tasks. This technique is suitable for cellular networks, where some users have high priority and need guaranteed access, while others have low priority and can tolerate delays.

Some of the advantages and disadvantages of these techniques are:

- SCA: It is simple and easy to implement. It avoids collisions and interference. It provides guaranteed access and performance. However, it wastes bandwidth and resources when some users are idle. - DCA: It is flexible and efficient. It utilizes bandwidth and resources optimally. It adapts to changing traffic conditions and user demands. It can accommodate new users or stations. However, it is complex and difficult to implement. It may cause collisions and interference. It may not provide guaranteed access and performance. It requires coordination and synchronization among users or stations .
- HCA: It is a compromise between SCA and DCA. It provides guaranteed access and performance to some users or stations, while allowing flexibility and efficiency to others. It balances the trade-off between bandwidth utilization and collision avoidance. However, it is still complex and difficult to implement. It may still cause collisions and interference among the dynamically allocated users or stations. ### Multiple access protocols for the notes of the Unit 4 - Medium Access Control and Local Area Networks in the subject of Computer Networks

- Multiple access protocols can be classified into three categories: random access, controlled access, and channelization .
- Random access protocols: In these protocols, all stations have equal priority to send data over the channel. There is no fixed time for sending data. Stations can sense the state of the channel (idle or busy) and decide when to transmit. Examples of random access protocols are ALOHA, Carrier Sense Multiple Access (CSMA), CSMA with Collision Detection (CSMA/CD), and CSMA with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA) .
- Controlled access protocols: In these protocols, the access to the channel is controlled by a central station or a distributed algorithm. Stations have to request permission to send data and wait for their turn. Examples of controlled access protocols are Reservation, Polling, and Token Passing .
- Channelization protocols: In these protocols, the channel is divided into smaller sub-channels, either in time, frequency, or code. Each station is assigned a sub-channel and can transmit data without interference from other stations. Examples of channelization protocols are Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA), Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA), and Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA) .
- Multiple access protocols are essential for the efficient and fair use of the network resources and the performance of the communication system.



### LAN standards

A local area network (LAN) is a data communication network connecting various terminals or computers within a building or limited geographical area. The connection among the devices could be wired or wireless . LAN standards are the specifications that define the physical and logical characteristics of a LAN, such as the transmission medium, the data rate, the access method, the addressing scheme, the error control, and the security. Some of the most common LAN standards are:

- **Ethernet**: It is the most widely used LAN standard, based on the IEEE 802.3 specification. It uses a bus or star topology and supports data rates of 10 Mbps, 100 Mbps, 1 Gbps, and 10 Gbps. It uses the carrier sense multiple access with collision detection (CSMA/CD) protocol to control the access to the shared medium  .
- **Token Ring**: It is a LAN standard based on the IEEE 802.5 specification. It uses a ring topology and supports data rates of 4 Mbps and 16 Mbps. It uses a token-passing protocol to control the access to the shared medium. A token is a special frame that circulates around the ring and grants the permission to transmit to the node that holds it  .
- **Wireless LAN**: It is a LAN standard based on the IEEE 802.11 family of specifications. It uses radio waves or infrared signals to transmit data between wireless devices. It supports data rates of up to 600 Mbps, depending on the frequency band, the modulation scheme, and the number of spatial streams. It uses the carrier sense multiple access with collision avoidance (CSMA/CA) protocol to control the access to the shared medium  .
- **Fiber Distributed Data Interface (FDDI)**: It is a LAN standard that uses optical fiber as the transmission medium. It supports data rates of 100 Mbps and uses a dual ring topology for redundancy and reliability. It uses a token-passing protocol to control the access to the shared medium .



### Link layer switches & bridges

- Link layer switches and bridges can also filter and isolate traffic within a LAN, reducing collisions and increasing bandwidth efficiency .

- Link layer switches and bridges can also perform error detection and correction on the frames they receive and transmit, using techniques such as CRC (cyclic redundancy check) or checksum .

- A bridge is a device that connects two or more LAN segments and forwards frames between them based on the MAC addresses of the destination and source hosts.
- A bridge operates at the data link layer of the OSI model and can filter, forward, or flood frames depending on the destination MAC address and the bridge's forwarding table.
- A bridge can also learn the MAC addresses of the hosts connected to its ports by observing the source MAC addresses of the incoming frames and updating its forwarding table accordingly.
- A bridge can create a loop-free logical topology for a network of LAN segments by running the spanning tree protocol (STP).
- The spanning tree protocol is a network protocol that builds a loop-free logical topology for Ethernet networks by disabling some of the redundant links between the bridges.
- The basic function of STP is to prevent bridge loops and the broadcast radiation that results from them.
- Spanning tree also allows a network design to include backup links providing fault tolerance if an active link fails.
- The bridges that participate in spanning tree protocol are often called spanning tree bridges.
- To construct a spanning tree, the bridges run a distributed algorithm that involves the following steps   :
  - Each bridge periodically broadcasts a configuration message out all of its ports to its neighbors. The configuration message contains the bridge's identifier, the identifier of the root bridge (the bridge with the lowest identifier in the network), the cost of the path from the bridge to the root bridge, and the port identifier of the port that sent the message. These messages are called bridge protocol data units (BPDUs) or hello messages .
  - Each bridge processes the BPDUs it receives from other bridges and updates its own information about the root bridge, the cost of the path to the root bridge, and the port that connects to the root bridge (the root port). The bridge also forwards the BPDUs to other ports, except the one that received the BPDU, after updating the cost of the path to the root bridge .
  - Each bridge determines which of its ports are designated ports, which are the ports that provide the lowest cost path from the LAN segment to the root bridge. The bridge compares the BPDUs it receives on each port with the BPDUs it sends on that port. If the received BPDU has a lower cost path to the root bridge than the sent BPDU, the port is not a designated port. If the received BPDU has the same cost path to the root bridge as the sent BPDU, the port is a designated port if the bridge has a lower identifier than the sender of the BPDU. Otherwise, the port is not a designated port .
  - Each bridge disables (blocks) all of its ports that are not root ports or designated ports. These ports are called non-designated ports. The bridge does not forward any frames or BPDUs on these ports. This way, the bridge eliminates any redundant links that could cause loops in the network .
  - The process is dynamic, so if an outage occurs then the spanning tree is recomputed. If the outage should partition the network into two pieces, both pieces will build spanning trees.
- The spanning tree algorithm was developed by Radia Perlman at the Digital Equipment Corporation and is the basis of the IEEE 802.1 specification for LAN bridges.



## Unit 5 - Network Layer

The network layer is the third layer of the OSI model, which is responsible for how a machine in a network can communicate with a machine in a different network. The network layer performs the following functions:

- **Addressing**: The network layer assigns a logical address to each device in the network, such as an IP address, which is used to identify the source and destination of the data packets. The network layer also performs address translation, such as NAT, which maps private IP addresses to public IP addresses.
- **Routing**: The network layer determines the best path for the data packets to reach the destination, based on factors such as distance, cost, congestion, and reliability. The network layer uses routing protocols, such as RIP, OSPF, EIGRP, and BGP, to exchange routing information with other routers and update their routing tables.
- **Fragmentation and reassembly**: The network layer divides the data packets into smaller fragments if they are larger than the maximum transmission unit (MTU) of the underlying network. The network layer also reassembles the fragments at the destination and checks for errors and missing fragments.
- **Congestion control and quality of service**: The network layer monitors the traffic load on the network and adjusts the transmission rate and priority of the data packets to avoid congestion and ensure quality of service. The network layer uses techniques such as queuing, scheduling, dropping, and marking to manage the network resources and traffic.

Some of the common network layer protocols are:

- **Internet Protocol (IP)**: IP is the most widely used network layer protocol, which provides connectionless and unreliable delivery of data packets across the internet. IP supports two versions: IPv4 and IPv6, which differ in their address format, header structure, and features.
- **Internet Control Message Protocol (ICMP)**: ICMP is a network layer protocol that is used to send error and control messages between devices on the internet, such as ping, traceroute, and destination unreachable.
- **Internet Group Management Protocol (IGMP)**: IGMP is a network layer protocol that is used to manage multicast groups on the internet, such as video streaming and online gaming. IGMP enables devices to join and leave multicast groups and routers to forward multicast traffic efficiently.
- **Address Resolution Protocol (ARP)**: ARP is a network layer protocol that is used to map a network layer address, such as an IP address, to a data link layer address, such as a MAC address, on a local area network (LAN). ### ARP
- ARP stands for Address Resolution Protocol.
- ARP is a network layer protocol that maps an IP address to a MAC address.
- ARP is used when a host wants to communicate with another host on the same local area network (LAN).
- ARP operates in two phases: request and reply.
- In the request phase, the sender broadcasts an ARP request packet to all hosts on the LAN, asking for the MAC address of the destination host with a given IP address.
- In the reply phase, the destination host responds with an ARP reply packet, containing its MAC address, to the sender.
- The sender then updates its ARP cache, which is a table that stores the IP-MAC mappings of known hosts on the LAN, and uses the MAC address to send the data packet to the destination host.
- ARP is a stateless protocol, meaning that it does not keep track of the status or history of the communication between hosts.
- ARP is vulnerable to spoofing attacks, where an attacker can send a fake ARP reply packet to trick the sender into updating its ARP cache with a wrong IP-MAC mapping, and then intercept or modify the data packets sent by the sender.



### RARP

RARP stands for Reverse Address Resolution Protocol. It is a network protocol that allows a host computer to obtain its IP address from a gateway server's ARP table or cache. RARP is used when the host computer does not have a permanent IP address assigned to it, such as in diskless workstations or bootstrapping devices. RARP operates on the network access layer of the TCP/IP protocol stack, and uses the same packet format as ARP. RARP works as follows:

- The host computer broadcasts a RARP request packet containing its MAC address to the network.
- The gateway server that has the RARP table receives the request and looks up the IP address that corresponds to the MAC address of the host computer.
- The gateway server sends a RARP reply packet containing the IP address of the host computer to the network.
- The host computer receives the RARP reply and configures its IP address accordingly.

Some of the advantages and disadvantages of RARP are:

- RARP is simple and easy to implement, as it does not require any configuration on the host computer.
- RARP reduces the administrative overhead of assigning and managing IP addresses for network devices.
- RARP is limited to local area networks, as it relies on broadcast messages that cannot cross routers or subnets.
- RARP is obsolete and has been replaced by more advanced protocols such as BOOTP and DHCP, which offer more features and flexibility.



# DHCP

- DHCP stands for Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol   .
- It is a network management protocol that automatically assigns IP addresses and other communication parameters to devices connected to an IP network using a client-server architecture   .
- It is based on the Bootstrap Protocol (BOOTP), which was designed for diskless workstations .
- It is defined by RFCs 2131 and 2132, and is an Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) standard.
- It reduces the need for manual configuration and administration of IP settings, and allows for dynamic allocation and reuse of IP addresses   .
- It consists of four main components: a DHCP client, a DHCP server, a DHCP relay agent, and a DHCP database.
- A DHCP client is a device that requests IP configuration information from a DHCP server .
- A DHCP server is a device that maintains a pool of available IP addresses and leases them to DHCP clients for a specified period of time .
- A DHCP relay agent is a device that forwards DHCP messages between DHCP clients and DHCP servers on different subnets.
- A DHCP database is a storage system that keeps track of the IP address assignments and other DHCP options for each DHCP client.
- The DHCP process involves four basic steps: discover, offer, request, and acknowledge (DORA)   .
- In the discover step, the DHCP client broadcasts a DHCPDISCOVER message to locate a DHCP server   .
- In the offer step, the DHCP server responds with a DHCPOFFER message that contains an IP address and other configuration parameters for the DHCP client   .
- In the request step, the DHCP client selects one of the offers and sends a DHCPREQUEST message to the DHCP server to confirm the IP address and other parameters   .
- In the acknowledge step, the DHCP server sends a DHCPACK message to the DHCP client to finalize the IP address assignment and update the DHCP database   .
- The DHCP client can also send a DHCPRELEASE message to the DHCP server to terminate the lease and release the IP address  .
- The DHCP server can also send a DHCPNAK message to the DHCP client to reject the request or indicate an invalid configuration  .
- The DHCP protocol supports various options that can be included in the DHCP messages, such as subnet mask, default gateway, domain name, DNS server, and time server  .
- The DHCP protocol can be used for IPv4 and IPv6 networks, with some differences in the message format and options .
- The DHCP protocol can be integrated with other network services, such as Dynamic DNS (DDNS), which allows for automatic update of DNS records when the IP address of a DHCP client changes .

### Routing
- Routing is the process of finding a path from a source to a destination in a network of nodes.
- Routing can be classified into two types: static and dynamic.
- Static routing is when the routes are fixed and predetermined by the network administrator or the network design. Static routing is simple, fast, and reliable, but it cannot adapt to changes in the network topology or traffic conditions.

- Source-based trees are constructed by the source node for each group. They use the shortest path from the source to each destination in the group. They have low delay and high bandwidth efficiency, but they require more storage and maintenance overhead.
- Group-shared trees are constructed by a designated node called the core or the rendezvous point for each group. They use the shortest path from the core to each destination in the group. They have low storage and maintenance overhead, but they may have higher delay and lower bandwidth efficiency.

### Static and dynamic routing

Static and dynamic routing are two methods used to determine how to send a packet toward its destination. They differ in the following aspects:

- **Configuration**: Static routes are configured in advance of any network communication. Dynamic routes are learned by routers through exchanging information with other routers.
- **Flexibility**: Static routes are fixed and do not change unless manually updated. Dynamic routes are adaptive and can change according to the network conditions.
- **Complexity**: Static routing is simple and easy to implement. Dynamic routing is complex and requires sophisticated routing algorithms and protocols.

- **Efficiency**: Static routing is more efficient for small networks with one or two routes. Dynamic routing is more efficient for large networks with multiple routes and frequent changes.
- **Computation and bandwidth requirements**: Static routing requires less compute power and bandwidth, as it only has one preconfigured route. Dynamic routing requires more compute power and bandwidth, as it adjusts routes based on distance vector or link state protocols.

### Congestion control algorithms

Congestion control algorithms are methods to regulate the flow of data packets in a network and prevent congestion, which can degrade the performance and reliability of the network. Congestion occurs when the demand for network resources exceeds the available capacity. Congestion control algorithms can be implemented at different layers of the network stack, such as the transport layer or the network layer.

Some of the common congestion control algorithms are:

- **Leaky bucket**: This algorithm simulates a leaky bucket that can hold a fixed number of tokens. Tokens are generated at a constant rate and added to the bucket. A packet can be sent only if there is a token available in the bucket. If the bucket is full, any incoming token is discarded. This algorithm limits the burstiness of the traffic and smooths out the data rate.

- **Token bucket**: This algorithm is similar to the leaky bucket, but it allows some burstiness in the traffic. The bucket can hold a maximum number of tokens, but tokens are not discarded if the bucket is full. Instead, they accumulate up to the bucket capacity. A packet can be sent only if there is a token available in the bucket. The token bucket algorithm can accommodate variable data rates as long as the average rate does not exceed the token generation rate.

- **Additive increase multiplicative decrease (AIMD)**: This algorithm is used by TCP to adjust the congestion window size, which determines how many packets can be sent without waiting for an acknowledgment. The algorithm increases the window size by one packet for every round trip time (RTT) if there is no packet loss, and decreases the window size by half if there is a packet loss. This algorithm probes the network capacity gradually and backs off quickly when congestion is detected.

- **Congestion avoidance and control (CAC)**: This algorithm is proposed by Jacobson and Karels to improve the performance of TCP in the presence of congestion. The algorithm uses two parameters: slow start threshold (ssthresh) and congestion window (cwnd). The algorithm operates in two phases: slow start and congestion avoidance. In the slow start phase, the cwnd is increased exponentially until it reaches the ssthresh or a packet loss occurs. In the congestion avoidance phase, the cwnd is increased linearly until a packet loss occurs. The algorithm also uses a fast retransmit and fast recovery mechanism to handle packet losses without reducing the cwnd too much.

- **Random early detection (RED)**: This algorithm is implemented at the network layer by routers to manage the queue length and avoid buffer overflow. The algorithm monitors the average queue length and compares it with two thresholds: minth and maxth. If the average queue length is below minth, the router accepts all incoming packets. If the average queue length is above maxth, the router drops all incoming packets. If the average queue length is between minth and maxth, the router drops incoming packets with a probability that increases with the queue length. This algorithm signals the sources to reduce their sending rate before the queue overflows.

### World Wide Web and Hyper Text Transfer Protocol

- The World Wide Web (WWW) is a system of interlinked hypertext documents that can be accessed via the Internet using a web browser.
- A hypertext document is a text file that contains links to other documents or resources, such as images, videos, or audio files.
- The links in a hypertext document are called hyperlinks, and they allow users to navigate from one document to another by clicking on them.
- The WWW was invented by Tim Berners-Lee and his team at CERN in 1989-1991, and it became the most popular way of accessing information on the Internet.
- The HyperText Transfer Protocol (HTTP) is the application layer protocol that enables the communication between web browsers and web servers on the WWW.
- HTTP is based on the client-server model, where a web browser is a client that requests a web page from a web server that hosts the website.
- HTTP uses the Uniform Resource Locator (URL) to identify and locate the web page that the client wants to access.
- A URL consists of four parts: the protocol (http), the domain name (www.example.com), the path (/index.html), and the optional query string (?q=keyword).
- HTTP follows a request-response cycle, where the client sends an HTTP request message to the server, and the server responds with an HTTP response message that contains the requested web page or an error code.
- HTTP is a stateless protocol, which means that each request-response pair is independent and the server does not keep track of the client's previous requests or actions.
- HTTP can also use the Transport Layer Security (TLS) protocol to encrypt and authenticate the communication between the client and the server, and this is called HTTPS (HTTP Secure).
- HTTPS uses the Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) or the newer Transport Layer Security (TLS) protocols to establish a secure connection between the client and the server, and it prevents eavesdropping, tampering, or impersonation by third parties.
- HTTPS is indicated by a padlock icon in the address bar of the web browser, and the URL starts with https instead of http.

### File Transfer Protocol

- File Transfer Protocol (FTP) is a standard communication protocol used for the transfer of computer files from a server to a client on a computer network.
- FTP is built on a client–server model architecture using separate control and data connections between the client and the server.
- The original specification for FTP was written by Abhay Bhushan in 1971.
- FTP exchanges data using two separate channels known as the command channel to authenticate the user, and the data channel to transfer the files.
- FTP can be used within an internal network of computers, or online between different web servers.
- FTP can transfer multiple files simultaneously.
- FTP requires an Internet connection to execute file transfers.
- FTP has two modes of operation: active mode and passive mode.
  - In active mode, the client starts listening for incoming data connections from the server on a port specified by the client.
  - In passive mode, the client initiates both the control and data connections to the server, and the server passively listens for the connections.
- FTP uses different commands to perform various operations, such as:
  - USER and PASS to provide the username and password for authentication.
  - LIST to list the files in the current directory on the server.
  - RETR to retrieve a file from the server.
  - STOR to store a file on the server.
  - QUIT to terminate the FTP session.
- FTP has some limitations and security risks, such as:
  - FTP does not encrypt the data or the credentials, making them vulnerable to interception and tampering.
  - FTP does not support file synchronization, compression, or integrity checking.
  - FTP does not handle network errors or interruptions gracefully, resulting in incomplete or corrupted file transfers.
- To overcome these limitations and risks, some alternative file transfer protocols have been developed, such as:
  - Secure File Transfer Protocol (SFTP), which uses SSH to encrypt the data and the credentials.
  - File Transfer Protocol Secure (FTPS), which uses SSL/TLS to encrypt the data and the credentials.
  - Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure (HTTPS), which uses SSL/TLS to encrypt the data and the credentials, and also supports web browsers as clients.

### Data compression

- Data compression is the process of reducing the amount of data needed for the storage or transmission of a given piece of information, typically by the use of encoding techniques.
- Data compression can be performed by using smaller strings of bits (0s and 1s) in place of the original string and using a ‘dictionary’ to decompress the data if required. Other techniques include the introduction of pointers (references) to a string of bits that the compression program has become familiar with or removing redundant characters.
- Data compression can be broadly classified into two types: lossless and lossy.
  - Lossless compression: It is a compression technique that preserves the original data without any loss of information. It is suitable for text, audio, and some image data that require high fidelity. The various algorithms used to implement lossless data compression are:
    - Run length encoding: It is a technique that replaces consecutive identical characters or symbols with a single character and a count of its repetitions. For example, the string "AAAAABBBBCCCC" can be compressed as "5A4B4C".
    - Differential pulse code modulation: It is a technique that encodes the difference between successive samples of a signal, rather than the absolute values. This reduces the number of bits needed to represent the signal, as the differences are usually smaller than the original values.
    - Dictionary based encoding: It is a technique that uses a predefined dictionary of common strings or symbols and replaces them with shorter codes. For example, the string "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" can be compressed as "1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9", where the dictionary is {"the":1, "quick":2, "brown":3, "fox":4, "jumps":5, "over":6, "lazy":7, "dog":8, " ":9}.
  - Lossy compression: It is a compression technique that discards some information from the original data, resulting in some loss of quality or accuracy. It is suitable for image, video, and some audio data that can tolerate some distortion. The various algorithms used to implement lossy data compression are:
    - JPEG: It is a technique that compresses images by dividing them into blocks of pixels and applying a discrete cosine transform (DCT) to each block. The DCT coefficients are then quantized and encoded using Huffman coding or arithmetic coding. The quantization step introduces some loss of information, as some coefficients are rounded off or set to zero.
    - MPEG: It is a technique that compresses video by exploiting the temporal and spatial redundancy in the frames. It uses motion estimation and compensation to encode the difference between successive frames, rather than the absolute values. It also uses DCT, quantization, and Huffman coding or arithmetic coding to compress the frames. The motion estimation and quantization steps introduce some loss of information, as some details are ignored or approximated.
    - MP3: It is a technique that compresses audio by applying a psychoacoustic model to the signal. The psychoacoustic model determines which frequencies are perceptible by the human ear and discards the ones that are not. It also uses DCT, quantization, and Huffman coding or arithmetic coding to compress the signal. The psychoacoustic model and quantization steps introduce some loss of information, as some sounds are masked or distorted.

- Data compression can be applied at different layers of the network, depending on the type and purpose of the data. For example, data compression can be applied at the application layer for specific applications, such as web browsing, email, or file transfer. Data compression can also be applied at the data link layer or the physical layer for general data transmission, such as in modems or routers.
- Data compression can have various benefits and drawbacks, depending on the type and degree of compression. Some of the benefits are:
  - Reduced storage space: Data compression can save disk space or memory by storing or caching compressed data.
  - Reduced transmission time: Data compression can reduce the bandwidth or the number of bits needed to transmit data over a network, resulting in faster data transfer or lower communication costs.
  - Reduced processing time: Data compression can reduce the computational complexity or the number of operations needed to process data, resulting in faster data processing



### Cryptography – basic concepts

Cryptography is the science and art of designing and using techniques to secure communication and data in the presence of adversaries. Cryptography has many applications, such as protecting the confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity of information, enabling secure transactions, authentication, digital signatures, and encryption.

Some basic concepts of cryptography are:

- **Plaintext**: The original message or data that needs to be encrypted or protected.
- **Ciphertext**: The encrypted or transformed version of the plaintext that is unreadable by unauthorized parties.
- **Encryption**: The process of converting plaintext into ciphertext using a secret key and an algorithm.
- **Decryption**: The process of converting ciphertext back into plaintext using the same or a different secret key and an algorithm.
- **Key**: A secret value that is used to encrypt or decrypt data. The key should be chosen randomly and securely, and should be kept secret from unauthorized parties.
- **Algorithm**: A set of rules or steps that are used to encrypt or decrypt data. The algorithm should be secure, meaning that it is hard to break or reverse without knowing the key.
- **Symmetric-key cryptography**: A type of cryptography where the same key is used to encrypt and decrypt data. The key should be shared securely between the sender and the receiver. Examples of symmetric-key algorithms are AES, DES, and RC4.
- **Asymmetric-key cryptography**: A type of cryptography where different keys are used to encrypt and decrypt data. The sender and the receiver each have a pair of keys: a public key and a private key. The public key can be shared openly, while the private key should be kept secret. The sender can encrypt data using the receiver's public key, and the receiver can decrypt it using their own private key. Examples of asymmetric-key algorithms are RSA, ECC, and DSA.
- **Hash function**: A function that maps any input data to a fixed-length output, called a hash or a digest. The hash function should be one-way, meaning that it is easy to compute the hash from the input, but hard to find the input from the hash. The hash function should also be collision-resistant, meaning that it is hard to find two different inputs that produce the same hash. Hash functions are used to verify the integrity and authenticity of data, such as passwords, digital signatures, and checksums. Examples of hash functions are SHA-256, MD5, and BLAKE2.
