

## Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS

- Virtual reality (VR) is a technology that creates an immersive and interactive simulation of a three-dimensional environment, using computer-generated graphics, sounds, and other sensory inputs.
- Virtual environments (VEs) are the specific settings or scenarios that are simulated in VR, such as a virtual classroom, a virtual museum, or a virtual battlefield.
- VR can be experienced using various devices, such as head-mounted displays (HMDs), gloves, controllers, treadmills, or haptic suits, that provide visual, auditory, tactile, or kinesthetic feedback to the user.
- VR can have various applications and benefits, such as education, entertainment, training, therapy, or social interaction, depending on the design and purpose of the VE.
- VR can also pose some challenges and risks, such as technical limitations, ethical issues, health effects, or social implications, depending on the quality and usage of the VR system.



### The historical development of VR

- VR stands for virtual reality, which is a computer-generated simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with by a user using special equipment, such as a headset and gloves.
- The concept of VR can be traced back to the 19th century, when panoramic paintings and stereoscopic photographs created immersive illusions of reality for the viewers.
- The first VR device was the Sensorama, invented by Morton Heilig in 1956. It was a cabinet that displayed stereoscopic images, sounds, smells, and vibrations to create a multisensory experience of various scenarios, such as a motorcycle ride or a belly dance.
- The term "virtual reality" was coined by Jaron Lanier in 1987, who founded the company VPL Research that developed some of the earliest VR products, such as the EyePhone and the DataGlove.
- The 1990s saw the emergence of consumer-oriented VR systems, such as the Sega VR, the Virtual Boy, and the Virtuality arcade machines. However, these products suffered from technical limitations, high costs, and low public interest, and failed to achieve widespread adoption.
- The 2000s witnessed the advancement of VR technology, especially in the fields of gaming, entertainment, education, and medicine. Some of the notable examples of VR applications in this period are the CAVE system, the Second Life virtual world, the Wii Remote, and the Google Cardboard.
- The 2010s marked the resurgence of VR as a mainstream phenomenon, thanks to the development of affordable and high-quality VR headsets, such as the Oculus Rift, the HTC Vive, and the PlayStation VR. These devices enabled immersive and interactive VR experiences for a large number of users, and sparked the growth of VR content and platforms, such as SteamVR, Oculus Home, and YouTube VR.
- The current trends and challenges of VR include improving the realism, comfort, and accessibility of VR devices and content, enhancing the social and collaborative aspects of VR, and addressing the ethical and psychological implications of VR.

### Computer Graphics for the notes of the Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS

- Virtual reality (VR) is the use of computer graphics systems in combination with various display and interface devices to provide the effect of immersion in the interactive 3D computer-generated environment . We call such an environment a virtual environment (VE).
- A virtual environment can simulate a real or an imaginary world, and can be designed for various purposes, such as entertainment, education, training, or research .
- A virtual environment typically consists of the following components:
  - A computer system that generates the graphics, sound, and other sensory information of the virtual world.
  - A display device that presents the virtual world to the user, such as a head-mounted display (HMD), a projection screen, or a monitor.
  - An input device that allows the user to interact with the virtual world, such as a joystick, a mouse, a glove, or a tracker.

  - Immersion refers to the extent to which the user feels present in the virtual world, and is influenced by factors such as the field of view, the resolution, the latency, and the stereoscopy of the display device.
  - Realism refers to the degree of correspondence between the virtual world and the real world, and is influenced by factors such as the quality, accuracy, and consistency of the graphics, sound, and other sensory information.
  - Interactivity refers to the degree of control and feedback that the user has over the virtual world, and is influenced by factors such as the responsiveness, the flexibility, and the naturalness of the input and feedback devices.
- Some examples of virtual environments are :
  - Virtual tourism, where the user can explore a virtual representation of a real or a fictional place, such as a museum, a city, or a planet.
  - Virtual training, where the user can learn a skill or a procedure in a virtual scenario, such as a flight simulator, a surgical simulator, or a fire drill.
  - Virtual gaming, where the user can play a game or a sport in a virtual setting, such as a first-person shooter, a racing game, or a golf game.
  - Virtual art, where the user can create or experience a virtual artwork, such as a painting, a sculpture, or a performance.



### Real-time computer graphics

- Real-time computer graphics is the sub-field of computer graphics focused on producing and analyzing images in real time .
- The term can refer to anything from rendering an application's graphical user interface (GUI) to real-time image analysis, but is most often used in reference to interactive 3D computer graphics, typically using a graphics processing unit (GPU), with video games the most noticeable users .
- Real-time rendering is often contrasted with offline rendering, which is the process of generating images from 3D models by using complex algorithms and techniques that are not feasible in real time.
- Real-time rendering requires a high level of performance and optimization, as well as trade-offs between quality and speed, to achieve interactive frame rates (typically 30 or 60 frames per second).
- Some of the techniques used in real-time rendering include rasterization, ray tracing, ray casting, ray marching, deferred shading, forward shading, shadow mapping, ambient occlusion, global illumination, anti-aliasing, texture mapping, bump mapping, normal mapping, displacement mapping, tessellation, animation, skeletal animation, inverse kinematics, physics simulation, collision detection, particle systems, and post-processing effects.
- Real-time computer graphics can be used for various applications, such as video games, virtual reality, augmented reality, simulations, training, education, entertainment, art, and visualization  .
- Real-time computer graphics can also adapt to autostereoscopy, which is a technique that allows the viewer to perceive 3D images without the need for special glasses or headsets.

### Requirements for VR

- VR is the simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with by a user through a VR device such as a headset, controller, or glove.
- VR requires the following components to create a realistic and immersive experience for the user:
  - A VR device that can track the user's head and body movements and provide feedback to the user's senses, such as vision, hearing, touch, and sometimes smell and taste.
  - A VR application that can generate and render the virtual environment and the objects within it, as well as handle the user's input and output.
  - A VR platform that can support the VR device and the VR application, such as a computer, a console, a smartphone, or a cloud service.
  - A VR network that can connect the VR device, the VR application, and the VR platform, as well as other VR users and devices, such as the internet, a local area network, or a wireless network.
- VR requires the following characteristics to achieve a high level of presence and immersion for the user:
  - High resolution and frame rate to provide clear and smooth visuals.
  - Low latency and high bandwidth to minimize the delay and lag between the user's actions and the VR feedback.
  - Wide field of view and stereoscopic vision to cover the user's natural range of sight and depth perception.
  - Spatial audio and haptic feedback to create realistic and directional sounds and sensations.
  - Accurate and consistent tracking and calibration to align the user's physical and virtual positions and orientations.
  - Intuitive and natural interaction and navigation to enable the user to manipulate and explore the virtual environment and objects.

## Unit 2 - Hardware Technologies for 3D User Interfaces

- Hardware technologies for 3D user interfaces are the devices and systems that enable the communication between a user (or users) and a 3D system.
- Hardware technologies can be classified into two main categories: input and output devices.
- Input devices allow the user to provide information, commands, or actions to the system, such as keyboards, mice, touchscreens, joysticks, trackers, gloves, etc.
- Output devices allow the system to provide information, feedback, or responses to the user, such as monitors, speakers, headphones, projectors, haptic devices, etc.
- Hardware technologies can also be categorized by the sensory modality they target, such as visual, auditory, or haptic.
- Visual devices display 3D graphics or images to the user, such as LCD screens, head-mounted displays, stereoscopic glasses, etc.
- Auditory devices produce 3D sounds or speech to the user, such as speakers, headphones, microphones, etc.
- Haptic devices provide 3D tactile or force feedback to the user, such as vibration motors, force-feedback joysticks, exoskeletons, etc.
- Hardware technologies can also be characterized by the degree of immersion they provide, such as non-immersive, semi-immersive, or fully immersive.
- Non-immersive devices are those that do not isolate the user from the real world, such as desktop monitors, keyboards, mice, etc.
- Semi-immersive devices are those that partially isolate the user from the real world, such as large screens, head-mounted displays, surround sound, etc.
- Fully immersive devices are those that completely isolate the user from the real world, such as virtual reality headsets, gloves, suits, etc.
- Hardware technologies can also be evaluated by the quality and performance they offer, such as resolution, refresh rate, latency, accuracy, etc.
- Resolution is the number of pixels or dots that a device can display or capture, such as 1920x1080 for a monitor or 640x480 for a camera.
- Refresh rate is the frequency at which a device can update its display or capture, such as 60 Hz for a monitor or 30 Hz for a camera.
- Latency is the delay between the user's action and the system's response, such as 10 ms for a keyboard or 100 ms for a tracker.
- Accuracy is the degree of error or deviation between the user's action and the system's response, such as 1 mm for a mouse or 10 cm for a tracker.



### Visual Displays for the notes of the Unit 2 - HARDWARE TECHNOLOGIES FOR 3D USER INTERFACES in the subject of Augmented & Virtual Reality

- Visual displays are output devices that present 3D representations of system state to the user .
- Visual displays can be classified into two categories: fully-immersive and semi-immersive.
  - Fully-immersive displays occlude the real world and provide a high degree of presence and immersion to the user. Examples are head-mounted displays, arm-mounted displays and virtual retinal displays.
  - Semi-immersive displays allow the user to see the real world and provide a lower degree of presence and immersion. Examples are monitors, surround-screen displays, workbenches, hemispherical displays and autostereoscopic displays .
- Visual display characteristics that affect the quality and performance of 3D user interfaces are :
  - Field of regard (FOR) and field of view (FOV): FOR is the total angular extent of the display, while FOV is the angular extent of the display that is visible to the user at any given time. A larger FOV can increase the sense of immersion and realism, but also increase the computational cost and the risk of simulator sickness.
  - Spatial resolution: The number of pixels per unit area or per unit angle of the display. A higher resolution can improve the visual quality and the perception of depth and distance, but also increase the computational cost and the power consumption.
  - Brightness, contrast and gamma correction: The luminance, the ratio of the brightest and darkest colors, and the nonlinear mapping of input intensity to output luminance of the display. These factors can affect the visibility, legibility and color reproduction of the display, as well as the user's comfort and fatigue.
  - Stereoscopy: The ability of the display to present different images to each eye of the user, creating a binocular disparity that enhances the perception of depth. Stereoscopic displays can be active (using shutter glasses or eye tracking) or passive (using polarized glasses or filters).
  - Refresh rate: The frequency at which the display updates the image. A higher refresh rate can reduce the latency, flicker and motion blur of the display, as well as the risk of simulator sickness. A refresh rate of at least 60 Hz is recommended for 3D user interfaces.



### Auditory Displays for 3D User Interfaces

- Auditory displays are devices that present information through the human auditory system, which is especially useful when supplying location and spatial information to the users.
- Auditory displays can complement or substitute visual displays, depending on the task and the environment. For example, auditory displays can provide feedback, warnings, cues, or ambient sounds in situations where visual attention is limited or overloaded.
- Auditory displays can be classified into two main types: 3D sound and sonification.
  - 3D sound is the generation of sound that simulates the natural perception of sound in three-dimensional space. 3D sound can be achieved by using spatial audio techniques, such as binaural, ambisonic, or wave field synthesis, that manipulate the sound signals to create the illusion of sound sources at different locations and distances .
  - Sonification is the transformation of certain types of information into sound. Sonification can be used to represent data, events, states, or processes that are not inherently auditory, such as temperature, speed, or network activity. Sonification can be useful for monitoring, exploring, or communicating complex or abstract information .
- Auditory displays can enhance 3D user interfaces for virtual environments by providing more immersive, realistic, and informative experiences. Auditory displays can also support 3D user interaction by providing feedback, guidance, or affordances for navigation, manipulation, or collaboration .
- Auditory displays and auditory user interfaces require careful design, evaluation, and integration with other modalities, such as vision, touch, or gesture. Some of the challenges and issues involved in auditory display design include sound quality, spatial accuracy, sound symbolism, sound aesthetics, sound mapping, sound attention, sound privacy, and sound fatigue .



### Haptic Displays

- Haptic displays are interfaces that provide haptic feedback, which is the sensation of touch, to the user .
- Haptic feedback can be divided into two types: cutaneous feedback, which is the stimulation of the skin receptors, and kinesthetic or proprioceptive feedback, which is the stimulation of the muscles and joints .
- Haptic displays can be used to create virtual objects, control virtual objects, enhance remote control of machines and devices, and provide tactile and force feedback in various applications.
- Haptic displays can be categorized by the type of stimuli or output they generate, such as force, vibration, motion, temperature, or electric current  .
- Haptic displays can also be categorized by the body part they target, such as the hand, the arm, the foot, the face, or the whole body.
- Haptic displays can range from small units that sit on the desktop or are worn on the hand to large robotic devices that can lift a person off the ground.
- Haptic displays can be based on different technologies, such as pneumatic, hydraulic, electric, magnetic, or piezoelectric actuators.
- Haptic displays can be designed to provide different levels of fidelity, realism, and immersion, depending on the application and the user's needs.

## Unit 3 - 3D User Interface Input Hardware

- 3D user interface input hardware are devices that capture and interpret the actions performed by the user in a 3D environment.
- The main characteristics of 3D user interface input hardware are:
  - Degrees of freedom (DOF): the number of independent ways that a body can move in space. For example, a 3D mouse can have six DOF: three for translation (x, y, z) and three for rotation (pitch, yaw, roll).
  - Input type/frequency of data: the kind and amount of data that the device generates. For example, discrete input devices produce single data values (e.g. Boolean values), while continuous input devices generate multiple data values (e.g. pixel coordinates).
  - Physical interaction: the amount and type of physical effort required by the user to operate the device. For example, some devices are passive (e.g. gloves) and some are active (e.g. wands).
- Some examples of 3D user interface input hardware are:
  - Keyboards: a set of keys that can be used to enter text, commands, or shortcuts. They are typically used in desktop 3D UIs and have low DOF and discrete input type.
  - 2D mice and trackballs: devices that can move a cursor on a 2D plane. They are also used in desktop 3D UIs and have low DOF and continuous input type.
  - Pen- and touch-based tablets: devices that can sense the position and pressure of a stylus or a finger on a 2D surface. They can be used for drawing, writing, or gesturing in 3D UIs and have low to medium DOF and continuous input type.
  - Joysticks: devices that can sense the direction and magnitude of a force applied to a stick. They can be used for navigation, manipulation, or selection in 3D UIs and have medium DOF and continuous input type.
  - Wands: devices that can sense the position and orientation of a handheld device in 3D space. They can be used for pointing, selecting, or manipulating in 3D UIs and have high DOF and continuous input type.
  - Gloves: devices that can sense the position and orientation of the hand and fingers in 3D space. They can be used for gesturing, grabbing, or manipulating in 3D UIs and have high DOF and continuous input type.
  - Trackers: devices that can sense the position and orientation of the head, body, or limbs in 3D space. They can be used for navigation, interaction, or immersion in 3D UIs and have high DOF and continuous input type.



### Input device characteristics for the notes of the Unit 3 - 3D USER INTERFACE INPUT HARDWARE in the subject of Augmented & Virtual Reality

- Input devices are hardware that allow the user to communicate with the system by providing input in physical 3D space or manipulating tools, sensors, or devices in 3D space.
- Input devices can be classified according to the following characteristics :
  - Degrees of freedom (DOFs): the number of independent parameters that define the position and orientation of an object in 3D space. For example, a 6-DOF device can report the x, y, z coordinates and the pitch, yaw, roll angles of an object.
  - DOF composition: the way the DOFs are grouped or separated in the input device. For example, a device can have integral DOFs, where all the DOFs are controlled by a single input, or separable DOFs, where each DOF is controlled by a different input.
  - Type of electronics: the type of signals that the input device uses to communicate with the system. For example, a device can use digital electronics, where the signals are discrete and binary, or analog electronics, where the signals are continuous and variable.
  - Range of reported values: the type and extent of the values that the input device can report to the system. For example, a device can report discrete values, where the values are fixed and finite, continuous values, where the values are variable and infinite, or hybrid values, where the values are a combination of discrete and continuous.
  - Sensing technology: the type of technology that the input device uses to sense the user's input or the environment. For example, a device can use active sensing, where the device emits a signal and measures the response, or passive sensing, where the device measures a signal that is already present.
  - Input modality: the type of input that the user can provide to the device. For example, a device can use gesture input, where the user performs a motion or a pose, or voice input, where the user speaks a command or a query.
  - Form factor: the shape, size, and weight of the input device. For example, a device can be handheld, wearable, or stationary.
  - Ergonomics: the design of the input device that affects the user's comfort, efficiency, and safety. For example, a device can have a natural or intuitive mapping, where the input corresponds to the expected output, or a feedback mechanism, where the device provides a response to the user's input.



### Desktop input devices for 3D user interface

- Desktop input devices are devices that allow the user to interact with a 3D user interface on a desktop system, such as a computer monitor or a projection screen.
- Desktop input devices can be classified into two categories: 2D input devices and 3D input devices.
- 2D input devices are devices that capture the user's input in two dimensions, such as the x and y coordinates of a mouse cursor or a touch screen. 2D input devices can be used to control a 3D user interface by mapping the 2D input to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a mouse to rotate a 3D object or to navigate a 3D scene.
- 3D input devices are devices that capture the user's input in three dimensions, such as the position and orientation of a hand or a head. 3D input devices can be used to control a 3D user interface by directly manipulating the 3D objects or the 3D viewpoint, such as using a hand gesture to grab and move a 3D object or to change the perspective of a 3D scene.
- Some examples of desktop input devices for 3D user interface are:

  - Keyboards: Keyboards are 2D input devices that allow the user to enter text or commands by pressing keys. Keyboards can be used to control a 3D user interface by assigning keys to specific functions or actions, such as using the arrow keys to move a 3D avatar or to switch between different 3D views.
  - 2D mice and trackballs: 2D mice and trackballs are 2D input devices that allow the user to move a cursor on a 2D surface by moving a physical device. 2D mice and trackballs can be used to control a 3D user interface by mapping the cursor movement to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a mouse to select and drag a 3D object or to pan and zoom a 3D scene.
  - Pen- and touch-based tablets: Pen- and touch-based tablets are 2D input devices that allow the user to draw or tap on a 2D surface by using a stylus or a finger. Pen- and touch-based tablets can be used to control a 3D user interface by mapping the drawing or tapping to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a pen to sketch a 3D shape or to rotate a 3D object or using a finger to swipe and pinch a 3D scene.
  - Joysticks: Joysticks are 3D input devices that allow the user to control a 3D user interface by moving a stick in different directions. Joysticks can be used to control a 3D user interface by mapping the stick movement to a 3D virtual location or orientation, such as using a joystick to fly a 3D airplane or to look around a 3D environment.
  - Desktop 6-DOF input devices: Desktop 6-DOF input devices are 3D input devices that allow the user to control a 3D user interface by moving and rotating a physical device in six degrees of freedom (DOF), which are the three translational DOF (x, y, z) and the three rotational DOF (pitch, yaw, roll). Desktop 6-DOF input devices can be used to control a 3D user interface by directly manipulating the 3D objects or the 3D viewpoint, such as using a 6-DOF device to grab and move a 3D object or to change the perspective of a 3D scene. Some examples of desktop 6-DOF input devices are the SpaceMouse, the Polhemus sensors, and the 3Dconnexion devices.



### Tracking Devices for 3D User Interface Input Hardware

- Tracking devices are hardware that measure the position, orientation, and/or motion of a sensor or a user in 3D space.
- Tracking devices enable 3D interaction by providing input to the system based on the user's spatial actions.
- Tracking devices can be classified into different types based on the technology they use, such as magnetic, mechanical, acoustic, inertial, optical, or hybrid .
- Tracking devices can also be categorized based on the body part they track, such as head, eye, hand, or full body .
- Tracking devices can be integrated into different forms of input devices, such as 3D mice, data gloves, handheld devices, or wearable devices .
- Tracking devices can also use direct human input, such as gestures, facial expressions, or brain signals, to obtain data from the user's body.
- Tracking devices have various applications in 3D user interfaces, such as navigation, manipulation, selection, or system control .
- Tracking devices have various challenges and limitations, such as accuracy, latency, occlusion, interference, calibration, or cost .

### Bounding Volume

- A bounding volume is a simplified shape that encloses a more complex object or a set of objects in a virtual scene.
- The purpose of a bounding volume is to speed up collision detection and other geometric operations by performing them on the simpler shape first, and then refining the results if necessary.
- Bounding volumes are usually chosen to be convex shapes, such as spheres, boxes, cylinders, or convex hulls, because they have simpler and faster intersection tests than concave shapes.
- Bounding volumes can be hierarchical, meaning that a large bounding volume can contain smaller bounding volumes that enclose subsets of the objects. This can further improve the efficiency of collision detection and other operations by culling out large portions of the scene that are not relevant.
- Bounding volumes can also be dynamic, meaning that they can change their shape and size according to the movement and deformation of the objects they enclose. This can make them more accurate and adaptable, but also more costly to update and maintain.

### Cullers and Occluders

- Cullers and occluders are techniques for improving the performance and realism of augmented and virtual reality applications by eliminating unnecessary rendering of objects or parts of objects that are not visible to the user.
- Culling is the process of discarding objects or polygons that are outside the view frustum, which is the region of space defined by the camera's position, orientation, and field of view. Culling can reduce the number of draw calls and the amount of geometry that needs to be processed by the graphics pipeline.
- Occlusion is the phenomenon of objects being hidden or partially hidden by other objects in the scene. Occlusion culling is the process of discarding objects or polygons that are occluded by other objects, such as walls, furniture, or people. Occlusion culling can further reduce the number of draw calls and the amount of geometry that needs to be processed by the graphics pipeline.
- Cullers and occluders can be classified into two categories: static and dynamic. Static cullers and occluders are based on precomputed information about the scene, such as spatial partitioning, visibility maps, or occlusion maps. Dynamic cullers and occluders are based on runtime information about the scene, such as depth buffers, occlusion queries, or depth sensors.
- Static cullers and occluders are more suitable for virtual reality applications, where the scene is mostly fixed and known in advance. Dynamic cullers and occluders are more suitable for augmented reality applications, where the scene is dynamic and unknown, and the virtual objects need to interact with the real environment.
- Cullers and occluders can improve the performance and realism of augmented and virtual reality applications by reducing the rendering workload and enhancing the visual coherence between the virtual and real worlds. However, cullers and occluders also introduce some challenges and limitations, such as accuracy, latency, complexity, and compatibility.

- 3D interaction techniques are methods for interacting with 3D environments and objects using 3D input devices and modalities.

### 3D Manipulation tasks

- 3D manipulation tasks are the actions that users perform on 3D objects in a virtual or augmented environment, such as moving, rotating, scaling, or deforming them.
- 3D manipulation tasks can be classified into two categories: direct and indirect manipulation.
  - Direct manipulation is when the user interacts with the 3D object directly, using a natural or physical input device, such as a hand, a finger, a stylus, or a glove. The user can feel a sense of presence and immersion in the virtual or augmented environment, as well as a high degree of control and feedback.
  - Indirect manipulation is when the user interacts with the 3D object indirectly, using a symbolic or abstract input device, such as a mouse, a keyboard, a joystick, or a menu. The user can perform complex and precise operations on the 3D object, but may lose some sense of presence and immersion in the virtual or augmented environment, as well as some degree of control and feedback.
- 3D manipulation tasks can also be classified into two types: transformation and deformation.
  - Transformation is when the user changes the position, orientation, or size of the 3D object, without altering its shape or topology. Transformation can be further divided into three subtypes: translation, rotation, and scaling.
    - Translation is when the user moves the 3D object along a certain direction or axis, or to a certain location or point.
    - Rotation is when the user rotates the 3D object around a certain axis or point, or to a certain angle or orientation.
    - Scaling is when the user changes the size of the 3D object uniformly or non-uniformly, or to a certain ratio or dimension.
  - Deformation is when the user changes the shape or topology of the 3D object, without altering its position, orientation, or size. Deformation can be further divided into two subtypes: free-form and constrained deformation.
    - Free-form deformation is when the user modifies the shape of the 3D object arbitrarily, using a variety of tools or techniques, such as sculpting, painting, or sketching.
    - Constrained deformation is when the user modifies the shape of the 3D object according to some predefined rules or constraints, such as bending, twisting, or extruding.



### Manipulation Techniques and Input Devices for 3D Interaction

- Manipulation techniques are methods for controlling the position, orientation, size, shape, and other attributes of objects in 3D virtual environments.
- Input devices are hardware components that allow users to provide input to the system by making movements in physical 3D space or manipulating tools, sensors, or devices in 3D space.
- Some of the common manipulation techniques and input devices for 3D interaction are:

  - **Direct manipulation**: The user directly grabs and moves the object with a 3D input device, such as a 3D mouse, a glove, a wand, or a tracked hand. The input device is usually mapped to the object with a one-to-one correspondence, and the user can see a graphical representation of the device or the hand on the screen. This technique provides a natural and intuitive way of manipulating objects, but it may suffer from occlusion, reachability, and fatigue issues.  
  - **Indirect manipulation**: The user indirectly controls the object with a 2D input device, such as a mouse, a touchpad, or a touchscreen. The input device is usually mapped to a 2D cursor on the screen, and the user can perform gestures or commands to manipulate the object in 3D. This technique allows the user to manipulate objects that are far away or occluded, but it may require more cognitive effort and reduce the sense of presence.  
  - **Widget-based manipulation**: The user interacts with graphical widgets, such as handles, dials, sliders, or menus, that are attached to the object or the environment. The widgets provide visual feedback and affordances for manipulating the object along specific degrees of freedom (DOFs). The user can use either 2D or 3D input devices to manipulate the widgets. This technique simplifies the manipulation task by reducing the number of DOFs, but it may clutter the screen and limit the flexibility of the user.  
  - **Gesture-based manipulation**: The user performs gestures with the hand, the finger, or the whole body to manipulate the object. The gestures are recognized by the system using sensors, cameras, or vision algorithms. The gestures can be either symbolic, such as drawing a circle to rotate the object, or mimetic, such as moving the hand as if holding the object. This technique provides a natural and expressive way of manipulating objects, but it may suffer from recognition errors, ambiguity, and lack of feedback.  
  - **Voice-based manipulation**: The user speaks commands or keywords to manipulate the object. The voice input is recognized by the system using speech recognition algorithms. The voice commands can be either discrete, such as saying "rotate left" to rotate the object, or continuous, such as saying "rotate faster" to increase the rotation speed. This technique allows the user to manipulate objects without using the hands, but it may suffer from recognition errors, latency, and noise.  
  - **Multi-modal manipulation**: The user combines two or more input modalities, such as touch, movement, gesture, or voice, to manipulate the object. The input modalities can be either complementary, such as using touch and movement to control the position and orientation of the object, or redundant, such as using gesture and voice to perform the same action. This technique can enhance the performance and usability of the manipulation task by providing more options and feedback to the user, but it may also increase the complexity and cognitive load of the user.

### Design Guidelines - 3D Travel Tasks

- 3D travel tasks are tasks that require the user to move through a 3D virtual environment, such as exploring, navigating, or searching for something.
- 3D travel tasks can be challenging for users, especially if they are unfamiliar with the virtual environment or the interaction techniques.
- Some design guidelines for 3D travel tasks are:

  - Provide clear and consistent feedback to the user about their position, orientation, and movement in the virtual environment. For example, use visual cues, such as landmarks, trails, or compasses, to help the user orient themselves and avoid getting lost.
  - Provide multiple modes of travel that suit different situations and user preferences. For example, offer both discrete and continuous travel techniques, such as teleportation, flying, or walking, and allow the user to switch between them easily.
  - Avoid inducing motion sickness or cybersickness in the user. For example, minimize the discrepancy between the user's physical and virtual motion, avoid sudden or unexpected changes in speed or direction, and match the field of view and perspective of the virtual camera to the user's natural vision.
  - Support the user's sense of presence and immersion in the virtual environment. For example, use realistic and consistent graphics, sounds, and haptics, and allow the user to interact with the environment and other virtual agents in natural and intuitive ways.
  - Consider the trade-offs between realism and usability. For example, while realistic physics and collisions may enhance the user's sense of realism, they may also hinder the user's ability to perform the task efficiently or comfortably. Therefore, balance the level of realism with the task requirements and user expectations.

### Design Guidelines - Theoretical Foundations of Wayfinding

Wayfinding is the process of finding one's way in a physical or virtual environment. Wayfinding involves cognitive, perceptual, and behavioral aspects of navigation. Wayfinding is especially important for 3D interaction techniques, as users may need to explore complex and unfamiliar 3D spaces, such as virtual or augmented reality environments.

Some design guidelines for wayfinding are:

- Create an identity at each location, different from all others. This helps users to recognize where they are and distinguish different places. Identity can be created by using distinctive architectural features, colors, textures, lighting, sounds, or symbols.
- Use landmarks to provide orientation cues and memorable locations. Landmarks are salient objects or features that can be easily seen and recognized from different viewpoints. Landmarks can help users to orient themselves, estimate distances and directions, and recall routes .
- Provide consistent and clear signage and graphic communication. Signage and graphic communication are visual elements that convey information about the environment, such as names, directions, distances, or instructions. Signage and graphic communication should be legible, visible, concise, and consistent in style and placement .
- Provide multiple modalities of information. Information can be presented in different modalities, such as visual, auditory, haptic, or olfactory. Providing multiple modalities of information can enhance user's perception, memory, and attention, and can also accommodate different user preferences and abilities.
- Provide feedback and feedforward. Feedback is information that confirms user's actions and outcomes, such as sounds, vibrations, or animations. Feedforward is information that anticipates user's actions and outcomes, such as previews, hints, or guides. Feedback and feedforward can help users to understand the consequences of their actions and plan their next steps.
- Provide overview and detail views. Overview and detail views are different levels of abstraction and granularity of the environment. Overview views provide a global perspective of the environment, such as maps, aerial views, or miniatures. Detail views provide a local perspective of the environment, such as first-person views, close-ups, or zooms. Overview and detail views can help users to comprehend the structure and layout of the environment and switch between different levels of focus.

### Multimodal System Control Techniques

Multimodal system control techniques are methods of using different input channels, such as speech, gesture, eye gaze, or touch, to control the system or application settings in a 3D user interface. These techniques can enhance the user experience and performance by allowing more natural and flexible ways of interacting with the 3D environment. Some of the advantages of using multimodal system control techniques are:

- Decoupling: Using an input channel that differs from the main input channel used for interaction with the environment can decrease user cognitive load. For example, using speech to switch between different modes or tools while using hand gestures to manipulate objects in 3D space can reduce the need for complex menus or buttons.
- Error reduction and correction: The use of multiple input channels can be very effective when the input is ambiguous or noisy, especially with recognition-based input like speech or gestures. For example, using eye gaze to disambiguate speech commands or using speech to correct gesture errors can improve the accuracy and robustness of the system.
- Expressiveness and efficiency: The use of multiple input channels can enable more expressive and efficient ways of communicating with the system. For example, using speech and gesture together can convey more information than using either modality alone, such as specifying the location, size, shape, and color of an object in 3D space.

Some of the challenges of using multimodal system control techniques are:

- Integration and fusion: The system needs to integrate and fuse the input from different modalities in a coherent and consistent way. For example, the system needs to determine the temporal and spatial relationships between speech and gesture input, and resolve any conflicts or redundancies between them.
- Feedback and guidance: The system needs to provide appropriate feedback and guidance to the user about the available input channels and the system state. For example, the system needs to indicate the recognition status and results of the input, and provide cues and hints for using the input channels effectively.
- Adaptation and personalization: The system needs to adapt and personalize the input channels and the system behavior to the user's preferences, abilities, and context. For example, the system needs to adjust the sensitivity and thresholds of the input channels, and provide different levels of assistance and feedback based on the user's skill and experience.

- Selection techniques are methods of specifying one or more 3D objects as the target of an interaction, such as pointing, ray-casting, or gesture recognition .
- Manipulation techniques are methods of changing the position, orientation, size, shape, or appearance of 3D objects, such as direct manipulation, virtual hand, or 3D widgets .
- Navigation techniques are methods of moving the user's viewpoint or the entire virtual scene in 3D space, such as walking, flying, or teleporting .
- System control techniques are methods of accessing and modifying the system parameters, such as menus, buttons, or voice commands .
- 3D interaction techniques should follow some design guidelines to ensure usability, efficiency, and user satisfaction, such as :
  - Match the interaction technique to the task and the user's goals.
  - Provide feedback and feedforward to inform the user of the system state and the possible actions.
  - Minimize the cognitive and physical load on the user by reducing the number of steps, modes, and mappings required for an interaction.
  - Support natural and intuitive interaction by leveraging the user's prior knowledge and skills.
  - Avoid occlusion and clutter of the virtual objects and the real environment by using appropriate visual cues and depth perception techniques.
  - Ensure consistency and compatibility of the interaction techniques across different platforms and devices.
  - Evaluate the interaction techniques using appropriate metrics and methods, such as user studies, questionnaires, or performance measures.



### Beyond Text and Number Entry for 3D Interaction Techniques

- Text and number entry are common tasks in many applications, but they can be challenging in 3D environments, especially in virtual reality (VR), where traditional input devices such as keyboards and mice are not available or suitable.
- There are different approaches to enable text and number entry in 3D interaction techniques, such as:
  - Selection-based text entry: The user selects characters from a virtual keyboard using a pointer, a gaze, or a gesture. This method is simple and familiar, but it can be slow and tedious. 
  - Speech-based text entry: The user speaks the text or numbers and the system recognizes them using speech recognition. This method is natural and fast, but it can be inaccurate and affected by noise. 
  - Gesture-based text entry: The user performs gestures that correspond to characters or words, such as drawing in the air or using hand signs. This method is expressive and intuitive, but it can be ambiguous and tiring. 
- Beyond text and number entry, there are other types of input that can be useful for 3D interaction techniques, such as:
  - Menu selection: The user chooses an option from a list of commands or actions. There are different ways to present and select menus in 3D environments, such as radial menus, pie menus, or 3D widgets. 
  - Object manipulation: The user interacts with virtual objects by grabbing, moving, rotating, scaling, or throwing them. There are different techniques to enable object manipulation in 3D environments, such as ray-casting, go-go, or HOMER. 
  - Navigation: The user moves through the virtual environment by walking, flying, or teleporting. There are different techniques to enable navigation in 3D environments, such as steering, pointing, or world-in-miniature.

### Difference between AR and VR

- AR and VR are both technologies that use computer graphics to visually alter the user's perception of reality, but they have different goals and methods  .
- AR stands for **augmented reality**, which means adding digital elements to the real-world environment, such as images, sounds, or text, that enhance the user's experience of the physical surroundings   .
- VR stands for **virtual reality**, which means creating a fully immersive digital environment that replaces the user's current setting, such as a simulated world, game, or movie, that isolates the user from the physical reality   .
- AR and VR differ in the following aspects    :
  - **Devices**: VR requires a headset device that covers the user's eyes and ears, and sometimes other accessories such as gloves or controllers, to provide a realistic sensory feedback. AR does not require a headset device, but can be accessed through various platforms such as smartphones, tablets, glasses, or projectors, that overlay digital information on the real-world view.
  - **Interaction**: VR users move and interact in a completely fictional world that is controlled by the system, and have no contact with the real world. AR users can control their presence and interaction in the real world, and have access to both the physical and digital information.
  - **Degree of immersion**: VR provides a high degree of immersion, as the user is fully immersed in a virtual environment that blocks out any external stimuli. AR provides a low degree of immersion, as the user is still aware of and connected to the real environment that is augmented by digital elements.
  - **Degree of realism**: VR aims to create a realistic simulation of a virtual environment that mimics the physical laws and properties of the real world, such as gravity, lighting, and sound. AR aims to create a seamless integration of digital elements with the real environment that enhances the user's perception and understanding of the physical world, such as annotations, animations, or instructions.



### Challenges with AR

Augmented reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real world with digital information and media, such as 3D models, videos, sounds, and texts. AR has many potential applications in various domains, such as education, entertainment, health, tourism, and industry. However, AR also faces some challenges that limit its adoption and development. Some of the main challenges are:

- **Hardware quality**: AR requires high-performance devices that can process complex graphics, track the user's position and orientation, and display the augmented content seamlessly. However, most of the current AR devices are either too expensive, too bulky, or too low-quality to provide a satisfactory user experience. For example, AR headsets are not widely available for consumers, and they often suffer from issues such as limited field of view, low resolution, and short battery life. Mobile AR solutions, such as smartphones and tablets, are more accessible and affordable, but they also have limitations in terms of screen size, camera quality, and computational power .

- **Content availability**: AR depends on the quality and quantity of the content that can be overlaid on the real world. However, creating and delivering AR content is not easy, as it requires specialized skills, tools, and platforms. Moreover, AR content needs to be context-aware, meaning that it should adapt to the user's location, environment, and preferences. However, there is a lack of standards and guidelines for developing and distributing AR content, as well as a lack of content repositories and catalogs that can facilitate the discovery and access of AR content .

- **Awareness and education**: AR is still a relatively new and unfamiliar technology for many people, who may not understand its benefits, use cases, and potential. Therefore, there is a need for raising awareness and educating the public about AR, as well as providing training and support for the users and developers of AR applications. Additionally, there is a need for addressing the ethical, legal, and social implications of AR, such as privacy, security, safety, and accessibility issues, and establishing regulations and policies that can protect the rights and interests of the AR stakeholders .

- **Technical issues**: AR involves many technical challenges that affect the functionality and usability of the AR applications. Some of these challenges are:

  - **Tracking and registration**: AR requires accurate and robust tracking and registration of the user's position, orientation, and movements, as well as the alignment and synchronization of the virtual and real objects. However, tracking and registration can be affected by various factors, such as lighting conditions, occlusions, dynamic environments, and sensor errors.

  - **Rendering and visualization**: AR requires realistic and immersive rendering and visualization of the augmented content, as well as smooth and consistent interaction and feedback. However, rendering and visualization can be affected by various factors, such as latency, occlusion, illumination, and ocular effects.

  - **User interface and interaction**: AR requires intuitive and natural user interface and interaction methods that can enable the user to control and manipulate the augmented content, as well as to communicate and collaborate with other users and agents. However, user interface and interaction can be affected by various factors, such as user preferences, cognitive load, social norms, and cultural differences.



### AR systems and functionality

- AR systems are systems that combine real and virtual worlds, allow real-time interaction, and align virtual and real objects accurately.
- AR systems can be classified into three types based on the display technology: head-mounted displays (HMDs), handheld displays, and spatial displays.
- AR systems can also be classified into four types based on the tracking technology: marker-based, markerless, location-based, and projection-based.
- AR systems can be used for various purposes, such as education, entertainment, gaming, tourism, healthcare, engineering, and design   .
- AR systems can provide benefits such as enhanced learning, improved collaboration, increased engagement, reduced errors, and better visualization.
- AR systems can also pose challenges such as technical limitations, user acceptance, privacy, security, and ethical issues.



### Augmented reality methods

Augmented reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real-world environment by adding digital elements to it, such as images, sounds, videos, or 3D models. AR can be used for various purposes, such as entertainment, education, training, navigation, gaming, and marketing.

There are different methods of creating and displaying AR content, depending on the type of device, the type of AR software, and the type of interaction. Some of the common methods are:

- **Marker-based AR**: This method uses a special visual object, such as a QR code, a logo, or a symbol, as a marker to trigger the AR content. The device's camera scans the marker and the AR software calculates its position and orientation to overlay the digital content on top of it. For example, a marker-based AR app can show a 3D model of a furniture item when the user scans a catalog page with the marker.
- **Marker-less AR**: This method does not require any marker to initiate the AR content. Instead, it uses the device's sensors, such as GPS, accelerometer, gyroscope, and compass, to determine the location and orientation of the device and the user. The AR software then overlays the digital content based on the context and the environment. For example, a marker-less AR app can show directions, landmarks, or nearby places on the device's screen when the user points the camera at the surroundings.
- **Projection-based AR**: This method uses a projector to cast the digital content onto a physical surface, such as a wall, a table, or a floor. The projector can be embedded in the device or be a separate device. The AR software can also use sensors or cameras to detect the user's gestures or movements and interact with the projected content. For example, a projection-based AR app can show a virtual keyboard on a desk that the user can type on with their fingers.
- **Superimposition-based AR**: This method replaces or enhances a part of the real-world environment with the digital content. The device's camera captures the environment and the AR software recognizes the object or the area to be replaced or enhanced. The AR software then overlays the digital content on top of the original environment. For example, a superimposition-based AR app can show how a user would look with different hairstyles, clothes, or accessories.



### Visualization Techniques for Augmented Reality

- Augmented Reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real world environment with synthetic information, such as images, text, sound, or video.
- Visualization techniques for AR aim to integrate the virtual and real information in a coherent and effective way, taking into account the user's task, context, and preferences.
- Some of the challenges and goals of AR visualization are:
  - Data integration: how to align, register, and fuse the virtual and real data in a consistent and accurate manner.
  - Scene understanding: how to provide the user with a clear and intuitive perception of the spatial and semantic relationships between the virtual and real objects.
  - Visual clutter: how to avoid occlusion, confusion, and distraction caused by the overlapping and competing visual elements in the AR scene.
  - Visual feedback: how to provide the user with appropriate and timely cues and guidance for interacting with the AR system and the environment.
- Some of the common methods and patterns for AR visualization are:
  - Spatial augmentation: placing the virtual information in the same 3D space as the real environment, using techniques such as projection, overlay, or annotation.
  - Non-spatial augmentation: placing the virtual information in a separate 2D space, such as a head-up display, a handheld device, or a wearable device.
  - Hybrid augmentation: combining spatial and non-spatial augmentation, using techniques such as lenses, portals, or magic mirrors.
  - Adaptive augmentation: adjusting the appearance, position, or behavior of the virtual information according to the user's context, preferences, or actions, using techniques such as filtering, highlighting, or animation.



### Wireless Displays in Educational Augmented Reality Applications

- Wireless displays are output devices that can receive and display wirelessly streamed AR content in real time.
- Wireless displays can be stereoscopic head mounted displays (HMDs) or TFT display modules that have been adapted to act as wireless receivers.
- Wireless displays have advantages for educational AR applications, such as:
  - Enabling collaboration and interaction among multiple users who can share or access personalized views or context sensitive information .
  - Eliminating the problems of cables that can restrict movement, cause safety hazards, and limit the number of displays/users .
  - Enhancing the immersion and engagement of users in the AR environment.
- Two examples of educational AR applications that use wireless displays are:
  - The AR Geometry application, which allows students to explore and manipulate geometric shapes and concepts in 3D space using a wireless HMD.
  - The AR Solar System application, which allows students to learn about the planets and their orbits using a wireless TFT display module.
- Some teaching scenarios that can benefit from wireless displays are:
  - A teacher can use a wireless display to monitor and guide the students' progress and activities in the AR application.
  - A student can use a wireless display to present his or her work to the class or to collaborate with other students in a group project.
  - A student can use a wireless display to access additional information or feedback that is not visible to other users.
- The evaluation of wireless displays in educational AR applications has shown positive results, such as:
  - Improved usability and user satisfaction .
  - Increased learning outcomes and motivation .
  - Reduced cognitive load and mental effort.



### Mobile Projection Interfaces for Augmented Reality

- Mobile projection interfaces are a type of augmented reality (AR) interfaces that use a mobile device with a built-in or attached projector to display digital information on physical surfaces.
- Mobile projection interfaces can overcome some of the limitations of handheld display devices, such as small screen size, limited field of view, and occlusion issues.
- Mobile projection interfaces can also enable new forms of interaction and collaboration, such as projecting on objects, walls, floors, or other people, and using gestures, touch, or voice to control the projection.
- Mobile projection interfaces can be classified into different application classes, depending on how they adapt to the object and context of projection, and how they use the camera of the mobile device. Some examples of these classes are:

  - Object-adaptive applications: These applications use the camera to detect the shape, size, color, or texture of the object of projection, and adjust the projection accordingly. For example, projecting a virtual keyboard on a table, or projecting a game on a book cover.
  - Context-adaptive applications: These applications use the camera to sense the environment and the user's location, orientation, or movement, and change the projection accordingly. For example, projecting a map on the ground, or projecting a guide on a wall.
  - Camera-controlled applications: These applications use the camera as an input device to capture the user's gestures, touch, or voice, and use them to manipulate the projection. For example, projecting a menu on the palm, or projecting a drawing tool on the finger.

- Mobile projection interfaces pose some challenges and opportunities for design and development, such as:

  - Projector quality: The brightness, resolution, contrast, and focus of the projector affect the visibility and clarity of the projection, especially in outdoor or bright environments.
  - Projection surface: The shape, size, color, texture, and reflectivity of the surface affect the quality and stability of the projection, and may require calibration or correction techniques.
  - Interaction techniques: The projection may not support direct touch or feedback, and may require alternative methods of interaction, such as gestures, voice, or physical buttons.
  - User experience: The projection may create a sense of presence, immersion, or engagement, but may also cause privacy, security, or social issues, depending on the context and audience of the projection.



### Marker-less tracking for augmented reality

- Marker-less tracking is a technique that allows augmented reality (AR) applications to display virtual content on top of real-world scenes without the need for predefined markers or targets.
- Marker-less tracking can use different methods to estimate the pose (position and orientation) of the camera relative to the scene, such as feature-based, model-based, or learning-based approaches.
- Feature-based methods detect and describe distinctive points (features) in the scene and match them with a database of known features to estimate the camera pose. Examples of feature-based methods are ORB, SIFT, and SURF.
- Model-based methods use a 3D model of the scene or an object to estimate the camera pose by aligning the model with the image features. Examples of model-based methods are virtual visual servoing and edge-based tracking.
- Learning-based methods use machine learning techniques to classify or regress the camera pose from the image data. Examples of learning-based methods are neural networks, random forests, and support vector machines.
- Marker-less tracking has many advantages over marker-based tracking, such as:
  - It can work with any scene or object, without requiring prior knowledge or preparation.
  - It can handle occlusions, changes in lighting, and dynamic environments better than marker-based tracking.
  - It can provide more immersive and realistic AR experiences, as the virtual content can blend seamlessly with the real-world scene .
- Marker-less tracking also has some challenges and limitations, such as:
  - It requires more computational resources and processing power than marker-based tracking.
  - It may suffer from drift, noise, or inaccuracies in the camera pose estimation, especially in large or complex scenes.
  - It may depend on the availability and quality of features or models in the scene or object.



### Enhancing Interactivity in AR Environments

- Interactivity is the degree to which a user can manipulate and influence the virtual content in an AR environment.
- Interactivity is important for creating engaging and immersive AR experiences that can enhance the user's perception, cognition, and emotion.
- Interactivity can be enhanced by using various techniques, such as:
  - Handheld AR: using mobile devices as AR displays and input devices, allowing users to interact with virtual objects through touch, gestures, and motion.
  - Spatial AR: using projectors or other devices to overlay virtual content on physical surfaces, allowing users to interact with virtual objects through natural hand movements and physical props.
  - Voice AR: using speech recognition and synthesis to enable users to communicate with virtual agents or control virtual content through voice commands and natural language.
  - Eye AR: using eye tracking and gaze estimation to enable users to interact with virtual content through eye movements and blinks.
  - Brain AR: using brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) to enable users to interact with virtual content through brain signals and mental commands.
- Interactivity can also be enhanced by using AI techniques, such as:
  - Computer vision: using image processing and recognition to enable virtual content to respond to the user's environment, such as detecting faces, objects, gestures, and scenes.
  - Machine learning: using data analysis and modeling to enable virtual content to adapt to the user's preferences, behavior, and context, such as personalizing the content, providing feedback, and generating recommendations.
  - Natural language processing: using text and speech processing and understanding to enable virtual content to communicate with the user in natural language, such as answering questions, providing information, and engaging in dialogue.
- Interactivity can also be enhanced by considering the user's needs, expectations, and goals, such as:
  - Usability: ensuring that the AR interface is easy to use, learn, and remember, and that it provides clear and consistent feedback and guidance.
  - Affordance: ensuring that the AR interface provides cues and hints that indicate the possible actions and interactions that the user can perform.
  - Engagement: ensuring that the AR interface is interesting, enjoyable, and motivating, and that it provides challenges, rewards, and feedback that match the user's skill level.
  - Presence: ensuring that the AR interface creates a sense of being in the AR environment and being connected to the virtual content, and that it minimizes distractions and interruptions from the real world.



### Evaluating AR Systems

- Evaluating AR systems is the process of assessing the quality, usability, effectiveness, and user satisfaction of AR applications and technologies.
- Evaluating AR systems is important for identifying the strengths and weaknesses of AR solutions, comparing different AR approaches, and improving the design and development of AR systems.
- Evaluating AR systems is challenging because of the complexity and diversity of AR systems, the lack of established standards and guidelines, the difficulty of measuring the impact of AR on the real world, and the ethical and social issues involved in AR research.
- Evaluating AR systems can be done using different methods and techniques, depending on the goals, context, and resources of the evaluation.
- Evaluating AR systems can be classified into two main categories: user-based and non-user-based evaluation.
  - User-based evaluation involves testing the AR system with real or potential users, either in controlled laboratory settings or in naturalistic field settings.
  - User-based evaluation can measure various aspects of the AR system, such as usability, user performance, user experience, user acceptance, user preferences, and user behavior.
  - User-based evaluation can use various methods and tools, such as questionnaires, interviews, observations, video recordings, eye tracking, physiological measures, and logging.
  - User-based evaluation can face various challenges, such as recruiting and motivating users, designing realistic and meaningful tasks, ensuring ecological validity, minimizing bias and interference, and analyzing large and complex data.
  - Non-user-based evaluation involves testing the AR system without involving users, either by using objective metrics or by using expert judgments.
  - Non-user-based evaluation can measure various aspects of the AR system, such as functionality, reliability, accuracy, efficiency, compatibility, and scalability.
  - Non-user-based evaluation can use various methods and tools, such as benchmarks, simulations, models, algorithms, heuristics, and guidelines.
  - Non-user-based evaluation can face various challenges, such as defining appropriate criteria and metrics, ensuring reliability and validity, generalizing the results, and integrating the results with user-based evaluation.
