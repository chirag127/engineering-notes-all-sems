

## Unit 1 - VIRTUAL REALITY AND VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS

- Virtual reality (VR) is a computer-generated simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with in a seemingly real or physical way by a person using special equipment, such as a headset, gloves, or controllers.
- Virtual environments (VEs) are specific types of VR that aim to create a realistic and immersive representation of a real or imagined world, where the user can navigate, explore, and manipulate objects.
- VR and VEs have many applications in various domains, such as entertainment, education, training, health care, engineering, and art.
- VR and VEs are based on several technologies and concepts, such as computer graphics, human-computer interaction, perception, presence, and immersion.
- Computer graphics are the methods and techniques for creating and displaying images, animations, and effects on a computer screen or other devices.
- Human-computer interaction (HCI) is the study and design of how humans and computers communicate and interact with each other, using various input and output devices, software, and interfaces.
- Perception is the process of acquiring, interpreting, and organizing sensory information from the external world, such as vision, hearing, touch, smell, and taste.
- Presence is the subjective feeling of being in a virtual environment, as if it were the actual physical environment, and of being there with other virtual or real entities.
- Immersion is the degree to which a virtual environment can create a sense of reality and involvement for the user, by providing realistic sensory stimuli, interactivity, and feedback.



### The historical development of VR

- Virtual reality (VR) is the simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with in a seemingly real way by a person using special equipment, such as a headset and gloves.
- The concept of VR dates back to the 1800s, when stereoscopes were invented to create the illusion of depth in photographs by using twin mirrors to project a single image.
- In the 1950s, Morton Heilig, a filmmaker and inventor, developed the Sensorama, a multimedia device that stimulated the senses of sight, sound, smell, and touch, creating a form of immersive cinema.
- In 1968, Ivan Sutherland and Bob Sproull created the first VR head-mounted display (HMD) system, called the Sword of Damocles, which was connected to a computer and tracked the user's head movements.
- In the 1970s and 1980s, VR research and technology development was mainly driven by the US Department of Defense, the National Science Foundation, and NASA, who used VR for training, simulation, and visualization purposes.
- In 1987, Jaron Lanier, a computer scientist and entrepreneur, coined the term "virtual reality" and founded VPL Research, a company that developed a range of VR equipment, such as the DataGlove and the EyePhone .
- In the 1990s, VR became more accessible and popular, with the emergence of arcade games, home consoles, and movies that featured VR, such as The Lawnmower Man and The Matrix. However, the limitations of the technology, such as high cost, low quality, and health issues, prevented VR from becoming mainstream.
- In the 2000s and 2010s, VR experienced a resurgence, thanks to the advances in computer graphics, hardware, software, and internet connectivity. Companies like Google, Facebook, Sony, Samsung, and HTC launched new VR products and platforms, such as Google Cardboard, Oculus Rift, PlayStation VR, Gear VR, and HTC Vive, that offered improved performance, affordability, and user experience.
- In the 2020s, VR is expected to continue to evolve and expand, with applications in various domains, such as entertainment, education, health care, tourism, gaming, social media, and art. Some of the trends and challenges that VR faces include: increasing realism, interactivity, and immersion; enhancing haptic and olfactory feedback; reducing latency, motion sickness, and cost; and addressing ethical, legal, and social issues.

### Visual Displays for 3D User Interfaces

Visual displays are output devices that present visual information to the user in 3D user interfaces. They can vary in size, shape, resolution, field of view, field of regard, and degree of immersion. Visual displays can be classified into two main categories: fully-immersive and semi-immersive.

- Fully-immersive displays are devices that completely occlude the real world and provide a 360-degree view of the virtual environment. Examples of fully-immersive displays are head-mounted displays (HMDs), arm-mounted displays, and virtual retinal displays. These displays have the advantage of providing a high sense of presence and realism, but they also have some drawbacks, such as limited resolution, weight, cost, and motion sickness.

- Semi-immersive displays are devices that partially occlude the real world and provide a limited view of the virtual environment. Examples of semi-immersive displays are monitors, surround-screen displays, workbenches, hemispherical displays, and autostereoscopic displays. These displays have the advantage of being more affordable, comfortable, and compatible with existing hardware, but they also have some drawbacks, such as limited field of view, field of regard, and immersion.

Some of the common characteristics of visual displays that affect the quality and performance of 3D user interfaces are:

- Spatial resolution: the number of pixels per unit area on the display. Higher resolution means more details and sharpness, but also more computational cost and power consumption.

- Field of view (FOV): the angular extent of the visible area on the display. Higher FOV means more coverage and immersion, but also more distortion and peripheral vision.

- Field of regard (FOR): the angular extent of the possible viewing area on the display. Higher FOR means more flexibility and exploration, but also more head or body movement and tracking.

- Brightness: the intensity of light emitted by the display. Higher brightness means more visibility and contrast, but also more glare and eye strain.

- Color: the range and quality of colors displayed by the display. Higher color means more realism and aesthetics, but also more complexity and calibration.

- Gamma correction: the adjustment of the brightness and contrast of the display to match the human perception of light. Higher gamma correction means more natural and balanced colors, but also more processing and calibration.

### Haptic Displays

Haptic displays are interfaces that provide users with a sense of touch by stimulating somatic receptors in the skin or muscles. Haptic displays can enhance the realism and interactivity of virtual environments, as well as provide feedback for remote control of machines and devices.

There are two main types of haptic feedback: cutaneous and kinesthetic. Cutaneous feedback refers to the stimulation of the skin receptors, such as pressure, vibration, temperature, and pain. Kinesthetic feedback refers to the stimulation of the muscle receptors, such as force, position, and movement.

Haptic displays can be classified by the type of output they generate, and the type of receptors they stimulate. Some examples of haptic displays are:

- Force feedback devices: These devices apply forces to the user's hand or body, such as joysticks, gloves, exoskeletons, or haptic vests. They can render kinesthetic feedback based on computational models, remote interaction, recordings, or data-driven approaches.
- Tactile displays: These devices stimulate the skin receptors of the user, such as pins, vibrators, or thermal elements. They can render cutaneous feedback based on textures, shapes, or patterns.
- Holographic haptic displays: These devices use ultrasound waves to create a sensation of touch in mid-air, without the need for a physical device or interface. They can render both cutaneous and kinesthetic feedback based on virtual objects or gestures.

Haptic displays can be used for various applications, such as:

- Education and training: Haptic displays can provide realistic and immersive learning experiences, such as simulating surgical procedures, exploring molecular structures, or practicing musical instruments.
- Entertainment and gaming: Haptic displays can enhance the enjoyment and engagement of users, such as feeling the recoil of a gun, the impact of a punch, or the texture of a virtual object.
- Communication and social interaction: Haptic displays can convey emotions and expressions, such as hugging, shaking hands, or kissing.
- Accessibility and assistance: Haptic displays can assist users with visual or auditory impairments, such as reading Braille, navigating environments, or recognizing sounds.



### Choosing Output Devices for 3D User Interfaces

Output devices, also called display devices, allow the machine to provide information or feedback to one or more users through the human perceptual system. Most of them are focused on stimulating the visual, auditory, or haptic senses.

Some of the factors to consider when choosing output devices for 3D user interfaces are:

- The type and amount of information to be displayed. For example, if the information is complex, dynamic, or spatial, then a 3D visual display may be more suitable than a 2D one. If the information is multimodal, then a combination of visual, auditory, and haptic output may be more effective than a single modality.
- The level of immersion and presence desired. Immersion refers to the degree to which the output device creates a realistic and believable representation of the virtual environment. Presence refers to the degree to which the user feels present in the virtual environment. Generally, output devices that provide a high level of immersion and presence, such as head-mounted displays or spatial audio systems, may enhance the user's engagement and performance in 3D user interfaces.
- The cost and availability of the output devices. Some output devices, such as stereoscopic projection displays or whole-hand haptic devices, may be expensive, bulky, or difficult to obtain. Therefore, the budget and resources of the project may limit the choice of output devices.
- The compatibility and integration of the output devices. Some output devices may not be compatible with the input devices, the software platform, or the hardware configuration of the system. For example, some head-mounted displays may require a specific graphics card or operating system to work properly. Therefore, the output devices should be chosen based on their compatibility and integration with the rest of the system.
- The user preferences and expectations. Some output devices may be more comfortable, intuitive, or enjoyable for the user than others. For example, some users may prefer a large-screen display over a head-mounted display, or a haptic glove over a 3D mouse. Therefore, the output devices should be chosen based on the user preferences and expectations.



# Unit 3 - 3D User Interface Input Hardware

- 3D user interface input hardware are devices that capture and interpret the actions performed by the user in a 3D environment.
- The main characteristics of 3D user interface input hardware are:
  - Degrees of freedom (DOF): the number of independent ways that a body can move in space. For example, a 3D mouse can have six DOF: three for translation (x, y, z) and three for rotation (pitch, yaw, roll).
  - Input type/frequency of data: the kind and amount of data that the device generates. For example, discrete input devices produce single data values (e.g. Boolean values), while continuous input devices generate multiple data values (e.g. pixel coordinates).
  - Physical interaction: the amount and type of physical effort required by the user to operate the device. For example, some devices are passive (e.g. gloves), while others are active (e.g. joysticks).
- Some examples of 3D user interface input hardware are:
  - Keyboards: a set of keys that can be used to enter text, commands, or shortcuts. They are typically used in desktop 3D UIs, and can have different layouts and sizes.
  - 2D mice and trackballs: devices that can sense the movement of the user's hand on a surface or a ball. They are also used in desktop 3D UIs, and can have different buttons and scroll wheels.
  - Pen- and touch-based tablets: devices that can sense the position and pressure of the user's finger or stylus on a surface. They can be used for drawing, writing, or selecting objects in 3D UIs.
  - Joysticks: devices that can sense the direction and magnitude of the user's hand movement on a stick. They can be used for navigating, flying, or controlling objects in 3D UIs.
  - Gloves: devices that can sense the position and orientation of the user's hand and fingers. They can be used for gesturing, manipulating, or interacting with objects in 3D UIs.
  - Trackers: devices that can sense the position and orientation of the user's head, body, or limbs. They can be used for tracking the user's viewpoint, posture, or motion in 3D UIs.



### Input device characteristics for the notes of the Unit 3 - 3D USER INTERFACE INPUT HARDWARE in the subject of Augmented & Virtual Reality

- Input devices are hardware that allow the user to communicate with the system by providing input in 3D space  .
- Input devices can be classified according to the following characteristics :
  - Degrees of freedom (DOFs): the number and type of independent parameters that define the position and orientation of the device or the user in 3D space. For example, a 6-DOF device can report three translational and three rotational parameters. DOFs can be integral (combined in a single device) or separable (divided among multiple devices).
  - Type of electronics: digital (binary) or analog (continuous) devices that report discrete or continuous values, respectively. For example, a button is a digital device that reports on or off, while a joystick is an analog device that reports a range of values.
  - Range of reported values: discrete (fixed number of values), continuous (infinite number of values), or hybrid (combination of discrete and continuous values) devices that report different levels of granularity. For example, a mouse wheel is a discrete device that reports a fixed number of steps, while a mouse movement is a continuous device that reports a continuous range of values.
  - Sensing technology: active (emit signals) or passive (receive signals) devices that use different methods to track the position and orientation of the device or the user in 3D space. For example, a magnetic tracker is an active device that emits a magnetic field, while a camera is a passive device that receives light.
  - Input modality: the type of input that the device can capture, such as position, orientation, gesture, voice, eye gaze, etc. For example, a glove is a device that can capture hand gestures, while a microphone is a device that can capture voice commands.
  - Interaction technique: the way the device is used to perform actions or tasks in the system, such as selection, manipulation, navigation, etc. For example, a ray-casting technique is an interaction technique that uses a device to point and select objects in 3D space, while a virtual hand technique is an interaction technique that uses a device to grab and move objects in 3D space.
- Input devices can be traditional (used for 2D interfaces) or spatial (designed for 3D interfaces). Traditional input devices include keyboards, mice, touchscreens, etc. Spatial input devices include trackers, gloves, wands, haptic devices, etc  .
- Input devices can be used for complementary input, which means that they can provide additional information or feedback to the user, such as sound, vibration, force, etc. For example, a haptic device can provide force feedback to the user when manipulating objects in 3D space .
- Input devices can be special purpose, which means that they are designed for specific tasks or applications in 3D interfaces. For example, a steering wheel is a special purpose input device for driving simulations, while a VR headset is a special purpose input device for immersive VR experiences .

- Bounding volumes can be tight or loose, meaning that they can fit the shape of the objects as closely as possible or leave some empty space around them. Tight bounding volumes are more accurate but more expensive to compute and test, while loose bounding volumes are faster but less precise.
- Bounding volumes are important for augmented and virtual reality applications, as they can enhance the realism and interactivity of the virtual scenes by enabling realistic physics, occlusion, and visibility effects.

### Cullers and Occluders

- Cullers and occluders are techniques for improving the performance and realism of augmented and virtual reality applications by eliminating unnecessary rendering of objects or parts of objects that are not visible to the user.
- Culling is the process of discarding objects or polygons that are outside the view frustum, which is the region of space that is projected onto the screen by the camera. Culling can reduce the number of draw calls and the amount of geometry that needs to be processed by the graphics pipeline.
- Occlusion is the phenomenon of objects blocking the view of other objects behind them. Occlusion culling is the process of discarding objects or polygons that are occluded by other objects in the scene, such as walls, furniture, or the user's own body. Occlusion culling can further reduce the number of draw calls and the amount of geometry that needs to be processed by the graphics pipeline.
- Cullers and occluders can also enhance the realism and immersion of augmented and virtual reality applications by creating a more consistent and natural interaction between the real and virtual worlds. For example, occlusion culling can prevent virtual objects from appearing in front of real objects that should occlude them, such as a virtual character behind a real door. This can improve the sense of presence and spatial awareness of the user.
- Cullers and occluders can be implemented in different ways, depending on the type and complexity of the application, the available hardware and software resources, and the desired trade-off between accuracy and performance. Some of the common methods are:

  - Frustum culling: This is the simplest and most widely used form of culling, which discards objects or polygons that are outside the view frustum. This can be done by testing the bounding volumes of the objects, such as spheres, boxes, or cylinders, against the six planes of the view frustum, and rejecting those that are completely outside. Frustum culling can be done on the CPU or the GPU, and can be combined with other culling techniques for more efficiency.
  - Back-face culling: This is a form of culling that discards polygons that are facing away from the camera, and therefore are not visible to the user. This can be done by testing the dot product of the polygon normal and the camera direction, and rejecting those that are negative. Back-face culling can be done on the GPU, and can reduce the number of polygons that need to be rasterized and shaded.
  - Portal culling: This is a form of culling that discards objects or polygons that are outside the portals, which are openings or windows that connect different regions or cells of the scene. This can be done by testing the bounding volumes of the objects against the portal planes, and rejecting those that are completely outside. Portal culling can be done on the CPU or the GPU, and can be useful for indoor scenes with complex geometry and occlusion.
  - Occlusion query: This is a form of occlusion culling that uses the GPU to test the visibility of objects or polygons by rendering them into an occlusion buffer, which is a low-resolution depth buffer that stores the depth values of the pixels. This can be done by issuing occlusion queries for the bounding volumes of the objects, and retrieving the number of pixels that passed the depth test. If the number is zero, the object is occluded and can be discarded. Occlusion query can be done on the GPU, and can be useful for dynamic scenes with moving objects and occluders.
  - Depth buffer: This is a form of occlusion culling that uses the depth buffer, which is a buffer that stores the depth values of the pixels that are rendered on the screen. This can be done by testing the depth values of the pixels that belong to the objects or polygons against the depth buffer, and rejecting those that are behind. Depth buffer can be done on the GPU, and can be useful for static scenes with pre-computed depth maps or depth images.
  - Depth sensor: This is a form of occlusion culling that uses a depth sensor, which is a device that captures the depth values of the real scene. This can be done by testing the depth values of the pixels that belong to the virtual objects or polygons against the depth sensor data, and rejecting those that are behind. Depth sensor can be done on the CPU or the GPU, and can be useful for augmented reality applications with real occluders.

- **Selection techniques** allow users to specify one or more 3D objects as the target of an interaction. Manipulation techniques can also support different levels of feedback, such as visual, auditory, or haptic feedback.
- **System control techniques** allow users to access and modify the parameters and functions of the 3D UI system. They can be based on graphical user interface (GUI) elements (such as buttons or sliders), natural user interface (NUI) elements (such as speech or gaze), or hybrid user interface (HUI) elements (such as biometric or brain input). - Using multimodal interaction techniques that combine different input modalities (such as vision, speech, gesture, etc.) to enhance the expressiveness and robustness of the interaction.

- Non-immersive VR: The user views the virtual environment on a screen or a monitor, and interacts with it using a mouse, a keyboard, or a joystick. The user is aware of the real environment and can easily switch between the two. An example of non-immersive VR is a video game or a flight simulator.

- The display device: The device that presents the virtual environment to the user, such as a screen, a monitor, a projector, or a HMD.
- The tracking device: The device that tracks the user's position, orientation, and movement in the real and virtual environments, such as a camera, a sensor, or a GPS.
- The input device: The device that enables the user to interact with the virtual environment, such as a mouse, a keyboard, a joystick, a controller, a glove, or a microphone.
- The output device: The device that provides feedback to the user from the virtual environment, such as a speaker, a headphone, a vibrator, or a fan.

### Challenges with AR

Augmented reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real world with digital information and media, such as 3D models, videos, and sounds. AR has many potential applications in various domains, such as education, entertainment, health, and industry. However, AR also faces some challenges that limit its adoption and development. Some of the main challenges are:

- **Hardware quality**: AR requires high-performance devices that can process complex graphics, track the user's position and orientation, and display the augmented content seamlessly. However, most of the current AR devices are either too expensive, too bulky, or too low-quality to provide a satisfying user experience. For example, some AR headsets have a narrow field of view, low resolution, or poor battery life. Some AR glasses have limited functionality or compatibility with other devices. Some AR smartphones have low processing power or camera quality. These hardware limitations affect the usability, comfort, and immersion of AR applications .

- **Content availability**: AR depends on the quality and quantity of the content that is available for the users. However, creating and delivering AR content is not easy, as it requires specialized skills, tools, and platforms. AR content also needs to be context-aware, adaptive, and interactive, which adds more complexity to the design and development process. Moreover, AR content needs to be compatible with different devices, formats, and standards, which poses a challenge for interoperability and scalability. Without engaging and relevant content, AR cannot attract and retain the users' attention and interest  .

- **Awareness and education**: AR is still a relatively new and unfamiliar technology for many people, who may not know its benefits, applications, and potential. Therefore, AR needs to raise awareness and educate the users about its capabilities and limitations, as well as its ethical and social implications. AR also needs to provide clear and intuitive instructions and feedback to the users, to help them understand and use the technology effectively. Furthermore, AR needs to address some of the common misconceptions and fears that people may have about AR, such as privacy, security, and health issues  .

- **Technical issues**: AR involves many technical challenges that affect the functionality and reliability of the technology. Some of these issues are:

  - **Tracking and registration**: AR needs to accurately track the user's location, orientation, and movements, as well as the position and shape of the real objects and environment. AR also needs to align and overlay the digital content with the real world, without any noticeable lag or jitter. However, tracking and registration are difficult to achieve in dynamic and complex scenarios, such as outdoors, indoors with occlusions, or with multiple users. Tracking and registration errors can cause misalignment, distortion, or disappearance of the augmented content, which can reduce the realism and immersion of AR .

  - **Interaction and usability**: AR needs to provide natural and intuitive ways for the users to interact with the augmented content and the real world. However, most of the current AR devices have limited input and output modalities, such as touch, gesture, voice, or gaze. These modalities may not be suitable or convenient for some AR scenarios, such as when the user's hands are busy, when the user is in a noisy environment, or when the user wants to avoid social awkwardness. Moreover, AR devices may not have consistent or standardized user interfaces, which can cause confusion and frustration for the users .

  - **Performance and scalability**: AR needs to provide high-quality graphics, sound, and animation, as well as fast and smooth rendering, processing, and communication. However, AR devices have limited resources, such as memory, battery, and bandwidth, which can affect the performance and scalability of AR applications. For example, some AR applications may consume too much power, drain the battery quickly, or overheat the device. Some AR applications may require too much data, cause network congestion, or depend on unreliable connectivity. These performance and scalability issues can affect the quality and continuity of AR experiences .

### Augmented reality methods

Augmented reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real-world environment with computer-generated content, such as images, sounds, videos, or text. AR allows users to interact with both the physical and the digital elements in a seamless way. AR can be used for various purposes, such as entertainment, education, training, navigation, gaming, and marketing.

There are different methods to create and deliver AR experiences, depending on the type of device, the type of content, and the type of interaction. Some of the common methods are:

- **Marker-based AR**: This method uses a camera and a special visual object, such as a QR code, a logo, or a symbol, to trigger the AR content. The AR software recognizes the marker and calculates its position and orientation to overlay the digital content on top of it. For example, a marker-based AR app can show a 3D model of a furniture item when the user scans a catalog page with the camera.

- **Marker-less AR**: This method does not require any predefined markers to activate the AR content. Instead, it uses the device's sensors, such as GPS, accelerometer, gyroscope, and compass, to determine the location and orientation of the device and the user. The AR software then displays the digital content based on the user's context and environment. For example, a marker-less AR app can show directions, landmarks, or nearby places on the screen when the user points the camera at the street.

- **Projection-based AR**: This method uses a projector to cast the digital content onto a physical surface, such as a wall, a table, or a floor. The projector can be embedded in the device or mounted externally. The AR software can track the user's gestures and movements to enable interaction with the projected content. For example, a projection-based AR app can show a virtual keyboard on the desk that the user can type on.

- **Superimposition-based AR**: This method replaces or enhances a part or the whole of the real-world view with the digital content. The AR software can use object recognition or image processing techniques to identify the target object and replace it with the digital one. For example, a superimposition-based AR app can show how a user would look with different hairstyles, clothes, or accessories.

- **Display-based AR**: This method uses a display device, such as a monitor, a tablet, or a head-mounted display (HMD), to show the digital content in front of the user's eyes. The display device can be fixed or wearable, and can have different levels of transparency and field of view. The AR software can use eye tracking, head tracking, or hand tracking to enable interaction with the digital content. For example, a display-based AR app can show a virtual menu, a map, or a notification on the user's glasses.



### Visualization Techniques for Augmented Reality

- Augmented Reality (AR) is a technology that enhances the real world environment with synthetic information, such as images, text, sound, or video.
- Visualization techniques for AR aim to present the synthetic information in a way that is coherent, effective, and intuitive for the user, while avoiding occlusion, clutter, and distraction.
- Some of the challenges and goals of AR visualization techniques are  :
  - Data integration: how to align and register the synthetic information with the real world, and how to handle occlusion and depth perception issues.
  - Scene understanding: how to provide the user with relevant and meaningful information about the real world, and how to support interaction and manipulation of the synthetic information.
  - User experience: how to design the synthetic information to suit the user's needs, preferences, and context, and how to evaluate the usability and effectiveness of the AR visualization.
- Some of the common methods and patterns for AR visualization techniques are  :
  - Overlay: the simplest and most common method, where the synthetic information is directly superimposed on the real world, either as a flat image or a 3D model. This method can provide a high level of realism and immersion, but can also suffer from occlusion and clutter problems.
  - Annotation: a method where the synthetic information is attached to a specific point or object in the real world, either as a label, a pointer, or a symbol. This method can provide a clear and concise way of conveying information, but can also be affected by occlusion and clutter issues, and may require precise tracking and registration.
  - Spatialization: a method where the synthetic information is placed in a specific location or direction in the real world, either as a billboard, a halo, or a cone. This method can provide a natural and intuitive way of indicating the location and direction of information, but can also be influenced by occlusion and clutter issues, and may require accurate tracking and registration.
  - Abstraction: a method where the synthetic information is transformed or simplified to reduce the amount of detail or complexity, either as a silhouette, a contour, or a skeleton. This method can provide a way of highlighting the essential features or structure of the information, but can also reduce the realism and immersion, and may require additional explanation or interpretation.
  - Illustration: a method where the synthetic information is enhanced or embellished to increase the visual appeal or expressiveness, either as a cartoon, a sketch, or a painting. This method can provide a way of attracting the user's attention or conveying a certain mood or style, but can also affect the realism and immersion, and may introduce ambiguity or distortion.
  - Hybrid: a method where the synthetic information is combined or integrated with the real world in a seamless or coherent way, either as a cutaway, a x-ray, or a magic lens. This method can provide a way of revealing the hidden or occluded information, or providing multiple perspectives or layers of information, but can also introduce complexity and inconsistency, and may require sophisticated rendering and interaction techniques.

# Mobile Projection Interfaces for Augmented Reality

- Mobile projection interfaces are devices that combine a camera and a projector to create interactive and immersive augmented reality (AR) applications.
- Mobile projection interfaces can be classified into three types, based on how they adapt to the object and the context of the projection :
  - Object-adaptive applications: These applications project information or graphics that are relevant to the object that is being captured by the camera. For example, projecting a recipe on a kitchen counter, or projecting a map on a wall.
  - Context-adaptive applications: These applications project information or graphics that are relevant to the environment or the situation of the user. For example, projecting a navigation guide on the road, or projecting a game on a table.
  - Camera-controlled applications: These applications use the camera as an input device to control the projection or interact with the projected content. For example, using gestures or face recognition to manipulate the projection, or using the camera as a pointer or a scanner.
- Mobile projection interfaces have several advantages over other types of AR displays, such as head-mounted displays or handheld displays  :
  - They can create large-scale and high-resolution AR experiences, without being limited by the size or the resolution of the screen.
  - They can project on any surface, without requiring special markers or trackers, and adapt to the shape and the texture of the surface.
  - They can support collaborative and social AR applications, by allowing multiple users to share and interact with the same projection.
  - They can enhance the user's sense of presence and immersion, by creating a seamless and natural integration between the physical and the virtual world.
- Mobile projection interfaces also have some challenges and limitations, such as :
  - The need for sufficient ambient light and contrast to ensure the visibility and the quality of the projection.
  - The need for accurate and robust calibration and alignment between the camera and the projector, to avoid distortion and misalignment of the projection.
  - The need for efficient and reliable power supply and cooling system, to avoid overheating and battery drain of the device.
  - The need for user-friendly and intuitive interaction techniques, to avoid occlusion and interference of the projection by the user's hands or body.

### Evaluating AR Systems

Augmented Reality (AR) systems are systems that combine real and virtual elements in a user's perception, enhancing the user's interaction with the real world. AR systems can be used for various purposes, such as education, entertainment, navigation, maintenance, and medical applications. However, designing and developing effective AR systems is not a trivial task, as it involves many challenges and trade-offs, such as:

- Choosing the appropriate hardware and software components for the AR system, such as display devices, tracking devices, sensors, and rendering engines.
- Designing the AR interface and interaction techniques that suit the user's needs, preferences, and context, such as the type of information, the level of realism, the degree of immersion, and the mode of input and output.
- Evaluating the AR system's usability, performance, and user experience, such as the accuracy, reliability, responsiveness, and satisfaction of the AR system.

Therefore, evaluating AR systems is an essential step in the AR development process, as it can provide valuable feedback and insights for improving the AR system's design and functionality. Evaluating AR systems can be done in different ways, depending on the goals, methods, and criteria of the evaluation. Some of the common types of evaluation for AR systems are:

- **User-based evaluation**: This type of evaluation involves testing the AR system with real users, either in a controlled laboratory setting or in a naturalistic field setting. User-based evaluation can measure various aspects of the AR system, such as its usability, effectiveness, efficiency, learnability, memorability, and user satisfaction. User-based evaluation can also collect qualitative data from the users, such as their opinions, preferences, feelings, and suggestions. User-based evaluation can use various methods and tools, such as questionnaires, interviews, observations, video recordings, eye tracking, physiological measurements, and logging.
- **Non-user-based evaluation**: This type of evaluation does not involve testing the AR system with real users, but rather uses other means to assess the AR system's quality and performance. Non-user-based evaluation can measure various aspects of the AR system, such as its accuracy, reliability, robustness, scalability, and compatibility. Non-user-based evaluation can also compare the AR system with other systems or benchmarks, such as existing AR systems, conventional systems, or theoretical models. Non-user-based evaluation can use various methods and tools, such as simulations, experiments, analysis, and metrics.
- **Perception and cognition evaluation**: This type of evaluation focuses on studying how the AR system affects the user's perception and cognition, such as their attention, awareness, memory, learning, and decision making. Perception and cognition evaluation can also investigate how the AR system influences the user's behavior and performance, such as their task completion, error rate, and reaction time. Perception and cognition evaluation can use various methods and tools, such as psychophysical experiments, cognitive tests, and brain imaging.
- **User experience evaluation**: This type of evaluation focuses on studying how the AR system affects the user's experience, such as their emotions, attitudes, motivations, and engagement. User experience evaluation can also explore how the AR system creates a sense of presence, immersion, and realism for the user. User experience evaluation can use various methods and tools, such as self-reports, interviews, observations, video recordings, and physiological measurements.

Evaluating AR systems is a complex and multidisciplinary process, as it requires considering various factors and perspectives, such as the user, the task, the environment, the technology, and the application domain. Evaluating AR systems can also be challenging and costly, as it may require special equipment, expertise, and resources. Therefore, evaluating AR systems should be planned and conducted carefully, following the best practices and guidelines in the AR research field. Evaluating AR systems can also be an iterative and collaborative process, as it can involve multiple stakeholders, such as developers, designers, researchers, and users. Evaluating AR systems can ultimately help to improve the quality and impact of the AR system, as well as to advance the knowledge and understanding of the AR field.
