Virtual reality (VR) is a computer-generated simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with in a seemingly real or physical way by a person using special electronic equipment, such as a helmet with a screen inside or gloves fitted with sensors.

Virtual environments (VEs) are computer-generated, interactive, three-dimensional environments that simulate physical environments or create imaginary worlds.

Some key points to consider when studying virtual reality and virtual environments are:

1. VR technology has advanced significantly in recent years, with the development of more sophisticated hardware and software.
2. VR can be used for a wide range of applications, including gaming, education, training, and therapy.
3. The use of VR can have both positive and negative effects on users, and it is important to consider the potential psychological and physiological impacts of prolonged use.
4. The design of virtual environments is a complex process that involves the consideration of many factors, including user experience, accessibility, and realism.
5. There are many challenges associated with the development and use of VR and VEs, including technical limitations, cost, and the need for specialized equipment and training.




### The historical development of VR

1. The development of virtual reality (VR) technology can be traced back to the 1950s .
2. The first VR head-mounted display (HMD) system, The Sword of Damocles, was invented in 1968 by computer scientist Ivan Sutherland and his student Bob Sproull .
3. Morton Heilig is considered one of the pioneers of VR, having created the first immersive cinema in 1962 .
4. The term “virtual reality” was popularized by Jaron Lanier in the 1980s .
5. The role of the federal government, particularly the Department of Defense and the National Science Foundation, was significant in the early research and development of VR technology in the United States .
6. VPL Research developed a range of VR equipment in the 1980s .
7. Between 1986-1989, Furness developed a flight simulator known as the Super Cockpit, which featured computer-generated 3D maps, advanced infrared and radar imagery, and real-time audio and visual feedback for the pilot .

### Real-time computer graphics

Real-time computer graphics are graphics that are generated and displayed in real-time, typically at a rate of 30 or 60 frames per second. These graphics are used in a variety of applications, including video games, simulations, and virtual reality environments.

1. **Rendering**: Real-time rendering is the process of generating an image from a model by means of computer programs. This involves the use of various algorithms and techniques to create realistic lighting, shading, and other visual effects.

2. **Hardware**: Real-time graphics require specialized hardware, such as graphics processing units (GPUs), to perform the complex calculations required for rendering in real-time.

3. **Software**: Real-time graphics also require specialized software, such as game engines, to manage the various components of the graphics pipeline, including the rendering, physics, and audio systems.

4. **Performance**: Real-time graphics must be able to maintain a consistent frame rate in order to provide a smooth and responsive user experience. This requires careful optimization of the graphics pipeline, as well as the use of techniques such as level of detail (LOD) and occlusion culling to reduce the computational load.

5. **Interactivity**: Real-time graphics are often used in interactive applications, where the user can interact with the virtual environment in real-time. This requires the use of input devices, such as keyboards, mice, and game controllers, as well as the ability to process user input and update the graphics in real-time.

6. **Virtual Reality**: Real-time graphics are a key component of virtual reality systems, which use head-mounted displays to immerse the user in a virtual environment. These systems require high-performance graphics hardware and software to generate realistic and responsive graphics in real-time.

### Haptic Displays

Haptic displays are interfaces that deliver haptic feedback, typically by stimulating somatic receptors to generate a sensation of touch. The rendered feedback is generally categorized into cutaneous as well as kinesthetic or proprioceptive feedback, respectively .

Haptic display represents a promising interface for human–computer interaction tools that provides users with nuanced information in a virtual environment. Humans utilize tactile and haptic information to determine object properties using thousands of mechanoreceptors located in the hands .

Haptic displays can be categorized by the type of stimuli/output they generate, and correspondingly by the type of sensory receptors that are stimulated. Kinesthetic display is concerned with the rendering of forces. The latter are generated based on computational models, remote interaction, recordings or data-driven approaches .

Due to the amount of information required to adequately represent surface and object characteristics, haptic displays (particularly force displays) require a more complete model of the world than worlds displayed only visually and/or aurally .

Holographic haptic displays use phased arrays of ultrasound emitters to focus ultrasound in the air, allowing users to touch, feel and manipulate three-dimensional virtual objects in mid-air using their bare hands, without the need for a physical device or interface .

### Input Device Characteristics

Input devices are hardware components that allow users to interact with a computer or other digital device by providing input or control signals. In the context of 3D user interfaces and virtual and augmented reality, input devices play a crucial role in enabling users to interact with and manipulate virtual objects and environments.

Some characteristics of input devices that are important for 3D user interfaces and virtual and augmented reality include:

1. **Degrees of freedom:** The number of independent movements or actions that an input device can detect and transmit. For example, a 3D mouse may have six degrees of freedom, allowing it to detect movement along the X, Y, and Z axes, as well as rotation around those axes.

2. **Resolution:** The smallest detectable change in position or movement that an input device can sense. Higher resolution input devices can detect finer movements and provide more precise control.

3. **Accuracy:** The degree to which an input device's measurements correspond to the true values of the physical quantities being measured. Accurate input devices provide reliable and consistent measurements.

4. **Responsiveness:** The speed with which an input device can detect and transmit changes in position or movement. Responsive input devices provide real-time feedback and enable smooth and natural interactions.

5. **Ergonomics:** The design of an input device with respect to the comfort and ease of use for the user. Ergonomic input devices are designed to minimize strain and fatigue and to maximize efficiency and productivity.

6. **Compatibility:** The ability of an input device to work with a particular computer or operating system. Compatible input devices can be easily integrated into existing systems and do not require additional software or hardware.

These are some of the key characteristics of input devices that are important for 3D user interfaces and virtual and augmented reality. Understanding these characteristics can help in the selection and design of input devices for these applications.

### Cullers and Occluders

Cullers and occluders are techniques used in computer graphics to improve the performance of rendering 3D scenes. They are commonly used in Augmented and Virtual Reality applications.

#### Cullers
- Cullers are algorithms that determine which objects in a 3D scene are visible to the camera and which are not.
- By identifying and discarding objects that are not visible, cullers can reduce the number of objects that need to be rendered, thus improving the performance of the rendering process.
- There are several types of culling algorithms, including frustum culling, occlusion culling, and backface culling.

#### Occluders
- Occluders are objects in a 3D scene that can block the view of other objects.
- By identifying and using occluders, the rendering engine can avoid rendering objects that are hidden behind occluders, thus improving performance.
- Occlusion culling is a technique that uses occluders to determine which objects are visible and which are not.

In summary, cullers and occluders are techniques used to improve the performance of rendering 3D scenes by reducing the number of objects that need to be rendered. They are commonly used in Augmented and Virtual Reality applications to provide a smooth and responsive user experience.

### Unit 8 - Augmented and Mixed Reality
#### Augmented Reality Methods

1. **Marker-based AR:** This method uses a camera and a visual marker to determine the center, orientation, and range of its spherical coordinate system. The marker is a simple pattern that the AR software can recognize, and the camera reads the marker and overlays a virtual image on top of it.

2. **Markerless AR:** This method uses a camera, accelerometer, and gyroscope to determine the position and orientation of the device. The AR software uses this information to overlay virtual images on the real world. This method is also known as location-based or position-based AR.

3. **Projection-based AR:** This method projects artificial light onto real-world surfaces. The projections can interact with the real world, for example, by changing the appearance of the surface or by creating the illusion of three-dimensional objects.

4. **Superimposition-based AR:** This method replaces the original view of an object with an augmented view of the same object. The AR software uses object recognition to determine the object and then overlays the augmented view on top of it.

5. **Mixed Reality:** This method combines real and virtual worlds to produce new environments and visualizations where physical and digital objects co-exist and interact in real-time.

### Marker-less Tracking for Augmented Reality

Marker-less tracking is a technique used in augmented reality (AR) applications to map environments and display AR content without the need for target images or objects (markers). This technique is also known as SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) marker-less AR tracking.

There are several algorithms and classifiers used for marker-less tracking in AR. For example, the ORB algorithm is used for feature detection and the FREAK algorithm is used for feature description. The classifiers used for the tracking task include KNN, Random Forest, Extremely Randomized Trees, SVM, and Bayes classifier.

Visual marker-less pose trackers mainly rely on natural feature points (often also called interest points or key points) visible in the user’s environment. To allow for an accurate pose determination, such natural feature points must meet several requirements, including fast computational time.

In summary, marker-less tracking is a technique used in AR applications to map environments and display AR content without the need for markers. Several algorithms and classifiers are used for this task, and natural feature points play a key role in the accuracy of the pose determination.

### Evaluating AR Systems

When evaluating Augmented Reality (AR) systems, there are several factors to consider. These include:

1. **Accuracy**: The accuracy of the AR system refers to how well the virtual objects are aligned with the real world. This is important for creating a seamless and immersive experience for the user.

2. **Latency**: Latency refers to the delay between the user's actions and the corresponding response from the AR system. Low latency is important for creating a responsive and interactive experience.

3. **Field of View**: The field of view refers to the extent of the real world that can be seen through the AR device. A larger field of view allows for a more immersive experience.

4. **Resolution**: The resolution of the AR system refers to the level of detail that can be displayed. Higher resolution allows for more detailed and realistic virtual objects.

5. **Portability**: Portability refers to how easy it is to use the AR system in different locations. A portable AR system allows for greater flexibility in its use.

6. **Ease of Use**: The ease of use of the AR system refers to how easy it is for the user to interact with the virtual objects. This is important for creating an intuitive and user-friendly experience.

7. **Cost**: The cost of the AR system is an important factor to consider, as it can impact the accessibility and adoption of the technology.

These are some of the key factors to consider when evaluating AR systems for use in Augmented and Mixed Reality applications. By considering these factors, one can select an AR system that best meets the needs of the user and the application.
