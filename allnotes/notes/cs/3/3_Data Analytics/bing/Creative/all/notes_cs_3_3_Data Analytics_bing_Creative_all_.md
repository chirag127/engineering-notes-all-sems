

## Unit 1 - Introduction to Data Analytics

- Data analytics is the process of collecting, organizing, analyzing, and interpreting data to gain insights and support decision making.
- Data analytics can be applied to various domains, such as business, science, engineering, health, education, and social sciences.
- Data analytics can be classified into four types, depending on the purpose and complexity of the analysis:
  - Descriptive analytics: summarizes what has happened in the past using historical data, such as charts, tables, and statistics.
  - Diagnostic analytics: explains why something has happened in the past using historical data, such as correlation, regression, and root cause analysis.
  - Predictive analytics: forecasts what is likely to happen in the future using historical and current data, such as machine learning, simulation, and scenario analysis.
  - Prescriptive analytics: recommends what should be done in the future using historical, current, and future data, such as optimization, decision analysis, and recommendation systems.
- Data analytics involves various steps, such as data collection, data preparation, data analysis, data visualization, and data communication.
  - Data collection: the process of acquiring data from different sources, such as databases, files, sensors, surveys, and web scraping.
  - Data preparation: the process of cleaning, transforming, and integrating data to make it suitable for analysis, such as handling missing values, outliers, duplicates, and inconsistencies.
  - Data analysis: the process of applying statistical and computational methods to data to discover patterns, trends, and relationships, such as descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing, clustering, and classification.
  - Data visualization: the process of presenting data in graphical or interactive forms to make it easier to understand and explore, such as charts, maps, dashboards, and infographics.
  - Data communication: the process of conveying the results and insights of data analysis to the intended audience, such as reports, presentations, and stories.
- Data analytics requires various skills, tools, and techniques, such as:
  - Data literacy: the ability to read, understand, and communicate data effectively.
  - Data ethics: the principles and practices of responsible and ethical use of data, such as privacy, security, and fairness.
  - Data management: the techniques and systems for storing, organizing, and accessing data, such as relational databases, data warehouses, and data lakes.
  - Data processing: the methods and frameworks for processing large and complex data, such as cloud computing, big data, and distributed computing.
  - Data programming: the languages and libraries for manipulating and analyzing data, such as Python, R, SQL, and pandas.
  - Data modeling: the approaches and algorithms for building and evaluating data-driven models, such as regression, classification, clustering, and neural networks.
  - Data visualization: the tools and principles for creating effective and engaging data visualizations, such as Tableau, Power BI, matplotlib, and ggplot2.
  - Data storytelling: the techniques and strategies for crafting and delivering data stories, such as narrative, context, and audience.



# Sources and nature of data

- Data is any type of information that can be measured, accessed or used in any way in a given study.
- Data can be used to answer certain research questions, test hypotheses, or draw conclusions.
- Data can be classified into two types: statistical and non-statistical .
  - Statistical data refers to data that is gathered for some official purposes, such as censuses, surveys, or experiments .
  - Non-statistical data refers to data that is collected for other administrative purposes or for the private sector, such as records, reports, or documents .
- Data can also be classified into two sources: internal and external.
  - Internal data refers to data that is generated within the organization or the research team, such as sales data, customer data, or employee data.
  - External data refers to data that is obtained from outside the organization or the research team, such as public data, market data, or academic data.
- The nature of data can be described by various characteristics, such as:
  - Quantitative or qualitative: Quantitative data is numerical and can be measured or counted, such as age, height, or income. Qualitative data is non-numerical and can be observed or described, such as color, taste, or opinion.
  - Discrete or continuous: Discrete data is finite and can only take certain values, such as number of children, gender, or marital status. Continuous data is infinite and can take any value, such as weight, temperature, or time.
  - Nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio: Nominal data is categorical and can only be named, such as religion, nationality, or hair color. Ordinal data is categorical and can be ranked or ordered, such as education level, satisfaction level, or pain level. Interval data is numerical and can be measured by equal intervals, such as temperature, IQ, or year. Ratio data is numerical and can be measured by a true zero point, such as height, weight, or income.
  - Primary or secondary: Primary data is original and collected directly by the researcher or the research team, such as interviews, observations, or experiments. Secondary data is derived and collected indirectly by someone else, such as books, journals, or websites.



# Classification of Data

Classification of data is the process of organizing data by relevant categories so that it may be used and protected more efficiently. Data classification can be done according to various criteria, such as:

- **Data type**: Data can be classified into different types based on the nature and format of the data, such as numerical, categorical, textual, image, audio, video, etc. For example, the data type of age is numerical, while the data type of gender is categorical.
- **Data source**: Data can be classified into different sources based on the origin and collection method of the data, such as primary, secondary, tertiary, internal, external, etc. For example, the data source of a survey is primary, while the data source of a census is secondary.
- **Data quality**: Data can be classified into different quality levels based on the accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, and validity of the data, such as high, medium, low, etc. For example, the data quality of a verified transaction is high, while the data quality of a missing value is low.
- **Data sensitivity**: Data can be classified into different sensitivity levels based on the potential impact of the data disclosure, misuse, or loss, such as public, private, internal, confidential, secret, top secret, etc. For example, the data sensitivity of a phone number is private, while the data sensitivity of a nuclear code is top secret .
- **Data state**: Data can be classified into different states based on the location and movement of the data, such as at rest, in process, or in transit. For example, the data state of a file stored on a hard drive is at rest, while the data state of a file sent over a network is in transit.

Data classification is important for data analytics because it helps to:

- Identify the most relevant and useful data for a specific purpose or problem
- Enhance the data quality and integrity by detecting and correcting errors, outliers, and anomalies
- Optimize the data storage and processing by choosing the appropriate format, structure, and compression
- Protect the data security and privacy by applying the appropriate encryption, access control, and audit policies
- Comply with the data regulations and standards by following the appropriate guidelines and procedures

  - Scalability: Semi-structured data can be stored and processed in distributed systems, such as cloud computing or big data platforms, which can handle large volumes and varieties of data .

# Characteristics of Data

Data is any form of information that has been gathered and organized in a meaningful format that can be processed further. Data can be used for various purposes, such as analysis, decision making, communication, etc. Data can be classified into different types, such as numerical, categorical, ordinal, etc. depending on the nature and level of measurement of the information.

To understand and evaluate data, it is important to consider its characteristics, such as:

- **Relevance**: This refers to how well the data matches the requirements and expectations of the user or the purpose of the analysis. Relevant data should be useful, meaningful, and appropriate for the context and the problem .
- **Consistency**: This refers to how reliable and stable the data is across different sources, systems, or time periods. Consistent data should have the same value and meaning whenever and wherever it is accessed or used .
- **Completeness**: This refers to how much of the data is available and how well it covers the scope and range of the analysis. Complete data should have no missing, incomplete, or redundant values or records.
- **Uniqueness**: This refers to how distinct and identifiable the data is from other data. Unique data should have no duplicates, overlaps, or conflicts with other data .

These characteristics of data can affect the quality, validity, and reliability of the data analysis and the results. Therefore, it is essential to assess and improve the data quality before performing any data analysis. Some of the methods to improve data quality include data cleaning, data validation, data transformation, data integration, etc.

# Evolution of analytic scalability

- Analytic scalability refers to the ability to handle large and complex data sets for analysis purposes.
- In the past, analytic scalability was limited by the separation of the data environment and the analytic environment, which required data to be extracted, transformed, and loaded (ETL) into a separate analytic platform.
- This process was time-consuming, costly, and prone to errors, and often resulted in data quality and consistency issues.
- Moreover, traditional analytic platforms, such as relational databases and data warehouses, were not designed to handle the volume, variety, and velocity of big data, which posed challenges for scalability, performance, and flexibility.
- To overcome these limitations, several paradigms have emerged to enable greater analytic scalability, such as:
  - The convergence of the data and analytic environments, which eliminates the need for ETL and allows analytics to be performed directly on the data sources, such as operational databases, data lakes, or streaming data .
  - Massively parallel processing (MPP) architectures, which distribute data and computation across multiple nodes or servers, and leverage parallel algorithms and techniques to speed up data processing and analysis  .
  - The cloud, which provides on-demand, scalable, and elastic computing resources and services, such as storage, processing, analytics, and machine learning, that can be accessed over the internet  .
  - Grid computing, which connects multiple computers or devices over a network to form a virtual supercomputer that can share resources and perform distributed and parallel computing tasks  .
  - MapReduce, which is a programming model and framework for processing and analyzing large and unstructured data sets in a distributed and parallel manner, using two phases: map and reduce  .
- These paradigms have enabled analytics to scale up to the challenges and opportunities of big data, and have facilitated the development and deployment of advanced analytics, such as data mining, machine learning, and artificial intelligence.

# Analysis vs Reporting

- Analysis and reporting are two important aspects of data analytics, but they are not the same.
- Analysis is the process of exploring, interpreting, and finding insights from data, using various methods such as statistics, machine learning, visualization, etc.
- Reporting is the process of communicating the results of analysis to a specific audience, using various formats such as tables, charts, dashboards, etc.
- Analysis and reporting have different goals, audiences, and outputs.
- The goal of analysis is to answer questions, test hypotheses, discover patterns, and generate insights from data.
- The goal of reporting is to inform, persuade, or educate the audience about the findings of analysis, using clear, concise, and accurate information.
- The audience of analysis is usually the analyst themselves, or other analysts, data scientists, or experts who are interested in the data and the methods used.
- The audience of reporting is usually the stakeholders, decision-makers, or customers who are interested in the outcomes and implications of the analysis, and who may have different levels of data literacy and domain knowledge.
- The output of analysis is usually a set of findings, insights, or recommendations, which may be presented in various forms such as code, notebooks, reports, etc.
- The output of reporting is usually a set of deliverables, such as tables, charts, dashboards, slides, etc., which should be designed to suit the needs and preferences of the audience, and to convey the key messages and actions from the analysis.

# Various Phases of Data Analytics Lifecycle

Data analytics lifecycle is a framework that guides the process of transforming data into insights that can be used to achieve business or project goals. It is based on the CRISP-DM methodology, which is an open-standard analytics model developed by IBM . The data analytics lifecycle consists of six phases, which are:

- **Business understanding**: This phase involves defining the problem or opportunity, identifying the objectives and requirements, and establishing the success criteria for the analytics project . It also involves understanding the domain and the context of the business or project.
- **Data understanding**: This phase involves collecting, exploring, and describing the data that is relevant to the problem or opportunity . It also involves assessing the quality, quantity, and availability of the data, and identifying any potential issues or gaps .
- **Data preparation**: This phase involves cleaning, transforming, and integrating the data to make it ready for analysis . It also involves selecting, sampling, and creating new features or variables from the data, and ensuring the data meets the requirements and assumptions of the analysis methods .
- **Data analysis**: This phase involves applying various techniques and tools to explore, model, and test the data to discover patterns, relationships, and insights . It also involves evaluating and comparing different models, and selecting the best ones based on the success criteria and the data quality .
- **Data validation**: This phase involves verifying and validating the results and insights obtained from the data analysis phase . It also involves assessing the reliability, robustness, and generalizability of the models and the insights, and identifying any limitations or risks .
- **Data presentation**: This phase involves communicating and presenting the results and insights to the stakeholders or the target audience in a clear, concise, and compelling way . It also involves providing recommendations, actions, or solutions based on the insights, and measuring the impact and value of the analytics project .

These six phases are not necessarily sequential or linear, but rather iterative and cyclical, meaning that they can be repeated or revisited as needed throughout the analytics project . The data analytics lifecycle helps to ensure that the analytics project is aligned with the business or project goals, and that the data is used effectively and ethically to generate meaningful and actionable insights.

# Multivariate Analysis

Multivariate analysis (MVA) is a method of analyzing data that involves more than two variables at the same time. It is used to find patterns, relationships, and effects among the variables, and to reduce the dimensionality of the data. MVA can be divided into two types: dependent and interdependent methods.

## Dependent Methods

Dependent methods are used when there is a dependent variable (also called a response or outcome variable) that is influenced by one or more independent variables (also called predictors or explanatory variables). The goal is to model the relationship between the dependent variable and the independent variables, and to test hypotheses about the effects of the independent variables. Some examples of dependent methods are:

- **Multivariate regression**: This method extends the simple linear regression to cases where there are multiple independent variables and/or multiple dependent variables. It can be used to estimate the coefficients of the regression equation, to test the significance of the independent variables, and to assess the goodness of fit of the model. There are different types of multivariate regression, such as multiple linear regression, multivariate logistic regression, and multivariate polynomial regression.

- **Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA)**: This method extends the analysis of variance (ANOVA) to cases where there is more than one dependent variable and one or more independent variables. It can be used to test the overall effect of the independent variables on the dependent variables, as well as the specific effects of each independent variable and their interactions. There are different types of MANOVA, such as one-way MANOVA, two-way MANOVA, and repeated measures MANOVA.

- **Discriminant analysis**: This method is used to classify observations into two or more groups based on a set of independent variables. It can be used to determine the optimal combination of independent variables that can discriminate between the groups, and to assign new observations to the groups based on their values of the independent variables. There are different types of discriminant analysis, such as linear discriminant analysis, quadratic discriminant analysis, and canonical discriminant analysis.

## Interdependent Methods

Interdependent methods are used when there is no clear distinction between dependent and independent variables, and the goal is to explore the structure and patterns of the data. These methods can be used to reduce the dimensionality of the data, to group similar observations or variables, and to visualize the data. Some examples of interdependent methods are:

- **Factor analysis**: This method is used to identify the underlying factors or dimensions that explain the variation and correlation among a set of variables. It can be used to reduce the number of variables by replacing them with a smaller number of factors, and to interpret the meaning and significance of the factors. There are different types of factor analysis, such as exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and principal component analysis.

- **Cluster analysis**: This method is used to group observations into clusters based on their similarity or dissimilarity on a set of variables. It can be used to discover the natural or hidden structure of the data, and to classify new observations into the existing clusters. There are different types of cluster analysis, such as hierarchical cluster analysis, k-means cluster analysis, and fuzzy cluster analysis.

- **Multidimensional scaling (MDS)**: This method is used to represent the distances or similarities among a set of observations or variables in a low-dimensional space, usually two or three dimensions. It can be used to visualize the data, to explore the relationships among the observations or variables, and to compare different measures of distance or similarity. There are different types of MDS, such as metric MDS, nonmetric MDS, and correspondence analysis.

# Bayesian modeling

Bayesian modeling is a statistical approach that uses probability to represent all uncertainty within a model, both the uncertainty regarding the output and the uncertainty regarding the input (aka parameters) to the model. Bayesian modeling is based on Bayes' theorem, which describes how to update the probability of an event or a hypothesis after obtaining new data or evidence.

Some of the advantages of Bayesian modeling are:

- It can incorporate prior knowledge or beliefs into the analysis, which can improve the accuracy and efficiency of the inference.
- It can handle complex and nonlinear models that are difficult to fit using frequentist methods.
- It can provide a full posterior distribution of the parameters and the predictions, which can quantify the uncertainty and variability of the estimates.
- It can be used for decision-making and policy evaluation by providing a quantitative assessment of the risks and benefits of different actions or interventions.

Some of the challenges of Bayesian modeling are:

- It requires specifying a prior distribution for the parameters, which can be subjective and controversial.
- It can be computationally intensive and time-consuming, especially for large and high-dimensional models.
- It can suffer from identifiability and convergence issues, which can affect the validity and reliability of the inference.
- It can be difficult to communicate and interpret the results to non-experts, especially the concept of posterior probability and credible intervals.

Some of the examples of Bayesian modeling are:

- Bayesian linear regression, which models the relationship between a response variable and one or more explanatory variables using a linear function and a normal prior distribution for the coefficients.
- Bayesian hierarchical modeling, which models the variation and dependence among groups or levels of data using a hierarchical structure and a prior distribution for the hyperparameters.
- Bayesian network, which models the conditional dependence and independence among a set of variables using a directed acyclic graph and a joint probability distribution.
- Bayesian inference for infectious disease modeling, which models the transmission dynamics and the epidemiological parameters of an infectious disease using a compartmental model and a prior distribution based on expert knowledge or historical data.

# Support Vector and Kernel Methods

## Support Vector Machines (SVMs)

- SVMs are a type of supervised learning algorithm that can perform classification and regression tasks.
- SVMs aim to find the optimal hyperplane that separates the data into different classes or predicts the output value for a given input.
- SVMs use a technique called the **margin maximization** to find the hyperplane that has the largest distance to the nearest data points of any class. This ensures that the hyperplane is robust and generalizable.
- The data points that are closest to the hyperplane are called the **support vectors**, and they determine the position and orientation of the hyperplane.
- SVMs can handle linearly separable and non-linearly separable data by using different types of **kernels**.

## Kernel Methods

- Kernel methods are a way of mapping the data into a higher-dimensional feature space, where the data may become linearly separable or more suitable for linear analysis.
- Kernel methods use a function called the **kernel function** to compute the inner product or similarity between two data points in the feature space, without explicitly transforming the data.
- Kernel methods are also known as the **kernel trick**, because they allow us to use linear algorithms in non-linear problems, by implicitly transforming the data with the kernel function.
- Kernel methods can be applied to various algorithms, such as SVMs, Gaussian processes, principal component analysis, ridge regression, and many others.
- Kernel methods can use different types of kernel functions, such as linear, polynomial, radial basis function (RBF), sigmoid, and others. The choice of the kernel function depends on the data and the task.

# Linear Systems Analysis & Nonlinear Dynamics

## Linear Systems Analysis

- A linear system is a system whose behavior can be described by a linear equation or a set of linear equations.
- A linear equation is an equation that satisfies the properties of superposition and homogeneity, which means that the sum of two solutions is also a solution, and that scaling a solution by a constant factor does not change its validity.
- A linear system can be represented by a matrix equation of the form **Ax = b**, where **A** is a matrix of coefficients, **x** is a vector of unknowns, and **b** is a vector of constants.
- A linear system can be solved exactly by various methods, such as Gaussian elimination, matrix inversion, or eigenvalue decomposition.
- A linear system can also be interpreted as a linear transformation that maps a vector space to another vector space, preserving the properties of vector addition and scalar multiplication.
- A linear system can be classified into different types, such as consistent or inconsistent, homogeneous or inhomogeneous, overdetermined or underdetermined, depending on the properties of the matrix **A** and the vector **b**.
- A linear system can be analyzed by various tools, such as matrix rank, determinant, trace, inverse, transpose, adjoint, eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and eigenspaces.

## Nonlinear Dynamics

- A nonlinear system is a system whose behavior cannot be described by a linear equation or a set of linear equations.
- A nonlinear system may exhibit complex and unpredictable phenomena, such as chaos, bifurcations, limit cycles, attractors, fractals, and self-organization.
- A nonlinear system can be represented by a nonlinear equation or a set of nonlinear equations, which may involve nonlinear functions, such as polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions, etc.
- A nonlinear system can be solved approximately by various methods, such as numerical integration, perturbation theory, linearization, or bifurcation analysis.
- A nonlinear system can also be interpreted as a nonlinear transformation that maps a vector space to another vector space, altering the properties of vector addition and scalar multiplication.
- A nonlinear system can be classified into different types, such as autonomous or non-autonomous, continuous or discrete, deterministic or stochastic, depending on the properties of the nonlinear equation or the set of nonlinear equations.
- A nonlinear system can be analyzed by various tools, such as phase portraits, Lyapunov exponents, Poincaré maps, bifurcation diagrams, and fractal dimensions.



# Rule Induction

# Neural Networks for Data Analysis

Neural networks are a type of machine learning technique that can learn complex patterns and relationships from data. Neural networks are inspired by the structure and function of the human brain, and consist of interconnected units called neurons that process information. Neural networks can be used for various data analysis tasks, such as regression, classification, image recognition, speech recognition, and more.

Some of the main concepts and components of neural networks are:

- **Input layer**: The first layer of a neural network that receives the data to be analyzed. The input layer can have one or more neurons, depending on the number of features or variables in the data.
- **Hidden layer**: One or more layers of a neural network that are between the input and output layers. The hidden layers perform computations and transformations on the data, and can have different numbers and types of neurons, depending on the architecture and design of the network.
- **Output layer**: The last layer of a neural network that produces the final result or prediction. The output layer can have one or more neurons, depending on the type of task and the number of classes or categories to be predicted.
- **Weights and biases**: The parameters of a neural network that determine how the information flows and is processed between the neurons. Weights are the values that multiply the inputs of each neuron, and biases are the values that are added to the weighted inputs. Weights and biases are learned and updated during the training process of the network, using a learning algorithm such as gradient descent.
- **Activation function**: A function that determines the output of each neuron, based on its weighted input and bias. Activation functions introduce nonlinearity and complexity to the network, and can have different forms and properties, such as sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, softmax, etc.
- **Loss function**: A function that measures the difference between the actual and predicted outputs of the network, and evaluates the performance and accuracy of the network. Loss functions can have different forms and properties, such as mean squared error, cross entropy, hinge loss, etc.
- **Backpropagation**: A technique that propagates the error or loss from the output layer to the input layer, and adjusts the weights and biases of the network accordingly, using the chain rule of calculus. Backpropagation is the main method for training neural networks, and requires a learning rate that determines the size of the updates.

Neural networks can have different architectures and designs, depending on the type and complexity of the data and the task. Some of the common types of neural networks are:

- **Feedforward neural network**: A simple and basic type of neural network that has one or more hidden layers, and passes the information from the input layer to the output layer in a forward direction, without any feedback or loops. Feedforward neural networks can be used for regression and classification tasks, and are easy to implement and understand.
- **Recurrent neural network**: A type of neural network that has one or more hidden layers, and passes the information from the input layer to the output layer in a forward direction, but also has feedback or loops that allow the network to store and use previous information. Recurrent neural networks can be used for sequential data, such as time series, text, speech, etc., and can capture temporal and contextual dependencies and patterns.
- **Convolutional neural network**: A type of neural network that has one or more hidden layers, and passes the information from the input layer to the output layer in a forward direction, but also has convolutional and pooling layers that perform operations such as filtering, feature extraction, and dimensionality reduction. Convolutional neural networks can be used for spatial data, such as images, videos, etc., and can capture spatial and structural patterns and features.

# Competitive Learning

- Competitive learning is a form of **unsupervised learning** in artificial neural networks, in which nodes compete for the right to respond to a subset of the input data.
- Competitive learning is based on the principle of **winner-takes-all** (WTA), where only the node with the highest activation or similarity to the input data is updated, while the others remain unchanged .
- Competitive learning can be used for **clustering**, **dimensionality reduction**, **feature extraction**, and **pattern recognition** .
- Competitive learning requires a **learning rate** parameter that controls the degree of adaptation of the winning node, and a **neighborhood function** that determines the influence of the winning node on its neighbors .
- Competitive learning can be implemented with **neural networks** that contain a hidden layer, which is commonly known as the **competitive layer**. Every competitive neuron is described by a vector of weights and calculates the similarity measure between the input data and the weight vector .
- Competitive learning can be divided into two types: **soft competitive learning** and **hard competitive learning**. Soft competitive learning allows multiple nodes to be updated according to their similarity to the input data, while hard competitive learning only updates the single winning node.
- Competitive learning can be contrasted with **collaborative learning** and **individualized learning**, which are other forms of learning that involve different degrees of interaction and cooperation among learners.
- Competitive learning has some advantages and disadvantages. Some advantages are that it can discover the inherent structure of the data, it can reduce the complexity of the data, and it can adapt to changing data distributions. Some disadvantages are that it can be sensitive to the initial conditions, it can suffer from the dead unit problem, and it can be affected by the curse of dimensionality.

# Mining frequent itemsets

- Frequent itemset mining (pattern mining) is a technique used to derive relationships such as regularities in customers’ shopping behavior in physical and online stores to discover association rules out of the relationships.
- Frequent itemsets are sets of items that appear together in a dataset with a frequency above a given threshold. For example, if the threshold is 50%, then a frequent itemset is a set of items that occurs in at least half of the transactions in the dataset.
- Frequent itemset mining is useful for many applications, such as market basket analysis, recommender systems, web mining, text mining, bioinformatics, and network analysis.
- Frequent itemset mining can be performed using different algorithms, such as Apriori, Eclat, and FP-growth. These algorithms differ in how they generate and prune candidate itemsets, how they count the support of itemsets, and how they store and traverse the data structure.
- Apriori is the most commonly used algorithm for frequent itemset mining. It works by generating candidate itemsets of increasing size and pruning those that are not frequent. It uses a bottom-up approach, starting from single-item itemsets and extending them to larger itemsets. It also uses the property that any subset of a frequent itemset must be frequent, which reduces the search space.
- Eclat is a variant of Apriori that uses a vertical data format, where each item is associated with a set of transaction identifiers (TIDs) that contain it. It generates candidate itemsets by intersecting the TIDs of frequent itemsets of smaller size. It avoids scanning the whole dataset multiple times, as Apriori does, and only requires counting the size of the TID sets.
- FP-growth is another algorithm for frequent itemset mining that uses a compact data structure called FP-tree, which stores the frequent items and their occurrence counts in a prefix tree. It generates frequent itemsets by recursively constructing conditional FP-trees and extracting frequent itemsets from them. It avoids generating and testing candidate itemsets, as Apriori and Eclat do, and only requires two scans of the dataset.

# Apriori Algorithm

- Apriori algorithm is a data mining technique for finding frequent itemsets and association rules in a transactional database  .
- Frequent itemsets are sets of items that appear together in a minimum number of transactions, called the support threshold  .
- Association rules are implications of the form X -> Y, where X and Y are itemsets, meaning that transactions containing X are likely to contain Y as well  .
- Apriori algorithm uses a bottom-up approach, where it starts with finding the frequent 1-itemsets (single items) and then iteratively generates larger and larger itemsets by joining and pruning the previous ones  .
- The join step combines two itemsets of size k to form a candidate itemset of size k+1, if they share the first k-1 items  .
- The prune step eliminates the candidate itemsets that have a subset that is not frequent, based on the Apriori property that all subsets of a frequent itemset must be frequent  .
- The algorithm stops when no more frequent or candidate itemsets can be generated  .
- The algorithm can be summarized as follows:

```
Apriori(T, min_sup)
  L1 = {frequent 1-itemsets};
  k = 2;
  while (Lk-1 is not empty) do
    Ck = apriori-gen(Lk-1); // generate candidates
    for each transaction t in T do
      Ct = subset(Ck, t); // find candidates in t
      for each candidate c in Ct do
        c.count++; // increment count
    Lk = {c in Ck | c.count >= min_sup}; // find frequent itemsets
    k++;
  end
  return U_k Lk; // return all frequent itemsets
```

- **Hierarchical clustering**: This technique builds a hierarchy of clusters by either merging smaller clusters into larger ones (agglomerative) or splitting larger clusters into smaller ones (divisive). # K-means Clustering Algorithm

- K-means clustering is a method of **vector quantization**, originally from signal processing, that aims to partition n observations into k clusters in which each observation belongs to the cluster with the nearest mean (cluster centers or cluster centroid), serving as a prototype of the cluster.
- K-means clustering is an **unsupervised learning algorithm**. There is no labeled data for this clustering, unlike in supervised learning.
- K-means clustering is one of the simplest and most popular unsupervised machine learning algorithms for data scientists.
- K-means clustering is a **centroid-based clustering** algorithm, which organizes the data into non-hierarchical clusters, in contrast to hierarchical clustering.
- K-means clustering is widely used in various business applications, such as customer segmentation, image segmentation, anomaly detection, etc.

## Steps of K-means Clustering Algorithm

- To perform K-means clustering, we must first specify the desired number of clusters K.
- Then, the K-means algorithm will assign each observation to exactly one of the K clusters.
- The algorithm works as follows:
  - Step 1: Randomly assign a number, from 1 to K, to each of the observations. These serve as initial cluster assignments for the observations.
  - Step 2: Iterate until the cluster assignments stop changing:
    - For each of the K clusters, compute the cluster centroid. The kth cluster centroid is the vector of the p feature means for the observations in the kth cluster.
    - Assign each observation to the cluster whose centroid is closest (where closest is defined using Euclidean distance).
- The algorithm converges when the assignments no longer change.
- The result is a partitioning of the data into K clusters.

## Advantages and Disadvantages of K-means Clustering Algorithm

- Some of the advantages of K-means clustering are :
  - It is simple and easy to implement.
  - It is computationally efficient and scalable for large data sets.
  - It can produce tight and compact clusters.
  - It can handle numerical data well.
- Some of the disadvantages of K-means clustering are :
  - It requires the number of clusters K to be specified in advance, which may not be easy to determine.
  - It is sensitive to the initial cluster assignments and may converge to a local optimum.
  - It is not suitable for clusters of different shapes, sizes, and densities.
  - It cannot handle categorical data well.



# Clustering High-Dimensional Data

Clustering high-dimensional data is the cluster analysis of data with anywhere from a few dozen to many thousands of dimensions. It is a challenging task due to the following problems :

- **The curse of dimensionality**: The data space becomes sparse and the distance between any two points becomes similar as the number of dimensions increases. This makes it hard to define meaningful clusters and distance measures.
- **The irrelevant and redundant features**: Not all dimensions are relevant for clustering, and some may even introduce noise or redundancy. This makes it hard to find the most informative and discriminative features for clustering.
- **The hidden and overlapping clusters**: The clusters may exist in different subspaces of the high-dimensional space, and they may overlap with each other. This makes it hard to identify the clusters and their boundaries.
- **The high computational complexity**: The clustering algorithms need to deal with a large amount of data and dimensions, which may require a lot of time and memory resources.

To overcome these problems, several approaches have been proposed for clustering high-dimensional data  , such as:

- **Subspace clustering**: This approach aims to find clusters that exist in different subspaces of the high-dimensional space, by searching for relevant dimensions for each cluster. It can handle the irrelevant and redundant features, and the hidden and overlapping clusters, but it may suffer from the high computational complexity and the difficulty of interpreting the results.
- **Feature selection**: This approach aims to reduce the dimensionality of the data by selecting a subset of features that are most relevant for clustering. It can handle the curse of dimensionality and the irrelevant and redundant features, but it may miss some hidden and overlapping clusters that exist in other features.
- **Feature extraction**: This approach aims to transform the high-dimensional data into a lower-dimensional space by applying some dimensionality reduction techniques, such as principal component analysis (PCA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), or autoencoders. It can handle the curse of dimensionality and the high computational complexity, but it may lose some information and distort the data structure in the process.
- **Ensemble clustering**: This approach aims to combine multiple clustering results from different subspaces, features, or algorithms, by using some consensus or co-association methods. It can handle the hidden and overlapping clusters, and the diversity of the data, but it may introduce some inconsistency and redundancy in the results.

These approaches can be used separately or in combination, depending on the characteristics and requirements of the data and the clustering task. Clustering high-dimensional data is an active and important research area in data mining, as it can provide useful insights and patterns for various applications, such as image analysis, text mining, bioinformatics, and recommender systems.

- Density-based clustering: This method is based on the idea that clusters are dense regions of data points that are separated by sparse regions. - The data is potentially infinite and cannot be stored in memory or disk.
- The data may evolve over time and the clusters may change dynamically.
- The data may be noisy, incomplete, or inconsistent.
- The data may have different arrival rates and formats.

To address these challenges, clustering algorithms for streaming data need to have the following properties:

- They need to be online, meaning that they can process each data point as it arrives without revisiting previous data points.
- They need to be incremental, meaning that they can update the clusters without recomputing them from scratch.
- They need to be scalable, meaning that they can handle high-volume and high-dimensional data efficiently.
- They need to be adaptive, meaning that they can detect and handle changes in the data distribution and the cluster structure.

# MapReduce

MapReduce is a framework for processing parallelizable problems across large datasets using a large number of computers (nodes), collectively referred to as a cluster or a grid. MapReduce enables distributed and scalable data analysis by dividing the input data into smaller chunks, applying a user-defined function (map) to each chunk, and then combining the results using another user-defined function (reduce).

## MapReduce Framework

A MapReduce framework (or system) is usually composed of three operations (or steps) :

- **Map**: each worker node applies the map function to the local data, and writes the output to a temporary storage. A master node ensures that only one copy of the redundant input data is processed.
- **Shuffle**: the worker nodes redistribute the data based on the output keys (produced by the map function), such that all data belonging to one key is located on the same worker node.
- **Reduce**: the worker nodes now process each group of output data, per key, in parallel. The reduce function is then applied to each group, which in turn produces a collection of values per key.

## MapReduce Example

A common example of MapReduce is the word count problem, where the goal is to count the frequency of each word in a large text corpus. The map function takes a line of text as input and outputs a key-value pair for each word, where the key is the word and the value is 1. The shuffle phase groups the key-value pairs by word, and the reduce function sums up the values for each word, resulting in the final word count.

## MapReduce Implementation

One of the most popular implementations of MapReduce is Apache Hadoop, an open-source framework that supports distributed storage and processing of large datasets. Hadoop consists of two main components: Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS), which provides reliable and scalable storage for the input and output data, and Hadoop MapReduce, which provides the programming model and the execution engine for the data analysis. Hadoop also provides other components and tools for data ingestion, processing, and management, such as Hadoop YARN, Hadoop Common, Hadoop Oozie, Hadoop Hive, Hadoop Pig, and Hadoop Spark.

## MapReduce Benefits and Challenges

MapReduce offers several benefits for data analysis, such as:

- **Scalability**: MapReduce can handle large volumes of data by distributing the workload across multiple nodes.
- **Fault-tolerance**: MapReduce can recover from node failures by replicating the data and re-executing the tasks on other nodes.
- **Simplicity**: MapReduce abstracts the details of parallelization, distribution, and coordination from the user, allowing them to focus on the logic of the map and reduce functions.

However, MapReduce also faces some challenges, such as:

- **Performance**: MapReduce may incur high overhead due to the shuffle and sort phases, which involve network communication and disk I/O.
- **Flexibility**: MapReduce may not be suitable for some types of problems that require iterative or interactive processing, such as graph algorithms or machine learning.
- **Complexity**: MapReduce may require writing low-level code in Java or other languages, which can be tedious and error-prone for some users.

# Sharding

Sharding is a database partitioning technique that splits a large database into smaller, more manageable parts called shards. Sharding can improve the performance, scalability, and availability of a database system by distributing the data and the workload across multiple servers or nodes. Sharding can also reduce the risk of data loss or corruption by replicating the shards on different servers or locations.

Some of the key concepts and benefits of sharding are:

- **Shard key**: A shard key is a column or a set of columns that determines how the data is partitioned and distributed across the shards. A good shard key should have high cardinality (many possible values), low skew (even distribution of values), and low correlation (no dependency between values).
- **Shard mapping**: A shard mapping is a mechanism that maps each shard key value to a specific shard. A shard mapping can be stored in a separate database, a configuration file, or a metadata service. A shard mapping can be static (fixed and predefined) or dynamic (changing and adaptive).
- **Shard placement**: A shard placement is a strategy that decides where to store each shard. A shard placement can be based on various factors, such as performance, availability, security, cost, or locality. A shard placement can be horizontal (splitting the data by rows) or vertical (splitting the data by columns).
- **Shard balancing**: A shard balancing is a process that adjusts the shard placement and the shard mapping to optimize the database system. A shard balancing can be triggered by various events, such as data growth, data deletion, data migration, server failure, server addition, or server removal. A shard balancing can be manual (performed by the administrator) or automatic (performed by the system).
- **Shard replication**: A shard replication is a technique that creates copies of the shards on different servers or locations. A shard replication can improve the availability, reliability, and durability of the database system by providing backup and failover options. A shard replication can be synchronous (updating the copies at the same time as the original) or asynchronous (updating the copies after a delay from the original).

# Interaction Techniques for Data Analytics

Interaction techniques are methods or tools that enable users to explore, analyze, and manipulate data in a visual or interactive way. Interaction techniques can help users gain insights, discover patterns, test hypotheses, and communicate findings from data. Some of the interaction techniques for data analytics are:

- **Regression analysis**: This technique estimates the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. For example, regression analysis can be used to predict sales based on advertising spending, customer demographics, and economic factors.
- **Factor analysis**: This technique reduces a large number of variables into a smaller number of factors that capture the underlying structure or dimensions of the data. For example, factor analysis can be used to identify the main factors that influence customer satisfaction or loyalty.
- **Cluster analysis**: This technique groups data points into clusters based on their similarity or proximity. For example, cluster analysis can be used to segment customers based on their behavior, preferences, or needs.
- **Segmentation analysis**: This technique divides a population into subgroups based on some criteria or characteristics. For example, segmentation analysis can be used to target different marketing campaigns to different customer segments.
- **Time series analysis**: This technique analyzes data that are collected over time and examines the trends, patterns, cycles, or seasonality in the data. For example, time series analysis can be used to forecast sales, demand, or inventory levels based on historical data.
- **Monte Carlo simulation**: This technique generates random samples from a probability distribution and computes the outcomes of a model or a scenario based on the samples. For example, Monte Carlo simulation can be used to estimate the risk, uncertainty, or variability of a project, a decision, or a system.
- **Discriminant analysis**: This technique classifies data points into predefined categories based on their features or attributes. For example, discriminant analysis can be used to predict whether a customer will buy a product or not based on their demographic and behavioral data.
- **Decision tree analysis**: This technique creates a tree-like structure that shows the possible outcomes and consequences of a decision or a problem. For example, decision tree analysis can be used to evaluate the best option among several alternatives based on their costs, benefits, and risks.

- **Interactive data visualization**: This technique uses graphical or visual elements to represent data and allows users to explore and manipulate the data directly within the visualization. - **Sentiment analysis**: This technique analyzes the emotions, opinions, or attitudes expressed in text or speech data. For example, sentiment analysis can be used to measure customer satisfaction, brand reputation, or social media sentiment based on the reviews, comments, or tweets of the users.

# Attribute and Data Types

## Introduction

- An attribute is a characteristic or feature that is measured for each observation or record in a dataset  .
- Attributes describe the data and are used to improve decision-making, identify trends, and optimize processes in data analytics.
- Attributes can have different types, depending on the nature and level of measurement of the data  .

## Types of Attributes

- There are several types of attributes that are commonly used in data analytics. These include:

  - Numeric attributes: These are attributes that represent quantitative data, such as numbers. There are two main types of numeric attributes: continuous and discrete .
    - Continuous attributes: These are attributes that can take any value within a range, such as height, weight, temperature, etc. They are usually measured with a scale or a device .
    - Discrete attributes: These are attributes that can take only a finite number of values, such as count, rank, score, etc. They are usually counted or assigned .
  - Categorical attributes: These are attributes that represent qualitative data, such as names, colors, categories, etc. They are usually labeled or grouped .
    - Nominal attributes: These are attributes that have no inherent order or ranking, such as gender, nationality, occupation, etc. They are used to identify or classify the data .
    - Ordinal attributes: These are attributes that have a meaningful order or ranking, such as grade, level, rating, etc. They are used to compare or evaluate the data .
  - Binary attributes: These are attributes that can take only two values, such as yes/no, true/false, 0/1, etc. They are a special case of nominal or ordinal attributes, depending on whether they have an order or not .

## Examples

- Here are some examples of attributes and their types for different datasets:

  - Contact information: name (nominal), address (nominal), email (nominal), phone number (discrete).
  - Customer feedback: satisfaction (ordinal), loyalty (ordinal), recommendation (binary), comment (nominal).
  - Product sales: product ID (nominal), price (continuous), quantity (discrete), revenue (continuous), profit (continuous).
  - Weather data: date (discrete), location (nominal), temperature (continuous), humidity (continuous), precipitation (continuous), wind speed (continuous), wind direction (nominal).

# Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics is a branch of statistics that summarizes or describes the characteristics of a data set, such as its mean, median, mode, standard deviation, range, frequency distribution, etc. Descriptive statistics can be used to present numerical data in tables, charts, graphs, or other visual forms, to make the data easier to understand and analyze. Descriptive statistics do not make any inferences or predictions about the population that the data set represents, but only describe the sample itself .

Some of the topics that are covered in descriptive statistics are:

- **Measures of central tendency**: These are numerical values that describe the center or typical value of a data set. The most common measures of central tendency are the mean, the median, and the mode. The mean is the average of all the values in the data set, the median is the middle value when the data set is ordered from smallest to largest, and the mode is the most frequent value in the data set. Different measures of central tendency may be more appropriate for different types of data or distributions .
- **Measures of variability**: These are numerical values that describe the spread or dispersion of a data set. The most common measures of variability are the range, the interquartile range, the variance, and the standard deviation. The range is the difference between the maximum and minimum values in the data set, the interquartile range is the difference between the 25th and 75th percentiles of the data set, the variance is the average of the squared deviations from the mean, and the standard deviation is the square root of the variance. These measures indicate how much the values in the data set vary from each other and from the mean .
- **Frequency distribution**: This is a table or graph that shows how often each value or category occurs in a data set. A frequency distribution can be used to summarize categorical data (such as gender, race, or color) or numerical data (such as height, weight, or age). A frequency distribution can be displayed as a frequency table, a histogram, a bar chart, a pie chart, or a line graph, depending on the type and level of measurement of the data .

Descriptive statistics are useful for exploring and presenting data, but they do not allow us to draw conclusions or make generalizations about the population from which the data was sampled. For that, we need inferential statistics, which use probability theory and hypothesis testing to make inferences or predictions about the population based on the sample data .

# Exploratory Data Analysis

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is the process of examining and summarizing data sets using various techniques, such as descriptive statistics, graphical displays, and data transformations. EDA aims to reveal the main features, patterns, and relationships in the data, as well as identify any potential outliers, errors, or anomalies. EDA is often the first step in data analysis, before applying more formal methods such as hypothesis testing, modeling, or machine learning.

## Benefits of EDA

- EDA can help to gain insights and understanding of the data, which can inform the choice of appropriate analytical methods and tools.
- EDA can help to identify and address any data quality issues, such as missing values, outliers, or inconsistencies.
- EDA can help to generate new hypotheses or research questions based on the observed patterns and trends in the data.
- EDA can help to communicate and visualize the data effectively, using charts, graphs, tables, or interactive dashboards.

## Steps of EDA

- Define the objective and scope of the analysis, such as the research question, the target variable, or the expected outcome.
- Import and load the data into a suitable format and environment, such as a spreadsheet, a database, or a programming language.
- Explore the data structure and metadata, such as the number of rows and columns, the data types, the variable names, and the units of measurement.
- Summarize the data using descriptive statistics, such as the mean, median, mode, standard deviation, range, quartiles, and frequency distribution of each variable.
- Visualize the data using graphical displays, such as histograms, boxplots, scatterplots, bar charts, pie charts, and heatmaps, to show the distribution, variation, correlation, and comparison of the variables.
- Transform the data using various techniques, such as scaling, normalization, standardization, binning, encoding, or imputation, to make the data more suitable for further analysis or modeling.
- Analyze the data using more advanced methods, such as hypothesis testing, clustering, classification, regression, or dimensionality reduction, to answer the research question or achieve the objective.

## Tools and Frameworks for EDA

There are many tools and frameworks available for performing EDA, depending on the type, size, and complexity of the data, as well as the preference and skill level of the analyst. Some of the most common and popular tools and frameworks are:

- Excel: A spreadsheet application that can import, store, manipulate, and visualize data using formulas, functions, charts, and pivot tables.
- SQL: A query language that can access, filter, join, aggregate, and analyze data stored in relational databases.
- Python: A general-purpose programming language that can perform EDA using various libraries, such as pandas, numpy, scipy, matplotlib, seaborn, and plotly.
- R: A statistical programming language that can perform EDA using various packages, such as dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2, and shiny.
- Tableau: A data visualization software that can connect to various data sources, create interactive dashboards, and share insights online.
