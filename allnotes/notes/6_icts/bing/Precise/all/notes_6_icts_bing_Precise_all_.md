  1. Social Contract Theory

#### Theories of origin of State in India

The origin of the state in ancient India has been a subject of study in political science. There are three main theories that explain the origin of the state in ancient India. These are:

1. **Social Contract Theory**: This theory suggests that the state was formed as a result of an agreement between individuals to surrender some of their rights and freedoms in exchange for protection and security provided by the state.

2. **Divine Origin Theory**: This theory suggests that the state was created by a divine power and that the rulers of the state were representatives of that divine power on earth.

3. **Organic Theory**: This theory suggests that the state is a natural and inevitable outcome of the growth and development of human society.

These theories provide different perspectives on the origin of the state in ancient India and the role of the state in society .



#### Evolutionary Theory of origin of State

The Evolutionary Theory of the origin of the state is one of the several theories that attempt to explain how states or governments came into existence. According to this theory, the state evolved from the family, which gradually grew in size and complexity, eventually forming a state.

1. The theory suggests that the earliest form of human society was the family, which was based on blood ties and the natural bond between parents and children.
2. As the family grew in size, it became necessary to establish rules and regulations to govern the behavior of its members. This led to the formation of clans or tribes, which were larger groups of related families.
3. Over time, these clans or tribes began to engage in trade and commerce with one another, leading to the development of more complex social and economic relationships.
4. As these relationships grew more complex, it became necessary to establish a system of governance to regulate them. This led to the formation of the state, which was a political organization with the power to enforce laws and maintain order.
5. The state, according to the evolutionary theory, emerged as a natural progression from the family, through the clan or tribe, to a more complex political organization.

This theory is based on the idea that human society has evolved over time, and that the state is a natural result of this evolution. It is important to note that this is just one of several theories about the origin of the state, and that there is no definitive answer to this question. Different scholars and theorists have proposed different explanations for the emergence of the state, and the debate continues to this day.



#### Force Theory of origin of State

The Force Theory of the origin of the state is one of the several theories that attempt to explain how states came into existence. According to this theory, the state originated from the use of force. Here are some key points to consider:

1. The theory suggests that the state was created when one group or individual used force to subjugate others and establish control over a territory.
2. This could have happened through conquest, where one group invaded and took control of another group's territory, or through the use of force within a group to establish a hierarchy and control.
3. The use of force allowed the ruling group or individual to establish and maintain order, and to create institutions and laws to govern the population.
4. The theory is often associated with the idea of "might makes right," where the powerful are able to impose their will on the less powerful.
5. Critics of the theory argue that it oversimplifies the complex processes involved in the formation of states and that it ignores other factors such as economic, social, and cultural developments.

Overall, the Force Theory of the origin of the state provides one perspective on how states may have come into existence through the use of force and the establishment of control over a territory and its population. However, it is important to note that this is just one theory among many, and that the actual processes involved in the formation of states are likely to be much more complex and multifaceted.



#### Mystical Theory of origin of State

The mystical theory of the origin of the state is one of the oldest theories that explains the origin of the state. According to this theory, the state is a divine institution that was created by God or gods. The rulers of the state were considered to be representatives of the divine power on earth and were therefore given the right to rule by divine authority.

Some key points of the mystical theory of the origin of the state are:

1. The state is a divine institution created by God or gods.
2. The rulers of the state are representatives of the divine power on earth.
3. The right to rule is given to the rulers by divine authority.
4. The state is considered to be a necessary part of the divine plan for the world.

This theory was prevalent in ancient civilizations such as Egypt, where the Pharaoh was considered to be a god-king, and in medieval Europe, where the divine right of kings was a widely accepted concept. However, this theory has largely been discredited in modern times, as it is based on religious beliefs rather than empirical evidence.



#### Contract Theory of origin of State

The Contract Theory of the origin of the state, also known as the Social Contract Theory, is one of the theories that explains the origin of the state. This theory emerged during the time of the sophists of Greece but gained recognition through the works of three great philosophers - John Locke, Thomas Hobbes, and Jean Jacques Rousseau .

According to this theory, the state came into being through a contract made between individuals and society, or a contract made among individuals themselves . The theory explains that before the state, there was a state of nature where there was neither state nor law, and individuals lived in natural conditions . In this state of nature, individuals' actions were bound only by their personal power and conscience .

The Social Contract Theory is concerned with the legitimacy of the state's authority over the individual . It explains how individuals came together to form a society and a state to protect their rights and interests.

### The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

The Seven Limbs of the State, also known as the Saptanga Theory, was formulated by Kautilya in his work Arthashastra. It refers to the seven organs or constituents of the state, which are as follows:

1. **Swamin** - The King: The King is independent and his responsibility for peace, justice, and stability is stressed.
2. **Amatya** - The Minister or Bureaucrats: The Minister or Bureaucrats are responsible for the administration of the state.
3. **Janapada** - The Land and the People or Territory: The Land and the People or Territory refers to the geographical area and population of the state.
4. **Durga** - The Fortress or Fort: The Fortress or Fort refers to the defensive structures of the state.
5. **Kosha** - The Treasury or Treasure: The Treasury or Treasure refers to the financial resources of the state.
6. **Danda** - The Army or Coercive Authority: The Army or Coercive Authority refers to the military power of the state.
7. **Mitra** - The Allies: The Allies refers to the friendly relations and alliances with other states.

This entire set-up of the kingdom was described as the Saptanga Theory in ancient India. The Saptanga Theory categorically identified the ideal type qualities/excellencies of the state, and determined whether a state would evolve into an inferior or superior entity.

#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

Purusārtha is a fundamental principle of social ethics in ancient Indian society. It refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. These four aims are Dharma (righteousness, moral values), Artha (prosperity, economic values), Kama (pleasure, love, psychological values), and Moksha (liberation, spiritual values)  .

- **Dharma**: This refers to the moral and ethical values that an individual must follow in their life. It is the foundation of the other three Purusārthas and is considered the most important .
- **Artha**: This refers to the pursuit of wealth and material prosperity. It is considered important for an individual to be able to provide for themselves and their family .
- **Kama**: This refers to the pursuit of pleasure and love. It is considered important for an individual to have a fulfilling personal life .
- **Moksha**: This refers to the pursuit of liberation and spiritual enlightenment. It is considered the ultimate goal of human life .

These four aims are not only meant to set an ideal objective but also to convey the right means to achieve them . The concept of Purusārtha is centuries old and is still relevant today .



#### Varnāshrama System in Society in Ancient India

The Varnāshrama system, also known as Varnashramadharma, was a social structure that developed in ancient Hindu society. The term ‘Varnashramadharma’ consists of three words - ‘varna’, ‘asrama’, and ‘dharma’.

- **Varna**: Theoretically, the Varna system refers to the segmental division of the Hindu society into four classes. These four classes, or varnas, are Brahman, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Sudra. Brahman is the class of the priests and teachers. Kshatriya is the class of the kings, warriors, and aristocrats. Vaishya is the class of traders, merchants, and people engaged in other professions. Sudra is the class of laborers and artisans.

- **Asrama**: Asrama is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras. The four ashramas are: Brahmacharya (student), Grihastha (householder), Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller), and Sannyasa (renunciate).

- **Dharma**: Dharma refers to the moral and religious duties of an individual. In the context of Varnāshrama, it refers to the duties and responsibilities associated with each varna and asrama.

The Varnāshrama system aimed to provide a framework for individuals to fulfill their moral and religious duties and to maintain social order. However, it is important to note that the system was not always strictly followed and there were instances of individuals moving between varnas and ashramas.

#### Four-class Classification of Society in Ancient India

Ancient Indian Hinduism imposed a strict social hierarchy called a "caste system". The Vedas described four main social classes: Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras. The Dalit (formerly known as "untouchables") are considered outside the system and treated poorly.

- **Brahmins** were the priests and scholars.
- **Kshatriyas** were the kings, governors, and warriors.
- **Vaishyas** were the cattle herders, agriculturists, artisans, and merchants.
- **Shudras** were the laborers and service providers.

The system of the four classes (caturvarnya) is fundamental to the views the traditional lawgivers held of society. They specified a different set of obligations for each: the task of the Brahman is to study and advise, the baron to protect, the Vaishya to cultivate, and the serf to serve.

#### Harappan Script

- The Harappan Script, also known as the Indus Script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilization .
- Most inscriptions containing these symbols are extremely short, making it difficult to judge whether or not they constituted a writing system used to record the as-yet unidentified language(s) of the Indus Valley Civilization .
- The script found in the Indus Valley from Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro is the oldest known script of India. Unfortunately, till now it has not been read satisfactorily. This has made the problem of the Indus Valley script even more intractable .
- Harappa Script is hieroglyphic in nature and decipherment can be attempted the way Egyptian hieroglyphs were decrypted .
- The Harappan script has long defied attempts to read it, and therefore the language remains unknown .
- The Indus Script combined both word signs and symbols with phonetic value. This type of writing system is known as "logo-syllabic", where some symbols express ideas or words while others represent sounds .
- This view is based on the fact that roughly 400 signs have been identified, which makes it unlikely that the Indus Script was solely phonetic .



#### Brahmi Script

- The Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script.
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi.
- The Brahmi writing system, or script, appeared as a fully developed universal one in South Asia in the third century BCE.
- It is a forerunner of all writing systems that have found use in South Asia with the exception of the Indus script of the third millennium BCE, the Kharosthi script, which originated in what today is northwestern Pakistan in the fourth or possibly fifth century BCE, the Perso-Arabic Scripts of the medieval period, and the Latin scripts of the modern period.
- The Brahmi script is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations.
- For example, the Lipisala samdarshana parivarta lists 64 lipi (scripts), with the Brahmi script starting the list.
- The Brahmi script was already divided into regional variants at the time of the earliest surviving epigraphy around the 3rd century BC.
- Cursives of the Brahmi script began to diversify further from around the 5th century AD and continued to give rise to new scripts throughout the Middle Ages.
- The Brāhmī alphabet is the ancestor of most of the 40 or so modern Indian alphabets, as well as Khmer and Tibetan.
- It is thought to have been modelled on the Aramaic or Phoenician alphabets, and appeared in India sometime before 500 BC.
- Another theory is that Brāhmī developed from the Indus or Harappa script, which was used in the Indus valley until around 1300 BC.



### The Vedas

- The Vedas are a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India.
- Composed in Vedic Sanskrit, the texts constitute the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the oldest scriptures of Hinduism.
- The term "veda" means "knowledge" and they are thought to contain the fundamental knowledge relating to the underlying cause of, function of, and personal response to existence.
- There are four Vedas: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda, and the Atharvaveda.
- The Vedas inform the religion of Hinduism (also known as Sanatan Dharma meaning “Eternal Order” or “Eternal Path”).
- The word Veda means wisdom, knowledge, or vision, and it serves to manifest the language of the gods in human speech.
- The laws of the Vedas have regulated the social, legal, domestic, and religious customs of Hindus up to the present day.
- All the obligatory duties of Hindus at birth, marriage, death, etc. are guided by Vedic rituals.



### The Upanishads

- The Upanishads are late Vedic Sanskrit texts that supplied the basis of later Hindu philosophy.
- They are commonly referred to as Vedānta, which has been interpreted as the "last chapters, parts of the Veda" and alternatively as "object, the highest purpose of the Veda".
- The aim of all Upanishads is to investigate the nature of Ātman (self), and "direct [ing] the enquirer toward it".
- There are more than 200 known Upanishads, one of which, the Muktikā Upanishad, predates 1656 CE and contains a list of 108 canonical Upanishads, including itself as the last.
- These are further divided into Upanishads associated with Shaktism (goddess Shakti), Sannyasa (renunciation, monastic life), Shaivism (god Shiva), Vaishnavism (god Vishnu).
- The Upanishads present a vision of an interconnected universe with a single, unifying principle behind the apparent diversity in the cosmos, any articulation of which is called brahman.
- Within this context, the Upanishads teach that brahman resides in the atman, the unchanging core of the human individual.



### The Ramayana

- The Ramayana is an ancient Indian epic, composed some time in the 5th century BCE.
- It was composed in Sanskrit by the sage Valmiki .
- The Ramayana was an important influence on later Sanskrit poetry and the Hindu life and culture.
- Its main characters were fundamental to the cultural consciousness of a number of nations, both Hindu and Buddhist.
- The Ramayana belongs to the genre of Itihasa, narratives of past events (purāvṛtta), which includes the Mahabharata, the Puranas, and the Ramayana.
- The genre also includes teachings on the goals of human life.
- The Ramayana is about the exile and then return of Rama, prince of Ayodhya.
- It consists of some 24,000 couplets divided into seven books.
- Valmiki taught the Ramayana to Rama's sons, the twins Lava and Kush.
- By tradition, it is known as the Adi Kavya (adi = original, first; kavya = poem).



# The Mahabharata

The Mahabharata is one of the two great Sanskrit epics of the Hindus, existing in its present form since c. It is one of the two Sanskrit epic poems of ancient India, the other being the Ramayana. The Mahabharata is an important source of information on the development of Hinduism between 400 BCE and 200 CE and is regarded by Hindus as both a text about dharma (Hindu moral law) and a history.

Above all, the Mahabharata is an exposition of dharma (codes of conduct), including the proper conduct of a king, of a warrior, of an individual living in times of calamity, and of a person seeking to attain moksha (freedom from samsara, or rebirth).



### Puranas

- Puranas are a vast genre of Indian literature about a wide range of topics, particularly about legends and other traditional lore .
- The word "Purana" means "ancient" or "old" in Sanskrit .
- Puranas are considered one of the most ancient Indian literature about Hinduism and are post-Vedic Texts .
- Puranas are known for the intricate layers of symbolism depicted within their stories .
- The composition of Puranas dates from the 4th century BCE to about 1,000 A.D .
- Puranas are popular encyclopedic collections of myth, legend, and genealogy, varying greatly as to date and origin .
- Puranas were written almost entirely in narrative couplets, in much the same easy flowing style as the two great Sanskrit epic poems, the Mahabharata and the Ramayana .
- The Puranas are ancient Hindu texts eulogizing various deities of the Hindu pantheon through divine stories .
- The multiple scriptures known by the name of Puranas can be categorized under the same class as the 'Itihasas' or Histories - the Ramayana and the Mahabharata .



### Buddhist Literature in Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit

- **Pali** is an old Prakrit dialect that combines several dialects. Lord Buddha delivered his discourses in Pali, and as a result, early Buddhist literature was written in Pali .
- **Prakrit** is a catch-all name for any language that differs from the mainstream one, Sanskrit. Pali is an old Prakrit dialect that combines several dialects .
- The first Buddhist works were written in Pali, the language spoken in Magadha and South Bihar. It is said that Lord Buddha delivered his sermons in Pali, and they have been recorded in the same language .
- Pali still serves as the canonical language of Buddhism in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Other Prakrit languages such as Sauraseni, Maharastri, Magadhi, and Gandhari embody various facets of the literatures of both the Brahmanical/Hindu and Buddhist traditions .
- The prosodic variety of Buddhist lyrics is great; about 30 different meters can be distinguished. Pali poems, with their new meters (often based on a musical phrase), stylistic features, figures of speech, and choice diction, foreshadow classical kāvya literature in Sanskrit, whose extant specimens date from a later period .
- Early Buddhist texts are therefore often in Prakrit (especially Gāndhārī) or some nonstandard form of Sanskrit, sometimes called Buddhist Sanskrit, an intermediate stage between some Prakrit and standard Sanskrit .



### Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit

- Jain literature refers to the literature of the Jain religion. It is a vast and ancient literary tradition, which was initially transmitted orally.
- The oldest surviving material is contained in the canonical Jain Agamas, which are written in Ardhamagadhi, a Prakrit (Middle-Indo Aryan) language.
- Early Jain literature is mostly written in the Prakrit language, which was a form of the Sanskrit language.
- Pali and Prakrit were the first languages to emerge from Sanskrit. Pali was used for the dissemination of Buddhistic ideas, whereas Prakrit was used for the dissemination of Jain doctrines.
- The majority of Buddhist literature is written in Pali, while Jain literature is written in Prakrit.




### Kautilya’s Arthashastra

- The Arthashastra is an Indian treatise on politics, economics, military strategy, the function of the state, and social organization .
- It is attributed to the philosopher and Prime Minister Kautilya (also known as Chanakya, Vishnugupta, l. c. 350-275 BCE) .
- Kautilya was instrumental in establishing the reign of the great king Chandragupta Maurya (r. c. 321-c.297 BCE), founder of the Mauryan Empire (322-185 BCE) .
- The Arthashastra refers to a practice of political diplomacy that emerged in India .
- Kautilya was a professor at Taxila University and later the Mauryan Empire’s Prime Minister .
- The Arthashastra is also known as “The Science of Material Gain” in Sanskrit .
- It is a singularly important Indian manual on the art of politics .
- Kautilya's Arthashastra depicts a bureaucratic welfare state, in fact some kind of socialized monarchy, in which the central government administers the details of the economy for the common good .
- In addition, Kautilya offers a work of genius in matters of foreign policy and welfare, including key principles of international relations .

### Six System Indian Philosophy

The Six Systems of Indian Philosophy are philosophical traditions that originated in the Indian subcontinent. These systems are known as Vaishesika, Nyaya, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva Mimansa, and Vedanta or Uttara Mimansa.

1. **Nyaya** - Founded by Akshapada Gautama, this school deals with logic and epistemology. It is known for its analysis of logic and its means of knowledge known as inference .
2. **Vaisheshika** - This is the atomistic school of Indian Philosophy.
3. **Samkhya** - This is an extremely complex school of philosophy that goes deep into the understanding of the creator and the creation.
4. **Yoga** - This system is closely related to the Samkhya philosophy.
5. **Purva Mimamsa** - This system focuses on the interpretation of the Vedas.
6. **Vedanta or Uttara Mimamsa** - This system is the conclusion of the Vedas and applies to the Upanishads, which were elaborations of the Vedas, and to the school that arose out of the study of the Upanishads.

Each of these systems differs in one way or the other in terms of its concepts, phenomena, laws, and dogmas.

### Various Philosophical Doctrines in India

Indian philosophy refers to the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. These include both orthodox (astika) systems, namely, the Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva-Mimamsa (or Mimamsa), and Vedanta schools of philosophy, and unorthodox (nastika) systems, such as Buddhism and Jainism .

Some common themes among Indian philosophies include concepts such as dharma, karma, samsara, reincarnation, dukkha, renunciation, and meditation. Almost all of them focus on the ultimate goal of liberation of the individual through a diverse range of spiritual practices (moksha, nirvana)  .

Indian thought has been concerned with various philosophical problems, significant among which are the nature of the world (cosmology), the nature of reality (metaphysics), logic, the nature of knowledge (epistemology), ethics, and the philosophy of religion .

Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought. Flexibility and the tendency to synthesize are also characteristic of Indian philosophy. Indian schools generally embody the doctrines of karma and rebirth; man must be perfected before he can achieve salvation .



### Heterodox Sects in India

Heterodox sects refer to the religious movements that arose in ancient India as a challenge to the orthodox Brahmanical traditions. These sects were founded by ascetic wanderers and teachers who attracted groups of followers and established various sects. Their philosophies encompassed antinomian (belief that divine grace takes away the necessity of obeying moral law), materialist and fatalist elements.

Some of the major heterodox sects that arose in ancient India include:

1. **Jainism**: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, in the 6th century BCE. Jainism is an ascetic religion that emphasizes non-violence and the attainment of liberation through the purification of the soul.

2. **Ajivika**: Founded by Makkhali Gosala in the 5th century BCE, Ajivika was a Śramaṇa movement and a major rival of Vedic religion, early Buddhism, and Jainism.

3. **Cārvāka**: Also known as Lokayata, Cārvāka was a materialist and atheist philosophical movement that rejected the authority of the Vedas and the existence of an afterlife.

These sects represented a significant challenge to the orthodox Brahmanical traditions and played an important role in the development of Indian philosophy and religion. They continue to have a significant influence on Indian culture and society to this day.

### Harappan Technologies in India and Ancient India

### Theatre as Cultural Heritage of India

1. India has a long, rich and illustrious history of folk theatre. Folk theatre is a composite art form in India, with a fusion of elements from music, dance, and many forms. It has deep roots in local identity and native culture and is an important indigenous tool of interpersonal communication .

2. India has the most ancient form of theatre as textual, sculptural, and dramatic -the Natya Shastra. It is the authentic text of theatre art, originated around 500 BC. It is classical and written in Sanskrit by Bharat Muni .

3. Theatre in ancient India played a major role in the overall enrichment of Indian tradition, culture, artistry and creativity since the remote past. The origin of theatre in ancient India has been marked as the result of the religious ritualism of the "Vedic Aryans" .

4. As theatre developed in India and began to flourish again, three types of historical Indian theatre evolved: the classical period, the traditional period, and the modern period .

5. The traditional regional forms of theatre have today reached the stage of heritage, with some, such as Koodiyattam officially being recognized by UNESCO as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity .

6. Kutiyattam, Sanskrit theatre, which is practised in the province of Kerala, is one of India’s oldest living theatrical traditions. Originating more than 2,000 years ago, Kutiyattam represents a synthesis of Sanskrit classicism and reflects the local traditions of Kerala .



# Drama as Cultural Heritage of India

- Traditional theatre has been prevalent in India since ancient times. It was enacted by bards who traveled from one village to another. The stories were based on epics of Mahabharat and Ramayan, as well as, the Puranas and Upanishads.
- Indian drama has originated and developed from the storehouse of Sanskrit in India. It has given its incredible impact and limit to perfection since ancient times. Drama is basically a form of performing arts, where stories are enacted by the utilization of dialogue, music, signal, and dance.
- Since the beginning of our civilization, music, dance, and drama have been an integral aspect of our culture. Initially, these art forms were used as a medium of propagation for religion and social reforms in which music and dance were incorporated to gain popularity. From the Vedic era to the medieval period, the performing arts remained an important source of educating the masses.
- Indian drama is a performance style all its own, including combinations of speech, gesture, music, dance, and sound. Indian drama was highly uninfluenced by other cultures.
- Urdu Drama evolved from the prevailing dramatic traditions of North India shaping Rahas or Raas as practiced by exponents like Wajid Ali Shah, Nawab of Awadh. His dramatic experiments led to the famous Inder Sabha of Agha Hasan Amanat and later this tradition took the shape of Parsi theatre.



# Painting as Cultural Heritage of India

- Indian art forms are a part of the rich culture and heritage of India. Beautiful bursts of color with vivid patterns and symbolic images capture the culture beautifully on the canvas.
- Indian art is among the oldest in the world, with archaeologists discovering paintings that were created over 70,000 years ago. Therefore, Indian paintings hold a special place in history and continue to influence artists to this day.
- Traditional art in India depicted cultural, religious, and political developments through clever metaphors.
- The earliest known Indian paintings can be dated to pre-historic times, such as those found in places like Bhimbetka rock shelters in Madhya Pradesh.
- Each painting style that arose in India symbolized traditions, practices, and ideas that had been passed down from past generations. Though early Indian paintings were created on walls, the art form was later adapted to more contemporary materials such as paper, fabric, canvas, and other materials.
- India has one of the world’s largest collections of songs, music, dance, theatre, folk traditions, performing arts, rites and rituals, paintings, and writings that are known as the ‘Intangible Cultural Heritage’ (ICH) of humanity.



# Martial Arts Traditions of India

- Martial arts are codified systems and traditions of combat practices, which are practised for a variety of reasons – self-defence, competition, physical health and fitness, entertainment, as well as mental, physical, and spiritual development.
- India has an ancient tradition in diverse martial arts.
- India's Martial traditions originated in South India.
- India has its own wrestling art called Khusti.
- Indian Martial arts have influenced Asian martial arts in significant ways.
- Kushti is one of the most popular martial arts in India. It started off during the Mughal times where Malla Yuddha, a local sport was combined with a Persian sport named Varzesh-e-Bastani.
- Indian martial arts refers to the fighting systems of the Indian subcontinent. A variety of terms are used for the English phrases “Indian martial arts”, deriving from ancient sources. While they may seem to imply specific disciplines (e.g. archery, armed combat), by Classical times they were used generically for all fighting systems.
- Kalaripayattu is often known as the ‘Mother Of All Martial Arts’, this 3000-year old art form originating from Kerala, draws inspiration from the raw power, swift movements and sinuous strength of majestic animals – the lion, tiger, elephant, wild boar, snake and crocodile.
- The ancient Manipuri Martial Art, also known as Huyen Lallong, is Thang-Ta. Swords and spears are used in Manipuri Martial arts, a powerful yet gracefully complex technique.
- Lathi is India’s oldest armed martial art. It is also regarded as one of the world’s earliest martial arts weapons.



### Fairs and Festivals as Cultural Heritage of India

India is known for its vibrant and diverse culture, which is reflected in the numerous fairs and festivals celebrated throughout the country. These events are an integral part of the country's cultural heritage and provide a glimpse into the rich traditions and customs of its people.

Some of the most famous fairs and festivals in India include:

1. **Modhera Dance Festival of Gujarat**: Also known as Uttarardh Mahotsavaor, this festival is a cultural celebration held within the premises of the Modhera Sun Temple.
2. **Pongal of South India**: Pongal is a harvest festival celebrated in the southern part of the country.
3. **Konark Dance Festival of Odisha**: This festival celebrates the classical dance forms of India.
4. **Jaisalmer Desert Festival**: This festival is held in the desert city of Jaisalmer and celebrates the rich culture and heritage of Rajasthan.
5. **Goa Carnival**: This festival is celebrated in the state of Goa and is known for its colorful parades and lively music.
6. **Nehru Trophy Boat Race of Kerala**: This boat race is held in the backwaters of Kerala and is a popular event among locals and tourists alike.
7. **Pushkar Mela**: This fair is held in the town of Pushkar in Rajasthan and is one of the largest camel fairs in the world.
8. **Bikaner Camel Festival**: This festival is held in the city of Bikaner in Rajasthan and celebrates the importance of camels in the region.

These are just a few examples of the many fairs and festivals celebrated in India. Each event is unique and provides a glimpse into the rich cultural heritage of the country.

# Indian’s Cultural Contribution to the World

India has made numerous contributions to the world in various fields. Here are some of the most significant contributions:

1. **Mathematics**: Hindus were precursors to mathematics. They made significant contributions to the field of mathematics, including the invention of the concept of zero and the decimal system.

2. **Architecture**: India is known for its picturesque and quirky urban architecture.

3. **Textiles**: The cotton fabrics produced in India are among the best in the world.

4. **Medicine**: Hindus knew the technique to sterilize and use drugs to heal the sick.

5. **Language**: India is the richest linguistic nation in the world.

6. **Art**: India has a rich tradition of art, including painting, sculpture, literature, music, dance, and theatre.

7. **Culture**: Indian culture has greatly increased in prevalence and popularity over the last few decades. All aspects of Indian culture have manifested in various forms throughout popular American culture.

These are just a few examples of the many contributions India has made to the world. India's diverse and talented population has introduced many precious things to the world, and its contributions have become the base for several modern innovations.



# Indian Cinema as a Performing Art in India

- India is a powerhouse of performing arts, a colorful and multi-hued ritual that showcases talent like nothing else.
- In the years to come, cinema in India evolved further as a potent art form capable of mirroring socio-political and economic issues plaguing India with films like Achhut Kanya (1936) and Sujata (1959).
- The year 1931 proved to be a landmark for Indian cinema as it marked the end of the silent era with Ardeshir Irani making India’s first sound film Alam Ara, made in Hindi/Urdu.
- The early commercial success of Phalke’s films not only paved the way for more such motion pictures but also set the ball rolling for cinema as a commercial art form.
- Indian cinema is known for producing the largest number of films in the world. India has one of Asia's largest film industries and employs thousands of people. The Indian film industry makes films in Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Bhojpuri, and other languages.
