### State in Ancient India

- The state in ancient India was a social organization with political power that ensured peace, order and happiness.
- The origin of the state in ancient India is not clear, but there are three main theories: social contract theory, divine origin theory and organic theory.
- Social contract theory: This theory suggests that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among the people to surrender some of their rights and freedoms to a ruler or a group of rulers in exchange for protection and welfare.
- Divine origin theory: This theory claims that the state was created by God or gods and that the ruler or rulers were chosen by divine will or descent.
- Organic theory: This theory holds that the state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the growth and development of human society, and that the ruler or rulers were the representatives or the head of the society.
- The types of states in ancient India were loosely categorized into monarchy, republic and oligarchy.
- Monarchy: This was the most prevalent type of state in ancient India, where the king was the supreme authority and the source of power for the ministers, governors, village councils, town councils and trade guilds . The king was assisted by a council of ministers and a bureaucracy. The king was also bound by the dharma (duty, law, morality) and the advice of the brahmins (priests, scholars, teachers).
- Republic: This was a type of state where the people or their representatives had a say in the governance and administration of the state. The republics were usually formed by clans, tribes or groups of people who shared a common ancestry, territory or occupation. The republics had a chief or a president who was elected by the assembly of the people or their representatives. The republics also had a council of elders or advisors who assisted the chief or the president.
- Oligarchy: This was a type of state where the power was concentrated in the hands of a few people or a class of people who dominated the rest of the society. The oligarchies were usually formed by the aristocrats, the wealthy, the military or the religious elites who controlled the resources, the land and the trade of the state. The oligarchies had a leader or a ruler who was either appointed by the oligarchs or inherited the position from his ancestors.



#### Theories of origin of State in India

The origin of state in India is a subject of debate among scholars of political science and history. There are different theories that attempt to explain how the state emerged and evolved in ancient India. Some of the prominent theories are:

- **Social Contract Theory**: This theory holds that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among individuals who gave up some of their natural rights and freedoms in exchange for security and order. The state was seen as a human creation based on rationality and consent. This theory is found in some ancient texts such as the Mahabharata, the Arthashastra, and the Manusmriti, which describe the state as a result of a contract between the king and the people, or between different clans and tribes. The king was bound by the law and the dharma, and the people had the right to resist or overthrow him if he violated the contract.  

- **Divine Origin Theory**: This theory maintains that the state was established by the will of God or the gods, and the king was the representative or the incarnation of the divine power. The state was seen as a sacred institution based on faith and obedience. This theory is found in some ancient texts such as the Vedas, the Ramayana, and the Puranas, which describe the state as a gift of the gods, or as a manifestation of the cosmic order. The king was regarded as the protector and the benefactor of the people, and the people had the duty to serve and worship him.  

- **Organic Theory**: This theory asserts that the state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the social and historical development of human society. The state was seen as a living organism that grew and changed according to the needs and conditions of the people. This theory is found in some ancient texts such as the Shantiparva of the Mahabharata, the Digha Nikaya of the Buddhist scriptures, and the Jain texts, which describe the state as a product of the evolution of human civilization, or as a response to the challenges and opportunities of the environment. The king was considered as the head or the heart of the social body, and the people had the role to cooperate and contribute to the common good.   

These theories are not mutually exclusive, and they may reflect different aspects and perspectives of the origin of state in India. They may also vary according to the time, place, and context of the ancient texts and traditions. The origin of state in India is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon that cannot be reduced to a single theory or explanation.

### The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

- The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) are a political concept given by Kautilya in his treatise Arthashastra  .
- The seven limbs are: King, Amatya (Bureaucrats), Janapada (Territory), Durga (Fort), Kosa (Treasure), Danda (Coercive Authority), and Mitra (Ally)  .
- The seven limbs are interdependent and interrelated, and the state cannot function without any of them .
- The king is the head of the state and the source of law and justice. He is responsible for the welfare and security of his subjects .
- The amatya are the ministers and officials who assist the king in administration and policy-making. They are selected on the basis of merit and loyalty .
- The janapada is the territory or population of the state. It is the source of revenue and manpower for the state. The king should protect and expand his janapada .
- The durga is the fort or capital of the state. It is the center of power and defense. It should be well-built and well-guarded .
- The kosa is the treasury or wealth of the state. It is the means of financing the state activities and maintaining the army. It should be well-managed and well-audited .
- The danda is the coercive authority or military force of the state. It is the instrument of enforcing law and order and deterring enemies. It should be well-trained and well-equipped .
- The mitra is the ally or friend of the state. It is the partner of the state in times of peace and war. It should be well-chosen and well-maintained .



### Society in Ancient India

- The society in ancient India was based on the concept of **Varna**, which was a four-fold classification of people into four categories: **Brahmins** (priests and scholars), **Kshatriyas** (warriors and rulers), **Vaishyas** (merchants and farmers), and **Shudras** (servants and laborers). 
- The Varna system was not rigid or hereditary, but based on one's qualities, actions, and duties. It was also influenced by the concept of **Karma**, which was the law of cause and effect that determined one's destiny in this life and the next.  
- The society in ancient India was also divided into various **Jatis**, which were sub-groups within each Varna based on one's occupation, region, language, and lineage. The Jatis had their own rules, customs, and traditions, and often formed guilds and associations.  
- The society in ancient India was also organized according to the **Ashrama** system, which was a four-stage division of one's life into four phases: **Brahmacharya** (student), **Grihastha** (householder), **Vanaprastha** (retired), and **Sannyasa** (renunciant). Each stage had its own duties, responsibilities, and goals, and was meant to help one achieve spiritual liberation.  
- The society in ancient India was influenced by various religions, such as **Hinduism**, **Buddhism**, **Jainism**, and **Sikhism**, which shaped the beliefs, values, and practices of the people. These religions taught the concepts of **Dharma** (righteousness), **Artha** (wealth), **Kama** (pleasure), and **Moksha** (liberation), which were the four aims of human life.  
- The society in ancient India was also enriched by various aspects of culture, such as **literature**, **art**, **architecture**, **music**, **dance**, **drama**, **science**, **mathematics**, **medicine**, **astronomy**, **philosophy**, and **law**, which reflected the creativity, diversity, and achievements of the people.  
- The society in ancient India was also marked by various forms of **political organization**, such as **monarchies**, **republics**, **empires**, and **federations**, which governed the different regions and kingdoms of the subcontinent. Some of the notable political entities in ancient India were the **Indus Valley Civilization**, the **Mauryan Empire**, the **Gupta Empire**, the **Harsha Empire**, and the **Mughal Empire**.  
- The society in ancient India was also characterized by various forms of **social change**, such as **urbanization**, **trade**, **migration**, **invasion**, **reform**, and **revival**, which affected the dynamics, interactions, and transformations of the people. Some of the factors that contributed to social change in ancient India were the **Aryan migration**, the **Persian and Greek invasions**, the **Silk Road trade**, the **Buddhist and Jain movements**, the **Muslim conquests**, and the **Bhakti and Sufi movements**.



#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

- Purusārtha (Sanskrit: पुरुषार्थ, IAST: Puruṣārtha) literally means "object (ive) of men" or "human endeavour".
- It is a key concept in Hinduism, and refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. The four puruṣārthas are Dharma (righteousness, moral values), Artha (prosperity, economic values), Kama (pleasure, love, psychological values) and Moksha (liberation, spiritual values).
- These are not only meant to set an ideal objective but also to convey the right means to achieve them. The four puruṣārthas are interrelated and interdependent, and are meant to harmonize the individual and social needs of a person.
- The concept of puruṣārtha is based on the belief that human life has a purpose and a direction, and that one should strive to fulfill one's potential and attain one's ultimate goal. The concept also implies a sense of duty, responsibility and ethics in one's actions.
- The concept of puruṣārtha has influenced the social structure, law, ethics, education, art and literature of ancient India. The four puruṣārthas were seen as the guiding principles of the four varnas (social classes) and the four āśramas (stages of life) in the Hindu society. The puruṣārthas also provided a framework for the development of various schools of thought and practices in Hinduism, such as Vedanta, Yoga, Sāṃkhya, Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, etc. 
- The concept of puruṣārtha is still relevant and applicable in the modern times, as it offers a balanced and holistic approach to life, that integrates the material, emotional, ethical and spiritual aspects of human existence. The concept also encourages a person to seek truth and attain salvation, by following one's dharma and pursuing one's artha, kāma and mokṣa in a harmonious and ethical manner.



#### Varnāshrama System in Society in Ancient India

- The varnāshrama system was a theoretical framework of social and moral order in ancient Hindu society .
- It consisted of two main components: varna and ashrama.
- Varna was the segmental division of the society into four classes: Brahman, Kshatriya, Vaishya and Shudra .
- Brahman was the class of priests and teachers, who were responsible for studying and teaching the sacred scriptures, performing rituals and advising the rulers .
- Kshatriya was the class of kings, warriors and aristocrats, who were responsible for protecting the society, administering justice and leading the military .
- Vaishya was the class of traders, merchants and farmers, who were responsible for producing and distributing wealth, engaging in commerce and supporting the other classes .
- Shudra was the class of servants, laborers and artisans, who were responsible for serving the other classes, performing menial tasks and following the instructions of their superiors .
- Ashrama was the system of stages of life that each individual had to go through according to their varna and age.
- There were four ashramas: Brahmacharya, Grihastha, Vanaprastha and Sannyasa.
- Brahmacharya was the stage of studentship, where the individual had to live with a guru and learn the skills and duties of their varna, as well as practice celibacy and discipline.
- Grihastha was the stage of householdership, where the individual had to marry, raise a family and perform the social and economic obligations of their varna, as well as enjoy the pleasures of life.
- Vanaprastha was the stage of retirement, where the individual had to withdraw from worldly affairs, renounce their possessions and attachments, and prepare for the final stage of life.
- Sannyasa was the stage of renunciation, where the individual had to give up their identity, social ties and worldly desires, and dedicate themselves to the pursuit of spiritual liberation.
- The varnāshrama system was meant to provide a harmonious and hierarchical structure of society, where each class and stage of life had its own duties, rights and privileges, and where the individual could progress towards the ultimate goal of moksha or liberation .
- However, the varnāshrama system was also criticized for being rigid, oppressive and discriminatory, especially towards the lower classes and women, who were denied access to education, freedom and equality .
- The varnāshrama system was also challenged by various social and religious movements, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Bhakti and Sikhism, that rejected the authority of the Vedas, the caste system and the ritualistic practices, and advocated for a more egalitarian and universal vision of humanity .



#### Āshrama or the Stages of Life in Society in Ancient India

- Āshrama (Sanskrit: आश्रम) is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras.
- The Āshrama system is one facet of the Dharma concept in Hinduism, which prescribes the duties and responsibilities of an individual according to their age, occupation, and social status .
- The four Āshramas are: 
  - Brahmacharya (student), marked by chastity, devotion, and obedience to one’s teacher. This stage lasts from childhood until the completion of education, usually around the age of 25.
  - Gṛhastha (householder), requiring marriage, the begetting of children, sustaining one’s family and helping support priests and holy men, and fulfillment of duties toward gods and ancestors. This stage lasts from the end of education until the age of 50.
  - Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller), involving gradual withdrawal from worldly affairs, detachment from material possessions, and contemplation of spiritual matters. This stage lasts from the age of 50 until the age of 75.
  - Sannyasa (renunciate), involving complete renunciation of worldly life, wandering as a mendicant, and seeking liberation from the cycle of birth and death. This stage lasts from the age of 75 until death.
- The Āshrama system was meant to provide a framework for the spiritual and moral development of an individual, as well as to maintain the social order and harmony in ancient India . However, not everyone followed the Āshrama system strictly, and some people chose to skip or combine some stages according to their personal preferences or circumstances.



#### Marriage in Society in Ancient India

- Marriage was a sacred institution that united two halves of life, man and woman, into a single organic whole.
- Marriage was also a social contract that established kinship ties, rights and duties, and mutual obligations between the spouses and their families .
- Marriage was regulated by various laws and customs, such as caste, gotra, varna, and kula, that determined the eligibility and compatibility of the partners .
- Marriage was performed through various rituals and ceremonies, such as the kanyadan, the saptapadi, the homa, and the mangalsutra, that symbolized the sanctity and permanence of the bond .
- Marriage was classified into eight main types, according to the mode of acquisition of the bride and the degree of consent involved, namely Brahma, Daiva, Arsha, Prajapatya, Gandharva, Asura, Rakshasa, and Pisaka .
- Marriage was influenced by various factors, such as economic, political, religious, and cultural, that shaped the norms and expectations of the spouses and their roles and responsibilities in the family and society .



#### Understanding Gender as a Social Category in Society in Ancient India

- Gender is a social construct that defines the roles, expectations and norms of men and women in a given society.
- Gender roles are influenced by various factors such as religion, culture, history, politics, economy and environment.
- In ancient India, gender roles were not fixed or rigid, but varied according to different periods, regions, classes and castes.
- In general, ancient India's society greatly favored men over women, especially in the domains of education, religion, law and politics.
- However, there were also some exceptions and variations in the status and rights of women in ancient India, depending on the sources of evidence and the interpretations of scholars.
- Some of the main points to understand gender as a social category in ancient India are:

  - In the Vedic period (1500-600 BCE), women were considered with respect and dignity. They could participate in religious rituals, learn the Vedas, compose hymns and become rishis (sages). They had the freedom to choose their husbands and could remarry if widowed. Some examples of prominent women in this period are Gargi, Maitreyi, Lopamudra and Ghosha.  
  - In the later Vedic period (600-300 BCE), the status of women declined due to the emergence of the varna (caste) system, the rise of patriarchy and the codification of laws. Women were denied access to education, religious ceremonies and public affairs. They were confined to the domestic sphere and subjected to the authority of their fathers, husbands and sons. They had to practice sati (self-immolation on the husband's pyre), child marriage and dowry. Some examples of oppressive laws in this period are the Manusmriti, the Narada Smriti and the Yajnavalkya Smriti.  
  - In the Mauryan period (321-185 BCE), the status of women improved slightly due to the influence of Buddhism and Jainism, which advocated non-violence, equality and compassion. Women could join the monastic orders, study the scriptures, preach the doctrines and attain salvation. They also enjoyed some economic and legal rights, such as inheritance, property and divorce. Some examples of influential women in this period are Chandragupta Maurya's wife Durdhara, Ashoka's wife Asandhimitra, Ashoka's daughter Sanghamitra and the Buddhist nun Dhammapala.  
  - In the Gupta period (320-550 CE), the status of women deteriorated further due to the revival of Brahmanism, the decline of Buddhism and Jainism, and the invasion of foreign rulers. Women were treated as objects of pleasure, beauty and fertility. They were denied education, religious rights and social mobility. They were subjected to polygamy, purdah (veil), devadasi (temple prostitution) and female infanticide. Some examples of oppressive practices in this period are the Kamasutra, the Harshacharita and the Kalidasa's plays.  
  - In the medieval period (550-1526 CE), the status of women varied according to the different regions, dynasties and religions. In general, women in the south and the east had more freedom and rights than women in the north and the west. Women in the Hindu kingdoms faced more discrimination and violence than women in the Muslim kingdoms. Women in the Buddhist and Jain communities had more opportunities and respect than women in the Brahmanical and Islamic communities. Some examples of contrasting women in this period are the Chola queen Sembiyan Mahadevi, the Rajput queen Padmavati, the Delhi sultan Razia, the poetess Mirabai and the saint Akka Mahadevi.  

- In conclusion, gender as a social category in ancient India was not a monolithic or static concept, but a dynamic and diverse one. It was shaped by various factors and changed over time and space. It reflected the complexities and contradictions of the ancient Indian society and culture.



#### The representation of Women in Historical traditions in Society in Ancient India

- Women in ancient India had a complex and varied status depending on the time period, the region, the religion, and the caste they belonged to.

- During the Vedic period (c. 1500-500 BCE), women also held an honored place in society and had access to the sacred knowledge of the Vedas. They could become rishis (sages), teachers, poets, and philosophers  .
- However, with the rise of the Brahmanical order and the emergence of the Manusmriti (c. 200 BCE-200 CE), a law code that prescribed the duties and rights of different classes and genders, women's status declined gradually. They were subjected to the authority of their fathers, husbands, and sons, and were denied the right to education, property, and independence  .
- During the Mauryan (c. 321-185 BCE) and Gupta (c. 320-550 CE) periods, women's status varied depending on the region and the ruler. Some women, especially from the royal and noble families, enjoyed some privileges and influence in political and religious affairs. For example, Chandragupta Maurya's chief consort Durdhara, Ashoka's wife Asandhimitra, and Chandragupta II's daughter Prabhavatigupta were influential figures in their respective courts . However, most women faced social restrictions and discrimination, and were confined to the domestic sphere  .
- During the medieval period (c. 600-1500 CE), women's status was influenced by the invasions, migrations, and cultural interactions of various groups, such as the Huns, the Turks, the Arabs, the Mongols, and the Europeans. Some women, especially from the lower castes and the tribal communities, enjoyed more freedom and mobility than the upper-caste women, who were subjected to the norms of purdah (veiling and seclusion) and sati (widow immolation) . Some women also rose to prominence as rulers, warriors, saints, poets, and artists, such as Razia Sultana, Rani Durgavati, Mirabai, Lal Ded, and Razia Bai .
- In conclusion, women in ancient India had a diverse and dynamic role in society, which changed according to the historical, geographical, religious, and cultural factors. They were not passive victims of patriarchy, but active agents of change and resistance. They contributed to the rich and varied heritage of ancient India in various fields.



#### Challenges faced by Women in Society in Ancient India

- Women in ancient India had different roles and rights depending on the time period, the region, the religion, and the caste they belonged to. In general, they faced more challenges and oppression as time went by and patriarchy became stronger.
- Some of the challenges faced by women in ancient India were:
  - Lack of autonomy and mobility: Women were dependent on their male relatives for their livelihood, protection, and decision-making. They had limited access to education, property, inheritance, and legal rights. They were expected to stay inside the home and perform domestic duties, and were restricted from traveling or interacting with strangers.
  - Child marriage and widowhood: Women were often married at a very young age, sometimes even before puberty, to men who were much older than them. They had no say in choosing their husbands or in matters of reproduction. They were subjected to physical, sexual, and emotional abuse by their husbands and in-laws. If their husbands died, they were either forced to commit sati (self-immolation on the funeral pyre of their husbands) or to live a life of misery and isolation as widows. They were considered inauspicious and unworthy of respect or happiness .
  - Social and religious discrimination: Women were considered inferior to men in terms of intellect, morality, and spirituality. They were denied the right to study the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, or to perform rituals and sacrifices. They were also subjected to various forms of exploitation and violence, such as prostitution, devadasi system (dedication of young girls to temples as servants of gods), niyoga system (practice of levirate marriage, where a widow had to cohabit with her husband's brother or relative to produce a son), and dasi system (practice of enslaving women captured in wars or raids)   .
- Despite these challenges, some women in ancient India also showed remarkable courage, wisdom, and leadership in various fields, such as politics, literature, arts, and religion. They contributed to the cultural and social development of the country and inspired generations of women to fight for their rights and dignity. Some examples of such women are Gargi, Maitreyi, Draupadi, Sita, Ahalya Bai Holkar, Mirabai, Rani Lakshmibai, and Savitribai Phule.



#### Four-class Classification of Society in Ancient India

- The four-class classification of society in ancient India is also known as the **varna system** or the **caste system**.
- The word varna means "color" or "quality" and refers to the division of people based on their innate characteristics and duties.
- The varna system is derived from the Hindu scriptures called the **Vedas**, which date back to around 1500 BCE.
- The Vedas described four main social classes: **Brahmins**, **Kshatriyas**, **Vaishyas**, and **Shudras** .
- The Brahmins were the priests and scholars who performed religious rituals and taught the sacred texts . They were considered the highest and purest class in the society.
- The Kshatriyas were the kings, governors, and warriors who protected and ruled the society . They were the second highest class and had the privilege of wearing sacred thread and studying the Vedas.
- The Vaishyas were the cattle herders, agriculturists, artisans, and merchants who produced and traded goods and services . They were the third highest class and also had the right to wear sacred thread and study the Vedas, but not to teach them.
- The Shudras were the laborers and service providers who served the other three classes . They were the lowest class and had no access to the Vedas or the sacred thread.
- The varna system was based on the concept of **dharma**, which means duty, law, or morality. Each class had a specific dharma to follow according to their nature and function in the society.
- The varna system was also influenced by the concept of **karma**, which means action, effect, or fate. According to this belief, the actions of a person in their current life would determine their status and class in their next life.
- The varna system was rigid and hierarchical, meaning that there was no mobility or equality among the classes. The classes were also endogamous, meaning that people could only marry within their own class.
- The varna system was not only a social but also a religious and legal system that governed the rights and duties of the people in ancient India. It was also a source of social identity and pride for the people who belonged to the upper classes.
- The varna system was challenged and criticized by some religious movements and thinkers, such as Buddhism, Jainism, and the Bhakti movement, which advocated for more egalitarian and universal values.
- The varna system also evolved and changed over time, giving rise to new sub-classes and groups, such as the **jatis** or the **castes**. The jatis were based on occupation, region, language, and sect, and numbered in the thousands.
- The varna system still exists in some form in modern India, although it is illegal and unconstitutional to discriminate based on caste. The caste system has been a source of social oppression, violence, and injustice for many people, especially the lower castes and the **Dalits** or the **untouchables**, who were considered outside the varna system and subjected to extreme marginalization and exclusion.



#### Slavery in Society in Ancient India

- Slavery was a universal social evil and had its origin even from the early period of history. In India, its origin is closely associated with the caste system.
- The early history of slavery in India is contested because it depends on the translations of terms such as dasa and dasyu. Greek writer Megasthenes in his work Indika, while describing the Maurya Empire states that slavery was banned in Indian society.
- Slavery system in India was very mild and most of the slaves were domestic slaves who were treated as members of the family. The Dharma Shastras of the 4th century B.C. were in a protesting mood against slavery. Slave trade was prohibited in the Shastras.
- Slavery existed in ancient India, where it is recorded in the Sanskrit Laws of Manu of the 1st century B.C. The institution was little documented until the British colonials in the 19th century made it an object of study because of their desire to abolish it.
- Many Africans travelled to India as slaves and traders, but eventually settled down here to play an important role in India's history of kingdoms, conquests and wars. Some of them, like Malik Ambar in Ahmadnagar, became generals, administrators and rulers.

### Evolution of script in India

- A script is a written symbolisation of speech. It consists of a set of characters that represent the sounds of a language.
- India has a long and rich history of writing. It has developed many scripts over time, some of which are still in use today, while others are extinct or historical.
- The earliest evidence of writing in India dates back to the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which used a pictographic script that has not been deciphered yet. This script is known as the Indus script or Harappan script.
- The next major phase of writing in India was the Brahmi script, which emerged around the 3rd century BCE and was used to write various languages, including Prakrit, Sanskrit, Pali, and Tamil. The Brahmi script is considered the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts, as well as some scripts of Sri Lanka, Tibet, Southeast Asia, and Central Asia.
- The Brahmi script evolved into different regional variants over time, depending on the linguistic and cultural influences. Some of the major branches of the Brahmi script are:

  - The Kharosthi script, which was used in the northwest of India, especially in Gandhara and Bactria, from the 3rd century BCE to the 4th century CE. It was derived from the Aramaic script and was written from right to left. It was used to write Prakrit and Gandhari languages.
  - The Gupta script, which was used in northern and central India from the 4th to the 6th century CE. It was a refined and elegant form of the Brahmi script and was used to write Sanskrit and Prakrit languages. It was also the basis of the Siddham script, which was used to write Buddhist texts and spread to China and Japan.
  - The Nagari script, which was used in northern India from the 7th century CE onwards. It was a descendant of the Gupta script and was used to write Sanskrit, Hindi, and other languages. It was also the precursor of the Devanagari script, which is the most widely used script in India today.
  - The Southern Brahmi script, which was used in southern India from the 3rd century BCE to the 6th century CE. It was used to write Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, and other languages. It was also the source of the Grantha script, which was used to write Sanskrit and Tamil, and the Vatteluttu script, which was used to write Malayalam and Tamil.
  - The Eastern Brahmi script, which was used in eastern India from the 3rd century BCE to the 13th century CE. It was used to write Magadhi, Pali, Bengali, Assamese, and other languages. It was also the origin of the Bengali-Assamese script, the Oriya script, and the Tibetan script.

- The evolution of the Indian scripts was influenced by various factors, such as the medium of writing, the style of writing, the regional and linguistic variations, the religious and cultural traditions, and the foreign contacts and invasions. Some of the common features of the Indian scripts are:

  - They are alphasyllabic, meaning they consist of consonants and vowels, where each consonant has an inherent vowel that can be modified by diacritical marks.
  - They are written from left to right, except for the Kharosthi script, which was written from right to left.
  - They are usually written in horizontal lines, except for the Tibetan script, which is written in vertical columns.
  - They have a headstroke or a horizontal line above the characters, which connects them and indicates the word boundary.
  - They have a complex system of conjuncts or ligatures, where two or more consonants are combined into a single character, sometimes with a change in shape or sound.
  - They have a rich and diverse set of symbols, such as numerals, punctuation marks, abbreviations, honorifics, and religious signs.

#### Harappan Script

- Harappan script is the name given to the symbols used by the Indus Valley Civilization, which flourished in the northwest of India and Pakistan from about 2500 to 1900 BCE.
- Harappan script is also known as Indus script, as it was first discovered in the ancient city of Harappa.
- Harappan script consists of about 400 signs, which vary in shape, size, and orientation . Some signs may represent words or concepts, while others may indicate sounds or syllables. This type of writing system is called logo-syllabic.
- Harappan script has not been deciphered yet, as there are no bilingual inscriptions or known underlying language to compare it with  . Many scholars have proposed different interpretations and hypotheses, but none have gained wide acceptance .
- Harappan script was mainly used for short inscriptions on seals, pottery, copper tablets, and other objects . The inscriptions usually contain 4 to 5 signs, but some may have up to 26 signs. The purpose and meaning of these inscriptions are still unknown .
- Harappan script reflects the culture and society of the Indus Valley Civilization, which was highly urbanized, organized, and sophisticated . The script may have been used for trade, administration, religion, or personal communication . The script also shows some regional variations and influences from other writing systems, such as Mesopotamian cuneiform.



#### Brahmi Script

- Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script.
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi .
- Brahmi script appeared as a fully developed script in the third century BCE and was used for writing various languages, such as Sanskrit, Prakrit, Pali, and others .
- Brahmi script is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations. For example, the Lalitavistara Sūtra lists 64 scripts, with Brahmi script starting the list.
- Brahmi script was already divided into regional variants at the time of the earliest surviving epigraphy around the third century BCE. Cursives of the Brahmi script began to diversify further from around the fifth century CE and continued to give rise to new scripts throughout the Middle Ages.
- Brahmi script is thought to have been modelled on the Aramaic or Phoenician alphabets, or possibly derived from the Indus script .

### Hindi Literature

Hindi literature is the literature written in the Hindi language, which includes various dialects and scripts. Hindi literature has a rich and diverse history, spanning from the medieval to the modern period. Some of the main features and topics of Hindi literature are:

  - Ritikal (riti or style period): from the 18th to the 19th century, dominated by the courtly and ornate style of poetry, influenced by Sanskrit and Persian aesthetics. Some of the famous writers of this period are Keshavdas, Bihari, Ghananand, Matiram and Dev .

## Module 3- Indian Religion, Philosophy, and Practices

- India is a diverse and pluralistic country with many religions, philosophies, and practices that have influenced its culture, society, and history.
- Hinduism is the oldest and most dominant religion in India, which developed from the Vedic religion of the ancient Indo-Aryans. Hinduism is a complex and diverse tradition that encompasses many schools of thought, sects, and rituals. Hinduism is based on the concepts of dharma (duty, law, morality), karma (action, cause and effect), samsara (cycle of rebirth), and moksha (liberation from samsara).
- Hinduism has six orthodox schools of philosophy that accept the authority of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. They are: Samkhya (dualism of matter and spirit), Yoga (discipline of body and mind), Nyaya (logic and epistemology), Vaisheshika (atomism and naturalism), Mimamsa (exegesis and ritual), and Vedanta (metaphysics and monism).
- Hinduism also has many heterodox schools of philosophy that reject the authority of the Vedas. They are: Buddhism (founded by Gautama Buddha, who taught the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path to end suffering and attain nirvana), Jainism (founded by Mahavira, who taught the principles of non-violence, non-attachment, and non-absolutism), and Charvaka (materialism and skepticism).
- Hinduism has many gods and goddesses, who are manifestations of the supreme reality, Brahman. Some of the most popular deities are Vishnu (the preserver), Shiva (the destroyer), Ganesha (the elephant-headed god of success), Lakshmi (the goddess of wealth), Durga (the goddess of power), and Saraswati (the goddess of learning).
- Hinduism has many festivals and celebrations, such as Diwali (the festival of lights), Holi (the festival of colors), Navratri (the festival of nine nights), Dussehra (the victory of good over evil), and Ganesh Chaturthi (the birthday of Ganesha).
- Hinduism has many practices and rituals, such as puja (worship), mantra (sacred sound), yoga (physical and mental exercise), meditation (concentration and awareness), pranayama (breath control), and ahimsa (non-violence).
- Hinduism has many sacred places and symbols, such as temples, shrines, rivers, mountains, cows, swastikas, and om.



### Pre-Vedic Religion in India

- Pre-Vedic religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the people who lived in the Indus Valley or Harappan civilization, before the arrival of the Indo-Aryan speakers who brought the Vedic religion to India.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was mainly based on the worship of nature, such as trees, animals, rivers, mountains, and celestial bodies. Some of the prominent deities were the Mother Goddess, the Horned God, the Pashupati (Lord of Animals), and the Proto-Shiva.
- The Pre-Vedic religion also involved the use of symbols, such as the swastika, the pipal tree, the bull, and the unicorn, which later became part of the Vedic and Hindu traditions. The Pre-Vedic religion also had a concept of ritual purity, as evidenced by the presence of bathing tanks and drainage systems in the Harappan cities.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was not based on any written scriptures, but on oral traditions and symbols. The Harappan script, which has not been deciphered yet, may have been used for administrative or commercial purposes, rather than for religious communication.
- The Pre-Vedic religion was gradually influenced and assimilated by the Vedic religion, which was brought by the Indo-Aryan speakers who migrated to India from the Central Asian steppes around 1500 BCE. The Vedic religion was based on the worship of many gods, such as Indra, Varuna, Agni, Surya, and Rudra, through the performance of sacrificial rituals, as described in the Vedas, the oldest sacred texts of Hinduism.
- The Vedic religion also incorporated some elements of the Pre-Vedic religion, such as the reverence for the cow, the worship of the fire, and the belief in reincarnation and karma. The Vedic religion also developed a complex system of social classes, known as the varna system, which divided the society into four main groups: the Brahmins (priests), the Kshatriyas (warriors), the Vaishyas (merchants), and the Shudras (servants).
- The Vedic religion underwent many changes and transformations over time, giving rise to various schools of thought and movements, such as the Upanishads, the Sramanas, the Bhakti, and the Tantra, which shaped the diversity and richness of Hinduism as we know it today.



### Vedic Religion in India

- Vedic religion was the religion of the ancient Indo-European-speaking peoples who entered India about 1500 BCE from the region of present-day Iran.
- It takes its name from the collections of sacred texts known as the Vedas, which are the oldest stratum of religious activity in India for which there exist written materials.
- The Vedas are composed in Vedic Sanskrit and consist of four collections: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda.
- The Vedic religion was based on the performance of sacrifices (yajna) to various gods (devas) who represented natural forces or aspects of the cosmic order.
- The Vedic religion also involved the worship of fire (agni), the sun (surya), the dawn (ushas), the storm (indra), the soma plant (soma), and other deities.
- The Vedic religion was characterized by a hierarchical social structure, with priests (brahmins) at the top, followed by warriors (kshatriyas), commoners (vaishyas) and servants (shudras).
- The Vedic religion also developed the concepts of karma, reincarnation, dharma, and the four stages of life (ashrama).
- The Vedic religion gradually evolved into Brahmanism or Brahminism, which emphasized the authority of the brahmins and the ritualistic interpretation of the Vedas.
- Brahmanism later gave rise to various schools of Hindu philosophy and sects, such as the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, the Puranas, and the six orthodox darshanas.



### Buddhism in India

- Buddhism is an ancient Indian religion that originated in the 6th century BCE in the region of Magadha (now Bihar) and spread throughout northern India and beyond.
- Buddhism is based on the teachings and life experiences of Siddhartha Gautama, also known as the Buddha (the awakened one), who attained enlightenment under a bodhi tree and taught the four noble truths and the eightfold path to end suffering and achieve nirvana.
- Buddhism has three main branches: Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana, each with different doctrines, practices and traditions. Theravada is the oldest and most conservative branch, followed mainly in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Mahayana is the largest and most diverse branch, followed mainly in East Asia and Central Asia. Vajrayana is the smallest and most esoteric branch, followed mainly in Tibet, Nepal and Mongolia.
- Buddhism flourished in India for over a thousand years, from the 3rd century BCE to the 12th century CE, and produced many schools, sects, texts, art, architecture and culture. Buddhism also influenced other Indian religions, such as Hinduism, Jainism and Sikhism, and vice versa.
- Buddhism in India faced many challenges and declines, such as the rise of Hindu revivalism, the invasions of foreign rulers, the corruption and degeneration of monastic institutions, and the loss of patronage and support from the masses. Buddhism eventually disappeared from most parts of India by the 13th century CE, except for some pockets in the Himalayan regions and the south.
- Buddhism in India was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries, due to the efforts of reformers, scholars, missionaries, social movements and political leaders. Some of the notable figures who contributed to the revival of Buddhism in India are Anagarika Dharmapala, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Dalai Lama, Bhikkhu Bodhi and Thich Nhat Hanh.
- Buddhism in India today is a minority religion, with about 8.4 million followers, or 0.7% of the population, according to the 2011 census. Most of the Buddhists in India belong to the Dalit, tribal or Himalayan communities, who converted to Buddhism as a way of emancipation, empowerment and identity. Buddhism in India also attracts many foreign tourists, pilgrims and seekers, who visit the ancient Buddhist sites and learn from the contemporary Buddhist teachers.



### Jainism in India

- Jainism is an Indian religion that teaches the path to spiritual purity and enlightenment through nonviolence and reducing harm to all living beings .
- Jainism originated in India at least 2,500 years ago and is one of the oldest religions in the world . It is also one of the three most ancient Indian religious traditions along with Hinduism and Buddhism.
- The spiritual goal of Jainism is to attain moksha, or liberation from the cycle of rebirth, by following the three jewels of right perception, right knowledge and right conduct .
- Jains believe in the existence of eternal souls (jivas) and non-soul substances (ajivas) that make up the universe . They also believe in the concept of karma, which binds the souls to the cycle of rebirth according to their actions .
- Jains follow the five vows of ahimsa (nonviolence), satya (truthfulness), asteya (non-stealing), brahmacharya (celibacy or chastity) and aparigraha (non-attachment or non-possessiveness) . These vows are observed more strictly by the ascetics (monks and nuns) than by the lay followers (householders) .
- Jains worship the 24 tirthankaras, or spiritual teachers, who have attained moksha and shown the way to others . The most recent and prominent tirthankara is Mahavira, who lived in the 6th century BCE and is considered the founder of Jainism by some scholars .
- Jains have built many temples, shrines and monuments across India, especially in places associated with the tirthankaras, such as Rajgir, Shravanabelagola, Palitana and Ranakpur . They also celebrate various festivals, such as Paryushana, Mahavir Jayanti, Diwali and Kshamavani .
- Jains are a small but influential minority in India, constituting about 0.4% of the population, or about 4.5 million people . They are mostly concentrated in the states of Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Gujarat and Karnataka. They are also the most affluent and educated religious group in India, with a majority belonging to the higher General Category caste. They have contributed significantly to the fields of business, education, politics, arts and culture in India.



### Six System Indian Philosophy

The six systems of Indian philosophy are:

- **Nyaya**: A school that deals with logic and epistemology. It was founded by Akshapada Gautama in the 2nd century BCE. It proposes four sources of valid knowledge: perception, inference, comparison and testimony. It also analyzes the nature of arguments, fallacies, syllogisms and proofs .
- **Vaisheshika**: A school that deals with metaphysics and ontology. It was founded by Kanada Kashyapa in the 6th century BCE. It proposes that reality is composed of six categories: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity and inherence. It also postulates that atoms are the ultimate constituents of matter .
- **Samkhya**: A school that deals with cosmology and psychology. It was founded by Kapila in the 6th century BCE. It proposes that reality is composed of two principles: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter). It also explains the evolution of the universe and the human mind from prakriti and the liberation of purusha from prakriti .
- **Yoga**: A school that deals with ethics and spirituality. It was founded by Patanjali in the 2nd century BCE. It proposes that the goal of human life is to attain kaivalya (isolation) of purusha from prakriti. It also prescribes an eightfold path of discipline and meditation to achieve this goal .
- **Purva Mimamsa**: A school that deals with hermeneutics and ritual. It was founded by Jaimini in the 3rd century BCE. It proposes that the Vedas are the source of valid knowledge and that the performance of Vedic sacrifices is the duty of human beings. It also analyzes the rules of interpretation and the methods of exegesis of the Vedas .
- **Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta**: A school that deals with theology and philosophy. It was founded by Badarayana in the 4th century BCE. It proposes that the Upanishads are the essence of the Vedas and that the ultimate reality is Brahman, the absolute and impersonal principle of existence. It also explores the relation between Brahman, the individual self (atman) and the world .



### Shankaracharya and Advaita Vedanta

- Shankaracharya, also known as Adi Shankara, was an Indian philosopher and theologian who expounded the doctrine of Advaita Vedanta, the non-dualistic school of Hindu philosophy  .
- Advaita Vedanta is based on the Upanishads, the ancient scriptures that form the final part of the Vedas, the sacred texts of Hinduism. Advaita Vedanta teaches that the ultimate reality is Brahman, the supreme self, which is identical to the individual self (atman) of each person .
- Shankaracharya was born in the 8th century CE in a Brahmin family in Kaladi, a village in present-day Kerala. He renounced the worldly pleasures at a very young age and became a wandering monk. He studied under various teachers, including Govinda Bhagavatpada, a disciple of Gaudapada, the author of the Mandukya Karika, a key text of Advaita Vedanta  .
- Shankaracharya wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras, and the Bhagavad Gita, the three foundational texts of Vedanta. He also composed original works, such as the Vivekachudamani, the Upadesasahasri, and the Crest-Jewel of Discrimination. He defended Advaita Vedanta against the criticisms of other schools of Hindu philosophy, such as Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, and Buddhism .
- Shankaracharya established four mathas, or monasteries, in the four corners of India: Sringeri in the south, Dwarka in the west, Puri in the east, and Badrinath in the north. He appointed four of his disciples as the heads of these mathas, and entrusted them with the task of preserving and propagating Advaita Vedanta. He also initiated the Dashanami order of sannyasins, or renunciates, who follow the ten names of Shankara  .
- Shankaracharya is regarded as one of the greatest philosophers and saints of India. He is revered by Advaita Vedantins as the incarnation of Shiva, the destroyer of ignorance. He is also honored by other Hindu traditions as a reformer and a unifier of Hinduism. He is credited with reviving the Vedic culture and the worship of the six deities: Shiva, Vishnu, Shakti, Ganesha, Surya, and Skanda  .
- Shankaracharya's influence on Indian thought and culture is immense. He is considered the founder of the Smarta tradition, which follows the Smriti texts and the Vedanta philosophy. He is also the inspiration for many later philosophers and teachers of Advaita Vedanta, such as Vachaspati Mishra, Padmapada, Sureshvara, Mandana Mishra, and Ramanuja. His works have been translated and interpreted by many scholars and seekers of truth, both in India and abroad .



### Various Philosophical Doctrines in India

- Indian philosophy refers to the systems of thought and reflection that were developed by the civilizations of the Indian subcontinent.
- Indian philosophy is characterized by its diversity, continuity, synthesis, and spiritual orientation .
- Indian philosophy accepts the authority of ancient philosophers and innovations are represented as extensions of older systems of thought.
- Indian philosophy shares many concepts such as dharma, karma, samsara, dukkha, renunciation, meditation, with almost all of them focusing on the ultimate goal of liberation of the individual from dukkha and samsara through diverse range of spiritual practices (moksha, nirvana).
- Indian philosophy can be broadly classified into two categories: orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) .
- Orthodox schools accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, while unorthodox schools reject or question the Vedas .
- The six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaisheshika, Purva-Mimamsa, and Vedanta .
- Sāṃkhya is a philosophical tradition which regards the universe as consisting of two independent realities: puruṣa (the self or soul) and prakṛti (the material nature).
- Yoga is a school similar to Sāṃkhya (or perhaps even a branch of it) which accepts a personal god and focuses on yogic practices for attaining liberation.
- Nyāya is a school of logic and epistemology which analyzes the sources and validity of knowledge and inference.
- Vaisheshika is a school of atomism and naturalism which postulates that the universe is composed of six categories of substances: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence.
- Purva-Mimamsa (or Mimamsa) is a school of ritual exegesis and hermeneutics which interprets the Vedas and prescribes the performance of sacrifices and ethical duties for attaining heaven.
- Vedanta is a school of metaphysics and theology which expounds the teachings of the Upanishads, the philosophical portions of the Vedas, and develops various systems of monism, dualism, and qualified monism.
- The three unorthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: Buddhism, Jainism, and Cārvāka .
- Buddhism is a religion and philosophy founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha) which teaches the four noble truths, the eightfold path, and the doctrine of dependent origination.
- Jainism is a religion and philosophy founded by Mahavira (the Jina) which teaches the principles of non-violence, non-absolutism, and non-possessiveness, and the doctrine of multiple viewpoints.
- Cārvāka is a school of materialism and skepticism which denies the existence of anything beyond the four elements of earth, water, fire, and air, and rejects the notions of soul, karma, and rebirth.



### Heterodox Sects in India

- Heterodox sects are those that do not accept the authority of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, and challenge the orthodox Brahmanical traditions.
- Heterodox sects emerged in the 6th century BCE as part of the Sramana movement, which was a reaction to the ritualistic and hierarchical Vedic religion.
- Heterodox sects advocated a path of renunciation, asceticism, and ethical conduct for the attainment of liberation from the cycle of rebirth.
- Some of the major heterodox sects in India are:

  - Jainism: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, Jainism teaches the doctrine of ahimsa (non-violence), anekantavada (non-absolutism), and aparigraha (non-attachment). Jains believe in the existence of soul and karma, and practice strict rules of vegetarianism, fasting, and non-injury to all living beings. Jainism has two main sects: Digambara (sky-clad) and Svetambara (white-clad).
  - Buddhism: Founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, Buddhism teaches the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path for the cessation of suffering and the attainment of nirvana. Buddhists do not believe in the existence of a permanent soul or a creator god, and emphasize the impermanence and interdependence of all phenomena. Buddhism has many branches, such as Theravada, Mahayana, Vajrayana, and Zen.
  - Ajivika: Founded by Makkhali Gosala, Ajivika is a materialist and deterministic sect that denies the existence of free will, soul, karma, and rebirth. Ajivikas believe that everything is predetermined by the cosmic force of niyati (fate), and that human actions have no moral consequences. Ajivika is considered to be an extinct sect, as no authentic texts or followers survive today.
  - Carvaka: Also known as Lokayata, Carvaka is a radical materialist and atheist sect that rejects the existence of anything beyond the four elements of earth, water, fire, and air. Carvakas deny the validity of inference, testimony, and analogy as sources of knowledge, and rely only on perception and logic. Carvakas advocate a hedonistic and pragmatic way of life, and ridicule the concepts of soul, karma, rebirth, and liberation.



### Bhakti Movement in India

- The Bhakti movement was a significant religious movement in medieval Hinduism that sought to bring religious reforms to all strata of society by adopting the method of devotion to achieve salvation.
- The movement preached against the caste system, ritualism, idolatry, and social inequalities, and emphasized the personal and emotional relationship between the devotee and the personal god or goddess .
- The movement originated in South India in the 7th-8th centuries CE with the Alvars (Vaishnavite saints) and the Nayanars (Shaivite saints), who composed devotional hymns in Tamil and visited various sacred shrines .
- The movement spread to North India in the 12th-13th centuries CE with the Bhakti saints such as Ramananda, Kabir, Nanak, Tulsidas, Surdas, Mirabai, and Chaitanya, who composed devotional songs in Hindi, Punjabi, Bengali, and other regional languages, and propagated the message of love, equality, and harmony .
- The movement had a profound impact on the Indian society and culture, as it challenged the authority of the Brahmins, the Vedas, and the orthodox rituals, and promoted the vernacular languages, literature, music, and art forms  .
- The movement also contributed to the growth of various sects and cults within Hinduism, such as the Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism, Sikhism, and the Bhakti sects of Ramanuja, Madhva, Vallabha, and Nimbarka  .



### Sufi movement in India

- Sufism is a mystical branch of Islam that emphasizes the direct experience of God through love, devotion, and ethical conduct.
- Sufism originated in the Middle East and Central Asia in the early centuries of Islam, and spread to India through the travels and teachings of Sufi saints or sheikhs.
- The Sufi movement in India began in the 13th century and reached its peak in the 15th and 16th centuries under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal Empire.
- The Sufi movement was a socio-religious movement that aimed to reform the society and promote harmony among different religious groups.
- The Sufis established various orders or silsilas, each with its own doctrines, rituals, and practices. Some of the prominent silsilas in India were Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi, and Shattari.
- The Sufis preached the message of love, tolerance, and service to humanity. They also composed poetry, music, and literature in various languages, such as Persian, Arabic, Urdu, and Hindi.
- The Sufis attracted a large number of followers from different backgrounds, including Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, and others. They also influenced the Bhakti movement, which was a parallel movement of devotional Hinduism.
- The Sufi movement contributed to the cultural, social, and religious diversity of India. It also left a lasting legacy of art, architecture, and literature. Some of the famous Sufi saints in India were Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, Nizamuddin Auliya, Amir Khusrau, Baba Farid, and Bulleh Shah.



### Socio religious reform movement of 19th century in India

- Socio religious reform movement of 19th century in India refers to the wave of reforming activities that influenced the Indian society and religion through the efforts of various samajas, societies and other organisations .
- The main causes of the emergence of the reform movements were:

### Astronomy in India and Ancient India

- Astronomy in India has a long history stretching from pre-historic to modern times. Some of the earliest roots of Indian astronomy can be dated to the period of Indus Valley civilisation or earlier.
- Astronomy in ancient India developed to a sophisticated level as the sages of ancient India were keen observers of the sky generation after generation. They recorded their observations and calculations in various texts, such as the Vedas, the Puranas, the Siddhantas, the Aryabhatiya, the Surya Siddhanta, and the Brihat Samhita.
- Astronomy in ancient India can be divided into two major classes. From the third-century BCE, India started having contact with Greece and other nations outside the subcontinent. So, the basic structure of Indian astronomy had outside influence although the astronomical concepts and the major parameters were India’s own.
- The first class of Indian astronomy is called Vedanga Jyotisha, which is based on the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. Vedanga Jyotisha deals with the measurement of time and the calendar, the determination of auspicious moments for rituals, and the prediction of eclipses and planetary movements.
- The second class of Indian astronomy is called Siddhantic astronomy, which is based on the Siddhantas, or treatises, that were composed by various astronomers from the fifth century CE onwards. Siddhantic astronomy deals with the mathematical and geometric aspects of astronomy, such as the calculation of the positions and motions of the Sun, the Moon, the planets, and the stars, the determination of the size and shape of the Earth, and the construction of astronomical instruments.
- Some of the most prominent astronomers of ancient India were Aryabhata, Varahamihira, Brahmagupta, Bhaskara, and Lalla. They made significant contributions to the fields of trigonometry, algebra, arithmetic, geometry, cosmology, and astrology. They also developed methods for solving various astronomical problems, such as the calculation of the sine, cosine, and tangent functions, the solution of quadratic and indeterminate equations, the estimation of pi, the derivation of the heliocentric model, and the explanation of lunar and solar eclipses  .
- Astronomy in ancient India was not only a science but also a part of the culture and religion of the people. Astronomy was used for determining the dates of festivals, ceremonies, and rituals, as well as for casting horoscopes and making predictions. Astronomy was also linked to the philosophy and mythology of the Hindus, who believed that the universe was created, maintained, and destroyed by the gods, and that the cycles of time were governed by the laws of karma and dharma .

### Physics in India and Ancient India

Physics is the branch of science that studies the nature and properties of matter and energy. Physics in India has a long and rich history, dating back to the ancient times. Here are some points to note about physics in India and ancient India:

- Indian philosophers developed various schools of thought that explored the concepts of elements, atoms, motion, gravity, light, heat, sound, and cosmology. Some of the prominent schools were Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, and Vedanta.  
- One of the earliest proponents of atomism in India was Maharishi Kanada, who founded the Vaisheshika school around the 6th century BCE. He postulated that all matter is composed of indivisible atoms (parmanu) that have different shapes, sizes, and qualities. He also proposed the idea of anu, which are the smallest units of space, time, and motion.  
- Another influential atomist in India was Nagarjuna, who was a Buddhist philosopher and alchemist in the 2nd century CE. He developed the concept of shunyata, which means emptiness or voidness. He argued that all phenomena are empty of inherent existence and depend on causes and conditions. He also suggested that atoms are not solid, but have pores and are in constant motion. 
- Indian astronomers and mathematicians made significant contributions to the fields of celestial mechanics, planetary motion, eclipses, calendar, and trigonometry. Some of the notable figures were Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, Varahamihira, Bhaskara, and Lalla. They developed sophisticated methods to calculate the positions and motions of the sun, moon, planets, and stars, as well as the duration and occurrence of eclipses. They also devised accurate systems of measurement and units of time.  
- Indian physicists also explored the nature of light, heat, sound, and electricity. For example, the Nyaya school proposed that light is composed of discrete particles that travel in straight lines. The Samkhya school suggested that heat is a form of energy that causes expansion and contraction of matter. The Vedanta school explained that sound is a vibration of air that can be perceived by the ear. The Vaisheshika school speculated that electricity is a type of fire that can be produced by friction.  
- Indian physics also had a strong connection with spirituality and metaphysics. Many Indian physicists believed that the ultimate reality is Brahman, which is the source and essence of all existence. They also believed that the human soul (atman) is a part of Brahman and can attain liberation (moksha) by realizing its true nature. They also explored the concepts of karma, reincarnation, and yoga, which are the laws and practices of the cosmic order.  

These are some of the main points about physics in India and ancient India. Physics in India has a rich legacy that reflects the diversity and depth of Indian culture and civilization.

Zinc was used for making brass, an alloy of copper and zinc, which was used for making coins, vessels, ornaments, and instruments. - **Agriculture and botany**: The people of northwestern India had a well-developed science of agriculture as early as 2500 BCE, as evidenced by the ruins of Mohenjo-daro, which have yielded seeds of wheat, barley, millet, dates, melons, cotton, and other crops. The ancient Indians also had knowledge of plant physiology, morphology, genetics, and ecology, and used various methods of irrigation, fertilization, pest control, and crop rotation. They also cultivated medicinal plants and herbs, and developed systems of Ayurveda and Siddha, which are based on the holistic healing of the body and mind using natural substances.
- **Zoology and animal husbandry**: The ancient Indians had a keen interest in observing and studying animals, both wild and domesticated. They had knowledge of animal anatomy, physiology, behavior, and ecology, and used animals for various purposes, such as food, clothing, transportation, warfare, and companionship. They also practiced animal husbandry, breeding, and veterinary medicine, and developed systems of animal welfare and ethics. They also revered some animals as sacred, such as the cow, the elephant, the monkey, and the snake, and incorporated them into their mythology and religion.
- **Human biology and medicine**: The ancient Indians had a sophisticated understanding of human biology and medicine, based on the concept of the four humors (blood, phlegm, bile, and wind) and the three doshas (vata, pitta, and kapha), which govern the health and disease of the body and mind. They also had knowledge of human anatomy, physiology, embryology, genetics, and psychology, and used various methods of diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and surgery. They also developed systems of yoga, meditation, and pranayama, which are based on the harmonization of the body, mind, and spirit.

Biology in India and ancient India was a rich and diverse field of knowledge, which influenced and was influenced by the culture, philosophy, and religion of the people. It also contributed to the development of biology in other parts of the world, such as China, Greece, Persia, and Arabia .



### Harappan Technologies in India and Ancient India

- The Harappan Civilization, also known as the Indus Valley Civilization, was one of the earliest urban cultures of the Indian subcontinent, dating from 3300 to 1300 BCE .
- The Harappans developed various technologies and innovations in different fields, such as:
  - **Metallurgy**: They used copper, bronze, lead, and tin to make tools, weapons, ornaments, and seals. They also knew how to smelt iron, but did not use it extensively .
  - **Standardization**: They devised a system of standardized weights and measures, based on a decimal scale and a unit of length called the Indus inch. They also used a uniform script, which has not been deciphered yet .
  - **Seal carving**: They produced thousands of seals, mostly made of steatite, with various motifs and inscriptions. The seals were used for trade, administration, and ritual purposes. Some of the seals depict animals, such as the unicorn, the bull, the elephant, and the rhinoceros .
  - **Hydraulic engineering**: They built sophisticated water management systems, such as wells, reservoirs, canals, drains, and baths. They also had a sanitation system, with toilets, drains, and sewers. They practiced rainwater harvesting and irrigation for agriculture .
  - **Urban planning**: They constructed planned cities, with grid-like layouts, fortified walls, and gateways. They also built public buildings, such as granaries, warehouses, and citadels. They used baked bricks of uniform size and shape for construction .
  - **Art and craft**: They created various forms of art and craft, such as pottery, terracotta figurines, beads, jewelry, ivory, and shell objects. They also painted and decorated their pottery and walls with geometric and floral patterns .



### Water Management in India and Ancient India

Water is a vital resource for human existence and development. India has a rich and diverse history of water management, dating back to the ancient civilizations of Harappa and Vedic India. Some of the key aspects of water management in India and ancient India are:

- **Hydrological knowledge**: Ancient Indians had a good understanding of the hydrological cycle, the seasonal variations of rainfall, the types of rivers and streams, and the methods of measuring and forecasting water availability. They also had a scientific approach to water quality, using natural materials and plants to purify and treat water .
- **Hydraulic structures**: Ancient Indians built various types of hydraulic structures, such as dams, reservoirs, canals, wells, tanks, and stepwells, to store, divert, distribute, and regulate water for various purposes, such as irrigation, domestic use, religious rituals, and urban planning. Some of these structures are still in use today, and some have been revived to cope with the current water crisis .
- **Water conservation**: Ancient Indians practiced various methods of water conservation, such as rainwater harvesting, watershed management, groundwater recharge, and water recycling. They also had a strong sense of water ethics, respecting water as a sacred and precious resource, and avoiding wastage and pollution .
- **Water governance**: Ancient Indians had a well-organized system of water governance, involving both state and community institutions. They had laws and regulations to ensure equitable and sustainable use of water, and to resolve water conflicts. They also had a participatory and cooperative approach to water management, involving various stakeholders, such as farmers, priests, engineers, and rulers .

Water management in India and ancient India is a fascinating and relevant topic, as it shows the wisdom and innovation of the past, and the challenges and opportunities of the present and future.



### Textile Technology in India and Ancient India

### Writing Technology in India and Ancient India

- Writing technology refers to the tools and methods used to create, preserve and transmit written texts.
- India has a rich and diverse history of writing technology, spanning from the ancient Indus Valley civilization to the modern digital era.
- Some of the main aspects of writing technology in India and ancient India are:

  - Writing systems: India has developed and used various writing systems, such as the syllabic Kharosthi and semialphabetic Brahmi scripts of ancient India, the Devanagari script of Sanskrit and Hindi, the Perso-Arabic script of Urdu, and the many regional scripts derived from Brahmi, such as Bengali, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam, Tamil, Telugu, etc. India also has a tradition of oral literature, such as the Vedas and the epics, that predates the written form.
  - Writing materials: India has used various materials for writing, such as stone, metal, clay, palm leaves, birch bark, paper, cloth, etc. Stone was the principal writing material in ancient India, as it was durable and widely available. Engravings on stone, such as the edicts of emperor Ashoka, are among the oldest surviving written records in India. Metal was also used for inscriptions, coins, seals, etc. Clay was used for making tablets, seals, and tokens. Palm leaves and birch bark were used for making manuscripts, especially for religious and literary texts. Paper was introduced in India by the Arabs in the 10th century CE, and became the dominant writing material by the 15th century CE. Cloth was used for making banners, flags, scrolls, etc.
  - Writing instruments: India has used various instruments for writing, such as styluses, pens, brushes, ink, etc. Styluses were used for engraving on stone, metal, or clay. Pens were made from reeds, bamboo, feathers, or metal. Brushes were made from animal hair or plant fibers. Ink was made from various substances, such as soot, charcoal, plant juices, minerals, etc. Ink was usually stored in inkpots or shells, and applied with pens or brushes on the writing surface.
  - Writing devices: India has used various devices for writing, such as tablets, scrolls, books, typewriters, computers, etc. Tablets were made from clay, stone, or metal, and were used for writing short texts, such as receipts, contracts, or letters. Scrolls were made from paper or cloth, and were used for writing longer texts, such as religious scriptures, legal documents, or histories. Books were made from paper or palm leaves, and were bound by stitching, stringing, or folding. Typewriters were introduced in India in the 19th century CE, and were used for writing official, commercial, or personal documents. Computers were introduced in India in the 20th century CE, and were used for writing, editing, storing, and transmitting texts in various formats and languages.
  - Writing innovations: India has contributed to various innovations in writing technology, such as the concept of zero, the decimal system, the place value system, the numerals, the chess notation, the musical notation, the shorthand, the Braille script, the transliteration software, etc. India also has a tradition of cryptography, steganography, and watermarking, which are techniques for encoding, hiding, or verifying written texts. India also has a tradition of calligraphy, which is the art of writing beautifully and elegantly. India also has a tradition of printing, which is the process of reproducing texts by mechanical means. India has used various methods of printing, such as block printing, lithography, offset printing, digital printing, etc.

- Writing technology in India and ancient India reflects the cultural, linguistic, religious, and historical diversity and richness of the region. Writing technology also influences and is influenced by the social, political, and economic aspects of the region. Writing technology is an important part of the history of science and technology in the Indian subcontinent.



### Pyrotechnics in India and Ancient India

Pyrotechnics is the science and art of creating fireworks and other effects involving the use of explosives, fire, smoke, sound and light. Pyrotechnics has a long and rich history in India, dating back to the medieval period. Here are some points to note about pyrotechnics in India and ancient India:

- One of the earliest notes of pyrotechnical shows in India is made by Abdur Razzaq, the ambassador of the Timurid Sultan Shahrukh to the court of the Vijayanagar king Devaraya II in 1443. He wrote that he witnessed a grand display of fireworks at the king's palace, which lasted for an hour and filled the sky with various shapes and colors.
- Fireworks and pyrotechnic shows existed as a form of royal entertainment in many medieval Indian kingdoms during festivals, events and special occasions like weddings. Manufacturing formulas for fireworks describing pyrotechnic mixtures are found within Kautukachintamani, a Sanskrit volume by Gajapati Prataparudradeva (1497-1539), a reputed king of Orissa .
- Gunpowder, an essential ingredient of fire crackers, was unknown in ancient India. It was introduced to India by the Mongols and the Arabs in the 13th century. The earliest reference to gunpowder in India is found in the Tarikh-i-Firishta (History of the Deccan) by Muhammad Qasim Firishta, who wrote that the Bahmani Sultanate used gunpowder and cannons in the siege of Warangal in 1363.
- The Mughal Empire, which ruled most of India from the 16th to the 18th century, was also fond of fireworks and pyrotechnics. The Mughal emperors, especially Akbar, Jahangir and Shah Jahan, patronized the art of fireworks and employed skilled pyrotechnicians to create spectacular displays for their courts and celebrations. The Ain-i-Akbari, a record of Akbar's administration, mentions several types of fireworks, such as rockets, fountains, wheels, stars, snakes and birds.
- The British East India Company, which gradually gained control over India from the 18th century onwards, also adopted the use of fireworks and pyrotechnics for their military and ceremonial purposes. They imported fireworks from China and Europe and also developed their own varieties. They used fireworks to celebrate their victories, such as the Battle of Plassey in 1757 and the Battle of Buxar in 1764. They also used fireworks to mark important occasions, such as the coronation of King George III in 1761 and the visit of King George V and Queen Mary to India in 1911.
- The Indian independence movement, which emerged in the late 19th and early 20th century, also used fireworks and pyrotechnics as a form of protest and resistance against the British colonial rule. The Indian National Congress, the main political party of the movement, organized fireworks displays to celebrate important dates, such as the first anniversary of the Quit India Movement in 1943 and the first anniversary of the Indian Independence Act in 1948. The Indian freedom fighters, such as Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad and Subhash Chandra Bose, also used fireworks and explosives to carry out attacks on the British targets, such as the Lahore Central Assembly in 1929 and the Chittagong Armoury Raid in 1930.
- Today, fireworks and pyrotechnics are an integral part of the Indian culture and tradition. They are widely used to celebrate various festivals, such as Diwali, Holi, Dussehra, Eid, Christmas and New Year. They are also used to mark personal and social events, such as weddings, birthdays, anniversaries and inaugurations. India is one of the largest producers and consumers of fireworks in the world, with an estimated annual turnover of over Rs. 20,000 crore. The major centers of fireworks production in India are Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu, Champahati in West Bengal, Shivkashi in Uttar Pradesh and Khairatabad in Telangana .

### India’s Dominance up to Pre-colonial Times and Ancient India

## Module 5- Cultural Heritage and Performing Arts

- Cultural heritage is the legacy of physical and intangible attributes of a group or society that are inherited from past generations, maintained in the present and bestowed for the benefit of future generations.
- Cultural heritage includes tangible culture (such as buildings, monuments, landscapes, books, works of art, and artefacts), intangible culture (such as folklore, traditions, language, and knowledge), and natural heritage (such as culturally significant landscapes, and biodiversity).

- Performing arts are often performed in front of an audience, but may also be performed for a specific purpose, such as ritual, education, or therapy.

- Performing arts can also reflect human creativity and innovation, and contribute to the social, economic, and cultural development of a community or a country.

### Indian Architecture in Ancient India and present times

- Indian architecture is rooted in the history, culture, and religion of India. It reflects the diversity and richness of Indian civilization and its interactions with other cultures and regions.
- Indian architecture can be broadly classified into two main categories: Hindu temple architecture and Indo-Islamic architecture. Both have distinctive features, styles, and influences that evolved over time and across regions.
- Hindu temple architecture is based on the principles of Vastu Shastra, a traditional system of architecture that prescribes the layout, design, and proportions of temples and other buildings. Hindu temples are usually dedicated to a specific deity and consist of a sanctum (garbhagriha), a hall (mandapa), and a tower (shikhara). Hindu temples can be further divided into Nagara, Dravidian, and Vesara styles, depending on the shape and ornamentation of the tower .
- Indo-Islamic architecture is the result of the fusion of Islamic and Indian architectural elements, especially during the medieval period. It is characterized by the use of arches, domes, minarets, and geometric patterns, as well as the incorporation of Hindu motifs and symbols. Some of the prominent examples of Indo-Islamic architecture are Rajput architecture, Mughal architecture, and Indo-Saracenic architecture.
- Rajput architecture is the style of architecture developed by the Rajput kingdoms of northern India from the 11th to the 19th century. It is influenced by both Hindu and Islamic styles, and features elaborate fortifications, palaces, temples, and cenotaphs. Some of the famous Rajput monuments are the Chittor Fort, the Amber Fort, and the Hawa Mahal.
- Mughal architecture is the style of architecture developed by the Mughal emperors of India from the 16th to the 18th century. It is influenced by Persian, Turkish, and Indian styles, and features symmetrical layouts, large domes, slender minarets, and intricate decorations. Some of the famous Mughal monuments are the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort, and the Jama Masjid.
- Indo-Saracenic architecture is the style of architecture developed by the British colonialists in India from the 19th to the early 20th century. It is influenced by Gothic, Renaissance, and Islamic styles, and features pointed arches, domes, turrets, and stained glass windows. Some of the famous Indo-Saracenic monuments are the Victoria Memorial, the Gateway of India, and the Rashtrapati Bhavan.
- The evolution of architecture in India is also influenced by the social, economic, and political changes that occurred in the country over time. The advent of the 20th century saw a growth in the economy and the population, which created a high demand for housing and urban planning. Globalization and migration also led to a shift in Indian architecture from a holistic point of view to a more individualistic and modern one.
- Some of the contemporary Indian architects who have contributed to the development of Indian architecture are Charles Correa, B.V. Doshi, Raj Rewal, Laurie Baker, and Balkrishna Doshi. They have experimented with different materials, techniques, and forms, and have created innovative and sustainable designs that reflect the Indian context and culture.
- Indian architecture is a living and dynamic expression of the Indian identity and heritage. It is a blend of tradition and modernity, diversity and unity, and continuity and change. It is a source of pride and inspiration for the people of India and the world.



### Engineering and Architecture as Cultural Heritage of India

- Engineering and architecture are two aspects of Indian culture that reflect the diversity, creativity, and innovation of the people of India.
- Engineering and architecture in India have been influenced by various factors, such as geography, climate, religion, history, politics, and foreign invasions.
- Engineering and architecture in India have also influenced other regions of the world, especially Asia, through the spread of Buddhism, Hinduism, and Islam.

Some of the examples are Hastinapura, Ayodhya, and Kausambi.

  - **Indo-Saracenic Architecture**: The period from 1857 CE to 1947 CE, when India was under British colonial rule and witnessed a fusion of Indian and European architectural styles. It is typified by the use of domes, towers, arches, and balconies. Some of the famous examples are Victoria Memorial, Gateway of India, Rashtrapati Bhavan, and Mysore Palace .

- Engineering and architecture in India are not only expressions of art and science, but also symbols of culture and identity. They reflect the values, beliefs, and aspirations of the people of India, and their interactions with other cultures and civilizations. They are a source of pride and inspiration for the present and future generations of India.

### Seals as Cultural Heritage of India

- Seals are small objects made of stone, metal, or other materials that have a carved or engraved design on one side and are used to stamp or seal documents, containers, or other items.
- Seals have been used in India since ancient times, especially during the Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1900 BCE), which was one of the earliest urban civilizations in the world.
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization are mostly square or rectangular in shape, and measure about 2-3 cm on each side. They are made of steatite, a soft stone that can be easily carved, and coated with alkali and baked to make them harder and more durable.
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization usually depict an animal, such as a bull, a buffalo, a rhinoceros, an elephant, a tiger, or a unicorn, along with a few symbols or signs that are considered to be part of an undeciphered script  . Some seals also show human figures, such as a yogi or a goddess.
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization were used for various purposes, such as identifying the owner or sender of goods, sealing clay vessels or wooden boxes, marking clay tablets or cloth, or as amulets or tokens of authority  .
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization reflect the culture, religion, trade, and art of the people who made and used them. They show the diversity and richness of the animal life in the region, the sophistication and skill of the craftsmen, the complexity and mystery of the script, and the possible beliefs and rituals of the society  .
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization are valuable sources of information and evidence for the history and archaeology of ancient India. They are also part of the cultural heritage and identity of modern India, as they represent one of the oldest and most advanced civilizations of the world  .
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization are not the only seals that are part of the cultural heritage of India. Seals of different shapes, sizes, materials, and designs have been used in India throughout history, by various dynasties, kingdoms, empires, religions, and communities.
- Seals of India have also been influenced by and exchanged with other cultures and regions, such as Persia, Greece, Rome, China, and Southeast Asia, reflecting the diversity and dynamism of Indian culture and history .
- Seals of India are still used today, especially by government officials, religious institutions, and educational organizations, as symbols of authority, authenticity, and identity.
- Seals of India are part of the traditions and cultural heritage of India, as they show the continuity and change of Indian civilization over time, and the connections and interactions of India with the rest of the world  .



### Coins as Cultural Heritage of India

- Coins are one of the most important sources of information about the history, culture, religion, economy, and politics of ancient and medieval India.
- Coins reflect the artistic skills, technological innovations, political aspirations, and religious beliefs of the people who minted and used them.
- Coins also provide valuable insights into the trade, commerce, and cultural interactions of India with other regions and civilizations of the world.
- The origin of coinage in India can be traced back to the first millennium BCE, when punch-marked coins made of silver and copper were used as a medium of exchange.
- The earliest coins were called karshapanas or panas, and they bore various symbols and marks, such as geometric shapes, animals, plants, and human figures.
- The coins of the Mauryan Empire (4th-2nd century BCE) were mostly punch-marked coins, but some cast coins of copper and lead were also issued. The Mauryan coins depicted the royal emblem of the lion, the peacock, the bull, and the elephant, as well as the Buddhist symbols of the wheel and the lotus.
- The post-Mauryan period (2nd-1st century BCE) saw the emergence of various regional kingdoms and dynasties, such as the Shungas, the Satavahanas, the Indo-Greeks, the Indo-Scythians, the Indo-Parthians, and the Kushanas. These rulers issued coins of different metals, shapes, sizes, and designs, reflecting their diverse cultural influences and artistic styles.
- The coins of the Indo-Greek kings (2nd-1st century BCE) were the first to bear the portraits and names of the rulers, as well as the Greek and Brahmi legends. The coins also depicted various Greek and Indian deities, such as Zeus, Apollo, Athena, Heracles, Lakshmi, Shiva, and Buddha.
- The coins of the Kushana Empire (1st-3rd century CE) were remarkable for their variety and richness of iconography. ### Indian Cinema as a Performing Art in India

- Indian cinema is a form of art that combines storytelling, performing arts, and science to create audio-visual narratives that reflect the culture, history, and diversity of India.
- Indian cinema has a rich and varied history that spans over a century, from the silent era to the present day, and encompasses different genres, languages, and regions of the country.
- Indian cinema has been influenced by various forms of performing arts, such as theater, music, dance, and puppetry, that have been part of the Indian tradition for centuries.
- Indian cinema has also contributed to the development and promotion of performing arts, by providing a platform for artists, performers, and writers to showcase their talents and reach a wider audience.
- Indian cinema has been shaped by the socio-political and economic changes that have occurred in India since its independence, and has reflected the aspirations, challenges, and struggles of the Indian people.
- Indian cinema has also been a source of inspiration and innovation for other forms of art, such as literature, painting, and photography, and has influenced the global film industry and culture.

Some of the main points to remember about Indian cinema as a performing art in India are:

- Indian cinema is a synthesis of various elements of performing arts, such as dialogue, music, song, dance, gesture, and expression, that create an emotional impact on the audience.
- Indian cinema follows the principles of the Natyasastra, an ancient treatise on drama and aesthetics, that defines the concept of rasa, or the essence of emotion, as the core of any artistic expression.
- Indian cinema has a diverse and dynamic nature, as it encompasses different styles, forms, and languages of filmmaking, such as Bollywood, regional cinema, parallel cinema, art cinema, and independent cinema.
- Indian cinema has a social and cultural significance, as it reflects the realities, values, and identities of the Indian society, and also challenges the stereotypes, norms, and prejudices that exist in it.
- Indian cinema has a global and universal appeal, as it transcends the boundaries of language, culture, and geography, and connects with the audiences across the world through its stories, characters, and emotions.
