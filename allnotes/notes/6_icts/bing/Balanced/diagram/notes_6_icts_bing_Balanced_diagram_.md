

# Indian Traditions, Culture and Society

- India is a diverse country with a rich and varied heritage of languages, religions, arts, cuisines, philosophies, and customs.
- India's culture and traditions are influenced by its ancient history, geography, and various invasions and migrations over the centuries.
- Some of the main aspects of Indian culture and traditions are:

  - **Arts and Language**: India has a long and distinguished tradition of literature, music, dance, architecture, painting, and sculpture. India has 22 official languages and hundreds of regional dialects. The most ancient and influential language is Sanskrit, which is the source of many Indo-European languages and the language of Hindu scriptures and classical literature. Other prominent languages include Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Gujarati, Malayalam, Kannada, and Punjabi. India also has a vibrant and diverse film industry, known as Bollywood, which produces movies in various languages and genres.
  - **Philosophy and Religion**: India is the birthplace of four major world religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. Hinduism is the oldest and most widely practiced religion in India, with a variety of sects, beliefs, and practices. Hinduism is based on the concept of dharma, or the cosmic order, and the cycle of reincarnation and karma. Buddhism and Jainism emerged as reform movements within Hinduism, emphasizing non-violence, compassion, and detachment from worldly desires. Sikhism was founded by Guru Nanak in the 15th century, as a monotheistic faith that combines elements of Hinduism and Islam. India also has a significant population of Muslims, Christians, Jews, Parsis, and other faiths, who have contributed to the religious diversity and tolerance of the country.
  - **Food and Drink**: The cuisine of India varies considerably across its various regions and ethnic communities. Indian food is known for its use of spices, herbs, grains, legumes, vegetables, fruits, dairy products, and meat. Some of the staple foods of India include rice, wheat, millet, lentils, chickpeas, potatoes, onions, garlic, ginger, chilies, turmeric, cumin, coriander, cardamom, saffron, and ghee. Some of the popular dishes of India include biryani, curry, dal, roti, naan, dosa, idli, samosa, chaat, pakora, tandoori, kebab, korma, paneer, raita, lassi, chai, and sweets such as halwa, jalebi, barfi, and kulfi. India also has a rich and varied tradition of vegetarianism, influenced by religious and ethical beliefs.
  - **Society and Culture**: India is a multi-ethnic, multi-lingual, and multi-cultural society, with a complex and dynamic social structure. India has a hierarchical system of social groups, known as castes, which are based on birth, occupation, and status. The caste system has been a source of social discrimination, oppression, and reform movements in India. India also has a patriarchal and patrilineal system of family and kinship, where the father is the head of the household and the eldest son inherits the property and responsibilities. However, there are also matrilineal and matriarchal communities in some parts of India, where the mother or the eldest daughter is the head of the family. India also has a diverse and vibrant culture of festivals, ceremonies, rituals, customs, and traditions, which celebrate various aspects of life, such as birth, marriage, death, harvest, seasons, religious events, and national occasions. Some of the major festivals of India include Diwali, Holi, Dussehra, Navratri, Eid, Christmas, Guru Nanak Jayanti, Buddha Purnima, Mahavir Jayanti, and Republic Day.

### State in Ancient India

- The state in ancient India was a social organization with political power that ensured peace, order and happiness.
- The origin of the state in ancient India is not clear, but there are three main theories: social contract theory, divine origin theory and organic theory.
- Social contract theory: This theory suggests that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among the people to surrender some of their rights and freedoms to a ruler or a group of rulers in exchange for protection and welfare.
- Divine origin theory: This theory claims that the state was created by God or gods and that the ruler or rulers were chosen by divine will or descent.
- Organic theory: This theory holds that the state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the growth and development of human society, and that the ruler or rulers were the representatives or the head of the society.
- The types of states in ancient India were loosely categorized into monarchy, republic and oligarchy.
- Monarchy: This was the most prevalent type of state in ancient India, where the king was the supreme authority and the source of power for the ministers, governors, village councils, town councils and trade guilds . The king was assisted by a council of ministers and a bureaucracy. The king was also expected to uphold the dharma (righteousness) and protect the people.
- Republic: This was a type of state where the people had a say in the governance through assemblies or councils of elders, nobles, warriors or citizens. The republics were usually small and autonomous, and often formed confederations or alliances with other republics for mutual defense or common interests. Some examples of republics in ancient India are the Sakyas, the Koliyas, the Mallas, the Vrijis and the Licchavis.
- Oligarchy: This was a type of state where the power was concentrated in the hands of a few individuals or families, who were either wealthy, influential or military leaders. The oligarchs often exploited the masses and oppressed the weaker sections of the society. Some examples of oligarchies in ancient India are the Nandas, the Mauryas, the Sungas and the Kanvas.
- The state in ancient India was influenced by various factors such as geography, climate, culture, religion, economy, trade, warfare, migration and invasion.
- The earliest state in ancient India was the Indus Valley Civilization, which flourished from 3300 BCE to 1300 BCE in the regions of modern-day Pakistan and northwest India. It was a highly urbanized and sophisticated civilization with advanced technology, standardized weights and measures, seal carving, metallurgy, writing, art and architecture.
- The later states in ancient India were mainly divided into two periods: the Vedic Period (1500 BCE to 600 BCE) and the Classical Period (600 BCE to 600 CE).
- The Vedic Period was marked by the arrival of the Indo-Aryan people from Central Asia, who brought with them the Vedic religion, culture and language. The Vedic states were mostly tribal and pastoral, with a hierarchical social structure based on the varna (caste) system. The Vedic states were also engaged in frequent wars with each other and with the indigenous people.
- The Classical Period was characterized by the emergence of large and powerful empires, such as the Magadha Empire, the Mauryan Empire, the Kushan Empire, the Gupta Empire and the Harsha Empire. The Classical states were more centralized and bureaucratic, with a codified legal system, a complex economy, a flourishing trade and a rich cultural and intellectual life. The Classical states were also influenced by various religious and philosophical movements, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Hinduism, Upanishads, Vedanta, Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa and Lokayata.



#### Theories of origin of State in India

The origin of state in India is a subject of debate among scholars of political science and history. There are different theories that attempt to explain how the state emerged and evolved in ancient India. Some of the prominent theories are:

- **Social Contract Theory**: This theory holds that the state was formed by a voluntary agreement among individuals who gave up some of their natural rights and freedoms in exchange for security and order. The state was seen as a human creation that could be changed or abolished by the consent of the people. This theory is found in some of the ancient texts such as the Mahabharata, the Arthashastra and the Manusmriti .
- **Divine Origin Theory**: This theory asserts that the state was established by the will of God or gods who chose a king or a dynasty to rule over the people. The king was considered as the representative or the incarnation of the divine power and was expected to uphold the dharma or the cosmic law. The state was seen as a sacred institution that could not be challenged or questioned by the people. This theory is found in some of the ancient texts such as the Ramayana, the Puranas and the Vedas .
- **Organic Theory**: This theory suggests that the state was a natural and inevitable outcome of the social and economic development of the human society. The state was seen as a living organism that grew and changed according to the needs and aspirations of the people. The state was also influenced by the geographical and cultural factors that shaped the identity and character of the people. This theory is found in some of the ancient texts such as the Shantiparva, the Digha Nikaya and the Upanishads  .

These theories are not mutually exclusive and may have some elements of truth and validity. However, they also have some limitations and criticisms. For example, the social contract theory may not account for the diversity and complexity of the Indian society and the role of caste, class and gender in the formation of the state. The divine origin theory may not explain the changes and conflicts that occurred in the political history of India and the role of human agency and rationality in the statecraft. The organic theory may not address the issues of justice and equality that are essential for the legitimacy and stability of the state. Therefore, the origin of state in India may not be explained by a single theory but by a combination of multiple factors and perspectives.



#### Evolutionary Theory of origin of State

- The Evolutionary Theory of origin of State is the theory that explains the state as the product of historical and social evolution over a long period of time  .
- According to this theory, the state is not a sudden or artificial creation, but a natural and gradual development of human society from simple to complex forms of organization  .
- The Evolutionary Theory traces the origin of the state from the primitive stages of human society, such as the family, the clan, the tribe, the village, the city, and the nation, and shows how various factors, such as population growth, economic activities, warfare, religion, law, and culture, influenced the formation and expansion of the state  .
- The Evolutionary Theory is regarded as the most scientific and convincing theory of origin of the state, as it is based on historical evidence and empirical data, and it accounts for the diversity and complexity of the state in different regions and times .
- The Evolutionary Theory also helps to understand the functions and purposes of the state, as it shows the state to be a response to the needs and challenges of human society, and a means of achieving social order, security, justice, and welfare  .

#### Mystical Theory of Origin of State

- The mystical theory of origin of state is also known as the **divine origin theory** or the **theory of divine right of kings** .
- The exponents of this theory believe that the state did not come into being by any effort of man, but by the **will of God** .
- According to this theory, the **king** or the **ruler** of the state is the **representative** or the **vicegerent** of God on earth .
- The king or the ruler has the **absolute authority** and the **infallible wisdom** to govern the state and the people .
- The people have the **duty** and the **obligation** to **obey** the king or the ruler without any question or resistance .
- The mystical theory of origin of state was popular in the **ancient** and the **medieval** times, especially in **Egypt**, **China**, **India**, **Persia**, **Greece**, **Rome**, and **Europe** .
- The mystical theory of origin of state was used to **justify** the **tyranny** and the **oppression** of the kings and the rulers over the people .
- The mystical theory of origin of state was **challenged** and **rejected** by the **modern** and the **scientific** theories of origin of state, such as the **historical** or the **evolutionary** theory  .
- The historical or the evolutionary theory explains that the state is the product of **growth**, a slow and steady **evolution** extending over a long period of time and ultimately shaping itself into the complex structure of a modern state  .
- The historical or the evolutionary theory is more **scientific**, **empirical**, and **realistic** than the mystical theory of origin of state  .



#### Contract Theory of Origin of State

- The contract theory of origin of state is a theory that explains how the state or society emerged from a voluntary agreement or contract among individuals or groups of people.
- The theory assumes that there was a pre-social or pre-political condition called the state of nature, where people lived in a natural state without any authority, law, or order.
- The theory also assumes that people in the state of nature faced various problems and inconveniences, such as insecurity, violence, fear, scarcity, and conflict.
- To overcome these problems and to secure their rights, interests, and welfare, people decided to enter into a contract or pact with each other, by which they agreed to give up some of their natural freedoms and submit to a common authority or government, which would protect them and enforce the laws.
- The theory further assumes that the contract or pact was based on the consent of the governed, and that the authority or government derived its legitimacy and power from the people who made the contract.
- The contract theory of origin of state has been developed and elaborated by various thinkers, such as Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, and Immanuel Kant, who have different views on the nature and terms of the contract, the role and function of the authority or government, and the rights and duties of the people.



### Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

The state is a political institution that exercises authority and control over a territory and its people. The origin and evolution of the state in ancient India is a complex and debated topic, but some general stages can be identified based on historical and archaeological evidence. These stages are:

- **Pre-state societies**: These were the earliest forms of human settlements in India, dating back to the Paleolithic and Neolithic periods. They were based on hunting, gathering, fishing, and primitive agriculture. They had no centralized authority, but rather followed kinship, clan, or tribal ties. Examples of such societies are the Harappan civilization, the megalithic cultures, and the Aryan tribes.
- **Janapadas**: These were the first states that emerged in India, around the 6th century BCE, along the river valleys of the Indus, the Ganges, and their tributaries. They were based on agricultural surplus, trade, commerce, and urbanization. They had a king or a chief as the head of the state, who was assisted by a council of elders or nobles. They also had a system of taxation, administration, and law. Examples of such states are Kuru, Panchala, Kosala, Magadha, etc.
- **Mahajanapadas**: These were the larger and more powerful states that developed from the janapadas, around the 5th century BCE, as a result of wars, alliances, and expansion. They had a more centralized and bureaucratic structure, with a king as the supreme ruler, who was supported by a large army, a court, and a ministry. They also had a more complex and diverse society, with various classes, castes, and religions. Examples of such states are Magadha, Kosala, Avanti, Vatsa, etc.
- **Empires**: These were the states that achieved imperial domination over a vast territory and a large population, around the 4th century BCE, as a result of conquest, diplomacy, and integration. They had a highly centralized and autocratic structure, with an emperor as the absolute sovereign, who was revered as a god or a representative of god. They also had a sophisticated and elaborate system of administration, taxation, law, culture, and religion. Examples of such states are the Mauryan empire, the Kushan empire, the Gupta empire, etc.
- **Regional kingdoms**: These were the states that emerged after the decline of the empires, around the 6th century CE, as a result of fragmentation, decentralization, and localism. They had a more feudal and hierarchical structure, with a king as the nominal ruler, who was dependent on the loyalty and support of his vassals, nobles, and generals. They also had a more diverse and dynamic society, with various ethnic, linguistic, and religious groups. Examples of such states are the Pallavas, the Chalukyas, the Pratiharas, the Rashtrakutas, etc.

### Council of Ministers in Ancient India

- The council of ministers was a group of advisers and officials who assisted the king in governing the state and implementing his policies.
- The council of ministers was also known as **Mantri-parishad**, **Mantri-mandala** or **Mantri-gana** in Sanskrit sources .
- The council of ministers was not a fixed or permanent body, but was formed according to the needs and preferences of the king. The number and rank of the ministers varied from time to time and from dynasty to dynasty.
- The council of ministers was headed by the **Prime Minister** or **Mahamantri**, who was the chief confidant and representative of the king. He presided over the meetings of the council and coordinated the activities of the other ministers.
- The other ministers included the **Chief Justice** or **Pradvivaka**, who was in charge of the judicial administration and the law code; the **Commander-in-Chief** or **Senapati**, who was responsible for the military affairs and defense of the realm; the **Treasurer** or **Sannidhata**, who managed the finances and revenue of the state; the **Foreign Minister** or **Duta**, who handled the diplomatic relations and negotiations with other states; and the **Home Minister** or **Antahpala**, who supervised the internal security and public order .
- The ministers were selected by the king based on their qualifications, character, loyalty and experience. They were expected to be well-versed in the scriptures, politics, law, economics, military science and other branches of knowledge. They were also expected to be honest, impartial, courageous, wise and devoted to the king and the welfare of the people .
- The council of ministers had various functions and powers, such as advising the king on important matters, executing his orders, administering the different departments of the state, framing laws and regulations, maintaining peace and order, collecting taxes, conducting wars, making alliances, and representing the king in his absence  .
- The council of ministers was an important institution in the ancient Indian polity, as it helped the king in carrying out his duties and responsibilities. ### Administration in Ancient India

- Administration in ancient India refers to the structures, procedures and behaviors that the ruling dynasties or emperors used to govern their respective territories and people.
- The earliest evidence of administration in ancient India can be traced to the Indus Valley civilization (2500-1700 BCE), which had a systematic and organized government in the cities of Mohenjodaro and Harappa. The Indus Valley civilization had a centralized authority, a uniform script, standardized weights and measures, urban planning, drainage system, public baths and granaries.
- After the decline of the Indus Valley civilization, the Vedic period (1500-600 BCE) witnessed the emergence of various tribal kingdoms and republics, which had different forms of administration. The most common form of government was monarchy, where the king was the supreme authority and was assisted by a council of ministers and advisers. The king performed various functions such as maintaining law and order, protecting the people, performing sacrifices, collecting taxes and leading the army. The king was also bound by the dharma (duty) and the rita (cosmic order) and was expected to rule with justice and benevolence.
- The Vedic period also saw the development of the concept of saptanga or the seven limbs of the state, which consisted of the king, the minister, the territory, the fort, the treasury, the army and the ally. These seven elements were considered essential for the stability and prosperity of the state.
- The later Vedic period (600-300 BCE) witnessed the rise of the Mahajanapadas or the sixteen great kingdoms, which competed with each other for supremacy. Some of these kingdoms were monarchical, such as Magadha, Kosala and Avanti, while some were republican, such as the Vajji, the Malla and the Shakya. The republican states had a more democratic form of administration, where the people elected their representatives and participated in the decision-making process. The republican states also had a federation of clans or tribes, which formed a confederacy for mutual defense and cooperation.
- The Mauryan Empire (321-185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian empire, which unified most of the Indian subcontinent under one rule. The Mauryan administration was highly centralized and efficient, with a well-defined hierarchy of officials and departments. The emperor was the head of the state and the supreme commander of the army. He was assisted by a chief minister, who was in charge of the civil administration, and a council of ministers, who advised him on various matters. The empire was divided into provinces, districts, sub-districts and villages, each with its own governor, magistrate, collector and other officials. The Mauryan administration also had a secret service, a postal system, a judicial system, a revenue system and a welfare system. The Mauryan administration was guided by the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft and governance, written by Kautilya, the chief minister of Chandragupta Maurya.
- The post-Mauryan period (185 BCE-320 CE) saw the fragmentation of the Indian subcontinent into various regional kingdoms, such as the Sunga, the Satavahana, the Kushana, the Shaka, the Gupta and the Harsha. These kingdoms had different forms of administration, but they generally followed the Mauryan model of centralization and bureaucracy. The kings were the supreme authorities and were assisted by ministers, governors, generals and other officials. The kings also patronized various religions, arts, sciences and literature. The Gupta Empire (320-550 CE) is considered the golden age of ancient India, as it witnessed the peak of political, economic, cultural and intellectual achievements.
- The medieval period (550-1200 CE) saw the emergence of various regional powers, such as the Pallava, the Chalukya, the Rashtrakuta, the Pala, the Pratihara, the Chola, the Chahamana and the Hoysala. These kingdoms had different forms of administration, but they generally followed the feudal system, where the king granted lands and privileges to his nobles, vassals and subordinates in exchange for their loyalty, service and tribute. The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) were the essential components of a political system according to Kautilya, the author of Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft and governance in ancient India. The seven limbs were:

- King (swamin): The king was the supreme authority and the head of the state. He was responsible for maintaining peace, justice, and stability in the realm. He was expected to be virtuous, wise, brave, and benevolent. He was assisted by a council of ministers and advisors.
- Minister (amatya): The minister was the chief executive officer of the state. He was in charge of the administration, taxation, law and order, and foreign affairs. He was expected to be loyal, efficient, skilled, and experienced. He was selected by the king based on merit and ability.
- Territory (janapada): The territory was the land and the people of the state. It was the source of revenue, resources, and manpower for the state. It was expected to be fertile, prosperous, and populous. It was protected by the fort and the army.
- Fort (durga): The fort was the defensive structure of the state. It was the place where the king, the minister, and the treasury were located. It was expected to be strong, secure, and well-equipped. It was built in strategic locations such as mountains, forests, or islands.
- Treasury (kosa): The treasury was the financial resource of the state. It was the accumulation of taxes, tributes, fines, and spoils of war. It was expected to be abundant, well-managed, and well-spent. It was used for the maintenance of the state, the welfare of the people, and the expansion of the territory.
- Army (danda): The army was the coercive power of the state. It was the force that enforced the law, defended the territory, and subdued the enemies. It was expected to be disciplined, trained, and loyal. It was composed of four branches: infantry, cavalry, chariots, and elephants.
- Ally (mitra): The ally was the external support of the state. It was the friendly state that shared common interests, values, and enemies with the state. It was expected to be reliable, trustworthy, and helpful. It was formed by treaties, marriages, or gifts.

#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

- Purusārtha (Sanskrit: पुरुषार्थ) means "object (ive) of human pursuit" or "human goal".
- It is a key concept in Hinduism that refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. These are: Dharma (righteousness, moral values), Artha (prosperity, economic values), Kama (pleasure, love, psychological values) and Moksha (liberation, spiritual values).
- The concept of Purusārtha is a fundamental principle of social ethics in ancient India. It is meant to set an ideal objective and also to convey the right means to achieve them. The four Purusārthas are not mutually exclusive, but interdependent and complementary. They are also flexible and adaptable to different stages of life, social contexts and individual preferences. 
- The concept of Purusārtha also reflects the diversity and pluralism of ancient Indian society. Different schools of thought, such as Vedanta, Mimamsa, Nyaya, Samkhya, Yoga, Buddhism and Jainism, have different interpretations and emphases on the four Purusārthas. Some may prioritize one over the other, or reject one or more of them altogether. For example, the Charvaka school of materialism rejects the idea of Moksha and focuses only on Artha and Kama. The Buddhist and Jain schools reject the authority of the Vedas and the concept of Dharma as defined by them. The Vedanta school considers Moksha as the ultimate and highest Purusārtha, while the Mimamsa school considers Dharma as the supreme goal. The Nyaya and Samkhya schools regard Artha and Kama as subordinate to Dharma and Moksha, while the Yoga school emphasizes the practice of meditation and self-discipline as the means to attain Moksha.   
- The concept of Purusārtha also has implications for the theory of Karma, which is the law of cause and effect that governs the cycle of rebirth (Samsara) in Hinduism. According to this theory, the actions and intentions of a person in this life determine their fate and quality of life in the next. The four Purusārthas provide a framework for understanding and evaluating one's actions and intentions, and also for choosing the appropriate course of action in different situations. By following the principles of Dharma, Artha, Kama and Moksha, one can achieve a balanced and harmonious life, and also progress towards the ultimate goal of liberation from Samsara.

- The varnāshrama system was not based on birth or heredity, but on qualities, actions and aptitudes of a person.
- The varnāshrama system was not a system of oppression or discrimination, but a system of mutual cooperation and interdependence among the different sections of society.



#### Āshrama or the Stages of Life in Society in Ancient India

- Āshrama (Sanskrit: आश्रम) is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras.
- The Āshrama system is one facet of the Dharma concept in Hinduism, which prescribes the duties and responsibilities of an individual according to their age, occupation, and social status .
- The four Āshramas are: 
  - Brahmacharya (student), marked by chastity, devotion, and obedience to one’s teacher. This stage lasts from childhood until the completion of education, usually around the age of 25.
  - Gṛhastha (householder), requiring marriage, the begetting of children, sustaining one’s family and helping support priests and holy men, and fulfillment of duties toward gods and ancestors. This stage lasts from the end of education until the age of 50.
  - Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller), involving gradual withdrawal from worldly affairs, detachment from material possessions, and contemplation of spiritual truths. This stage lasts from the age of 50 until the age of 75.
  - Sannyasa (renunciate), involving complete renunciation of worldly life, wandering as a mendicant, and seeking liberation from the cycle of birth and death. This stage lasts from the age of 75 until death.
- The Āshrama system was meant to provide a framework for the spiritual and moral development of an individual, as well as to maintain the social order and harmony in ancient India .

#### Understanding Gender as a Social Category in Society in Ancient India

- Gender is a social construct that defines the roles, expectations and norms associated with different sexes in a given society.
- In ancient India, gender roles were not fixed or rigid, but varied according to the time period, region, caste, class and religion.
- Generally, ancient India's society greatly favored men over women, and women were often subordinate to men in terms of rights, education, property and freedom.
- However, there were also some exceptions and variations in the status and role of women in ancient India, depending on the sources and contexts.
- Some of the main points to understand gender as a social category in society in ancient India are:

  - In the Vedic period (1500-600 BCE), women were considered with respect and dignity, and had access to education and religious rituals. Women could also choose their husbands and remarry if widowed .

Women were expected to be obedient and loyal to their husbands and fathers, and were confined to the domestic sphere. They lost their rights to education, property and inheritance, and were subjected to practices such as child marriage, sati (self-immolation of widows) and dowry .
  - In the Mauryan period (321-185 BCE), the status of women varied according to their class and occupation. Women from the upper classes were secluded and oppressed, while women from the lower classes and the Buddhist and Jain communities had more freedom and opportunities. Women could also participate in trade, agriculture and crafts, and some women became rulers, such as Prabhavati Gupta and Rudramadevi .
  - In the Gupta period (320-550 CE), the status of women further deteriorated as the society became more feudal and orthodox. Women were denied education and public life, and were subjected to harsh laws and punishments. They were also exploited and discriminated by the caste system and the religious institutions. However, some women still excelled in literature, art and science, such as Kalidasa's Shakuntala, Banabhatta's Yashomati and Aryabhata's Lilavati .
  - In the medieval period (600-1700 CE), the status of women was influenced by the invasions, migrations and cultural interactions of various groups, such as the Turks, Mongols, Arabs, Persians and Europeans. Women faced violence, insecurity and oppression, and were often treated as spoils of war or objects of trade. They were also forced to follow strict codes of conduct and dress, such as purdah (veiling) and jauhar (mass suicide of women to avoid capture). However, some women also resisted and challenged the patriarchal norms, such as Razia Sultana, Rani Durgavati, Mirabai and Nur Jahan .



#### The representation of Women in Historical traditions in Society in Ancient India

Some of the negative aspects were the exploitation, oppression, and discrimination of women by the colonial rulers, the orthodox sections of society, and the patriarchal norms  .

- Women in ancient India had different roles and rights depending on the time period, the region, the religion, and the caste they belonged to. Generally, they faced more challenges and oppression as time went by and patriarchy became stronger.

  - Child marriage and widowhood. Women were often married off at a very young age, sometimes even before puberty, to older men.   - Sati, Pardha, and Devadasi systems. Sati was a practice where a widow had to sacrifice herself on the funeral pyre of her husband, either voluntarily or under coercion, to prove her loyalty and chastity. Pardha was a practice where women had to cover their faces and bodies with veils and stay indoors to avoid the gaze of men. Devadasi was a practice where young girls were dedicated to temples as servants of the gods and goddesses, and were often exploited sexually by the priests and the elites .
  - Dasi, Niyog, and Polygamy. Dasi was a term used for female slaves who were captured, bought, or sold by the Aryans and other invaders. They were treated as property and had no rights or dignity. Niyog was a practice where a widow or a barren woman had to cohabit with a man other than her husband, usually a relative or a sage, to produce a son for her husband's lineage. Polygamy was a practice where a man could have more than one wife, either for political, economic, or personal reasons. Women had to share their husbands and had no voice or choice in the matter  .
- Women in ancient India also had some positive aspects in their lives, such as the respect and reverence given to them as mothers, wives, and goddesses, the freedom and equality enjoyed by some women in the early Vedic period, the contributions and achievements of some women in the fields of literature, arts, religion, and politics, and the presence of some social and religious movements that advocated for women's rights and dignity  . #### Four-class Classification of Society in Ancient India

- The four-class classification of society in ancient India is also known as the **varna system** or the **caste system**.
- It is based on the division of labor and the hierarchy of social status according to the Hindu scriptures, especially the Vedas .
- The four classes are:
  - **Brahmins**: the priests, scholars, teachers, and spiritual leaders of the society. They are considered the highest and most pure class, and they perform rituals, sacrifices, and ceremonies for the gods and the people     .
  - **Kshatriyas**: the rulers, warriors, administrators, and protectors of the society. They are the second highest class, and they are responsible for maintaining law and order, defending the country, and enforcing justice     .
  - **Vaishyas**: the farmers, traders, merchants, artisans, and business people of the society. They are the third highest class, and they are responsible for producing and distributing goods and services, and supporting the economy     .
  - **Shudras**: the laborers, servants, and workers of the society. They are the lowest and most impure class, and they are responsible for performing menial and unskilled tasks, and serving the higher classes     .
- The four classes are not equal in rights, privileges, duties, or opportunities. They are determined by birth and cannot be changed by choice or merit .
- The four-class classification of society in ancient India is one of the oldest and most influential social systems in the world, and it has shaped the culture, religion, politics, and economy of India for thousands of years .



#### Slavery in Society in Ancient India

- Slavery was a universal social evil and had its origin even from the early period of history. In India, its origin is closely associated with the caste system.
- The early history of slavery in India is contested because it depends on the translations of terms such as dasa and dasyu. Some scholars suggest that these terms referred to servile classes, while others argue that they denoted enemies or outsiders.
- Greek writer Megasthenes in his work Indika, while describing the Maurya Empire in the 4th century BCE, states that slavery was banned in Indian society.
- However, other sources indicate that slavery existed in ancient India, where it is recorded in the Sanskrit Laws of Manu of the 1st century BCE. The institution was little documented until the British colonials in the 19th century made it an object of study because of their desire to abolish it.
- Slavery system in India was very mild and most of the slaves were domestic slaves who were treated as members of the family. The Dharma Shastras of the 4th century BCE were in a protesting mood against slavery. Slave trade was prohibited in the Shastras.
- Slaves in ancient India were mainly acquired through war, debt, sale by parents, or punishment for crimes. They could be freed by their masters, or by paying a ransom, or by performing meritorious deeds.
- Slaves in ancient India had some rights and privileges, such as owning property, marrying, and practicing religion. They could also rise to high positions in society, such as ministers, generals, and rulers .
- Slavery in ancient India was not based on race or ethnicity, but on social and economic factors. There were also instances of voluntary slavery, where people offered themselves as slaves to a deity or a temple.
- Slavery in ancient India declined with the advent of Buddhism and Jainism, which preached equality and non-violence. The Gupta Empire (4th-6th century CE) also discouraged slavery and promoted the welfare of the lower classes.
- Slavery in ancient India was different from the slavery in other parts of the world, such as Greece, Rome, or America, where slaves were treated as chattel and had no rights or dignity. Slavery in ancient India was more humane and flexible, and allowed for social mobility and emancipation.

### Evolution of script in India

- A script is a written symbolisation of speech. It is a collection of characters used to write one or more languages.
- India has a long history of writing, dating back to the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which used a pictographic script that is still undeciphered.
- The earliest deciphered script of India is Brahmi, which was used to write the edicts of Emperor Ashoka (c. 3rd century BCE) and other inscriptions in various languages, such as Prakrit, Sanskrit, Pali, and Tamil.
- Brahmi is the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts, as well as the scripts of Sri Lanka, Tibet, Southeast Asia, and some parts of Central Asia.
- Brahmi evolved into different regional variants over time, depending on the cultural, linguistic, and geographical factors. Some of the major branches of Brahmi are:

  - Northern Brahmi: This includes scripts such as Kharosthi, Gandhari, Saka, Bactrian, and Kushana, which were used in the northwestern regions of India and Central Asia. These scripts were influenced by the Aramaic script of the Persian Empire and had a right-to-left direction of writing.
  - Southern Brahmi: This includes scripts such as Tamil-Brahmi, Vatteluttu, Grantha, and Pallava, which were used in the southern regions of India and Sri Lanka. These scripts were influenced by the Dravidian languages and had a rounded shape due to the use of palm leaves as writing material.
  - Eastern Brahmi: This includes scripts such as Kalinga, Siddhamatrika, and Proto-Bengali, which were used in the eastern regions of India and Southeast Asia. These scripts were influenced by the Magadhi Prakrit and had a vertical stroke at the end of each character.
  - Western Brahmi: This includes scripts such as Gupta, Nagari, Sharada, and Modi, which were used in the western and central regions of India and Nepal. These scripts were influenced by the Sanskrit language and had a horizontal line at the top of each character.

- Due to the greater emphasis on quicker and easier writing rather than monumental inscriptions, some of the Brahmi scripts developed into cursive styles that formed the basis of India's modern scripts. Some of the modern scripts of India are:

  - Devanagari: This is the most widely used script in India, as it is the official script of Hindi, the most spoken language in India. It is also used to write other languages, such as Sanskrit, Marathi, Nepali, and Konkani. It is derived from the Nagari script of Western Brahmi and has a distinctive horizontal line at the top of each character.
  - Bengali-Assamese: This is the script used to write the Bengali and Assamese languages, as well as some other languages of eastern India and Bangladesh. It is derived from the Proto-Bengali script of Eastern Brahmi and has a rounded shape with a vertical stroke at the end of each character.
  - Gujarati: This is the script used to write the Gujarati language, as well as some other languages of western India and Pakistan. It is derived from the Modi script of Western Brahmi and has a cursive style with a horizontal line at the top of each character.
  - Gurmukhi: This is the script used to write the Punjabi language, as well as some other languages of northern India and Pakistan. It is derived from the Sharada script of Western Brahmi and has a horizontal line at the top of each character and a vertical line at the left of each word.
  - Kannada: This is the script used to write the Kannada language, as well as some other languages of southern India and Sri Lanka. It is derived from the Grantha script of Southern Brahmi and has a rounded shape with a horizontal line at the top of each character.
  - Malayalam: This is the script used to write the Malayalam language, as well as some other languages of southern India and Sri Lanka. It is derived from the Vatteluttu script of Southern Brahmi and has a rounded shape with a loop at the end of each character.
  - Odia: This is the script used to write the Odia language, as well as some other languages of eastern India and Bangladesh. It is derived from the Kalinga script of Eastern Brahmi and has a rounded shape with a vertical stroke at the end of each character.

### Evolution of languages in India

- India is a multilingual country with over 780 languages spoken by its people.
- The linguistic history of India can be traced back to the prehistoric period when various language families emerged and interacted with each other.
- The major language families in India are the Indo-Aryan, the Dravidian, the Austroasiatic and the Sino-Tibetan.
- The Indo-Aryan languages are derived from the Indo-European languages that originated in Central Asia and spread to India through migrations and invasions. They are spoken by about 75% of the population and include languages such as Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Urdu and Sanskrit.
- The Dravidian languages are native to South India and are spoken by about 20% of the population. They include languages such as Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam and Tulu. They have a distinct grammatical structure and vocabulary from the Indo-Aryan languages and are influenced by the ancient Harappan civilization.
- The Austroasiatic languages are spoken by about 3% of the population, mostly in the eastern and central regions of India. They include languages such as Santali, Mundari, Khasi and Munda. They are related to the languages of Southeast Asia and have a rich oral tradition.
- The Sino-Tibetan languages are spoken by about 1% of the population, mostly in the northeastern and Himalayan regions of India. They include languages such as Tibetan, Nepali, Manipuri and Bodo. They are related to the languages of China and have a complex tonal system.
- The evolution of Indian languages is influenced by various factors such as geography, culture, religion, politics, trade and education. Some of the major influences are:
  - The Vedic period (1500-500 BCE) when the Sanskrit language and literature flourished and became the basis of the Indo-Aryan languages.
  - The Mauryan period (321-185 BCE) when the Prakrit languages and the Brahmi script developed and became the medium of administration and communication.
  - The Gupta period (320-550 CE) when the classical Sanskrit reached its peak and the Nagari script emerged as the precursor of the Devanagari script.
  - The medieval period (600-1500 CE) when the regional languages and scripts evolved from the Prakrits and the Apabhramshas and were influenced by the Persian, Arabic and Turkish languages due to the Islamic invasions and rule.
  - The colonial period (1500-1947 CE) when the English language and the Roman script were introduced and became the language of education, law, trade and administration due to the British rule.
  - The post-independence period (1947-present) when the Hindi language and the Devanagari script were adopted as the official language and script of the Indian Union and the other languages were given the status of scheduled languages and regional languages.
  - The contemporary period (2000-present) when the English language and the Roman script have become the dominant language and script of the internet, media, science and technology and have influenced the other Indian languages.

- The evolution of Indian languages is a dynamic and ongoing process that reflects the diversity and unity of the Indian culture and society.



#### Harappan Script

- Harappan script is the name given to the symbols found on the inscriptions of the Indus Valley Civilisation, which flourished in the northwest of India and Pakistan from about 2500 to 1900 BCE.
- Harappan script is also known as Indus script, as it was discovered during the excavation of Harappa, one of the major cities of the civilisation.
- Harappan script consists of about 400 signs, which vary in shape, size and orientation. Some signs are pictorial, representing animals, plants, objects or abstract concepts, while others are geometric or linear .
- Harappan script is written from right to left, sometimes with a boustrophedon (alternating direction) pattern. The inscriptions are usually very short, ranging from one to 17 signs, and are found on various objects such as seals, pottery, copper tablets, tools and jewellery .
- Harappan script has not been deciphered yet, despite many attempts by various scholars. The main challenges are the lack of bilingual inscriptions, the unknown underlying language(s), the brevity and diversity of the texts, and the absence of a clear grammatical or syntactic structure .
- Harappan script is one of the oldest known writing systems in the world, and it reflects the cultural and economic complexity of the Indus Valley Civilisation. It is also a source of mystery and fascination for historians, linguists and archaeologists.



#### Brahmi Script

- Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script.
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi .
- Brahmi script appeared as a fully developed script in the third century BCE and was used for writing various languages, such as Sanskrit, Prakrit, Pali, and others .
- Brahmi script is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations. For example, the Lalitavistara Sūtra lists 64 scripts, with Brahmi script starting the list.
- Brahmi script was already divided into regional variants at the time of the earliest surviving epigraphy around the 3rd century BC. Cursives of the Brahmi script began to diversify further from around the 5th century AD and continued to give rise to new scripts throughout the Middle Ages.
- Brahmi script is thought to have been modelled on the Aramaic or Phoenician alphabets, or possibly derived from the Indus or Harappa script.
- Brahmi script consists of 33 consonants and 9 vowels, which can be combined to form syllables. Brahmi script is written from left to right and has no case distinction.
- Brahmi script has a number system that uses nine symbols for the digits 1 to 9, and a separate symbol for zero. The symbols for 10, 100, and 1000 are derived from the symbols for 2, 3, and 4 by adding a horizontal line.
- Brahmi script has various signs and marks to indicate punctuation, vowel length, nasalization, aspiration, and other features of pronunciation.
- Brahmi script is usually written on stone, metal, clay, wood, palm leaves, or birch bark.

### The Vedas

- The Vedas are a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India.
- The Vedas are composed in Vedic Sanskrit, the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the oldest scriptures of Hinduism.
- The word Veda means "knowledge" in Sanskrit, and the Vedas are thought to contain the fundamental knowledge relating to the underlying cause of, function of, and personal response to existence.
- The Vedas are divided into four collections: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda.
- The Rigveda is the oldest and most important of the Vedas, containing 1,028 hymns to various gods and natural phenomena.
- The Yajurveda contains the formulas and rituals for performing sacrifices and ceremonies.
- The Samaveda consists of musical chants and melodies for the Rigvedic hymns.
- The Atharvaveda contains spells, charms, and magical incantations for various purposes.
- The Vedas have regulated the social, legal, domestic and religious customs of Hindus up to the present day.
- The Vedas are considered to be revealed by the gods to the sages, and are therefore regarded as sacred and authoritative.



### The Upanishads

- The Upanishads are ancient Hindu scriptures that explore the nature of reality, the self, and the supreme being.
- The word Upanishad means "to sit down closely", implying the intimate dialogue between a teacher and a student.
- The Upanishads are part of the Vedas, the oldest and most authoritative Hindu texts, but they are considered the end or the essence of the Vedas (Vedanta).
- There are more than 200 Upanishads, but only 13 are considered the principal or the classical ones. They are: Isha, Kena, Katha, Prashna, Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Chandogya, Brihadaranyaka, Kaushitaki, Shvetashvatara, and Maitri.
- The Upanishads date from the 8th to the 6th century BCE, but some may be later additions. They are composed in Sanskrit and use various literary forms, such as stories, dialogues, poems, and hymns.
- The main theme of the Upanishads is the identity of the individual self (atman) and the universal self (brahman). The Upanishads teach that the atman is the immortal and unchanging essence of the human being, and that brahman is the ultimate reality that pervades everything in the universe. The Upanishads assert that the realization of this identity leads to liberation (moksha) from the cycle of birth and death (samsara).
- The Upanishads also discuss various topics related to ethics, cosmology, psychology, metaphysics, and epistemology. They introduce concepts such as karma, dharma, maya, yoga, and the four stages of life (ashramas). They also present different methods of meditation, contemplation, and self-inquiry to attain the knowledge of the self and brahman.
- The Upanishads have influenced many schools of Hindu philosophy, such as Advaita Vedanta, Vishishtadvaita, Dvaita, and others. They have also inspired other religious traditions, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and theosophy. They are regarded as the source of wisdom and spirituality by many Hindus and seekers of truth.



The Ramayana is a Hindu epic that narrates the life and adventures of Rama, the prince of Kosala, and his wife Sita. It is one of the most influential and revered texts in Indian culture and literature. Here are some of the main points of the Ramayana:

- The Ramayana begins with the birth of Rama, who is the seventh incarnation of the god Vishnu. He is destined to defeat the demon king Ravana, who has terrorized the world with his evil deeds .
- Rama grows up as a virtuous, wise, and powerful prince. He wins the hand of Sita, the daughter of King Janaka of Mithila, by breaking the bow of Shiva, a divine weapon that no one else could lift or string .
- Rama is exiled from his kingdom by his stepmother Kaikeyi, who wants her son Bharata to be the king instead. Rama accepts his fate and leaves with Sita and his loyal brother Lakshmana. They live in the forest for 14 years, facing many dangers and challenges .
- Ravana, who is attracted by Sita's beauty, kidnaps her and takes her to his island kingdom of Lanka. He tries to persuade her to marry him, but she remains faithful to Rama. Rama and Lakshmana search for Sita, with the help of Hanuman, the monkey king, and his army of monkeys and bears .
- Rama and his allies wage a war against Ravana and his army of demons. Rama kills Ravana with a special arrow given to him by the sage Agastya. He rescues Sita and returns to his kingdom with her. He is crowned as the king of Kosala and rules with justice and righteousness .
- The Ramayana ends with a test of Sita's purity, as some people doubt her chastity after being in Ravana's captivity. Rama asks Sita to prove her innocence by entering a fire. Sita does so and emerges unharmed, as she is protected by the fire god Agni. Rama and Sita are reunited and live happily ever after .

The Ramayana is a complex and rich story that has many versions, interpretations, and adaptations. It explores themes such as dharma (duty), karma (action and consequence), bhakti (devotion), and moksha (liberation). It also showcases the values of courage, loyalty, compassion, and wisdom. It is a source of inspiration and guidance for many Hindus and people of other faiths and cultures.



### The Mahabharata

- The Mahabharata is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of India, along with the Ramayana .
- It is a long and complex narrative that covers the history, culture, religion, and philosophy of ancient India.
- It tells the story of the conflict between two groups of cousins, the Kauravas and the Pandavas, who fight for the throne of Hastinapura, a kingdom in northern India .
- The main events of the Mahabharata are the Kurukshetra War, a 18-day battle that ends with the victory of the Pandavas, and the Bhagavad Gita, a dialogue between the Pandava prince Arjuna and the god Krishna, who advises him on his duty and the meaning of life.
- The Mahabharata is divided into 18 books, or parvas, that contain about 74,000 verses, making it one of the longest poems in the world.
- The Mahabharata is considered to be both a historical account of the origins of Hinduism and a code of ethics for the followers of dharma, the moral and cosmic order .
- The Mahabharata is also a source of many stories, characters, and teachings that have influenced Indian literature, art, and culture for centuries.



### Puranas

- Puranas are considered as the fifth Veda, after the four Vedas (Rig, Sama, Yajur, and Atharva), which are the primary scriptures of Hinduism.

- Puranas are also categorized according to the three aspects of the divine: Sattva (purity), Rajas (passion), and Tamas (ignorance). Each Purana is devoted to a specific deity or theme, such as Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, or the Sun .
- Puranas are not considered as authoritative or binding as the Vedas, but rather as supplementary and popular texts that explain and illustrate the Vedic teachings in an accessible way .
- Puranas are also sources of historical, cultural, and artistic information, as they reflect the beliefs, values, and traditions of various periods and regions of India .



### Buddhist Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Buddhist literature is the collection of writings that preserve the teachings and traditions of Buddhism.
- Buddhist literature can be divided into two main categories: canonical and non-canonical.
- Canonical literature refers to the scriptures that are considered authoritative and authentic by various Buddhist schools and traditions. Non-canonical literature includes commentaries, biographies, stories, poems, and other works that are not part of the official canon, but are still valued for their historical, doctrinal, or literary significance.
- The canonical literature of Buddhism is written in various languages, depending on the region and time period of its composition. The most prominent languages are Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit.
- Pali is an ancient language that is closely related to Sanskrit, but has some differences in grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation. It is said that the Buddha delivered his sermons in Pali, or a dialect similar to it, and that his disciples recorded them in the same language. Pali is the canonical language of Theravada Buddhism, the oldest and most conservative branch of Buddhism, which is mainly practiced in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. The Pali canon, also known as the Tipitaka or the Three Baskets, consists of three main collections: the Vinaya Pitaka (the Basket of Discipline), which contains the rules and regulations for the monastic community; the Sutta Pitaka (the Basket of Discourses), which contains the dialogues and discourses of the Buddha and his disciples; and the Abhidhamma Pitaka (the Basket of Higher Teaching), which contains the systematic analysis and exposition of the Buddhist doctrine and psychology.
- Prakrit is a generic term for any language that is derived from or influenced by Sanskrit, but is not Sanskrit itself. Prakrit languages are usually simpler and more colloquial than Sanskrit, and reflect the regional and social variations of the speakers. Prakrit languages were widely used in ancient and medieval India for literature, administration, and communication. Some of the Prakrit languages that are relevant for Buddhist literature are Sauraseni, Maharastri, Magadhi, and Gandhari. Prakrit is the canonical language of some of the early Buddhist schools, such as the Mahasanghikas, the Sarvastivadins, and the Dharmaguptakas, which are collectively known as the Hinayana or the Lesser Vehicle. The Prakrit canon, also known as the Agama or the Collection, consists of four main collections: the Dirgha Agama (the Long Collection), which contains long discourses of the Buddha; the Madhyama Agama (the Middle Collection), which contains medium-length discourses of the Buddha; the Samyukta Agama (the Connected Collection), which contains short discourses of the Buddha grouped by topic; and the Ekottara Agama (the Numerical Collection), which contains discourses of the Buddha arranged by numbers.
- Sanskrit is an ancient and classical language that is considered the language of the gods and the scholars in Hinduism. It is a highly refined and sophisticated language that has a complex grammar, a rich vocabulary, and a poetic beauty. Sanskrit is the canonical language of Mahayana Buddhism, the later and more liberal branch of Buddhism, which is mainly practiced in Tibet, China, Japan, and Korea. The Sanskrit canon, also known as the Mahayana Sutras or the Great Vehicle Sutras, consists of various texts that expound the Mahayana teachings and practices, such as the Prajnaparamita Sutras (the Perfection of Wisdom Sutras), which emphasize the concept of emptiness; the Lotus Sutra, which reveals the universal Buddhahood of all beings; the Avatamsaka Sutra (the Flower Garland Sutra), which describes the interpenetration of all phenomena; the Pure Land Sutras, which extol the virtues and merits of the Buddha Amitabha and his paradise; and the Vajrayana Sutras (the Diamond Vehicle Sutras), which introduce the esoteric and tantric methods of achieving enlightenment.



### Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Jain literature refers to the literature of the Jain religion, which is one of the oldest and major religions of India.
- Jain literature is a vast and ancient literary tradition, which was initially transmitted orally by the teachers and disciples of Jainism.
- Jain literature is composed in various languages, such as Prakrit, Pali, Sanskrit, Apabhramsha, Hindi, Gujarati, Tamil, Kannada and Rajasthani.
- The most important and authoritative Jain texts are the canonical Jain Agamas, which are written in Ardhamagadhi, a Prakrit (Middle-Indo Aryan) language.
- The Jain Agamas consist of 12 Angas (limbs), 12 Upangas (subsidiary limbs), 6 Chhedasutras (disciplinary codes), 4 Mulasutras (root texts), 10 Prakirnas (miscellaneous texts) and 2 Culikas (appendices).
- The Jain Agamas contain the teachings of the 24 Tirthankaras (ford-makers), the spiritual leaders of Jainism, especially Mahavira, the last and most prominent Tirthankara.
- The Jain Agamas deal with various topics, such as ethics, cosmology, karma, rebirth, liberation, non-violence, asceticism, meditation, logic, epistemology and metaphysics.
- The Jain Agamas were compiled and codified in the 6th century CE in Valabhi, Gujarat, by the council of Jain monks and scholars.
- The Jain Agamas are divided into two main sects: the Digambaras (sky-clad) and the Svetambaras (white-clad), who differ in their interpretation and preservation of the texts.
- The Digambaras believe that the original Jain Agamas were lost and only some secondary texts, such as the Purvas, the Shatkhandagama and the Kasayaprabhrta, are available.
- The Svetambaras believe that they have preserved the original Jain Agamas and have also added some later texts, such as the Niryuktis, the Bhashyas, the Churnis and the Tikas, as commentaries and explanations.
- Apart from the canonical Jain Agamas, there are many other Jain texts written in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit, which are classified as non-canonical or extra-canonical literature.
- Some of the prominent Jain texts written in Pali are the Jaina Suttas, the Jaina Vinaya, the Jaina Abhidhamma and the Jaina Niddesa, which are similar to the Buddhist Pali Canon, but with a different perspective and emphasis.
- Some of the prominent Jain texts written in Prakrit are the Gathasaptasati, the Bhaktamara Stotra, the Samayasara, the Pravachanasara, the Niyamasara, the Pancastikayasara and the Tattvarthasutra, which are poetic, devotional, philosophical and doctrinal works.
- Some of the prominent Jain texts written in Sanskrit are the Kavyas, the Mahakavyas, the Stotras, the Subhashitas, the Natakas, the Alankaras, the Shastras and the Puranas, which are literary, artistic, scientific and historical works.
- Some of the famous Jain authors who wrote in Sanskrit are Haribhadra, Hemachandra, Siddhasena, Somadeva, Jinasena, Asadhara, Pujyapada, Akalanka, Vidyananda and Vidyadhara.

### Six System Indian Philosophy

The six systems of Indian philosophy are:

- **Nyaya**: A school that deals with logic and epistemology. It was founded by Akshapada Gautama in the 2nd century BCE. It proposes four sources of valid knowledge: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. It also analyzes the nature of arguments, fallacies, and proofs .
- **Vaisheshika**: A school that explains the nature of reality in terms of atoms and their combinations. It was founded by Kanada Kashyapa in the 6th century BCE. It proposes six categories of existence: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence. It also discusses the concepts of causation, liberation, and ethics .
- **Samkhya**: A school that provides a dualistic framework of purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter). It was founded by Kapila in the 6th century BCE. It enumerates 25 principles of reality, including the three gunas (qualities) of sattva (goodness), rajas (activity), and tamas (inertia). It also explains the process of evolution, bondage, and liberation of the purushas .
- **Yoga**: A school that prescribes a practical method of attaining liberation through physical and mental discipline. It was founded by Patanjali in the 2nd century BCE. It is based on the Samkhya philosophy, but adds the concept of Ishvara (God) as the supreme purusha. It outlines eight limbs of yoga: yama (restraints), niyama (observances), asana (postures), pranayama (breath control), pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), and samadhi (absorption) .
- **Purva Mimamsa**: A school that focuses on the interpretation and application of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. It was founded by Jaimini in the 3rd century BCE. It advocates the performance of rituals and duties as the means of attaining happiness and heaven. It also analyzes the nature of language, meaning, and authority .
- **Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta**: A school that expounds the philosophical essence of the Upanishads, the concluding part of the Vedas. It was founded by Badarayana in the 4th century BCE. It proposes various theories of the relationship between Brahman (the supreme reality), Atman (the individual self), and the world. It also discusses the concepts of maya (illusion), karma (action), and moksha (liberation) .

These are the six systems of Indian philosophy. They represent different perspectives and approaches to understanding the truth. They also influenced each other and other schools of thought in India and beyond.



### Shankaracharya and Advaita Vedanta

- Shankaracharya, also known as Adi Shankara, was an Indian philosopher and theologian who expounded the doctrine of Advaita Vedanta, the non-dualistic school of Hindu philosophy .
- He was born in the 8th century CE in Kaladi, a village in Kerala, and renounced the worldly pleasures at a very young age to pursue spiritual knowledge.
- He traveled across India, debating and defeating various schools of thought, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Mimamsa, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, and Samkhya, and establishing the supremacy of Advaita Vedanta .
- He also wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras, and the Bhagavad Gita, explaining the basic ideas of Advaita Vedanta and reconciling the apparent contradictions in the scriptures .

- He is regarded as the most authoritative philosopher of Advaita Vedanta and one of the greatest thinkers of Indian history .
- Advaita Vedanta is the school of Hindu philosophy that asserts the non-duality of reality, meaning that there is only one ultimate reality, called Brahman, which is identical with the innermost self (atman) of all beings.

- Advaita Vedanta teaches that the goal of human life is to realize one's identity with Brahman and attain liberation from the cycle of birth and death (samsara).

- Advaita Vedanta was systematized and defended by Shankaracharya and his followers, who wrote commentaries and sub-commentaries on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras, and the Bhagavad Gita, the three foundational texts of Vedanta .

- Indian philosophy shares many concepts such as dharma, karma, samsara, dukkha, renunciation, meditation, with almost all of them focusing on the ultimate goal of liberation of the individual from dukkha and samsara through diverse range of spiritual practices (moksha, nirvana) .
- Indian philosophy can be broadly classified into two categories: orthodox (astika) and unorthodox (nastika) .
- Orthodox schools accept the authority of the Vedas, the ancient scriptures of Hinduism, while unorthodox schools reject or question the Vedas .
- The six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaisheshika, Purva-Mimamsa, and Vedanta .
- Sāṃkhya is a philosophical tradition which regards the universe as consisting of two independent realities: puruṣa (the self or soul) and prakṛti (the material nature).
- Yoga is a school similar to Sāṃkhya (or perhaps even a branch of it) which accepts a personal god and focuses on yogic practices for attaining liberation.
- Nyāya is a school of logic and epistemology which analyzes the sources and validity of knowledge and inference.
- Vaisheshika is a school of atomism and naturalism which postulates that the universe is composed of six categories of substances: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence.
- Purva-Mimamsa (or Mimamsa) is a school of exegesis and ritual which interprets the Vedas and prescribes the performance of sacrifices and duties for attaining heaven and liberation.
- Vedanta is a school of metaphysics and theology which expounds the teachings of the Upanishads, the philosophical portions of the Vedas, and develops various systems of monism, dualism, and qualified monism.
- The three unorthodox schools of Indian philosophy are: Buddhism, Jainism, and Cārvāka .
- Buddhism is a religion and philosophy founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha) which teaches the four noble truths, the eightfold path, and the doctrine of dependent origination.
- Jainism is a religion and philosophy founded by Mahavira (the Jina) which teaches the principles of non-violence, non-absolutism, and non-possessiveness, and the doctrine of multiple viewpoints.
- Cārvāka is a school of materialism and skepticism which denies the existence of anything beyond the four elements of earth, water, fire, and air, and rejects the notions of soul, karma, and liberation.



### Heterodox Sects in India

Heterodox sects are those that do not accept the authority of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. They emerged in the 6th century BCE as a reaction to the ritualistic and hierarchical Vedic religion. They challenged the caste system, the sacrificial rites, and the metaphysical doctrines of the Brahmins, the priestly class. They advocated alternative paths of salvation, such as asceticism, meditation, and ethical conduct. Some of them also developed new philosophical systems and cosmologies.

Some of the major heterodox sects in India are:

- **Jainism**: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara or spiritual teacher, Jainism teaches the doctrine of ahimsa or non-violence towards all living beings. It also emphasizes the concepts of anekantavada or multiplicity of viewpoints, syadvada or conditional truth, and aparigraha or non-attachment. Jains believe in the existence of soul and karma, but reject the idea of a creator god. They practice strict asceticism and follow five vows: non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy, and non-possession.
- **Buddhism**: Founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha or the enlightened one, Buddhism teaches the four noble truths: the truth of suffering, the truth of the cause of suffering, the truth of the end of suffering, and the truth of the path that leads to the end of suffering. The path consists of the eightfold path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. Buddhists believe in the concepts of rebirth, karma, and nirvana, but reject the idea of a permanent soul or self. They practice meditation and follow the five precepts: abstaining from killing, stealing, lying, sexual misconduct, and intoxication.
- **Ajivika**: Founded by Makkhali Gosala, a contemporary of Mahavira and Buddha, Ajivika teaches the doctrine of niyati or determinism, which holds that everything is predetermined by fate and nothing can be changed by human effort or will. It also teaches the concepts of antarabhava or intermediate state, jati or birth, and samsara or cycle of rebirth. Ajivikas believe in the existence of soul and karma, but reject the idea of free will or moral responsibility. They practice extreme asceticism and follow the twelve vows: abstaining from killing, stealing, lying, sexual activity, eating after noon, eating more than once a day, eating from a bowl, eating cooked food, eating animal products, wearing clothes, using perfumes, and sleeping on a bed.



### Bhakti Movement in India

- The Bhakti movement was a significant religious movement in medieval Hinduism that sought to bring religious reforms to all strata of society by adopting the method of devotion to achieve salvation.
- The movement preached against the caste system, ritualism, idolatry, and social inequalities, and emphasized the personal and emotional relationship between the devotee and the deity .
- The movement originated in South India in the 7th-8th centuries CE with the Alvars (Vaishnavite saints) and the Nayanars (Shaivite saints), who composed devotional hymns in Tamil and visited various sacred shrines .
- The movement spread to North India in the 12th-13th centuries CE with the Bhakti saints such as Ramananda, Kabir, Nanak, Tulsidas, Surdas, Mirabai, and Chaitanya, who propagated their teachings in the vernacular languages such as Hindi, Punjabi, Bengali, and Marathi .
- The movement also influenced the development of Sikhism, a monotheistic religion founded by Guru Nanak in the 15th century CE, which incorporated elements of both Hinduism and Islam .
- The impact of the Bhakti movement on Indian society was profound and lasting. It challenged the authority of the Brahmins and the orthodox rituals, and promoted the equality and dignity of all human beings regardless of their caste, gender, or creed .
- It also enriched the literature, music, art, and culture of India, as the Bhakti poets and singers expressed their love and devotion to God in various forms and styles, such as bhajans, kirtans, dohas, padas, and ragas .
- The Bhakti movement was a major force in the spiritual and social transformation of India, and it continues to inspire millions of people across the world with its message of universal love and harmony .



### Sufi movement in India

- Sufism is a mystical branch of Islam that emphasizes the direct experience of God through love, devotion, and ethical conduct.
- Sufism originated in the Middle East and Central Asia in the early centuries of Islam, and spread to India through the travels and teachings of Sufi saints or sheikhs.
- The Sufi movement in India began in the 13th century and reached its peak in the 15th and 16th centuries under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal Empire.
- The Sufi movement was a socio-religious movement that aimed to reform the society and promote harmony among different religious groups.
- The Sufi movement was influenced by the Vedantic philosophy and Buddhism of India, and also incorporated elements of Hinduism and Sikhism in its practices and beliefs.
- The Sufi movement was organized into various orders or silsilas, each with its own founder, lineage, doctrine, and rituals. Some of the prominent silsilas in India were Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi, and Shattari.
- The Sufi saints preached the message of love, tolerance, service, and equality, and attracted followers from all walks of life, including Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, and others.
- The Sufi saints also composed poetry, music, and literature in various languages, such as Persian, Arabic, Urdu, Hindi, and Punjabi, and enriched the cultural heritage of India.
- The Sufi movement had a significant impact on the social, religious, and political history of India. It challenged the orthodoxy and rigidity of the Islamic law and the Hindu caste system, and fostered a spirit of syncretism and pluralism in the Indian society.
- The Sufi movement also contributed to the spread of Islam in India, especially in the rural areas, by adapting to the local customs and traditions, and by providing spiritual guidance and social welfare to the masses.
- The Sufi movement faced opposition and persecution from some of the orthodox Muslim rulers and clerics, who considered it as heretical and deviant. The Sufi movement also declined in the 18th and 19th centuries due to the rise of the British colonialism and the modernization of the Indian society.
- However, the Sufi movement still survives and thrives in India, with millions of devotees visiting the shrines and tombs of the Sufi saints, and participating in their festivals and ceremonies. The Sufi movement also continues to inspire and influence the art, music, literature, and spirituality of India.



### Socio religious reform movement of 19th century in India

- Socio religious reform movement refers to the efforts of various individuals and groups to reform and modernize the Indian society and religion in the 19th century.
- The main causes of the reform movement were:
  - The impact of western education and liberal ideas, which exposed the Indians to the concepts of democracy, human rights, rationalism, and scientific temper.
  - The criticism of the orientalists and the Christian missionaries, who highlighted the evils and superstitions of the Hindu religion and society, such as idolatry, caste system, sati, child marriage, etc.
  - The rise of nationalism and the desire to revive the glory of the ancient Indian civilization, which inspired the Indians to reform their religion and culture from within.
- The main types of the reform movement were:
  - The Brahmo Samaj, founded by Raja Ram Mohan Roy in 1828, which advocated monotheism, abolition of caste system, widow remarriage, women education, and social service.
  - The Prarthana Samaj, founded by Atmaram Pandurang in 1867, which followed the ideals of the Brahmo Samaj and also worked for the upliftment of the depressed classes and the emancipation of women.
  - The Arya Samaj, founded by Swami Dayanand Saraswati in 1875, which rejected idolatry, polytheism, rituals, priesthood, and caste system, and promoted the Vedas as the source of true knowledge and social reform.
  - The Ramakrishna Mission, founded by Swami Vivekananda in 1897, which propagated the message of Vedanta, universal brotherhood, service to humanity, and religious harmony.
  - The Theosophical Society, founded by Madame Blavatsky and Colonel Olcott in 1875, which aimed to revive the ancient wisdom of the East and to promote the study of comparative religion, philosophy, and science.
  - The Aligarh Movement, founded by Sir Syed Ahmed Khan in 1875, which sought to reform the Muslim society and religion by promoting western education, rationalism, and social service.
  - The Singh Sabha Movement, founded by Khem Singh Bedi and others in 1873, which aimed to reform the Sikh religion and society by purging it of the Hindu and Muslim influences and by promoting the teachings of the Guru Granth Sahib.
- The main impacts of the reform movement were:
  - It challenged the orthodoxy and conservatism of the Indian society and religion and paved the way for social and religious change.
  - It created a new class of educated and enlightened Indians, who became the leaders of the nationalist movement and the social reformers of the 20th century.
  - It fostered a sense of pride and self-respect among the Indians and inspired them to resist the colonial domination and exploitation.
  - It contributed to the growth of a composite and modern Indian culture, which was based on the synthesis of the best elements of the East and the West.

### Agriculture in India and Ancient India

### Medicine in India and Ancient India

- Medicine in India is a diverse and ancient field of knowledge and practice that has evolved over thousands of years.

### Metallurgy in India and Ancient India

Metallurgy is the science and art of extracting and processing metals from ores and other sources. Metallurgy in India and ancient India has a long and rich history, dating back to the 3rd millennium BCE. Some of the main points of metallurgy in India and ancient India are:

- Copper was one of the first metals to be used by humans in India, as evidenced by copper artefacts from the Chalcolithic period (c. 2500-1500 BCE) found in various sites such as Khetri in Rajasthan and Mitathal in Haryana .
- Iron was also known and used in India since ancient times, as attested by iron implements and weapons from the Vedic period (c. 1500-500 BCE) and the Iron Age (c. 1200-600 BCE) found in various sites such as Hastinapura in Uttar Pradesh and Hallur in Karnataka .
- Zinc was another metal that was extracted and refined in India as early as the 4th to 3rd century BCE, using a sophisticated distillation technique that was developed at Zawar in Rajasthan. Zinc was used for making brass, an alloy of copper and zinc, and for medicinal purposes .
- Gold and silver were also known and valued in India since ancient times, as evidenced by the numerous references to them in the Vedas and other texts, and by the coins and jewellery made of them by various dynasties and kingdoms. Gold and silver were obtained from placer deposits, river beds, and mines .
- Lead, tin, and mercury were also known and used in India since ancient times, as part of the 'seven metals of antiquity'. Lead and tin were used for making bronze, an alloy of copper and tin, and for soldering and coating. Mercury was used for making amalgams, such as cinnabar, and for alchemical and medicinal purposes .

### Pottery as Cultural Heritage of India

- Pottery is one of the most ancient and tangible forms of Indian art, dating back to the prehistoric times .
- Pottery reflects the cultural diversity and richness of India, as different regions have their own styles, techniques, motifs and materials of pottery making .
- Pottery also serves as a source of information and evidence for studying the history, society, economy, religion and trade of various periods and civilizations of India .
- Some of the major types of pottery in India are:

  - **Terracotta pottery**: This is the most common and widespread type of pottery, made from clay and baked in a kiln or sun-dried. It is usually reddish-brown in color and decorated with incised, stamped or painted designs. Terracotta pottery is used for making various utilitarian and decorative items, such as pots, lamps, figurines, toys, etc. Terracotta pottery is found in many parts of India, such as Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Rajasthan, Gujarat, etc  .

  - **Glazed pottery**: This is a type of pottery that has a thin layer of glass-like coating on the surface, which makes it shiny and colorful. Glazed pottery is made by applying a mixture of clay, quartz, glass and metal oxides on the clay body and firing it at high temperatures. Glazed pottery is used for making tableware, tiles, vases, etc. Glazed pottery is found in places like Delhi, Khurja, Jaipur, etc .

  - **Blue pottery**: This is a type of glazed pottery that has a distinctive blue color, derived from cobalt oxide. Blue pottery is made by using quartz and not clay, which makes it more durable and resistant to cracking. Blue pottery is decorated with floral and geometric patterns, inspired by Persian and Mughal art. Blue pottery is mainly found in Jaipur, where it was introduced by the Mongol artisans in the 14th century .

  - **Black pottery**: This is a type of pottery that has a dark black color, achieved by reducing the oxygen supply during the firing process. Black pottery is made by using a special type of clay, rich in iron, and polishing it with oil or vegetable juices. Black pottery is known for its metallic luster and smooth texture. Black pottery is mainly found in Nizamabad (Uttar Pradesh) and Longpi (Manipur) .

  - **Paper mache pottery**: This is a type of pottery that is made by using paper pulp and glue, instead of clay. Paper mache pottery is molded into various shapes and sizes, and painted with bright colors and motifs. Paper mache pottery is light-weight and eco-friendly. Paper mache pottery is mainly found in Kashmir, where it was introduced by the Persian craftsmen in the 15th century .

- Pottery is a living craft tradition of India, that is still practiced extensively by the rural and urban potters, who use both traditional and modern methods and tools. Pottery is also a part of the cultural festivals and rituals of India, such as Diwali, Durga Puja, Ganesh Chaturthi, etc  .
- Pottery is a cultural heritage of India, that showcases the creativity, skill and diversity of the Indian artisans, and also contributes to the socio-economic development and environmental sustainability of the country .

### Dance as Cultural Heritage of India

### Music as Cultural Heritage of India

Music is an integral part of the cultural heritage of India. It reflects the diversity and richness of the Indian civilization, which has been influenced by various historical, religious, and regional factors. Music of India can be broadly classified into two main categories: classical music and folk music. 

- Classical music is the art music that follows the rules and principles of ancient texts and traditions. It is based on the concept of raga, which is a melodic framework that evokes a certain mood or emotion, and tala, which is a rhythmic cycle that organizes the beats and accents. Classical music is further divided into two main styles: Hindustani music, which is prevalent in northern and central India, and Carnatic music, which is dominant in southern India. Both styles have their own distinct features, such as instruments, forms, compositions, and genres. Classical music is performed by highly trained and skilled musicians, who improvise and elaborate on the raga and tala according to their creativity and mastery. Classical music is considered to be a sacred and refined art form that connects the human soul with the divine. Some of the famous classical musicians of India are Ravi Shankar, Bismillah Khan, M.S. Subbulakshmi, and Lata Mangeshkar.  

- Folk music is the popular music that reflects the local culture and traditions of different regions and communities of India. It is based on the oral transmission and collective participation of the people, who sing and play music for various occasions, such as festivals, weddings, rituals, and celebrations. Folk music is more flexible and diverse than classical music, as it incorporates various elements, such as languages, dialects, melodies, rhythms, instruments, and genres. Folk music is also influenced by the social, political, and environmental changes that affect the lives of the people. Folk music is considered to be a lively and expressive art form that connects the people with their roots and identity. Some of the famous folk musicians of India are Bhupen Hazarika, Gurdas Maan, Ila Arun, and Shubha Mudgal.  

Music of India is a cultural heritage that showcases the unity and diversity of the Indian nation. It is a source of pride and inspiration for the people, who cherish and preserve their musical traditions and also innovate and experiment with new forms and genres. Music of India is a universal language that transcends the boundaries of time, space, and culture, and communicates the emotions and values of the human spirit.



### Theatre as Cultural Heritage of India

- The Natya Shastra defines two types of theatre: natya, which is based on mythology and history, and nataka, which is based on contemporary events and social issues. It also classifies theatre into ten types according to the mood (rasa) and the hero (nayaka).

### Painting as Cultural Heritage of India

- Painting is one of the oldest and most diverse forms of art in India, dating back to prehistoric times.

### Martial Arts Traditions of India

- Martial arts are codified systems and traditions of combat practices, which are practised for a variety of reasons – self-defence, competition, physical health and fitness, entertainment, as well as mental, physical, and spiritual development.
- India has an ancient tradition in diverse martial arts, dating back to the Vedic period and influenced by the geography, culture, and history of the subcontinent.
- Some of the most famous martial arts in India are:

  - **Kalaripayattu**: Often known as the ‘Mother Of All Martial Arts’, this 3000-year old art form originating from Kerala, draws inspiration from the raw power, swift movements and sinuous strength of majestic animals – the lion, tiger, elephant, wild boar, snake and crocodile. It involves strikes, kicks, grappling, weaponry, and healing methods.
  - **Silambam**: A traditional Tamil martial art that uses a long bamboo staff as the main weapon, along with other weapons such as swords, daggers, and spears. It is known for its fluid and circular movements, and its emphasis on footwork, agility, and timing.
  - **Thang-Ta**: The ancient Manipuri martial art, also known as Huyen Lallong, that uses swords and spears as the main weapons, along with shields, axes, and daggers. It is a powerful yet gracefully complex technique that combines offensive and defensive maneuvers, and also incorporates ritual dances and healing practices.
  - **Gatka**: A Sikh martial art that originated in Punjab and was used by the Khalsa army in the 18th and 19th centuries. It involves the use of various weapons, such as swords, daggers, sticks, and shields, as well as unarmed combat. It is based on the principle of one spirit in two bodies, and requires coordination, speed, and accuracy.
  - **Kushti**: One of the most popular martial arts in India, also known as Pehlwani or traditional wrestling. It started off during the Mughal times where Malla Yuddha, a local sport was combined with a Persian sport named Varzesh-e-Bastani. It involves grappling, throws, locks, and pins, and is practised on a clay or dirt pit called akhada.
  - **Lathi**: India’s oldest armed martial art, also regarded as one of the world’s earliest martial arts weapons. It involves the use of a bamboo or iron staff, ranging from 4 to 12 feet in length, as well as shields, swords, and axes. It is mainly practised in rural areas of northern and eastern India, and is used for self-defence, law enforcement, and social control.

- These are some of the examples of the rich and diverse martial arts traditions of India, which reflect the cultural and historical heritage of the country. There are many more martial arts in India, such as Mardani Khel, Sqay, Thoda, Inbuan, and Niyuddha, that have their own unique features and significance.

- Indian cinema has also been influenced by the classical and folk traditions of Indian performing arts, such as theatre, dance, music, and puppetry .
- Indian cinema has adopted and adapted the concepts of rasa (emotion), natya (drama), and nritta (dance) from the ancient treatise on performing arts, the Natyashastra, written by Bharata Muni.
- Indian cinema has also been influenced by the Indian People's Theatre Association (IPTA), a progressive cultural movement that emerged in the 1940s and aimed to use theatre and cinema as a medium of social change and awareness.
- Indian cinema has produced many genres, styles, and movements, such as the parallel cinema, the new wave cinema, the masala cinema, the regional cinema, and the art cinema, among others.
- Indian cinema has also produced many acclaimed and influential filmmakers, actors, and technicians, such as Satyajit Ray, Raj Kapoor, Guru Dutt, Ritwik Ghatak, Shyam Benegal, Nargis, Amitabh Bachchan, A.R. Rahman, and many more.
- Indian cinema has also gained global recognition and appreciation, winning awards and accolades at various international film festivals and platforms, such as the Cannes Film Festival, the Academy Awards, the BAFTA Awards, and the Golden Globe Awards.
- Indian cinema is a vibrant and dynamic form of art that continues to grow and innovate, exploring new themes, techniques, and formats, such as the digital cinema, the web series, the documentary cinema, and the animation cinema.
