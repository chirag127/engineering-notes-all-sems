# Stages of State Formation in Ancient India

- State formation in ancient India is the process of emergence and development of political entities that exercised authority and control over a territory and population in the Indian subcontinent.
- State formation is linked with the growth and spread of agriculture, trade, industry and urbanisation, which created and appropriated surplus resources and enabled the emergence of complex societies.
- The stages of state formation in ancient India can be broadly divided into four phases: the Vedic period, the Mahajanapada period, the Mauryan period and the post-Mauryan period.

## The Vedic Period

- The Vedic period (c. 1500-600 BCE) is the earliest phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the arrival and settlement of the Indo-Aryan speaking people in the north-western part of the subcontinent.
- The Vedic society was organised into tribes (jana) and clans (vish), which were led by chiefs (raja) and elders (sabha and samiti). The tribes were often engaged in conflicts and alliances with each other over land, cattle and resources.
- The Vedic state was based on kinship, ritual and sacrifice, and had a limited territorial scope and administrative apparatus. The Vedic state was also influenced by the indigenous cultures and peoples, such as the Dasas and the Nagas, who had their own forms of polity and religion.

## The Mahajanapada Period

- The Mahajanapada period (c. 600-300 BCE) is the second phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the emergence and expansion of sixteen large territorial states (mahajanapadas) in the northern and central parts of the subcontinent.
- The Mahajanapadas were formed by the consolidation and integration of smaller tribal and clan-based polities, as well as by the conquest and annexation of neighbouring regions. The Mahajanapadas were also influenced by the urbanisation, commerce and cultural exchange that resulted from the contact with the Persian and Greek empires in the west and the Nanda empire in the east.
- The Mahajanapada state was based on monarchy, bureaucracy and taxation, and had a wider territorial scope and administrative apparatus. The Mahajanapada state was also characterised by the diversity and plurality of political forms, such as republics (gana-sangha), oligarchies (samgha) and federations (sreni).

## The Mauryan Period

- The Mauryan period (c. 321-185 BCE) is the third phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the establishment and decline of the first pan-Indian empire under the Mauryan dynasty.
- The Mauryan empire was founded by Chandragupta Maurya, who defeated the Nanda empire and the Greek invaders, and expanded his domain to cover most of the Indian subcontinent and parts of Central Asia and Afghanistan. The Mauryan empire reached its zenith under Ashoka, who consolidated and administered his empire through a centralised and hierarchical bureaucracy, and propagated his policy of dhamma (righteousness) through edicts and missions.
- The Mauryan state was based on imperialism, centralisation and welfare, and had an unprecedented territorial scope and administrative apparatus. The Mauryan state was also marked by the integration and coexistence of diverse cultures, religions and peoples, such as the Buddhists, the Jains, the Ajivikas and the Greeks.

## The Post-Mauryan Period

- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE-300 CE) is the fourth phase of state formation in ancient India, marked by the fragmentation and diversification of political entities in the Indian subcontinent.
- The post-Mauryan period witnessed the decline and disintegration of the Mauryan empire, and the emergence and expansion of various regional and local powers, such as the Sungas, the Kanvas, the Satavahanas, the Kushanas, the Sakas, the Parthians, the Indo-Greeks, the Nagas, the Guptas and the Vakatakas.

# The Seven Limbs of the State in Ancient India

- The seven limbs of the state (saptanga) is a political theory formulated by Kautilya in his treatise Arthashastra, which deals with the art of governance and statecraft.
- The theory posits that a state consists of seven essential elements or organs that support its existence and functioning. These are:
  - King: The ruler or sovereign who is the head of the state and the source of law and justice. He is responsible for the welfare, security and stability of the state and its people. He is independent and has the supreme authority over the other limbs of the state.
  - Amatya: The ministers or bureaucrats who assist the king in administration and policy-making. They are chosen for their loyalty, competence and expertise in various fields. They are accountable to the king and the people for their actions and decisions.
  - Janapada: The territory or land of the state, which includes its natural resources, population, culture and economy. It is the basis of the state's power and prosperity. It should be well-protected, cultivated and expanded by the king and his officials.
  - Durga: The fort or capital of the state, which serves as the center of political, military and cultural activities. It is the seat of the king and his court, and the symbol of the state's prestige and strength. It should be well-fortified, well-planned and well-maintained by the king and his engineers.
  - Kosa: The treasury or wealth of the state, which includes its revenue, expenditure, assets and liabilities. It is the source of the state's financial and material resources, and the means of its development and welfare. It should be well-managed, well-audited and well-spent by the king and his accountants.

# Society in Ancient India

#### Purusārtha in Society in Ancient India

- Purusārtha (Sanskrit: पुरुषार्थ) means "object (ive) of human pursuit" or "human goal".
- It is a key concept in Hinduism that refers to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. These are:
  - Dharma (righteousness, moral values)
  - Artha (prosperity, economic values)
  - Kama (pleasure, love, psychological values)
  - Moksha (liberation, spiritual values)
- The concept of Purusārtha is a fundamental principle of social ethics in ancient India. It is meant to set an ideal objective and also to convey the right means to achieve them.
- The four Purusārthas are not mutually exclusive, but rather interdependent and complementary. They are also flexible and adaptable according to the individual's stage of life, social class, and personal inclination.
- The concept of Purusārtha is based on the belief that human life is a sacred journey of the soul, and that the ultimate goal is to attain moksha, or liberation from the cycle of birth and death. Moksha can be achieved by following one's dharma, or duty, according to one's varna (social class) and ashrama (stage of life).
- Dharma is the first and foremost Purusārtha, as it provides the ethical foundation for the other three. Dharma is the moral law that governs the individual and the society, and it is based on the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. Dharma also means the inherent nature or essence of a thing, and the duty or obligation that arises from it. Dharma varies according to one's varna and ashrama, and also according to the context and circumstances. Dharma is the means to achieve artha, kama, and moksha, and also the end in itself. 
- Artha is the second Purusārtha, and it means wealth, prosperity, and material well-being. Artha is necessary for the survival and comfort of the individual and the society, and it also enables the fulfillment of one's dharma and kama. Artha should be acquired and used in accordance with dharma, and not for selfish or immoral purposes. Artha is also a means to achieve moksha, as it can be used for charity, service, and spiritual pursuits. 
- Kama is the third Purusārtha, and it means desire, pleasure, and enjoyment. Kama includes both sensual and aesthetic pleasures, as well as love, affection, and emotional satisfaction. Kama is a natural and legitimate human goal, and it is also a source of happiness and motivation. Kama should be pursued in moderation and in harmony with dharma and artha, and not at the expense of them. Kama is also a means to achieve moksha, as it can lead to detachment, renunciation, and devotion. 
- Moksha is the fourth and final Purusārtha, and it means liberation, freedom, and salvation. Moksha is the ultimate goal of human life, and it is the state of being free from ignorance, attachment, and suffering. Moksha is achieved by realizing one's true self, which is identical with Brahman, the supreme reality. Moksha can be attained by various paths, such as knowledge, action, devotion, and meditation. Moksha is the end of all Purusārthas, and it is also the source of all bliss and peace. 
- The concept of Purusārtha reflects the holistic and dynamic vision of human life in ancient India. It provides a framework for understanding the purpose and meaning of life, and also for guiding the individual and the society towards the highest good. The concept of Purusārtha also shows the diversity and unity of Hinduism, as it accommodates different values, goals, and paths within a common worldview.



#### Varnāshrama System in Society in Ancient India

- It consisted of four varnas (classes) and four ashramas (stages of life), each with specific duties and responsibilities.

  - Brahman: the class of priests and teachers, who performed rituals and imparted knowledge.

  - Vaishya: the class of traders, merchants and people engaged in other professions, who contributed to the economic prosperity and welfare of the society.

  - Brahmacharya: the stage of studentship, where one learned the scriptures and the skills of one's varna under a guru (teacher).
  - Grihastha: the stage of householdership, where one married, raised a family and fulfilled one's social and religious obligations.
  - Vanaprastha: the stage of forest dwelling, where one retired from worldly affairs and pursued spiritual goals in solitude or in the company of like-minded people.

#### Āshrama or the Stages of Life in Society in Ancient India

- Āshrama (Sanskrit: आश्रम) is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras.
- The Āshrama system is one facet of the Dharma concept in Hinduism, which prescribes the duties and responsibilities of an individual according to their age, occupation, and social status .
- The four Āshramas are:

  - Brahmacharya (student): This stage lasts from childhood until the completion of education, usually around the age of 25. The student is expected to live a life of celibacy, devotion, and obedience to their teacher (guru), and to study the scriptures and other subjects  .
  - Gṛhastha (householder): This stage begins with marriage and lasts until the age of 50 or 60. The householder is expected to fulfill their duties towards their family, society, and the gods, by earning a livelihood, raising children, performing rituals, and supporting the other Āshramas  .
  - Vanaprastha (forest walker/forest dweller): This stage begins when the householder has fulfilled their obligations and renounces worldly attachments. The forest dweller retires to a secluded place, usually in the wilderness, and devotes themselves to spiritual pursuits, such as meditation, penance, and service to humanity  .
  - Sannyasa (renunciate): This stage is the final and highest stage of life, in which the individual renounces all worldly ties and possessions, and seeks liberation (moksha) from the cycle of birth and death. The renunciate lives a life of detachment, austerity, and wisdom, and is respected as a spiritual authority by the society  .

- The Āshrama system was not rigid or compulsory, and some individuals could skip or combine some stages, or remain in one stage for their entire life, depending on their personal choice and circumstances .
- The Āshrama system was a way of organizing the social and spiritual life of the ancient Indian society, and reflected the values and ideals of Hinduism. It aimed to provide a balanced and harmonious development of the individual and the society, and to prepare the individual for the ultimate goal of liberation  .

# Four-class Classification of Society in Ancient India

- Ancient India had a social hierarchy called a **caste system** that divided people into four main classes or **varnas**.
- The caste system was based on the concept of **dharma**, or the moral and religious duty of each person according to their birth and occupation.
- The four classes were:
  - **Brahmins**: the priests, scholars, and teachers who performed rituals and studied the sacred texts     . They were considered the highest and purest class in the society.
  - **Kshatriyas**: the rulers, warriors, and administrators who protected and governed the society     . They were the second highest class and had the duty of courage and leadership.
  - **Vaishyas**: the farmers, traders, merchants, and artisans who produced and exchanged goods and services     . They were the third highest class and had the duty of honesty and enterprise.
  - **Shudras**: the laborers, servants, and workers who performed menial and low-skilled tasks     . They were the lowest class and had the duty of obedience and service.
- The caste system was rigid and hereditary, meaning that people could not change their class or marry outside their class.
- The caste system also had many subgroups or **jatis** that were based on regional, occupational, and sectarian differences.
- The caste system was influenced by the religious and cultural beliefs of Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism, as well as the political and economic changes in the history of India.



#### Slavery in Society in Ancient India

- Slavery was a universal social evil and had its origin even from the early period of history. In India, its origin is closely associated with the caste system .
- The institution of slavery existed in ancient India, although it operated in much milder form than in the ancient civilizations of the west. Its operation was at a low key that foreign travelers like Megasthenes did not note it, for we do not get any reference in his account about slavery.
- The early history of slavery in the Indian subcontinent is contested because it depends on the translations of terms such as dasa and dasyu. Some scholars interpret these terms as slaves, while others argue that they were tribes or classes of people.
- Slavery existed in ancient India, where it is recorded in the Sanskrit Laws of Manu of the 1st century BCE. The institution was little documented until the British colonials in the 19th century made it an object of study because of their desire to abolish it.
- Slavery in ancient India was mainly of two types: domestic slavery and agricultural slavery. Domestic slaves were employed in households for various services, such as cooking, cleaning, guarding, etc. Agricultural slaves were used for cultivating land, herding cattle, etc .
- Slaves in ancient India were mostly obtained from war captives, debtors, criminals, orphans, and foreigners. Some people also sold themselves or their children into slavery due to poverty or famine. Slaves were considered as the property of their masters and had no rights or freedom .
- Slavery in ancient India was not based on race or skin color, but on social and economic factors. There were also instances of Africans being brought to India as slaves and traders, who eventually settled down here and played an important role in India's history of kingdoms, conquests, and wars. Some of them, like Malik Ambar and Malik Kafur, rose to become powerful rulers and reformers.
- Slavery in ancient India was not as harsh or oppressive as in other parts of the world, as there were some rules and regulations that governed the treatment of slaves. The Laws of Manu prescribed certain duties and responsibilities for both masters and slaves, and also laid down some provisions for the emancipation of slaves. Some slaves were also able to earn their freedom by performing meritorious deeds or by paying a ransom .
- Slavery in ancient India gradually declined with the advent of Buddhism and Jainism, which preached the principles of non-violence, equality, and compassion. The Mauryan emperor Ashoka, who embraced Buddhism, banned slavery in his empire and freed many slaves. The Gupta dynasty, which is considered as the golden age of ancient India, also witnessed a decline in slavery due to the prosperity and stability of the society .

# Evolution of script in India

- A script is a written symbolisation of speech. It is a collection of characters used to write one or more languages.
- India has a long history of writing, dating back to the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500-1900 BCE), which used a pictographic script known as the Indus script.
- The most influential script in India's history is the Brahmi script, which was used to write the edicts of Emperor Ashoka (c. 3rd century BCE) and the ancient Buddhist and Jain texts .
- The Brahmi script is the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts, as well as the scripts of Sri Lanka, Tibet, Southeast Asia, and some parts of Central Asia .
- The Brahmi script evolved into different regional variants over time, depending on the linguistic, cultural, and political factors. Some of the major variants are :
  - Kharosthi: used in the northwest of India, especially in Gandhara and Bactria, from the 3rd century BCE to the 4th century CE. It was derived from the Aramaic script and written from right to left.
  - Gupta: used in the north and central India, especially during the Gupta Empire (c. 4th-6th century CE). It was a more refined and elegant form of Brahmi, and the basis of the Nagari script.
  - Nagari: used in the north and west of India, especially in the medieval period. It was a more angular and vertical form of Gupta, and the precursor of the Devanagari script.
  - Siddhamatrika: used in the east and northeast of India, especially in Bengal and Bihar, from the 6th to the 12th century CE. It was a more rounded and horizontal form of Gupta, and the ancestor of the Bengali and Tibetan scripts.
  - Grantha: used in the south of India, especially in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, from the 6th to the 19th century CE. It was a more complex and ornate form of Brahmi, and the source of the Tamil and Malayalam scripts.
  - Vatteluttu: used in the south of India, especially in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, from the 8th to the 16th century CE. It was a more simplified and curved form of Grantha, and the origin of the Sinhala script.
- Due to the use of different writing materials, such as palm leaves, birch bark, stone, metal, and paper, the scripts of India also developed different styles and features, such as rounded shapes, diacritical marks, conjunct consonants, and vowel signs  .

  - Devanagari: used to write Hindi, Sanskrit, Marathi, Nepali, and other languages. It is the most widely used script in India and the official script of the Indian government.
  - Bengali: used to write Bengali, Assamese, Manipuri, and other languages. It is the second most widely used script in India and the official script of Bangladesh and some Indian states.
  - Tamil: used to write Tamil, Badaga, Irula, and other languages. It is the oldest and most conservative script in India, retaining many features of the ancient Brahmi script.
  - Malayalam: used to write Malayalam, Sanskrit, and other languages. It is the youngest and most complex script in India, having developed from the Grantha script in the 16th century CE.

#### Harappan Script

- Harappan script is the name given to the symbols found on the inscriptions of the Indus Valley Civilisation, which flourished in the northwest of India and Pakistan from about 2500 to 1900 BCE .
- Harappan script is also known as Indus script, as it was discovered during the excavation of Harappa, one of the major cities of the civilisation.
- Harappan script consists of about 400 signs, which vary in shape, size and orientation . Some signs are pictorial, representing animals, plants, objects or abstract concepts, while others are geometric or linear .
- Harappan script is written from right to left, sometimes with a boustrophedon (alternating direction) pattern . The inscriptions are usually very short, ranging from one to 17 signs, and are found on various objects such as seals, pottery, copper tablets, tools, jewellery and ivory  .
- Harappan script has not been deciphered yet, despite many attempts by various scholars and methods  . The main challenges are the lack of bilingual inscriptions, the unknown underlying language(s), the brevity and diversity of the texts, and the absence of a clear grammatical or syntactic structure  .
- Harappan script is one of the oldest known writing systems in the world, and it reflects the culture and society of the Indus Valley Civilisation  . It is a valuable source of information and a fascinating topic of research for historians, linguists and archaeologists  .

- Brahmi script is the earliest writing system developed in India after the Indus script.
- It is one of the most influential writing systems; all modern Indian scripts and several hundred scripts found in Southeast and East Asia are derived from Brahmi .
- Brahmi script appeared as a fully developed script in the third century BCE and was used for writing various languages, such as Sanskrit, Prakrit, Pali, and others .
- Brahmi script is mentioned in the ancient Indian texts of Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism, as well as their Chinese translations. For example, the Lalitavistara Sūtra lists 64 scripts, with Brahmi script starting the list.
- Brahmi script is composed of 33 to 40 consonants and 9 to 16 vowels, depending on the region and the language . It is written from left to right and has a distinctive horizontal line at the top of the letters, called the headstroke .
- Brahmi script has many regional variants and evolved into different scripts over time . Some of the major descendants of Brahmi script are Devanagari, Bengali, Tamil, Sinhala, Tibetan, and Khmer .
- Brahmi script is also the source of several historical scripts, such as Kharosthi, Pallava, Gupta, Siddham, and Sharada  .
- Brahmi script is considered a deciphered script, but some aspects of its origin, development, and interpretation are still debated by scholars .



# The Vedas

- The Vedas are a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India. They are composed in Vedic Sanskrit and constitute the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the oldest scriptures of Hinduism.
- The word Veda means "knowledge" or "vision" and it serves to manifest the language of the gods in human speech. The Vedas are thought to contain the fundamental knowledge relating to the underlying cause of, function of, and personal response to existence.
- The Vedas are divided into four collections: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda. Each collection consists of four parts: the Samhitas (hymns), the Brahmanas (rituals), the Aranyakas (forest texts) and the Upanishads (philosophy).
- The Vedas are considered to be revealed by the gods to the ancient sages (rishis) who heard them in a state of meditation and transmitted them orally to their disciples. The Vedas are also regarded as eternal, infallible and authoritative by most Hindus.
- The Vedas have regulated the social, legal, domestic and religious customs of Hindus up to the present day. All the obligatory duties of Hindus at birth, marriage, death etc. are guided by Vedic rituals.



# The Upanishads

- The Upanishads are ancient Hindu scriptures that explore the nature of reality, the self, and the supreme being.
- The word Upanishad means "to sit down closely", implying the intimate relationship between the teacher and the student who receive the secret teachings.
- The Upanishads are part of the Vedas, the oldest and most authoritative Hindu texts, but they are considered the end or the essence of the Vedas (Vedanta).
- There are more than 200 Upanishads, but only 13 are considered the principal or the classical Upanishads. They are: Isa, Kena, Katha, Prasna, Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Chandogya, Brhadaranyaka, Svetasvatara, Kausitaki, and Maitri.
- The Upanishads were composed between 800 and 500 BCE, during a period of intellectual and spiritual development in India known as the Upanishadic or the Vedantic period.
- The Upanishads present a vision of an interconnected universe with a single, unifying principle behind the apparent diversity in the cosmos, called Brahman. Brahman is the ultimate reality, the source and the goal of all existence.
- The Upanishads also teach that Brahman resides in the Atman, the unchanging core of the human individual. The Atman is the true self, the essence of one's being, beyond the body, mind, and ego.
- The Upanishads aim to reveal the identity of the Atman and the Brahman, and the way to attain the realization of this identity, which is the highest goal of human life. This realization is called moksha, or liberation from the cycle of birth and death (samsara).
- The Upanishads use various methods to convey their teachings, such as stories, dialogues, analogies, metaphors, paradoxes, and aphorisms. Some of the most famous teachings of the Upanishads are: "Tat tvam asi" (You are that), "Aham brahmasmi" (I am Brahman), "Neti neti" (Not this, not this), and "Prajnanam brahma" (Consciousness is Brahman).
- The Upanishads have influenced many schools of Hindu philosophy, such as Advaita (non-dualism), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism), and Dvaita (dualism), as well as other religions, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and even Western philosophy.



# The Ramayana

The Ramayana is a Hindu epic that narrates the life and adventures of Rama, the prince of Kosala, and his wife Sita. The Ramayana is composed of seven books and consists of about 24,000 verses. The main themes of the Ramayana are dharma (duty), karma (action and consequence), and bhakti (devotion).

The following are the main points of the Ramayana:

- Rama is the eldest son of King Dasaratha of Kosala and is considered to be an incarnation of the god Vishnu. He is virtuous, brave, and skilled in archery. He marries Sita, the daughter of King Janaka of Mithila, after breaking the bow of Shiva in a contest.
- Rama's stepmother, Kaikeyi, is influenced by her maid Manthara to ask Dasaratha to banish Rama to the forest for 14 years and to crown her son Bharata as the king. Dasaratha reluctantly agrees, but dies of grief soon after. Rama, Sita, and Rama's brother Lakshmana leave for the forest, while Bharata refuses to accept the throne and vows to serve Rama as his regent.
- In the forest, Rama encounters many sages, animals, and demons. He befriends the monkey king Sugriva and his minister Hanuman, who help him in his search for Sita, who is abducted by the demon king Ravana of Lanka. Ravana wants to marry Sita, but she resists his advances and remains faithful to Rama.
- Rama and his allies build a bridge across the ocean and invade Lanka. They fight a fierce war with Ravana and his army of demons. Rama kills Ravana with the help of a divine weapon given by the sage Agastya. He rescues Sita and returns to Ayodhya with her and his companions.
- Rama is crowned as the king of Kosala and rules with justice and prosperity. However, he hears a rumor that some people doubt Sita's chastity, as she had lived in Ravana's palace for a long time. He decides to banish Sita to the forest, where she gives birth to twin sons, Lava and Kusha. They are raised by the sage Valmiki, who teaches them the Ramayana.
- Rama performs a horse sacrifice to assert his sovereignty and sends his brothers to protect the horse. They encounter Lava and Kusha, who capture the horse and challenge Rama's army. Rama himself comes to fight them and recognizes them as his sons. He reunites with Sita, who proves her purity by undergoing a fire test. However, Sita is unhappy with Rama's treatment of her and asks the earth to swallow her. Rama is heartbroken and returns to Ayodhya with his sons. He rules for many years and then departs to his heavenly abode with his brothers and devotees.



# The Mahabharata

- The Mahabharata is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of India, along with the Ramayana .
- It is a historical and religious text that tells the story of the struggle for supremacy between two groups of cousins, the Kauravas and the Pandavas, who belong to the Bharata dynasty  .
- The main plot of the Mahabharata revolves around the Kurukshetra War, a 18-day battle that took place in the 13th or 14th century B.C. between the Kauravas and the Pandavas for the throne of Hastinapura, the capital of the Kuru kingdom  .
- The Mahabharata also contains many sub-stories, characters, and philosophical discourses that explore various aspects of Hinduism, such as dharma (duty, morality, and law), karma (action and consequence), moksha (liberation), and reincarnation  .
- The Mahabharata is divided into 18 books or parvas, each with a different theme and number of chapters. The first book, called the Adi Parva, introduces the main characters and the background of the story. The last book, called the Svargarohana Parva, describes the death and ascent to heaven of the Pandavas and their allies.
- The Mahabharata is considered to be the longest epic poem in the world, with about 74,000 verses, 1.8 million words, and 200,000 lines. It is about four times longer than the Ramayana and ten times longer than the Iliad and the Odyssey combined.
- The Mahabharata is attributed to the sage Vyasa, who is also a major character in the story. He is said to have dictated the epic to the god Ganesha, who wrote it down. However, the Mahabharata is also believed to be the result of centuries of oral tradition and multiple authors and redactors.
- The Mahabharata is a revered and influential text in Hinduism, as it contains the Bhagavad Gita, one of the most important scriptures of the religion. The Bhagavad Gita is a dialogue between the Pandava prince Arjuna and the god Krishna, who acts as his charioteer and guide, on the eve of the Kurukshetra War. The Bhagavad Gita teaches Arjuna the principles of dharma, karma, and bhakti (devotion) and how to perform his duty as a warrior and a king.
- The Mahabharata is also a source of inspiration and adaptation for various forms of art, literature, and media, such as paintings, sculptures, dramas, films, comics, and games. The Mahabharata has been translated into many languages and cultures, and has influenced the literature and mythology of other regions, such as Southeast Asia, Central Asia, and Europe.



# Puranas

- Puranas are a genre of ancient Indian literature that contain stories, legends, myths, and other traditional lore about various aspects of Hinduism, such as gods, goddesses, sages, kings, dynasties, cosmology, ethics, rituals, and philosophy.  
- Puranas are considered as the fifth Veda, after the four Vedas (Rig, Yajur, Sama, and Atharva), which are the oldest and most authoritative scriptures of Hinduism. Puranas are also regarded as smriti (remembered) texts, as opposed to shruti (heard) texts like the Vedas.  
- Puranas are composed in Sanskrit verse, mostly in the form of narrative couplets (shlokas). They date from the 4th century BCE to the 10th century CE, and vary greatly in their length, content, style, and origin. Some Puranas are sectarian, focusing on a particular deity or sect, while others are more universal and eclectic.  
- Puranas are divided into two categories: Mahapuranas (great Puranas) and Upapuranas (minor Puranas). There are 18 Mahapuranas and 18 Upapuranas, although some sources list more or less. Each Mahapurana consists of five sections: Sarga (creation), Pratisarga (recreation), Vamsha (genealogy of gods and sages), Manvantara (periods of Manu, the first human), and Vamshanucharita (history of royal dynasties).  
- Puranas are meant to be read, heard, and recited by the common people, as they provide moral and spiritual guidance, as well as entertainment and cultural knowledge. Puranas are also sources of information for various arts, sciences, and social institutions of ancient India. Puranas are often illustrated with paintings, sculptures, and icons that depict the stories and characters.



# Buddhist Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Buddhist literature is the collection of writings that preserve the teachings and traditions of Buddhism.
- Buddhist literature can be divided into two main categories: canonical and non-canonical.
- Canonical literature refers to the scriptures that are considered authoritative and authentic by various Buddhist schools and traditions. Non-canonical literature refers to the writings that are not accepted as scripture, but are still valuable for understanding the history, culture, and practice of Buddhism.
- Buddhist literature can also be classified according to the language in which it was written. The three main languages of Buddhist literature are Pali, Prakrit, and Sanskrit.

## Pali Literature

- Pali is an ancient language that was spoken in the regions of Magadha and South Bihar in India. It is derived from a dialect of Prakrit, which was a vernacular form of Sanskrit.
- Pali is the language of the Theravada school of Buddhism, which is the dominant form of Buddhism in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Theravada means "the way of the elders" and claims to preserve the original teachings of the Buddha.
- Pali literature consists mainly of the Pali Canon, which is the collection of scriptures that Theravada Buddhists regard as the word of the Buddha. The Pali Canon is also known as the Tipitaka, which means "the three baskets". The Tipitaka is divided into three sections:

  - The Vinaya Pitaka, which contains the rules and regulations for the monastic community of monks and nuns.
  - The Sutta Pitaka, which contains the discourses and dialogues of the Buddha and his disciples on various topics of doctrine and practice.
  - The Abhidhamma Pitaka, which contains the systematic analysis and exposition of the teachings of the Buddha in terms of psychology, philosophy, and ethics.

- Pali literature also includes commentaries, sub-commentaries, chronicles, biographies, and other works that explain and elaborate on the Pali Canon. Some of the most famous Pali works are:

  - The Dhammapada, which is a collection of verses that summarize the essence of the Buddha's teachings.
  - The Jataka, which is a collection of stories that illustrate the previous lives of the Buddha and his disciples.
  - The Milindapanha, which is a dialogue between the Greek king Menander and the Buddhist monk Nagasena on various aspects of Buddhism.
  - The Visuddhimagga, which is a comprehensive manual of Theravada doctrine and practice written by the scholar Buddhaghosa.

## Prakrit Literature

- Prakrit is a generic term for the languages that were spoken in ancient India as opposed to the classical language of Sanskrit. Prakrit languages include Pali, Ardhamagadhi, Sauraseni, Maharastri, Magadhi, Gandhari, and others.
- Prakrit literature is the literature that was written in these languages, mainly by the followers of Jainism and Buddhism. Jainism and Buddhism were heterodox movements that challenged the authority and orthodoxy of Brahmanism, the dominant religion of ancient India based on the Vedas and Sanskrit.
- Prakrit literature reflects the diversity and creativity of these movements, as well as their social and cultural contexts. Prakrit literature can be broadly divided into two types: canonical and non-canonical.
- Canonical literature refers to the scriptures that are considered authoritative and authentic by various Jain and Buddhist schools and traditions. Non-canonical literature refers to the writings that are not accepted as scripture, but are still valuable for understanding the history, culture, and practice of Jainism and Buddhism.
- Prakrit literature can also be classified according to the language in which it was written. The two main languages of Prakrit literature are Ardhamagadhi and Maharastri.

### Ardhamagadhi Literature

- Ardhamagadhi is an ancient language that was spoken in the regions of Magadha and South Bihar in India. It is derived from a dialect of Prakrit, which was a vernacular form of Sanskrit.
- Ardhamagadhi is the language of the Svetambara school of Jainism, which is one of the two main sects of Jainism. Svetambara means "white-clad" and refers to the practice of wearing white clothes by the monks and nuns of this sect. Svetambara Jains believe that their scriptures are the original and authentic teachings of Mahavira, the 24th and last Tirthankara or spiritual



# Jain Literature in Pali, Prakrit and Sanskrit

- Jain literature refers to the literature of the Jain religion, which is one of the oldest and major religions of India.
- Jain literature is a vast and ancient literary tradition, which was initially transmitted orally by the teachers and disciples of Jainism.
- Jain literature can be broadly divided into two categories: canonical and non-canonical.
- Canonical literature consists of the sacred texts that are accepted as authoritative by the Jains. They are also known as Agamas or Siddhantas.
- Non-canonical literature consists of the texts that are not accepted as authoritative by all Jains, but are still valuable for their historical, philosophical, ethical, and literary significance. They include commentaries, biographies, stories, poems, and other genres.
- Jain literature is written in various languages, such as Prakrit, Pali, Sanskrit, Apabhramsha, Hindi, Gujarati, and others.
- Prakrit is a term used to denote the languages that are derived from Sanskrit, but are simpler and more colloquial. Prakrit was widely used as a medium of communication and literature in ancient and medieval India.
- Pali is a form of Prakrit that was used as the language of the Buddhist scriptures and literature. Pali was also influenced by other languages, such as Magadhi and Ardhamagadhi.
- Sanskrit is the classical and literary language of India, which is derived from the Vedic language. Sanskrit is considered as the language of the gods and the scholars in Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism.
- Jain canonical literature is mostly written in Ardhamagadhi, which is a form of Prakrit that was spoken in the region of Magadha, where Mahavira, the founder of Jainism, lived and preached.
- Jain canonical literature consists of 45 texts, which are divided into four groups: Angas, Upangas, Prakirnas, and Chhedasutras. These texts deal with various aspects of Jain doctrine, ethics, cosmology, history, and ritual.
- Jain canonical literature was compiled and codified in the 6th century CE in Valabhi, Gujarat, by the Svetambara sect of Jainism. The Digambara sect of Jainism does not accept the authenticity of these texts, and considers only 12 texts as canonical, which are lost.
- Jain non-canonical literature is written in various languages, such as Prakrit, Pali, Sanskrit, Apabhramsha, and others. Some of the prominent authors and works of Jain non-canonical literature are:
  - Haribhadra (8th century CE), a prolific writer and philosopher who wrote in Sanskrit and Prakrit. Some of his works are Anekanta Jayapataka, Yogadrishti Samuccaya, and Dhurtakhyana.
  - Hemachandra (12th century CE), a polymath and scholar who wrote in Sanskrit and Prakrit. Some of his works are Trishashtishalakapurushacharita, Yogashastra, and Abhidhanachintamani.
  - Jinasena (9th century CE), a Digambara monk and poet who wrote in Sanskrit. His magnum opus is Mahapurana, which narrates the lives of the 63 great men of Jainism.
  - Banarasidas (16th century CE), a merchant and poet who wrote in Braj Bhasha, a form of Hindi. His autobiography, Ardhakathanaka, is considered as the first autobiography in Indian literature.
  - Siddharasi (10th century CE), a Svetambara monk and poet who wrote in Sanskrit. His Upamitibhava Prapancha Katha is a collection of stories that illustrate the Jain doctrine of anekantavada, or the multiplicity of viewpoints.



# Kautilya's Arthashastra

- The Arthashastra is an ancient Indian treatise on politics, economics, military strategy, the function of the state, and social organization.
- It is attributed to Kautilya (also known as Chanakya, Vishnugupta, l. c. 350-275 BCE), who was a professor at Taxila University and later the Prime Minister of the Mauryan Empire under Chandragupta Maurya (r. c. 321-c.297 BCE), the founder of the Mauryan dynasty (322-185 BCE)  .
- The Arthashastra is considered to be one of the earliest and most comprehensive manuals on the art of politics, covering topics such as administration, diplomacy, law, taxation, welfare, espionage, defense, and war  .
- The Arthashastra is divided into 15 books, each dealing with a specific aspect of statecraft. The books are:

  - Book I: Concerning Discipline
  - Book II: The Duties of Government Superintendents
  - Book III: Concerning Law
  - Book IV: The Removal of Thorns
  - Book V: The Conduct of Courtiers
  - Book VI: The Source of Sovereign States
  - Book VII: The End of the Six-Fold Policy
  - Book VIII: Concerning Vices and Calamities
  - Book IX: The Work of an Invader
  - Book X: Relating to War
  - Book XI: The Conduct of Corporations
  - Book XII: Concerning a Powerful Enemy
  - Book XIII: Strategic Means to Capture a Fortress
  - Book XIV: Secret Means
  - Book XV: The Plan of a Treatise

- The Arthashastra reflects the political and social conditions of ancient India, as well as the worldview and values of Kautilya. It advocates a pragmatic and utilitarian approach to governance, based on the principle of artha (material gain or prosperity). It also emphasizes the importance of dharma (righteousness or duty) and niti (policy or ethics) as the foundations of a stable and prosperous state .
- The Arthashastra is a valuable source of historical and cultural information, as well as a guide for modern-day political and economic analysis. It has influenced many thinkers and leaders throughout history, such as Jawaharlal Nehru, Chanakya Pandit, and M. K. Gandhi .



# Famous Sanskrit Authors in India

Sanskrit is one of the oldest and most influential languages in India. It is the language of the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Mahabharata, the Ramayana, and many other classical texts. Sanskrit literature is rich and diverse, spanning various genres and periods. Here are some of the famous Sanskrit authors in India:

- **Kalidasa**: He is considered to be the greatest Sanskrit poet and dramatist, who lived in the 4th or 5th century CE. He wrote two epic poems, Kumārasambhava and Raghuvaṃśa, which depict the lives of the gods and kings. He also wrote three plays, Mālavikāgnimitram, Abhijñānaśākuntalam, and Vikramorvaśīyam, which are known for their romantic and lyrical style. He is admired for his mastery of language, imagery, and emotion .
- **Bharavi**: He was a 6th century CE poet who wrote the Kirātārjunīya, an epic poem that narrates the encounter between Arjuna and Shiva in the form of a hunter. The poem is known for its complex and ornate style, as well as its philosophical and moral themes.
- **Bhartṛhari**: He was a 5th century CE poet and philosopher who wrote three collections of verses, the Śatakatraya, on the topics of love, politics, and renunciation. He is also attributed with the Vākyapadīya, a treatise on grammar and linguistics.
- **Bhāsa**: He was a 3rd or 4th century BCE playwright who wrote thirteen plays, mostly based on the Mahabharata and the Ramayana. His plays are notable for their realism, humor, and innovation. He is regarded as one of the earliest and most original Sanskrit dramatists.
- **Bhatta Narayana**: He was a 7th century CE poet who wrote the Venīsaṃhāra, a play that depicts the abduction and rescue of Draupadi by the Pandavas. The play is praised for its dramatic and emotional intensity, as well as its poetic beauty.
- **Banabhatta**: He was a 7th century CE poet and prose writer who served as the court poet of King Harsha. He wrote the Harṣacarita, a biography of Harsha, and the Kādambarī, a romantic novel that tells the story of a love affair between a prince and a princess. He is known for his elegant and elaborate style, as well as his use of fantasy and imagination.
- **Vidyadhar Shastri**: He was a 20th century poet who wrote two epic poems, Sārasvatīkaṇṭhābharaṇam and Rāmāyaṇamañjarī, as well as several shorter poems, plays, and songs of praise. He received the Jnanpith Award, India's highest literary award, in 2009. He is recognized for his contribution to the revival and modernization of Sanskrit literature.



# Telugu Literature

- Telugu literature is the body of works written in the Telugu language, a Dravidian language spoken mainly in the states of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana in India.
- Telugu literature consists of various genres, such as poetry, prose, drama, folklore, and song lyrics. Some of the prominent forms of Telugu literature are śataka, padya, gadya, kāvya, prabandha, nāṭaka, and janapada.
- Telugu literature has a long and rich history, dating back to at least the 11th century CE, when the poet Nannaya translated the Mahabharata into Telugu. Some of the notable periods of Telugu literature are:
  - The Age of Nannaya (11th-14th centuries), which saw the emergence of classical Telugu poetry and the development of Telugu grammar and lexicon.
  - The Age of Tikkana (13th-14th centuries), which witnessed the completion of the Telugu Mahabharata by Tikkana and the rise of courtly and devotional poetry by poets like Nannechoda and Palkuriki Somanatha.
  - The Age of Srinatha (14th-15th centuries), which marked the peak of Telugu kāvya (poetic composition) and prabandha (narrative prose) by Srinatha and other poets like Potana, Errana, and Somanatha.
  - The Age of Krishnadevaraya (16th century), which coincided with the golden era of the Vijayanagara Empire and produced some of the finest Telugu poets and writers, such as Krishnadevaraya, Allasani Peddana, Pingali Surana, and Tenali Ramakrishna.
  - The Age of Bhakti (15th-17th centuries), which saw the flourishing of devotional literature and the emergence of new sects and movements, such as Veerashaivism, Vaishnavism, and Shaktism. Some of the prominent bhakti poets were Annamacharya, Tyagaraja, Ramadasu, and Bhadrachala Ramadasu.
  - The Age of Modernity (18th-20th centuries), which witnessed the influence of Western education, culture, and literature on Telugu literature and the emergence of new genres, such as novel, short story, essay, and biography. Some of the modern Telugu writers were Gurajada Apparao, Kandukuri Veeresalingam, Viswanatha Satyanarayana, Sri Sri, and R. K. Narayan.



### Kannada Literature

Kannada literature is the body of written works in the Kannada language, a member of the Dravidian family spoken mainly in the Indian state of Karnataka. The Kannada script is the writing system used in Kannada literature. Kannada literature has a rich and long history, dating back to the 6th century AD. Some of the main features of Kannada literature are:

- Kannada literature can be divided into four main periods: ancient (6th to 13th century), medieval (14th to 18th century), modern (19th to 20th century) and contemporary (21st century).
- Kannada literature covers a wide range of topics, including grammar, philosophy, prosody, rhetoric, chronicles, biography, history, drama, cuisine, dictionaries and encyclopedias. It also has works on scientific subjects such as medicine, mathematics and astrology.
- Kannada literature has produced many eminent poets, writers and scholars, such as Pampa, Ranna, Harihara, Basavanna, Akka Mahadevi, Kumara Vyasa, Sarvajna, Purandara Dasa, Kanaka Dasa, Raghavanka, Kuvempu, Bendre, Shivaram Karanth, U.R. Ananthamurthy, Girish Karnad, Chandrashekhara Kambara and many others.
- Kannada literature has been influenced by various literary traditions, such as Jainism, Shaivism, Vaishnavism, Bhakti, Veerashaivism, Haridasa, Vachana, Navya, Dalit, Bandaya, Navodaya and others. It has also been influenced by other languages and cultures, such as Sanskrit, Prakrit, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Persian, Arabic, English and others.
- Kannada literature has been recognized and awarded by various national and international institutions, such as the Sahitya Akademi, the Jnanpith Award, the Padma Shri, the Padma Vibhushan, the Bharatiya Gnanpith and the Nobel Prize. Kannada is also one of the 22 official languages of India and one of the classical languages of India.



# Malayalam Literature

- Malayalam literature is the body of writing in the Malayalam language, a South-Dravidian language spoken in the Indian state of Kerala .
- The earliest extant literary work in Malayalam is Ramacharitam, a narrative poem based on the Ramayana, composed in the late 12th or early 13th century .
- The subsequent period saw the emergence of two distinct styles of literature: pattu (song) and manipravalam (mixed language).
  - Pattu literature was mainly composed in pure Malayalam, using native meters and vocabulary. It was influenced by folk songs and ballads, and often had a popular and oral character. Some of the notable pattu works are Ramakathapattu, Bhagavad Gita, and Krishnagatha .
  - Manipravalam literature was composed in a mixture of Malayalam and Sanskrit, using the Grantha script. It was influenced by the classical Sanskrit literature and the Tamil bhakti tradition. It was mainly devoted to erotic and devotional themes, and often had a courtly and refined character. Some of the notable manipravalam works are Unnunilisandesam, Niranam poets, and Lilatilakam .
- The modern period of Malayalam literature began in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with the rise of social reform movements, print media, and literary associations. It witnessed the emergence of various genres such as novels, short stories, essays, dramas, and poetry, and the development of diverse literary trends and movements such as romanticism, realism, modernism, and postmodernism.
  - Some of the prominent writers of the modern period are Kumaran Asan, Vallathol Narayana Menon, Ulloor S. Parameswara Iyer, Kesavadev, Thakazhi Sivasankara Pillai, Vaikom Muhammad Basheer, M.T. Vasudevan Nair, O.V. Vijayan, Kamala Surayya, and M. Mukundan .
- Malayalam literature has also produced many notable works in the fields of travelogue, biography, memoir, criticism, translation, and children's literature.
  - Some of the notable works in these fields are Varthamanappusthakam, the first travelogue in any Indian language, written by Paremmakkal Thoma Kathanar in 1785; Ente Katha, the autobiography of Kamala Surayya; Jeevithathinte Oru Samaram, the memoir of K.R. Narayanan, the former president of India; and Chemmeen, the novel by Thakazhi Sivasankara Pillai, translated into many languages and adapted into a film .
- Malayalam literature has also received many awards and recognition, both at the national and international levels. Some of the awards are Jnanpith Award, Sahitya Akademi Award, Kerala Sahitya Akademi Award, Ezhuthachan Puraskaram, Vayalar Award, and Crossword Book Award .



# Sangama Literature in India

- Sangama literature is the earliest known literature in the Tamil language, composed between the 1st and 4th centuries CE by poets belonging to three literary academies (sangams) in Madurai, India .
- Sangama literature consists of 18 works, including eight anthologies of poems (Ettuthokai), ten long poems (Pattuppattu), and a grammar treatise (Tolkappiyam) .
- Sangama literature is broadly classified into two categories: akam (inner) and puram (outer) . Akam poetry deals with love, romance, and eroticism, while puram poetry deals with heroism, war, and public life .

# Pre-Vedic Religion in India

- Pre-Vedic religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the people who lived in the Indus Valley or Harappan civilization, before the arrival of the Indo-Aryan speakers who brought the Vedic religion to India.
- The Pre-Vedic religion is also known as the Dravidian religion, as it is associated with the Dravidian-speaking people who are the native inhabitants of South India.
- The Pre-Vedic religion is characterized by the worship of nature, animals, and fertility goddesses, as well as the use of seals, symbols, and scripts that have not been deciphered yet .
- The Pre-Vedic religion is also considered to be a form of Hinduism, as it shares some common elements with the later Vedic and post-Vedic Hindu traditions, such as the concept of karma, reincarnation, and yoga.
- The Pre-Vedic religion is also influenced by the Agamas, which are non-Vedic texts that deal with the worship of Shiva, Vishnu, and Shakti, as well as the rituals, ethics, and philosophy of Hinduism.
- The Pre-Vedic religion is largely lost to history, as the Harappan civilization declined and disappeared around 1500 BCE, due to various factors such as environmental changes, invasions, and migrations.
- The Pre-Vedic religion is still preserved in some aspects of the folk and tribal religions of India, as well as in some of the ancient temples and shrines that are dedicated to the Pre-Vedic deities.



# Vedic Religion in India

- Vedic religion was the religion of the ancient Indo-European-speaking peoples who entered India about 1500 BCE from the region of present-day Iran.
- It takes its name from the collections of sacred texts known as the Vedas, which are the oldest stratum of religious activity in India for which there exist written materials.
- The Vedas are composed in Vedic Sanskrit and consist of four collections: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda and the Atharvaveda.
- The Vedic religion was based on the performance of rituals and sacrifices to various gods and goddesses, who represented natural forces and cosmic principles.
- Some of the prominent Vedic deities were Indra (the king of gods and the god of thunder), Agni (the god of fire and the mediator between humans and gods), Varuna (the god of water and the upholder of moral order), Soma (the god of the intoxicating drink and the source of ecstasy), and Ushas (the goddess of dawn and the bringer of light).
- The Vedic religion also had a concept of a universal soul or spirit called Brahman, which was the source and essence of everything.
- The Vedic religion was not a unified or organized system, but rather a diverse and evolving tradition that varied according to time, place, and social group.
- The Vedic religion gradually transformed into Brahmanism or Brahminism, which emphasized the authority and supremacy of the Brahmins (the priestly class) and the Vedas.
- Brahmanism later gave rise to Hinduism, which incorporated elements from other religious and cultural traditions of India, such as Buddhism, Jainism, and the local folk religions.



# Buddhism in India

- Buddhism is an ancient Indian religion that originated in the 6th century BCE in the region of Magadha (now Bihar) and spread throughout northern India and beyond.
- Buddhism is based on the teachings and life experiences of Siddhartha Gautama, also known as the Buddha (the awakened one), who renounced his princely life and attained enlightenment under a bodhi tree.
- Buddhism teaches the four noble truths: the truth of suffering, the truth of the cause of suffering, the truth of the cessation of suffering, and the truth of the path to the cessation of suffering.
- Buddhism also teaches the eightfold path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration, which are the means to achieve nirvana, the ultimate goal of liberation from the cycle of rebirth (samsara).
- Buddhism has two main branches: Theravada and Mahayana. Theravada is the oldest and most conservative form of Buddhism, which follows the Pali canon of scriptures and emphasizes the practice of meditation and morality. Mahayana is a more diverse and expansive form of Buddhism, which follows various sutras and doctrines and emphasizes the role of bodhisattvas, compassionate beings who postpone their own enlightenment to help others.
- Buddhism flourished in India for over a thousand years, from the 3rd century BCE to the 12th century CE, and produced many schools, sects, and traditions, such as the Madhyamaka, Yogacara, Pure Land, Zen, and Vajrayana. Buddhism also influenced Indian art, architecture, literature, philosophy, and culture.
- Buddhism spread from India to other parts of Asia, such as Central Asia, China, Tibet, Japan, Korea, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and beyond, through various routes of trade, pilgrimage, and missionary activity. Buddhism adapted to different cultures and contexts and developed into various regional and local forms.
- Buddhism declined in India from the 8th century CE onwards, due to various factors, such as the rise of Hinduism, the invasions of Muslim rulers, the corruption and degeneration of Buddhist monasteries, and the loss of patronage and support from the laity. Buddhism survived in some pockets of India, such as Ladakh, Sikkim, and Arunachal Pradesh, and was revived in the 20th century by the efforts of reformers, such as B.R. Ambedkar, who converted millions of Dalits (untouchables) to Buddhism.



# Jainism in India

- Jainism is an Indian religion that teaches the path to enlightenment is through nonviolence and reducing harm to living things as much as possible.
- Jainism is one of the world’s oldest religions, originating in India at least 2,500 years ago. The spiritual goal of Jainism is to become liberated from the endless cycle of rebirth and to achieve an all-knowing state called moksha.
- Jainism has always existed and will always exist, according to Jain doctrine. It is considered one of the three most ancient Indian religious traditions still in existence and an integral part of South Asian religious culture, along with Hinduism and Buddhism.
- Jains are the only religious group in India where a majority say they are members of a higher General Category caste. Most Indians (68%) are members of lower castes (Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes or Other Backwards Classes), compared with 20% of Jains who identify with these communities.
- Jainism is practiced by about 4.5 million people in India, which is less than 1% of the total population. The states with the highest proportion of Jains are Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Madhya Pradesh.
- Jainism has two main sects: Digambara and Svetambara, which differ in their views on asceticism, scriptures, and the status of women. Both sects follow the five main vows of ahimsa (nonviolence), satya (truthfulness), asteya (non-stealing), brahmacharya (celibacy or chastity), and aparigraha (non-attachment or non-possessiveness).



# Six System Indian Philosophy

The six system Indian philosophy refers to six schools of thought that originated in ancient India and represent different aspects of Hindu philosophy. They are:

- **Nyaya**: The school of logic and epistemology, founded by Akshapada Gautama in the 2nd century BCE. It deals with the methods of acquiring valid knowledge and the criteria of truth. It also discusses topics such as perception, inference, analogy, verbal testimony, self-awareness, and the nature of the self, God, and liberation .
- **Vaisheshika**: The school of atomism and naturalism, founded by Kanada Kashyapa in the 6th century BCE. It deals with the classification of reality into six categories: substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, and inherence. It also proposes that all physical objects are composed of indivisible atoms that combine in different ways to form complex entities .
- **Samkhya**: The school of dualism and realism, founded by Kapila in the 6th century BCE. It deals with the analysis of the principles of existence, known as purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter). It also explains the evolution of the universe, the nature of the mind, the theory of the three gunas (qualities of nature), and the path of liberation through discrimination .
- **Yoga**: The school of discipline and practice, founded by Patanjali in the 2nd century BCE. It deals with the methods of controlling the fluctuations of the mind and attaining the state of samadhi (absorption). It also outlines the eight limbs of yoga, which are: yama (restraints), niyama (observances), asana (postures), pranayama (breath control), pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), and samadhi (absorption) .
- **Purva Mimamsa**: The school of ritual and exegesis, founded by Jaimini in the 3rd century BCE. It deals with the interpretation of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, and the performance of the Vedic rituals. It also defends the authority of the Vedas, the theory of karma (action and its consequences), and the concept of dharma (duty and morality) .
- **Uttara Mimamsa or Vedanta**: The school of metaphysics and spirituality, founded by Badarayana in the 4th century BCE. It deals with the essence of the Vedas, especially the Upanishads, which are the philosophical texts that explore the nature of reality, the self, and the supreme reality. It also develops various systems of thought, such as Advaita (non-dualism), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism), and Dvaita (dualism), based on different interpretations of the Upanishads .



# Shankaracharya and Advaita Vedanta

- Shankaracharya, also known as Adi Shankara, was an Indian philosopher and theologian who expounded the doctrine of Advaita Vedanta, one of the six orthodox schools of Hindu philosophy .
- Advaita Vedanta is a non-dualistic system of thought that holds that the ultimate reality is Brahman, the supreme self, which is identical to the individual self (atman) of each being.
- Shankaracharya was born in the 8th century CE in a Brahmin family in Kaladi, a village in present-day Kerala . He renounced the worldly pleasures at a very young age and became a wandering ascetic.
- Shankaracharya traveled across India, engaging in debates with scholars of various schools of thought, such as Buddhism, Jainism, Mimamsa, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, and Samkhya, and establishing his authority as a master of Vedanta .
- Shankaracharya wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras, and the Bhagavad Gita, the three foundational texts of Vedanta, as well as independent works such as Vivekachudamani and Upadesasahasri, which explain the core concepts of Advaita Vedanta .
- Shankaracharya also composed devotional hymns, such as Bhaja Govindam and Saundaryalahari, which express his reverence for various deities, such as Shiva, Vishnu, Devi, and Ganesha .
- Shankaracharya established four mathas (monastic institutions) in the four corners of India, namely Sringeri in the south, Dwarka in the west, Puri in the east, and Badrinath in the north, which are headed by his disciples and successors, known as Shankaracharyas .
- Shankaracharya is credited with reviving the Hindu tradition and culture, which was under the influence of Buddhism and other heterodox sects, and unifying the diverse schools of Hindu philosophy under the umbrella of Vedanta .
- Shankaracharya's philosophy of Advaita Vedanta has been influential in the development of modern Indian thought, as well as in the dialogue with other religious and philosophical traditions, such as Christianity, Islam, and Western philosophy .
- Shankaracharya is regarded as one of the greatest sages and thinkers of India, and is revered by millions of Hindus as a saint and an incarnation of Shiva .

# Heterodox Sects in India

- Heterodox sects are those that do not accept the authority of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, and challenge the orthodox Brahmanical traditions.
- Heterodox sects emerged in the 6th century BCE as part of the Sramana movement, which was a reaction to the social, political and religious changes in the Indian society at that time.
- Heterodox sects advocated different paths of liberation from the cycle of rebirth, such as asceticism, renunciation, non-violence, rationalism, materialism, and fatalism.
- Some of the major heterodox sects in India are:

  - Jainism: Founded by Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, Jainism teaches the doctrine of ahimsa (non-violence), anekantavada (multiple perspectives), and aparigraha (non-attachment). Jainism is divided into two main sects: Digambara (sky-clad) and Svetambara (white-clad).
  - Buddhism: Founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, Buddhism teaches the four noble truths, the eightfold path, and the concept of nirvana (cessation of suffering). Buddhism is divided into three main branches: Theravada (the way of the elders), Mahayana (the great vehicle), and Vajrayana (the diamond vehicle).

# Bhakti Movement in India

- The Bhakti movement was a significant religious movement in medieval Hinduism that sought to bring religious reforms to all strata of society by adopting the method of devotion to achieve salvation.

- The movement spread to North India in the 12th-13th centuries CE with the influence of the Vaishnava saints like Ramananda, Kabir, and Nanak, and the Shaiva saints like Basava, Gorakhnath, and Ravidas .

# Sufi movement in India

- Sufism is a mystical branch of Islam that emphasizes the direct experience of God through love, devotion, and ethical conduct.

- The Sufi movement in India began in the 13th century and reached its peak in the 15th and 16th centuries under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal Empire.

  - The rise of a new middle class of educated and professional Indians, who were influenced by the modern ideas and values and wanted to reform their society and religion according to them.

  - The Brahmo Samaj, founded by Raja Ram Mohan Roy in 1828, which aimed to reform the Hindu religion by rejecting idolatry, polytheism, rituals, priesthood, caste system, sati, child marriage and purdah, and promoting monotheism, rationalism, humanism, social service and women's education and emancipation .

  - The Theosophical Society, founded by Madame Blavatsky and Colonel Olcott in 1875, which aimed to promote the study of ancient religions and philosophies, especially Hinduism and Buddhism, and to establish a universal brotherhood of humanity irrespective of race, creed or color .
  - The Aligarh Movement, founded by Sir Syed Ahmed Khan in 1875, which aimed to reform the Muslim society and religion by promoting modern education, rationalism, social service and loyalty to the British government .
  - The Singh Sabha Movement, founded by Khem Singh Bedi and others in 1873, which aimed to reform the Sikh religion and society by removing the corruptions and superstitions, and restoring the purity and glory of Sikhism .
  - The Satyashodhak Samaj, founded by Jyotirao Phule in 1873, which aimed to liberate the lower castes and women from the exploitation and oppression of the upper castes, and to establish a society based on truth, justice and equality .
  - The Young Bengal Movement, led by Henry Vivian Derozio and his students in the 1820s and 1830s, which aimed to spread the spirit of free inquiry, rationalism, liberalism and nationalism among the youth of Bengal, and to challenge the orthodoxy and conservatism of the Hindu society .

# Physics in India and Ancient India

Physics is the branch of science that deals with the nature and properties of matter and energy. Physics in India has a long and rich history, dating back to the ancient times. Some of the main topics and achievements of physics in India and ancient India are:

- **Elements and atoms:** Indian philosophers proposed various theories of elements and atoms, based on observation and logic. The Nyaya and Vaisheshika schools of thought identified five basic elements: earth, water, fire, air and ether. They also postulated that these elements were composed of indivisible and eternal atoms (parmanu), which could combine in different ways to form complex substances. The Buddhist atomists Dharmakirti and Dignaga further developed the concept of atoms and their properties, such as shape, size, motion, and causality. They also suggested that atoms had a dual nature of being both particles and waves, which is similar to the modern quantum theory   .

- **Energy and radiation:** Indian physicists and astronomers made significant contributions to the understanding of energy and radiation, especially in relation to the sun and the stars. They recognized that energy and mass were equivalent, and that heat and light were manifestations of energy. They also proposed that light consisted of discrete particles (anu), which could travel in straight lines or be reflected or refracted by different media. They also studied the nature and effects of solar and lunar eclipses, the phases of the moon, the seasons, the tides, and the planetary motions .

- **Gravity and motion:** Indian mathematicians and physicists developed sophisticated methods of measuring and calculating the gravitational force and the motion of celestial bodies. They used trigonometry, algebra, geometry, and calculus to derive accurate formulas and tables for predicting the positions and movements of the sun, the moon, and the planets. They also applied the principles of gravity and motion to terrestrial phenomena, such as the flight of projectiles, the pendulum, the balance, and the lever. They also formulated the concepts of inertia, momentum, and acceleration, which are the basis of classical mechanics .

- **Sound and music:** Indian physicists and musicians explored the nature and properties of sound and music, and their effects on the human mind and body. They discovered the relation between the frequency, wavelength, and amplitude of sound waves, and the pitch, loudness, and quality of sound. They also devised various musical instruments, scales, modes, and rhythms, based on the principles of harmony, resonance, and vibration. They also studied the psychological and physiological aspects of sound and music, such as the emotions, moods, and health benefits they could produce .

- **Optics and vision:** Indian physicists and ophthalmologists investigated the phenomena of optics and vision, and their applications to medicine and art. They understood the structure and function of the human eye, and the process of vision. They also developed methods of diagnosing and treating various eye diseases, such as cataract, glaucoma, and myopia. They also experimented with lenses, mirrors, prisms, and colors, and their effects on light and vision. They also used optics and vision to create realistic and aesthetic paintings, sculptures, and architecture .

These are some of the main topics and achievements of physics in India and ancient India. Physics in India has a rich legacy of scientific inquiry and discovery, which has influenced and inspired many other fields of knowledge and culture.

# Medicine in India and Ancient India

- Medicine in India is a diverse and ancient field of knowledge and practice that encompasses various systems of health care, such as Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, Yoga, Naturopathy, and Homeopathy.
- Ayurveda is the most ancient system of Indian medicine, which is based on the Vedas, the sacred texts of Hinduism. Ayurveda means "the science of life" and it aims to balance the three doshas (vata, pitta, and kapha) that govern the body, mind, and spirit. Ayurveda uses herbs, minerals, metals, diet, lifestyle, and massage to prevent and treat diseases .
- Siddha is another ancient system of Indian medicine, which originated in Tamil Nadu. Siddha means "perfected one" and it is based on the concept of 96 tattvas (elements) that make up the human body. Siddha uses herbs, metals, minerals, animal products, and alchemy to cure diseases and promote longevity.
- Unani is a system of medicine that was developed by the Greeks and Arabs and later adopted by the Indians. Unani means "Greek" and it is based on the theory of four humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile) that determine the temperament and health of a person. Unani uses herbs, minerals, animal products, diet, exercise, and cupping to balance the humors and treat diseases.
- Yoga is a system of physical, mental, and spiritual practices that originated in India and aims to achieve harmony and union with the supreme reality. Yoga involves various postures, breathing techniques, meditation, and ethical principles to enhance health, well-being, and self-awareness.
- Naturopathy is a system of medicine that relies on the healing power of nature and the vital force of the body. Naturopathy uses natural methods such as diet, water, sunlight, air, massage, and fasting to stimulate the body's self-healing ability and prevent diseases.
- Homeopathy is a system of medicine that was founded by a German physician, Samuel Hahnemann, in the 18th century and later popularized in India. Homeopathy is based on the principle of "like cures like", which means that a substance that causes symptoms in a healthy person can cure the same symptoms in a sick person. Homeopathy uses highly diluted substances, called remedies, to stimulate the body's immune system and restore health.
- Medicine in ancient India was highly advanced and sophisticated, as evidenced by the works of the ancient Indian physicians, such as Charaka, Sushruta, Vagbhata, Nagarjuna, and Patanjali. The temples are often decorated with sculptures, carvings, and paintings depicting gods, goddesses, and mythological scenes. Some examples of Hindu temple architecture are the Khajuraho temples, the Brihadeeswarar temple, and the Konark Sun temple.
- Indo-Islamic architecture is the result of the fusion of Islamic and Indian elements, especially during the Mughal and Rajput periods. The Mughal architecture is known for its use of red sandstone, white marble, arches, domes, minarets, and gardens. The Rajput architecture is known for its use of hill forts, palaces, and water features. Some examples of Indo-Islamic architecture are the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort, and the Hawa Mahal.
- South Indian architecture is influenced by the Dravidian, Pallava, Chola, Vijayanagara, and Nayaka dynasties. The South Indian temples are distinguished by their pyramidal towers, pillared halls, and ornate sculptures. Some examples of South Indian architecture are the Meenakshi Amman temple, the Hampi ruins, and the Mahabalipuram monuments.
- Indo-Saracenic architecture is a style that emerged during the British colonial rule, combining elements of Indian, Islamic, and Gothic architecture. The style is characterized by the use of domes, arches, spires, turrets, and balconies. Some examples of Indo-Saracenic architecture are the Victoria Memorial, the Gateway of India, and the Mysore Palace.
- Indian architecture is not only a reflection of its cultural heritage, but also a source of engineering marvels. The ancient and medieval Indian architects and engineers used various techniques and materials to create structures that are still standing today, such as the iron pillar of Delhi, the stepwells of Gujarat, the Kailasa temple of Ellora, and the Qutub Minar of Delhi.
- Indian architecture also faces the challenges of conservation and preservation, as many of the heritage buildings and monuments are threatened by natural disasters, urbanization, vandalism, and neglect. There is a need for more awareness, research, and innovation to protect and restore the architectural heritage of India .



# Sculptures as Cultural Heritage of India

- Sculptures are three-dimensional art forms that are created by carving, modelling, casting, or assembling various materials such as stone, metal, wood, clay, or plastic.
- Sculptures have been an integral part of the Indian culture since ancient times, reflecting the religious, social, political, and artistic aspects of the diverse and rich civilization.
- Some of the earliest sculptures in India are found in the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 3300-1300 BCE), such as the famous bronze Dancing Girl from Mohenjo-daro, which shows the skill and sophistication of the metalworkers of that era.
- The Mauryan period (c. 322-185 BCE) witnessed the emergence of stone sculptures, such as the Ashoka pillars and the lion capital at Sarnath, which symbolize the power and authority of the emperor Ashoka, as well as his patronage of Buddhism.
- The post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE-320 CE) saw the development of various regional styles of sculptures, such as the Gandhara school, which blended Greek and Indian influences to depict the life and teachings of Buddha in a realistic manner, and the Mathura school, which created the first anthropomorphic images of Buddha and other deities in a stylized and idealized way.
- The Gupta period (c. 320-550 CE) is considered the golden age of Indian sculpture, as it reached its peak of excellence and refinement. The sculptures of this period are characterized by grace, elegance, and harmony, as seen in the famous Buddha head from Sarnath, the Vishnu reclining on the serpent Ananta from Deogarh, and the rock-cut caves of Ajanta and Ellora, which are adorned with exquisite paintings and sculptures depicting various scenes from Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain mythology and history.
- The medieval period (c. 550-1200 CE) witnessed the proliferation of temple architecture and sculpture in India, as various dynasties and kingdoms patronized different religious sects and artistic traditions. Some of the notable examples of temple sculptures are the Khajuraho temples of the Chandela dynasty, which are famous for their erotic and sensual sculptures depicting human and divine love, the Sun temple of Konark of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, which is a monumental representation of the sun god Surya and his chariot, and the Brihadeshwara temple of the Chola dynasty, which is a masterpiece of Dravidian architecture and sculpture, featuring a colossal Nandi (bull) statue and numerous bronze icons of Shiva and other deities.
- The modern period (c. 1200-present) saw the influence of Islamic, European, and other foreign cultures on Indian sculpture, as well as the emergence of new forms and mediums of expression, such as terracotta, ivory, wood, metal, and marble. Some of the prominent modern sculptors of India are Ramkinkar Baij, who is regarded as the father of modern Indian sculpture, Nandalal Bose, who was a pioneer of the Bengal school of art, Amrita Sher-Gil, who was a leading figure of the Indian modernist movement, and M.F. Husain, who was one of the most celebrated and controversial artists of India.



# Seals as Cultural Heritage of India

- Seals are small objects made of stone, metal, or other materials that have a carved or engraved design on one side and are used to stamp or seal documents, containers, or other items.
- Seals have been used in India since ancient times, especially during the Indus Valley Civilization (2500-1900 BCE), which was one of the earliest urban civilizations in the world.
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization are mostly square or rectangular in shape, and measure about 2-3 cm on each side. They are made of steatite, a soft stone that can be easily carved and baked to harden it. Some seals are also made of copper, terracotta, faience, or ivory.
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization have two main features: an animal motif and a script. The animal motif is usually a realistic depiction of a wild or domestic animal, such as a bull, a buffalo, a rhinoceros, an elephant, a tiger, a goat, or a unicorn. The script is a series of symbols or signs that are arranged in one or more lines above or below the animal motif. The script is still undeciphered, and its meaning and language are unknown.
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization were used for various purposes, such as trade, administration, identification, or ritual. They were stamped on clay tablets, tags, or sealings that were attached to goods, documents, or containers. They were also worn as amulets or ornaments by the people. Some seals may have had religious or symbolic significance, such as the famous Pashupati seal, which shows a horned figure surrounded by animals and is interpreted as a representation of a proto-Shiva or a fertility deity.
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization are important sources of information about the culture, society, economy, and religion of the ancient Indians. They reflect the artistic skill, technological innovation, and cultural diversity of the people. They also show the contact and exchange of ideas and goods with other civilizations, such as Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Central Asia.
- Seals of the Indus Valley Civilization are part of the cultural heritage of India, as they are among the earliest and most distinctive expressions of Indian art and writing. They are also part of the world heritage, as they are recognized by UNESCO as part of the archaeological sites of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, which are inscribed on the World Heritage List. They are preserved and displayed in various museums and institutions in India and abroad, such as the National Museum in Delhi, the Archaeological Survey of India, the British Museum, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

- The origin of coinage in India can be traced back to the first millennium BCE, when punch-marked coins made of silver and copper were used as a medium of exchange.
- The earliest coins were called karshapanas or panas, and they had symbols or marks punched on them, such as geometric shapes, animals, plants, or human figures.
- The Mauryan Empire (4th-2nd century BCE) issued the largest number of punch-marked coins, which had the royal emblem of a peacock or a lion on them.
- The post-Mauryan period (2nd-1st century BCE) saw the emergence of various regional powers, such as the Indo-Greeks, the Shakas, the Parthians, and the Kushanas, who introduced new types of coins in India.

- The Shakas and the Parthians followed the Indo-Greek model of coinage, but also added some distinctive features, such as the depiction of the king on horseback or the use of Iranian or Kharoshthi scripts.
- The Kushanas (1st-3rd century CE) issued the most diverse and artistic coins in ancient India, which had images of the king, various deities, animals, and symbols, as well as legends in Greek, Brahmi, and Kharoshthi scripts.
- The Kushana coins also show the influence of Roman, Persian, and Chinese cultures, as they used gold, silver, and copper metals, and adopted the Roman weight standard and the Persian fire altar motif.
- The Gupta Empire (4th-6th century CE) is considered the golden age of Indian coinage, as they issued the most beautiful and elegant coins in ancient India, which had realistic portraits of the kings, and legends in Sanskrit and Brahmi scripts.
- The Gupta coins also depicted various aspects of the royal life, such as hunting, playing musical instruments, performing rituals, or riding elephants.
- The Gupta coins also show the religious diversity of India, as they had images of Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain deities, such as Vishnu, Shiva, Lakshmi, Buddha, and Mahavira.
- The post-Gupta period (6th-12th century CE) witnessed the decline of a unified empire and the rise of regional kingdoms, such as the Pallavas, the Chalukyas, the Rashtrakutas, the Pandyas, the Cholas, the Pratiharas, the Palas, and the Chauhans, who issued their own coins in different styles and metals.

# Pottery as Cultural Heritage of India

- Pottery is one of the most ancient and tangible forms of Indian art. It reflects the cultural diversity and creativity of the people who have inhabited the Indian subcontinent for thousands of years.
- Pottery has been used for various purposes, such as cooking, storing, serving, religious rituals, and artistic expression. Pottery also plays an important role in studying culture and reconstructing the past, as it provides clues about the social, economic, and technological aspects of different civilizations and periods.
- The earliest evidence of pottery in India dates back to the prehistoric settlements of Lahuradewa and Mehrgarh, which are older than the Indus Valley Civilization. The Indus Valley Civilization, which flourished between 2600 and 1900 BCE, was one of the first cultures to use pottery on a large scale. The pottery of this civilization was mainly wheel-made, and featured geometric patterns, animal motifs, and glazed surfaces.
- After the decline of the Indus Valley Civilization, pottery continued to develop and diversify in different regions and cultures of India. Some of the notable types of pottery in India are:

  - Black and red ware: This type of pottery is characterized by its black and red color scheme, and is found in various sites of the Iron Age and the early historical period. It is associated with the Vedic culture and the Mahajanapadas.
  - Painted grey ware: This type of pottery is distinguished by its fine grey fabric and painted designs, usually in black. It is found in the sites of the later Vedic period and the Mahabharata era, and is linked with the rise of urbanization and state formation in northern India.
  - Northern black polished ware: This type of pottery is known for its glossy black surface and metallic luster. It is found in the sites of the Mauryan and post-Mauryan period, and is indicative of the high level of craftsmanship and trade in this period.
  - Red ware: This type of pottery is characterized by its red color and plain or decorated surface. It is found in various sites of the Gupta and post-Gupta period, and is associated with the classical age of Indian art and culture.
  - Glazed ware: This type of pottery is characterized by its use of glaze, a thin layer of glass-like substance that gives a shiny and colorful appearance to the pottery. It is found in various sites of the medieval and modern period, and is influenced by the Islamic and Persian styles of pottery. Some of the famous centers of glazed pottery in India are Khurja, Sindh, Multan, and Delhi.
  - Terracotta: This type of pottery is made from clay that is baked and hardened. It is used for making various objects, such as figurines, toys, sculptures, and architectural elements. Terracotta is one of the most widespread and popular forms of pottery in India, and is found in various regions and cultures, such as Bengal, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, and Manipur.

- Pottery is still a living and vibrant craft tradition in India, and is practiced by many communities and artisans across the country. Pottery is also an eco-friendly and sustainable form of art, as it uses natural materials and can be recycled or reused. Pottery is a part of the cultural heritage of India, and showcases the rich and diverse history and creativity of the Indian people.

# Music as Cultural Heritage of India

- Music is an integral part of the cultural heritage of India. It reflects the diversity, history and evolution of the Indian civilization.

- Music of India has also been influenced by various external sources, such as Persian, Arabic, Turkish, Central Asian, European and American music. These influences have resulted in the emergence of new genres, such as ghazal (poetic songs), thumri (semi-classical songs), filmi (cinema music), pop, rock, jazz, fusion and electronic music. These genres have contributed to the global recognition and popularity of Indian music.



# Theatre as Cultural Heritage of India

- Theatre is a form of performing art that involves actors, dialogue, music, dance, and spectacle to create a dramatic representation of a story or a theme.
- Theatre in India has a long and rich history, dating back to the Vedic period (1500-500 BCE), when rituals and hymns were performed as a form of worship and entertainment.
- The earliest text on theatre in India is the Natya Shastra, written by Bharata Muni around 500 BCE. It is a comprehensive treatise on the theory and practice of theatre, covering aspects such as dramaturgy, aesthetics, stagecraft, genres, styles, and emotions.
- Theatre in India has been influenced by various cultural, religious, and linguistic traditions, resulting in a diversity of forms and expressions. Some of the major types of theatre in India are:

  - Classical theatre: Based on the rules and conventions of the Natya Shastra, classical theatre is performed in Sanskrit or Prakrit languages, and follows the genres of nataka (drama) and prakarana (comedy). Some of the famous playwrights of classical theatre are Kalidasa, Bhasa, Sudraka, and Vishakhadatta. Some of the famous plays of classical theatre are Abhijnana Shakuntalam, Swapnavasavadatta, Mrichchhakatika, and Mudrarakshasa.
  - Folk theatre: Based on the local traditions and cultures of different regions, folk theatre is performed in vernacular languages, and incorporates elements of music, dance, and improvisation. Folk theatre is often associated with festivals, rituals, and social issues, and reflects the identity and values of the communities. Some of the popular forms of folk theatre are Nautanki, Tamasha, Bhavai, Jatra, Yakshagana, and Koodiyattam.
  - Modern theatre: Based on the influences of Western theatre and the socio-political changes in India, modern theatre emerged in the 19th and 20th centuries, and experimented with new themes, forms, and techniques. Modern theatre is performed in various languages, and addresses issues such as nationalism, colonialism, social justice, and individualism. Some of the prominent playwrights of modern theatre are Rabindranath Tagore, Mohan Rakesh, Vijay Tendulkar, Girish Karnad, and Mahesh Dattani. Some of the notable plays of modern theatre are Raktakarabi, Ashadh Ka Ek Din, Ghashiram Kotwal, Tughlaq, and Dance Like a Man.

- Theatre in India is a cultural heritage that showcases the diversity, creativity, and vitality of the Indian people. Theatre in India is also a medium of education, communication, and social change, that challenges, entertains, and inspires the audiences.

# Fairs and Festivals as Cultural Heritage of India

- India is a land of diverse cultures, religions, languages, and traditions.   - **Dussehra**: A festival of triumph celebrated in September or October, when the defeat of the demon king Ravana by Lord Rama is reenacted. People watch plays, processions, and effigies of Ravana being burned. It is also a festival of culture, art, and music.
  - **Ganesh Chaturthi**: A festival of the elephant-headed god Ganesh celebrated in August or September, when idols of Ganesh are installed in homes and public places. People worship Ganesh, offer him sweets, and immerse the idols in water after 10 days.
  - **Navratri**: A festival of the nine forms of the goddess Durga celebrated in September or October, when people worship Durga, perform dances, and wear colorful clothes. It is also a festival of power, devotion, and femininity.
  - **Pushkar Mela**: A fair held in the holy town of Pushkar in Rajasthan in November, when thousands of camels, horses, and cattle are traded. People also take a dip in the sacred lake, visit the temples, and enjoy the cultural and musical events .
  - **Jaisalmer Desert Festival**: A festival held in the golden city of Jaisalmer in Rajasthan in February, when the desert comes alive with camel races, folk dances, puppet shows, turban tying contests, and firework displays. It is also a festival of the desert culture and heritage .
  - **Goa Carnival**: A carnival held in the coastal state of Goa in February or March, when the streets are filled with colorful floats, masks, costumes, and music. People also enjoy the beach parties, food stalls, and cultural shows. It is also a festival of fun, frolic, and freedom .
  - **Konark Dance Festival**: A festival held in the ancient sun temple of Konark in Odisha in December, when classical dancers from all over the country perform in front of the temple. People also enjoy the crafts fair, the sand art exhibition, and the cultural programs. It is also a festival of the dance and music heritage of India .

- India is a diverse country with a rich and varied cultural heritage. India has one of the world’s largest collections of songs, music, dance, theatre, folk traditions, performing arts, rites and rituals, paintings and writings that are known, as the ‘Intangible Cultural Heritage’ (ICH) of humanity.

  - India has nominated the Hoysala Temples, a group of 13th century temples in Karnataka, for inscription in the World Heritage List, for their architectural and artistic excellence.
  - India has got back a 10th century Goat-Headed Yogini Statue, which was stolen from a temple in Uttar Pradesh and smuggled to France, after a legal battle of 22 years.
  - India has witnessed a surge in contemporary art, which explores new trends and themes in Indian paintings, such as social issues, identity, spirituality, and urbanization.
  - India has celebrated its 72nd Republic Day with a grand parade, which showcased its cultural diversity and achievements in various fields. The parade also featured a tableau of the Ram Temple in Ayodhya, which is under construction after a historic verdict by the Supreme Court.

  - India has also witnessed a rise in cultural festivals and events, such as the Sydney WorldPride, which brings the LGBT community together, and the Jaipur Literature Festival, which celebrates the literary and creative talents of India and the world.



# Indian's Cultural Contribution to the World

India is a country of diverse and rich culture, heritage and history. It has made many contributions to the world in various fields such as mathematics, architecture, medicine, language, art and more. Some of the major contributions of India to the world are:

- **Mathematics**: India is the birthplace of the decimal system, the concept of zero, the place value system, algebra, trigonometry, geometry, calculus and many other branches of mathematics. India also invented the numerals that are used worldwide today .
- **Architecture**: India has a long and varied tradition of architecture, ranging from the ancient Indus Valley civilization to the modern marvels of today. India is known for its temples, palaces, forts, monuments, mosques, churches and other structures that reflect the diversity of its culture and religion. Some of the famous examples of Indian architecture are the Taj Mahal, the Qutub Minar, the Khajuraho temples, the Hampi ruins, the Ajanta and Ellora caves and the Lotus Temple .
- **Textiles**: India is one of the largest producers and exporters of cotton, silk, wool and other fabrics in the world. India is also the origin of many types of textiles, such as muslin, calico, chintz, khadi, brocade, bandhani, ikat and more. India also introduced the technique of printing and dyeing fabrics using natural colors and motifs .
- **Medicine**: India has a rich and ancient tradition of medicine, known as Ayurveda, which means the science of life. Ayurveda is based on the principles of balance, harmony and natural healing. India also developed the concepts of surgery, anesthesia, plastic surgery, vaccination, dentistry and more. India also gave the world the first medical encyclopedia, the Charaka Samhita, and the first surgeon, Sushruta .
- **Language**: India is a multilingual country, with 22 official languages and hundreds of regional languages and dialects. India is also the source of many languages that are spoken around the world, such as Sanskrit, Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam and more. India also influenced the development of many other languages, such as English, Persian, Arabic, Greek, Latin and more. India also gave the world the oldest and most influential literary works, such as the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Ramayana, the Mahabharata, the Bhagavad Gita and more .
- **Art**: India has a rich and diverse tradition of art, encompassing painting, sculpture, pottery, metalwork, jewelry, dance, music, theatre, cinema and more. India is known for its vibrant and colorful art forms, such as the Madhubani paintings, the Pattachitra paintings, the Tanjore paintings, the Rajasthani miniatures, the Mughal paintings, the Buddhist sculptures, the Chola bronzes, the Meenakari jewelry, the Bharatanatyam dance, the Kathakali dance, the Carnatic music, the Hindustani music, the Bollywood movies and more .
- **Religion**: India is the birthplace of four major religions of the world, namely Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism. India is also the home of many other religions, such as Islam, Christianity, Judaism, Zoroastrianism and more. India is a secular and tolerant country, where people of different faiths coexist peacefully and respectfully. India also gave the world the concepts of karma, dharma, reincarnation, nirvana, yoga, meditation and more .

These are some of the major contributions of India to the world, but there are many more that cannot be listed here. India is a country that has enriched the world with its culture, knowledge, wisdom and creativity. India is a country that deserves respect and admiration from the world.



# Indian Cinema as a Performing Art in India

- Indian cinema is a form of performing art that combines storytelling, acting, music, dance, and visual effects to create a powerful medium of expression and entertainment.
- Indian cinema has a long and rich history that dates back to the silent era of the early 20th century, when pioneers like Dadasaheb Phalke and Raja Harishchandra made the first feature films in India.
- Indian cinema has been influenced by various cultural and artistic traditions, such as the classical Indian theatre, the folk theatre, the Indian epics, the Sanskrit drama, the Parsi theatre, and the Western theatre .
- Indian cinema has also been shaped by the social and political changes that occurred in India, such as the colonial rule, the independence movement, the partition, the nation-building, the emergency, the liberalization, and the globalization.
- Indian cinema has produced many genres and styles, such as the mythological films, the historical films, the social films, the musical films, the comedy films, the action films, the thriller films, the horror films, the romance films, the parallel cinema, the art cinema, the regional cinema, and the new wave cinema .
- Indian cinema has also given birth to many stars and legends, such as Prithviraj Kapoor, Devika Rani, Ashok Kumar, Dilip Kumar, Raj Kapoor, Nargis, Guru Dutt, Madhubala, Meena Kumari, Rajesh Khanna, Amitabh Bachchan, Sharmila Tagore, Hema Malini, Rajinikanth, Kamal Haasan, Sridevi, Aamir Khan, Shah Rukh Khan, Salman Khan, Madhuri Dixit, Kajol, Aishwarya Rai, Irrfan Khan, Priyanka Chopra, and many more .
- Indian cinema has also contributed to the development of other performing arts, such as music, dance, theatre, and television, by providing a platform for many artists, composers, singers, dancers, directors, writers, and producers .
- Indian cinema has also been recognized and appreciated by the global audience and critics, by winning many awards and accolades, such as the Oscars, the Cannes, the Berlin, the Venice, the BAFTA, the Golden Globe, and the National Film Awards .
- Indian cinema is a performing art that reflects the diversity, the complexity, the beauty, and the dynamism of India and its people. It is a source of pride, joy, and inspiration for millions of Indians and lovers of cinema around the world.
