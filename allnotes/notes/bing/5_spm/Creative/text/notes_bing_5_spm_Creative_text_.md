

# Software Project Management

Software project management is the process of planning and leading software projects. It is a sub-discipline of project management in which software projects are planned, implemented, monitored and controlled.

Software project management involves the following phases and best practices:

- **Initiation**: This phase defines the scope, objectives, feasibility, and stakeholders of the software project. It also involves identifying the project sponsor, the project manager, and the project team.
- **Planning**: This phase involves creating a detailed plan for the software project, including the tasks, milestones, deliverables, resources, budget, schedule, quality standards, risk management, and communication plan. The plan should be aligned with the project scope and objectives, and should be approved by the project sponsor and stakeholders.
- **Execution**: This phase involves executing the tasks and activities according to the project plan, and managing the resources, budget, quality, and risks. It also involves coordinating and communicating with the project team, the project sponsor, the stakeholders, and the end-users.
- **Monitoring and Control**: This phase involves tracking and measuring the progress and performance of the software project, and comparing them with the project plan. It also involves identifying and resolving any issues, changes, or deviations that may arise during the project. The project manager should report the project status and performance to the project sponsor and stakeholders regularly, and seek their feedback and approval as needed.
- **Closure**: This phase involves completing and delivering the software project, and ensuring that it meets the expectations and requirements of the project sponsor, the stakeholders, and the end-users. It also involves conducting a project review and evaluation, documenting the lessons learned, and celebrating the project success.

Software project management requires the use of project management software, which is software used for project planning, scheduling, resource allocation and change management. Project management software can help the project manager and the project team to control costs and manage budgeting, quality management and documentation, and also may be used as an administration system. Some examples of project management software are Microsoft Project, Jira, Trello, and Asana.



## Unit - 1 - Project Evaluation and Project Planning

- Project evaluation is the process of assessing the feasibility, desirability, and viability of a software project before initiating it.
- Project planning is the process of defining the scope, objectives, tasks, resources, schedule, budget, and quality standards of a software project.
- Project evaluation and planning are essential for software project management, as they help to align the expectations of the stakeholders, identify the risks and opportunities, and ensure the efficient and effective use of resources.
- Project evaluation and planning involve the following steps:

  - **Project initiation**: This is the first step, where the project idea is proposed and the need and justification for the project are established. The project sponsor, who is the person or organization that provides the funding and support for the project, initiates the project and appoints the project manager, who is responsible for leading and coordinating the project activities.
  - **Project feasibility study**: This is the second step, where the project manager conducts a preliminary analysis of the technical, economic, social, legal, and environmental aspects of the project to determine its feasibility. The project manager also identifies the project stakeholders, who are the individuals or groups that have an interest or influence on the project, and their expectations and requirements. The project manager prepares a feasibility report that summarizes the findings and recommendations of the feasibility study.
  - **Project selection**: This is the third step, where the project sponsor evaluates the feasibility report and decides whether to approve, reject, or modify the project proposal. The project sponsor may use various criteria and methods to compare and rank the project alternatives, such as cost-benefit analysis, return on investment, net present value, internal rate of return, payback period, etc. The project sponsor also considers the strategic alignment, risk exposure, and resource availability of the project. The project sponsor communicates the project selection decision to the project manager and other stakeholders.
  - **Project definition**: This is the fourth step, where the project manager defines the scope, objectives, deliverables, and acceptance criteria of the project. The project scope is the extent of the work that needs to be done to produce the project deliverables, which are the tangible or intangible outcomes of the project. The project objectives are the specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound goals that the project aims to accomplish. The project acceptance criteria are the standards and specifications that the project deliverables must meet to be accepted by the customer or end-user. The project manager prepares a project charter that documents the project definition and authorizes the project initiation.
  - **Project organization**: This is the fifth step, where the project manager identifies the roles, responsibilities, and relationships of the project team members and other stakeholders. The project manager also determines the project governance structure, which is the framework of policies, procedures, and processes that guide and control the project activities and decisions. The project manager prepares an organization chart that shows the hierarchy and reporting lines of the project team and stakeholders, and a responsibility assignment matrix that shows the allocation of tasks and authority among the project team members.
  - **Project estimation**: This is the sixth step, where the project manager estimates the amount of time, cost, and resources required to complete the project. The project manager may use various techniques and tools to estimate the project parameters, such as expert judgment, analogy, parametric, bottom-up, top-down, three-point, etc. The project manager also considers the factors that may affect the accuracy and reliability of the estimates, such as uncertainty, complexity, scope changes, risks, etc. The project manager prepares an estimation report that documents the assumptions, methods, and results of the project estimation.
  - **Project scheduling**: This is the seventh step, where the project manager defines the sequence, duration, and dependencies of the project tasks and milestones. The project manager also identifies the critical path, which is the longest sequence of tasks that determines the minimum time required to complete the project. The project manager may use various techniques and tools to create and manage the project schedule, such as work breakdown structure, network diagram, Gantt chart, PERT chart, etc. The project manager prepares a project schedule that shows the start and finish dates of the project tasks and milestones, and the allocation of resources and responsibilities.
  - **Project budgeting**: This is the eighth step, where the project manager allocates the funds and resources to the project tasks and activities. The project manager also identifies the sources and methods of funding and financing the project, such as internal or external, equity or debt, etc. The project manager may use various techniques and tools to prepare and control the project budget, such as cost baseline, cost variance, cost performance index, earned value analysis, etc. The project manager prepares a project budget that shows the planned and actual expenditures



### Importance of Software Project Management

Software project management is the process of planning, organizing, executing, monitoring and controlling software development activities to achieve specific goals within a given time and budget. Software project management is important for several reasons, such as:

- It helps to develop a product that meets the needs and expectations of the customers and stakeholders.
- It helps to align the software development activities with the business objectives and strategies of the organization.
- It helps to manage the risks, issues, changes and quality of the software product throughout the development lifecycle.
- It helps to optimize the use of resources, such as time, money, people, tools and techniques, for the software development project.
- It helps to communicate and collaborate effectively with the project team and other stakeholders, such as sponsors, users, vendors and regulators.
- It helps to measure and evaluate the performance and progress of the software development project and take corrective actions if needed.
- It helps to deliver a software product that is reliable, usable, maintainable and scalable.



### Activities in Software Project Management

Software project management is the process of leading, organizing, and controlling a software development project. It involves a range of activities, such as:

- **Scope management**: This activity involves defining and controlling the scope of the software product, including its features, functions, and requirements. Scope management helps to ensure that the project delivers what the client expects and avoids scope creep or gold plating.
- **Project planning and tracking**: This activity involves creating and maintaining a project plan that outlines the tasks, milestones, deliverables, dependencies, resources, and schedule of the project. Project planning and tracking helps to monitor the progress and performance of the project, identify and resolve issues, and manage changes and risks.
- **Project resource management**: This activity involves allocating and managing the human, financial, and physical resources of the project, such as the team members, budget, equipment, and tools. Project resource management helps to optimize the use and availability of the resources, and ensure that the project meets the quality and cost objectives.
- **Scheduling management**: This activity involves estimating and assigning the duration and effort of the project tasks, and sequencing them according to their dependencies and priorities. Scheduling management helps to create a realistic and feasible project timeline, and adjust it as needed to cope with delays or changes.
- **Project communication management**: This activity involves planning and executing the communication strategy of the project, including the stakeholders, channels, frequency, and format of the communication. Project communication management helps to keep the project team and the clients informed and engaged, and facilitate collaboration and feedback.
- **Estimation management**: This activity involves estimating the size, complexity, and cost of the software product, and the time and effort required to develop it. Estimation management helps to provide a basis for planning, budgeting, and scheduling the project, and to measure its feasibility and profitability.
- **Project risk management**: This activity involves identifying, analyzing, and mitigating the potential risks that may affect the project, such as technical, operational, financial, or legal risks. Project risk management helps to prevent or reduce the impact of the risks, and to prepare contingency plans and responses.
- **Project configuration management**: This activity involves managing the changes and versions of the software product and its components, such as the code, documents, and data. Project configuration management helps to ensure the integrity and consistency of the software product, and to track and control the changes and their effects.



### Methodologies in Software Project Management

- A methodology is a set of principles, tools and techniques that are used to plan, execute and manage software projects.
- A methodology helps project managers lead team members and manage work while facilitating team collaboration.
- There are many different methodologies, and they all have pros and cons.
- Some of the most popular methodologies are:

  - **Agile**: Agile is an iterative and incremental approach that emphasizes customer feedback, adaptive planning, self-organizing teams and frequent delivery of working software. Agile methods include Scrum, Kanban, XP, Lean and others.
  - **Waterfall**: Waterfall is a linear and sequential approach that follows a predefined set of phases, such as requirements, design, implementation, testing and deployment. Waterfall is suitable for projects with clear and stable scope, but it can be rigid and inflexible to changes.
  - **Scrum**: Scrum is a framework for implementing agile principles in software development. Scrum uses fixed-length iterations called sprints, where a cross-functional team works on a prioritized list of features called a product backlog. Scrum roles include the product owner, the scrum master and the development team.
  - **Kanban**: Kanban is a method for visualizing and managing the flow of work in software development. Kanban uses a board with columns representing different stages of the process, such as to-do, in progress and done. Kanban limits the number of work items in each column to avoid bottlenecks and waste.
  - **XP**: XP stands for Extreme Programming, which is an agile method that focuses on delivering high-quality software through practices such as pair programming, test-driven development, continuous integration, refactoring and collective ownership.
  - **Lean**: Lean is a philosophy that aims to eliminate waste and maximize value in software development. Lean principles include defining value from the customer's perspective, optimizing the value stream, implementing pull systems, empowering teams, implementing continuous improvement and delivering fast.
  - **PRINCE2**: PRINCE2 stands for Projects in Controlled Environments, which is a structured and scalable methodology that covers the entire project lifecycle. PRINCE2 defines seven principles, seven themes and seven processes that guide the project management. PRINCE2 is widely used in the UK and other countries.
  - **Critical Chain Project Management**: Critical Chain Project Management (CCPM) is a method that focuses on managing the resources and dependencies of a project. CCPM identifies the critical chain, which is the longest sequence of tasks that depend on each other and on resource availability. CCPM uses buffers to protect the critical chain from delays and disruptions.
  - **Adaptive Project Framework**: Adaptive Project Framework (APF) is a method that adapts to the changing needs and expectations of the customer. APF uses cycles of planning, execution and review, where the customer provides feedback and the project scope is adjusted accordingly. APF aims to deliver the maximum business value in the shortest time.
  - **Critical Path Method**: Critical Path Method (CPM) is a technique that calculates the shortest possible duration of a project by identifying the critical path, which is the longest sequence of tasks that must be completed on time. CPM helps to schedule, monitor and control the project activities and resources.
  - **eXtreme Project Management**: eXtreme Project Management (XPM) is a method that deals with complex and uncertain projects that require rapid and flexible responses. XPM uses principles such as embracing change, empowering teams, delivering results and learning from experience. XPM is often used for software testing and coding.
  - **Benefits Realization Management**: Benefits Realization Management (BRM) is a method that focuses on the outcomes and benefits of a project, rather than the outputs and deliverables. BRM involves identifying, planning, measuring and managing the benefits throughout the project lifecycle and beyond. BRM helps to align the project with the strategic goals and objectives of the organization.



### Categorization of Software Projects in spm

Software Project Management (SPM) is a proper way of planning and leading software projects. It is a part of project management in which software projects are planned, implemented, monitored, and controlled.

Software projects can be categorized based on several different factors, such as:

- **Development Model**: This refers to the methodology or framework used to organize and execute the software development process. Some common development models are Agile, Waterfall, Iterative, and Incremental.
- **Platform**: This refers to the type of hardware and software environment where the software application runs. Some common platforms are Desktop, Mobile, Web-based, and Embedded.
- **Scope**: This refers to the size and complexity of the software project, as well as the number of stakeholders, requirements, and resources involved. Some common scopes are Small, Medium, and Large.
- **User Interaction**: This refers to the mode of communication between the software application and the end-users. Some common user interactions are Batch and Interactive.
- **Maturity Level**: This refers to the stage of development and evolution of the software application. Some common maturity levels are New, Legacy, and Mature.
- **Open-Source Software Participation**: This refers to the extent of involvement and contribution of the software project to the open-source software community. Some common levels of participation are None, Partial, or Full.
- **Delivery Method**: This refers to the way the software application is deployed and accessed by the end-users. Some common delivery methods are Internal, Outsource, Hosted, or Cloud-Based.
- **Purpose**: This refers to the main objective or goal of the software project, as well as the expected benefits and outcomes. Some common purposes are Educational, Entertainment, Business, Social, Scientific, etc..

These categories are not mutually exclusive, and a software project can belong to more than one category at a time. Categorizing software projects can help in identifying the characteristics, challenges, and best practices of each type of project, and thus improve the planning and management of software projects  .



### Setting Objectives in SPM

- SPM stands for Software Project Management, which is the discipline of planning, organizing, directing, and controlling the activities of a software project to achieve specific goals.
- Setting objectives is one of the key activities in SPM, as it defines the scope, purpose, and expected outcomes of the project.
- Objectives should be SMART, which means Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound.
- Specific objectives state what the project will deliver, how it will be done, and who will be involved.
- Measurable objectives quantify the results or benefits of the project, and provide criteria for evaluating its success.
- Achievable objectives are realistic and feasible, considering the available resources, constraints, and risks.
- Relevant objectives align with the needs and expectations of the stakeholders, and support the strategic goals of the organization.
- Time-bound objectives set a clear deadline or schedule for the project, and define the milestones and deliverables along the way.
- Setting objectives helps to clarify the project scope, avoid ambiguity and confusion, communicate the project vision, motivate the project team, and monitor the project progress and performance.



### Management Principles in SPM

SPM stands for Software Project Management or Strategic Portfolio Management, depending on the context. Both are related to managing projects or portfolios in a systematic and effective way. Here are some of the common principles of SPM in both domains:

- **Define and document requirements**: A clear and detailed specification of the project or portfolio objectives, scope, deliverables, constraints, assumptions, risks, and stakeholders is essential for successful SPM. Requirements should be aligned with the strategic vision and goals of the organization and should be validated and verified by the relevant parties .
- **Manage changes effectively**: Changes are inevitable in any project or portfolio, due to internal or external factors. SPM requires a proactive and flexible approach to handle changes, by establishing a change management process, assessing the impact and feasibility of changes, communicating and negotiating changes with stakeholders, and updating the project or portfolio plan accordingly .
- **Prioritize projects**: SPM involves selecting and prioritizing the most valuable and feasible projects or initiatives that support the strategic objectives and vision of the organization. SPM requires a rigorous and transparent evaluation and ranking of projects based on multiple criteria, such as strategic alignment, expected benefits, costs, risks, and resource availability .
- **Maintain quality**: Quality is a key factor for the success and satisfaction of any project or portfolio. SPM requires a commitment to quality standards and best practices, by defining quality metrics and criteria, implementing quality assurance and control measures, conducting quality reviews and audits, and ensuring continuous improvement and learning .
- **Utilize automation**: Automation can enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of SPM, by reducing manual errors, saving time and costs, and improving consistency and accuracy. SPM can leverage automation tools and techniques, such as software applications, templates, workflows, dashboards, and reports, to facilitate and streamline various aspects of SPM, such as planning, tracking, reporting, and decision making .
- **Establish communication protocols**: Communication is vital for the coordination and collaboration of any project or portfolio. SPM requires a clear and consistent communication strategy and plan, by identifying the communication needs, channels, frequency, and format for each stakeholder group, and ensuring timely and relevant information exchange and feedback .
- **Manage risk**: Risk is the uncertainty or variability associated with any project or portfolio outcome. SPM requires a proactive and systematic approach to identify, analyze, prioritize, mitigate, and monitor risks, and to prepare contingency and recovery plans for potential issues or crises .
- **Utilize a systematic approach**: SPM is a complex and dynamic process that requires a structured and disciplined methodology and framework, such as the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK) or the Strategic Portfolio Management Process (SPMP), to guide and support the SPM activities and tasks, and to ensure alignment and integration with the organizational strategy and processes   .



### Management Control in SPM

Management control in software project management (SPM) is the process of monitoring and controlling project activities to ensure the project meets its goals and stays on budget. It involves setting performance targets, measuring progress towards those targets, and taking corrective action to ensure the project is on track.

Some of the aspects of management control in SPM are:

- **Scope management**: This is the process of defining and controlling what is and is not included in the project. It helps to avoid scope creep, which is the tendency of the project to expand beyond its original objectives.
- **Cost management**: This is the process of estimating, budgeting, and controlling the costs of the project. It helps to ensure the project is delivered within the allocated resources and provides a basis for measuring the return on investment (ROI) of the project.
- **Schedule management**: This is the process of defining, sequencing, and controlling the activities and milestones of the project. It helps to ensure the project is completed within the agreed time frame and meets the expectations of the stakeholders.
- **Quality management**: This is the process of ensuring that the project deliverables meet the specified quality standards and satisfy the customer requirements. It involves planning, performing, and monitoring quality activities throughout the project life cycle.
- **Risk management**: This is the process of identifying, analyzing, and mitigating the potential threats and opportunities that may affect the project. It helps to reduce the uncertainty and increase the likelihood of project success.
- **Communication management**: This is the process of planning, executing, and controlling the flow of information among the project team, stakeholders, and customers. It helps to ensure that everyone involved in the project is well-informed, engaged, and aligned with the project objectives.
- **Supplier performance management (SPM)**: This is the process of evaluating and managing the performance of the suppliers who provide goods and services to the project. It helps to ensure that the suppliers meet the quality, cost, and delivery expectations and contribute to the project value.

Management control in SPM is a form of risk management, as it helps to spot and resolve project issues before they become serious problems. It also offers a variety of benefits, such as:

- Improving the efficiency and effectiveness of the project processes and outcomes
- Enhancing the customer satisfaction and loyalty
- Increasing the stakeholder confidence and trust
- Aligning the project strategy and work with the business goals and vision
- Providing a basis for learning and improvement for future projects

Management control in SPM requires a proactive and adaptive approach, as the project environment is dynamic and complex. It also requires the use of various tools and techniques, such as:

- Project management software, which helps to plan, execute, and monitor the project activities and resources
- Project management methodologies, which provide a framework and best practices for managing the project life cycle
- Project management standards, which provide a common language and terminology for the project management processes and deliverables
- Project management metrics, which help to measure and evaluate the project performance and progress
- Project management reports, which help to communicate and document the project status and results

Management control in SPM is a vital and challenging aspect of software project management, as it involves managing multiple and interrelated factors that affect the project success. It requires a combination of skills, knowledge, and experience, as well as the collaboration and cooperation of the project team, stakeholders, and customers.



### Risk Evaluation in SPM

Risk evaluation is the systematic process of assessing the likelihood and impact of potential risks on a software project. Risk evaluation is an important part of the Software Project Management (SPM) process, as it helps to identify and prioritize the risks that need to be managed and mitigated. Risk evaluation can be done using various methods, such as qualitative, quantitative, or hybrid approaches. Some of the best risk evaluation methods are:

- **Risk matrix**: A risk matrix is a simple tool that uses a two-dimensional table to assign a risk rating to each risk based on its probability and severity. The risk rating can be expressed as a color code, a numerical value, or a descriptive term. The risk matrix can help to visualize and compare the risks and to determine the appropriate risk response strategies. 
- **Risk register**: A risk register is a comprehensive document that records all the identified risks, their characteristics, their causes, their effects, their risk ratings, and their risk response plans. A risk register can help to track and monitor the risks throughout the project lifecycle and to update the risk information as the project progresses. A risk register can also help to communicate the risks to the stakeholders and to report the risk status. 
- **Risk breakdown structure**: A risk breakdown structure is a hierarchical representation of the sources and categories of risks that affect a project. A risk breakdown structure can help to organize and classify the risks and to identify the areas of the project that are most exposed to risk. A risk breakdown structure can also help to allocate the resources and responsibilities for risk management. 
- **Risk analysis techniques**: Risk analysis techniques are methods that use mathematical models, statistical data, or expert opinions to estimate the probability and impact of the risks. Risk analysis techniques can be qualitative, such as risk ranking, risk scoring, or risk mapping, or quantitative, such as decision tree analysis, Monte Carlo simulation, or sensitivity analysis. Risk analysis techniques can help to measure and quantify the risks and to support the decision-making process.



### Strategic Program Management in Software Project Management

- Strategic program management is a way of managing multiple related software projects that share a common goal and vision, such as developing a new software product or service, or improving an existing one.
- Strategic program management helps to align the software projects with the strategic business objectives and priorities of the organization, and to deliver the expected benefits and value to the stakeholders.
- Strategic program management involves coordinating and overseeing the software projects within the program, as well as managing the interdependencies, risks, issues, resources, costs, and quality across the program.
- Strategic program management requires a high-level perspective and a holistic approach to planning, executing, monitoring, controlling, and closing the software projects within the program.
- Strategic program management also requires effective communication, collaboration, and governance among the program manager, the project managers, the project teams, and the other stakeholders involved in the program.
- Strategic program management can help to achieve the following benefits for the organization and the software projects:

  - Connect the software projects to the strategic business goals and vision of the organization, and ensure that they are aligned and consistent with them.
  - View and manage the interdependencies, dependencies, and conflicts among the software projects, and optimize the use of the available resources, time, and budget across the program.
  - Simplify the resource allocation, prioritization, and decision-making processes for the software projects, and avoid duplication, overlap, or gaps in the scope and deliverables of the program.

  - Foster a culture of collaboration, innovation, and learning among the project teams and the stakeholders, and leverage the best practices, knowledge, and lessons learned from the software projects within the program.



### Stepwise Project Planning in SPM

Stepwise project planning in software project management is the process of breaking down the development process into smaller, manageable steps. This makes it easier for the project manager to plan and track progress, as each step is clearly defined. It also allows for tasks to be divided up and delegated to different team members.

The steps involved in stepwise project planning are :

- **Step 0: Select project.** This involves choosing a project that is feasible, beneficial, and aligned with the organization's goals and strategies.
- **Step 1: Identify project scope and objectives.** This involves defining the boundaries, deliverables, and expected outcomes of the project, as well as the quality, cost, and time constraints.
- **Step 2: Identify project infrastructure.** This involves identifying the resources, roles, responsibilities, and communication channels that will be used for the project, as well as the standards, methods, and tools that will be followed.
- **Step 3: Analyze project characteristics.** This involves analyzing the complexity, size, and risks of the project, as well as the requirements, assumptions, and dependencies that affect the project.
- **Step 4: Identify project products and activities.** This involves identifying the main outputs and inputs of the project, as well as the tasks and subtasks that need to be performed to produce them. This can be done using techniques such as work breakdown structure, product breakdown structure, or product flow diagram.
- **Step 5: Estimate effort for each activity.** This involves estimating the amount of time, effort, and resources that each activity will require, based on the characteristics and requirements of the project. This can be done using techniques such as analogy, expert judgment, or parametric models.
- **Step 6: Identify activity risks.** This involves identifying the potential threats and opportunities that may affect the project, as well as their likelihood and impact. This can be done using techniques such as risk identification, risk analysis, or risk management.
- **Step 7: Allocate resources.** This involves assigning the available resources, such as people, equipment, or materials, to the activities, based on the estimates and priorities of the project. This can be done using techniques such as resource leveling, resource allocation, or resource scheduling.
- **Step 8: Review/publicize plan.** This involves reviewing the plan for completeness, consistency, and feasibility, as well as communicating the plan to the stakeholders, such as the project team, the customers, or the sponsors. This can be done using techniques such as plan review, plan presentation, or plan approval.
- **Step 9: Execute plan/lower levels of planning.** This involves implementing the plan and monitoring the progress, as well as refining the plan as needed, based on the feedback and changes that occur during the project. This can be done using techniques such as plan execution, plan control, or plan revision.

Stepwise project planning is an iterative and flexible approach that can be adapted to different types of projects and situations. It helps to ensure that the project is well-defined, well-organized, and well-managed.



## Unit - 2 - Project Life Cycle and Effort Estimation

- A project life cycle is a series of phases that a project goes through from its initiation to its closure.
- The phases are sequential and usually overlapping, depending on the project management approach used.
- The project life cycle provides a framework for managing the project and its deliverables.
- The project life cycle can be divided into four main phases: initiation, planning, execution, and closure.

### Initiation Phase

- The initiation phase is the first phase of the project life cycle, where the project idea is generated and evaluated.
- The main activities in this phase are:

  - Identifying the project sponsor, who is the person or organization that provides the resources and authority for the project.
  - Defining the project scope, which is the extent of the work to be done and the expected outcomes of the project.
  - Developing the project charter, which is a document that formally authorizes the project and defines its objectives, scope, stakeholders, and high-level requirements.
  - Conducting a feasibility study, which is an analysis of the technical, economic, social, and environmental aspects of the project to determine its viability and benefits.
  - Establishing the project team, which is the group of people who will perform the project work and have specific roles and responsibilities.

### Planning Phase

- The planning phase is the second phase of the project life cycle, where the project objectives are refined and the project plan is developed.
- The main activities in this phase are:

  - Defining the project requirements, which are the specific needs and expectations of the project stakeholders and customers.
  - Developing the project management plan, which is a document that describes how the project will be executed, monitored, and controlled.
  - Creating the work breakdown structure (WBS), which is a hierarchical decomposition of the project scope into manageable deliverables and tasks.
  - Estimating the project resources, which are the human, material, equipment, and financial resources needed for the project.
  - Estimating the project duration, which is the amount of time required to complete the project.
  - Estimating the project cost, which is the total amount of money required to execute the project.
  - Developing the project schedule, which is a graphical representation of the project activities, their dependencies, and their start and finish dates.
  - Developing the project budget, which is a detailed allocation of the project cost to the project activities and deliverables.
  - Developing the project quality plan, which is a document that defines the quality standards, criteria, and methods for the project.
  - Developing the project risk plan, which is a document that identifies the potential risks, their impact, and their response strategies for the project.
  - Developing the project communication plan, which is a document that defines the information needs, channels, and frequency for the project stakeholders and team members.
  - Developing the project procurement plan, which is a document that describes the goods and services to be acquired from external sources for the project.

### Execution Phase

- The execution phase is the third phase of the project life cycle, where the project deliverables are produced and the project plan is implemented.
- The main activities in this phase are:

  - Performing the project work, which is the execution of the tasks and deliverables defined in the WBS and the project plan.
  - Managing the project resources, which is the allocation, utilization, and monitoring of the resources assigned to the project.
  - Managing the project quality, which is the assurance and control of the quality of the project deliverables and processes.
  - Managing the project risks, which is the implementation and tracking of the risk response strategies and the identification of new risks.
  - Managing the project communication, which is the exchange and distribution of the project information among the project stakeholders and team members.
  - Managing the project procurement, which is the selection, contracting, and administration of the external suppliers and vendors for the project.

### Closure Phase

- The closure phase is the fourth and final phase of the project life cycle, where the project is formally completed and closed.
- The main activities in this phase are:

  - Delivering the project deliverables, which is the transfer and acceptance of the project outputs by the project customers and stakeholders.
  - Evaluating the project performance, which is the measurement and analysis of the project results against the project objectives and criteria.
  - Conducting the project audit, which is a systematic and independent review of the project processes, documents, and outcomes.
  - Documenting the project lessons learned, which is the identification and recording of the best practices, successes, failures, and recommendations for future projects.

### Software Process and Process Models

- A software process is a set of activities for designing, implementing, and testing a software system. It defines the tasks, roles, resources, and deliverables involved in software development. A software process also specifies the quality standards, methods, and tools to be used throughout the project lifecycle.  
- A software process model is an abstraction of the software process. It describes the main phases and the order of execution of a software process. A software process model helps to plan, manage, monitor, and control the software development process.   
- There are different types of software process models, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Some of the common software process models are:  
  - Waterfall model: A linear and sequential model that follows a fixed set of steps from requirements analysis to maintenance. Each phase must be completed and verified before moving to the next one. This model is simple, easy to understand, and suitable for well-defined and stable projects. However, it does not accommodate changes easily, and it does not provide early feedback or prototypes to the customers.  
  - Prototyping model: A model that involves building a partial or preliminary version of the software product to elicit feedback from the customers and refine the requirements. This model allows for early detection and correction of errors, and it increases customer satisfaction and involvement. However, it can also lead to unrealistic expectations, scope creep, and increased costs and time.  
  - Incremental model: A model that divides the software product into smaller and independent modules that are developed and delivered incrementally. This model reduces the risk and complexity of the project, and it provides faster and frequent delivery of working software. However, it also requires careful planning and coordination, and it may result in inconsistent or incompatible modules.  
  - Spiral model: A model that combines the iterative and risk-driven aspects of the prototyping model and the waterfall model. It consists of four phases: planning, risk analysis, engineering, and evaluation. Each phase is repeated until the software product meets the customer's expectations. This model is flexible, adaptive, and suitable for complex and large-scale projects. However, it is also expensive, time-consuming, and dependent on the expertise and judgment of the developers.  
  - Iterative model: A model that involves repeating the same set of activities over different versions or iterations of the software product. Each iteration adds more functionality and features to the software product until it is complete and satisfactory. This model allows for incremental improvement, feedback, and learning. However, it also requires clear and stable requirements, and it may result in rework and duplication of effort.  
  - Agile model: A model that follows a set of principles and values that emphasize customer collaboration, adaptive planning, frequent delivery, and continuous improvement. It uses an iterative and incremental approach, where the software product is developed and delivered in short cycles or sprints. It also involves cross-functional and self-organizing teams, and various practices and techniques such as Scrum, Kanban, XP, etc. This model is responsive, flexible, and customer-oriented. However, it also requires high commitment, communication, and discipline from the team members, and it may not be suitable for some types of projects or organizations.  
  - Hybrid model: A model that combines the features and benefits of two or more software process models. For example, some projects may use a waterfall model for the initial phases, and then switch to an agile model for the later phases. Or, some projects may use a spiral model with prototyping and incremental delivery. A hybrid model can be customized and tailored to fit the specific needs and characteristics of the project. However, it can also be complex, inconsistent, and difficult to manage and monitor.

### Choice of Process Models in Software Project Management

A software process model is an abstraction of the software development process that specifies the stages and order of a process. Different software process models have different advantages and disadvantages depending on the nature and scope of the software project. Some of the factors that influence the choice of a software process model are:

- The size and complexity of the software project
- The requirements and expectations of the customers and stakeholders
- The skills and experience of the software development team
- The available resources and budget
- The level of risk and uncertainty
- The degree of flexibility and adaptability

Some of the common software process models are:

- **Waterfall model**: This is a linear and sequential model that divides the software development process into distinct phases, such as requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, and maintenance. Each phase must be completed before moving to the next one, and there is no going back to a previous phase. This model is suitable for simple and well-defined projects with stable and clear requirements, but it is rigid and inflexible for changing or evolving requirements.
- **Iterative model**: This is a cyclical model that repeats the software development process in small iterations, each producing a working version of the software. Each iteration involves planning, analysis, design, implementation, and testing, and feedback from the customers and stakeholders is incorporated in the next iteration. This model is suitable for complex and dynamic projects with unclear or changing requirements, but it requires more time and effort for managing the iterations and ensuring consistency and quality.
- **V model**: This is an extension of the waterfall model that emphasizes the verification and validation of the software at each phase. The V model maps the testing activities to the corresponding development activities, such as unit testing to implementation, integration testing to design, system testing to requirements analysis, and acceptance testing to customer needs. This model is suitable for projects that require high quality and reliability, but it is also rigid and inflexible for changing or evolving requirements.
- **Incremental model**: This is a combination of the waterfall and iterative models that divides the software development process into multiple increments, each producing a partial version of the software. Each increment follows the waterfall model, but the increments are delivered to the customers and stakeholders in an iterative manner. This model is suitable for projects that have modular and independent features or functionalities, but it requires careful planning and coordination of the increments and their dependencies.
- **Spiral model**: This is a risk-driven model that combines the iterative model with risk analysis and management. The spiral model consists of four quadrants: planning, risk analysis, engineering, and evaluation. Each quadrant represents a cycle of the software development process, and the cycles are repeated until the software is completed. This model is suitable for large and complex projects that involve high levels of risk and uncertainty, but it requires extensive expertise and experience in risk assessment and mitigation.
- **Agile model**: This is a value-driven model that focuses on delivering software that meets the needs and expectations of the customers and stakeholders in a fast and flexible manner. The agile model follows the principles of the Agile Manifesto, such as customer collaboration, working software, responding to change, and individuals and interactions. The agile model uses various methodologies and frameworks, such as Scrum, Kanban, XP, and Lean, to implement the software development process in short and frequent iterations, called sprints. This model is suitable for projects that require high customer satisfaction and adaptability, but it requires strong communication and collaboration among the software development team and the customers and stakeholders.



### Rapid Application Development in Software Project Management

- Rapid Application Development (RAD) is an agile project management strategy popular in software development.
- The key benefit of a RAD approach is fast project turnaround, making it an attractive choice for developers working in a fast-paced environment like software development.
- The main features of RAD are:
  - Less emphasis on specific planning and more on adaptive and iterative development.
  - Use of powerful development tools and techniques, such as pre-built, reusable code libraries and frameworks.
  - Frequent prototyping and testing with continuous client feedback and approval .
  - Incremental delivery of functional modules or components.
- The main phases of RAD are:
  - Requirements planning: In this phase, the project scope, objectives, and resources are defined and agreed upon by the stakeholders.
  - User design: In this phase, the developers work closely with the clients to create and refine prototypes of the user interface and functionality of the application .
  - Construction: In this phase, the developers use the prototypes and feedback from the previous phase to build the final application using the chosen tools and frameworks.
  - Cutover: In this phase, the application is deployed, tested, and integrated with the existing systems. Any issues or defects are resolved and the application is ready for use.
- The main advantages of RAD are:
  - Reduced development time and cost.
  - Improved quality and customer satisfaction.
  - Increased flexibility and adaptability to changing requirements.
  - Enhanced collaboration and communication among the project team and stakeholders.
- The main disadvantages of RAD are:
  - Higher risk of scope creep and feature creep due to frequent changes and additions.
  - Dependency on skilled and experienced developers and tools.
  - Difficulty in managing complex and large-scale projects.
  - Potential loss of control and oversight due to less documentation and formal processes.

### Basics of Software Estimation in SPM

Software estimation is the process of predicting the effort, cost, and duration of a software project based on the available information and assumptions. Software estimation is an essential activity in software project management (SPM) as it helps to plan, monitor, and control the project.

Some of the basic concepts and techniques of software estimation are:

- **Software size**: Software size is a measure of the amount of functionality or complexity of a software product. Software size can be expressed in different units, such as lines of code (LOC), function points (FP), use cases, user stories, etc. Software size is one of the main factors that affect the effort, cost, and duration of a software project.
- **Software productivity**: Software productivity is a measure of the output or value delivered by a software team or organization per unit of input or resource. Software productivity can be expressed in different units, such as LOC per person-month, FP per person-hour, etc. Software productivity is influenced by various factors, such as the skills and experience of the team, the quality of the requirements, the tools and methods used, the complexity of the project, etc.
- **Software estimation models**: Software estimation models are mathematical formulas or algorithms that relate software size and productivity to the effort, cost, and duration of a software project. Software estimation models can be classified into two types: empirical models and analytical models. Empirical models are based on historical data and statistical analysis, such as COCOMO, SLIM, etc. Analytical models are based on theoretical assumptions and logical reasoning, such as function point analysis, use case points, etc.
- **Software estimation techniques**: Software estimation techniques are methods or procedures that help to apply software estimation models and obtain software estimates. Software estimation techniques can be classified into two types: formal techniques and informal techniques. Formal techniques are systematic and rigorous, such as parametric estimation, algorithmic estimation, etc. Informal techniques are intuitive and heuristic, such as expert judgment, analogy, etc.
- **Software estimation accuracy**: Software estimation accuracy is a measure of the closeness of the software estimates to the actual outcomes of the software project. Software estimation accuracy can be expressed in different ways, such as percentage error, mean absolute error, mean squared error, etc. Software estimation accuracy is affected by various factors, such as the quality and availability of the information, the uncertainty and variability of the project, the assumptions and biases of the estimators, etc.



### Effort and Cost Estimation Techniques in spm

- Effort and cost estimation are important activities in software project management (SPM) that help to plan, monitor and control the project resources and schedule.
- Effort estimation is the process of predicting the amount of human effort and time required to complete a software project, while cost estimation is the process of estimating the total cost of the project, including hardware, software, personnel, training, maintenance and other expenses.
- There are various techniques for effort and cost estimation, which can be classified into three categories: expert judgment, algorithmic models and analogy-based methods.
- Expert judgment is a technique that relies on the experience and intuition of experts who have worked on similar projects before. The experts provide their estimates based on their knowledge of the project scope, complexity, quality and risks. Expert judgment is a simple and quick technique, but it can be subjective, inconsistent and biased by personal factors.
- Algorithmic models are mathematical formulas that use historical data and project parameters to calculate the effort and cost of a software project. The most widely used algorithmic model is the COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model), which was developed by Barry Boehm in 1981. COCOMO estimates the effort and cost based on the size of the software (measured in lines of code or function points), the project type (organic, semi-detached or embedded) and the cost drivers (factors that affect the productivity and quality of the software, such as personnel capability, reliability, complexity, etc.). Algorithmic models are objective and consistent, but they can be inaccurate, outdated and inflexible to changing requirements and technologies.
- Analogy-based methods are techniques that use the effort and cost data of similar completed projects to estimate the effort and cost of a new project. The similarity is measured by comparing the project attributes, such as size, functionality, domain, quality, etc. Analogy-based methods are based on the principle that projects with similar characteristics will have similar effort and cost. Analogy-based methods are adaptive and realistic, but they require a large and reliable database of historical projects and a suitable similarity measure.



### COSMIC Full Function Points in spm

- COSMIC stands for Common Software Measurement International Consortium, which is an organization that develops and maintains a standard method for measuring software functional size.
- Software functional size is the amount of functionality that a software provides to its users, regardless of the technology, quality, or complexity of the software.
- COSMIC function points are a unit of measure of software functional size, based on the data movements between the software and its environment  .
- The data movements are classified into four types: Entry, Exit, Read, and Write.
  - Entry: a data movement from the software's environment to the software, initiated by a user or another software.
  - Exit: a data movement from the software to its environment, initiated by the software.
  - Read: a data movement from persistent storage to the software, initiated by the software.
  - Write: a data movement from the software to persistent storage, initiated by the software.
- Each data movement is counted as one COSMIC function point, regardless of the number or size of the data attributes involved.
- The software functional size is the sum of all the COSMIC function points of all the functional processes in the software.
- A functional process is a set of data movements that satisfies a user's or another software's functional requirement.
- COSMIC function points can be used to estimate, measure, or benchmark the software size, effort, productivity, quality, or value  .
- COSMIC function points are applicable to any type of software, from embedded systems to business applications, and from real-time to batch processing  .
- COSMIC function points are consistent and independent of the technology, language, or methodology used to build the software  .



### COCOMO II in SPM

- COCOMO II stands for Constructive Cost Model II, which is a software cost estimation model developed at the University of Southern California  .
- COCOMO II is an update of the original COCOMO model published in 1981, which was based on the data from the 1970s  .
- COCOMO II aims to address the software development practices in the 1990s and 2000s, such as rapid application development, reuse, prototyping, and object-oriented programming .
- COCOMO II consists of three sub-models: Application Composition, Early Design, and Post-Architecture .
  - Application Composition model is used for estimating the effort and schedule of projects that use rapid application development or integrated computer-aided software engineering tools .
  - Early Design model is used for estimating the effort and schedule of projects in the early stages of software development, when only the size and functionality of the system are known .
  - Post-Architecture model is used for estimating the effort and schedule of projects after the software architecture is defined and the detailed design is underway .
- COCOMO II uses the following formula to estimate the effort (in person-months) of a software project :

  Effort = A * Size<sup>B</sup> * M

  where:

  - A is a constant that depends on the sub-model and the data source .
  - Size is the estimated size of the software product in thousands of source lines of code (KSLOC) or function points (FP) .
  - B is an exponent that reflects the economy or diseconomy of scale .
  - M is a multiplier that reflects the effects of various cost drivers, such as product complexity, personnel capability, development environment, and reuse .

- COCOMO II uses the following formula to estimate the schedule (in months) of a software project :

  Schedule = C * Effort<sup>D</sup>

  where:

  - C is a constant that depends on the sub-model and the data source .
  - Effort is the estimated effort in person-months .
  - D is an exponent that reflects the effects of various schedule drivers, such as required reliability, development flexibility, and team cohesion .

- COCOMO II is a simple and well-tested model that provides about 20% cost and 70% time estimate accuracy. It can be calibrated and tailored to specific organizations and projects using historical data and expert judgment.

## Unit - 3 - Activity Planning and Risk Management

### Activity Planning

- Activity planning is the process of identifying and organizing the tasks and resources needed to complete a software project within the given constraints of time, budget and quality.
- Activity planning involves the following steps:
  - Define the project scope and objectives
  - Decompose the project into manageable activities or work packages
  - Estimate the effort, duration and cost of each activity
  - Identify the dependencies and constraints among the activities
  - Create a project schedule or plan that shows the sequence and timing of the activities
  - Allocate and assign the resources (human, material, equipment, etc.) to the activities
  - Monitor and control the progress and performance of the activities
  - Update and revise the plan as needed to accommodate changes and uncertainties

### Risk Management

- Risk management is the process of identifying, analyzing, prioritizing and mitigating the potential threats and opportunities that may affect the success of a software project.
- Risk management involves the following steps:
  - Identify the sources and types of risks that may affect the project
  - Analyze the probability and impact of each risk on the project objectives
  - Prioritize the risks based on their severity and urgency
  - Develop and implement risk response strategies to avoid, reduce, transfer or accept the risks
  - Monitor and review the risks and their status throughout the project lifecycle
  - Communicate and document the risk information and actions to the relevant stakeholders

### Forward Pass & Backward Pass Techniques in SPM

- SPM stands for Software Project Management, which is the discipline of planning, organizing, executing, and controlling software projects.
- Forward pass and backward pass are two techniques used in SPM to analyze the project network diagram and determine the project duration, critical path, and slack or float of the activities.
- A project network diagram is a graphical representation of the logical sequence and dependencies of the project activities. It consists of nodes (circles) and arrows (lines) that show the start and end of each activity and the direction of the workflow.
- Forward pass is a technique to move forward through the network diagram from the start node to the end node, calculating the early start (ES) and early finish (EF) dates for each activity. ES is the earliest possible date that an activity can start, and EF is the earliest possible date that an activity can finish.
- Backward pass is a technique to move backward through the network diagram from the end node to the start node, calculating the late start (LS) and late finish (LF) dates for each activity. LS is the latest possible date that an activity can start without delaying the project, and LF is the latest possible date that an activity can finish without delaying the project.
- The difference between EF and LF (or ES and LS) for each activity is called the slack or float, which is the amount of time that an activity can be delayed or advanced without affecting the project duration or the start of the succeeding activities.
- The critical path is the longest path in the network diagram, which determines the minimum project duration. It consists of the activities that have zero slack or float, meaning that any delay or advancement in these activities will affect the project duration or the start of the succeeding activities.
- The forward pass and backward pass techniques can be applied using the following steps:

  1. Draw the project network diagram with the activity names, durations, and dependencies.
  2. Assign a start node with ES = 0 and EF = 0, and an end node with LF = project duration and LS = project duration.
  3. Perform the forward pass by moving from left to right through the network diagram, using the following formulas:
     - ES = maximum EF of all immediate predecessors
     - EF = ES + activity duration
  4. Perform the backward pass by moving from right to left through the network diagram, using the following formulas:
     - LF = minimum LS of all immediate successors
     - LS = LF - activity duration
  5. Calculate the slack or float for each activity by using the following formulas:
     - Slack = LF - EF (or LS - ES)
  6. Identify the critical path by tracing the activities that have zero slack or float.

### Monte Carlo Simulation in SPM

Monte Carlo simulation is a technique that uses random sampling of data to estimate the possible outcomes of a project or a decision. It is a useful tool for risk management and project planning, as it helps to account for the uncertainty and variability of different factors that affect the project. Monte Carlo simulation can also help to evaluate the feasibility, profitability, and performance of a project under different scenarios.

Some of the steps involved in performing a Monte Carlo simulation are:

- Define the problem or the objective of the simulation. For example, you may want to estimate the total cost, duration, or quality of a project.
- Identify the input variables and their probability distributions. These are the factors that influence the outcome of the problem, such as costs, resources, time, quality, etc. You need to assign a probability distribution to each input variable, such as normal, uniform, triangular, etc., based on the available data or expert judgment.
- Define the output variable and the transfer equation. This is the result that you want to measure or predict, such as the project cost, duration, or quality. You also need to specify how the output variable is calculated from the input variables, using a mathematical formula or a logical relationship.
- Generate random values for the input variables and calculate the output variable. This is done by using a random number generator to produce values for each input variable, according to their probability distributions. Then, you plug these values into the transfer equation and compute the output variable. This process is repeated many times, usually thousands or millions, to create a large sample of possible outcomes.
- Analyze the results and draw conclusions. You can use statistical tools, such as histograms, frequency tables, mean, median, standard deviation, confidence intervals, etc., to summarize and visualize the distribution of the output variable. You can also compare the results with the expected or desired values, and identify the best-case, worst-case, and most likely scenarios. You can also perform sensitivity analysis to determine which input variables have the most impact on the output variable.

Monte Carlo simulation is a powerful and flexible technique that can help you to make better decisions and manage risks in project management. However, it also has some limitations and challenges, such as:

- It requires a lot of data and assumptions to define the input variables and their probability distributions. The quality and accuracy of the simulation depend on the validity and reliability of these data and assumptions.
- It can be computationally intensive and time-consuming, especially for complex problems with many input variables and iterations. You may need to use specialized software or tools to perform the simulation efficiently and effectively.
- It does not provide a single or definitive answer, but a range of possible outcomes with different probabilities. You still need to use your judgment and experience to interpret the results and make the final decision.

The trend-line rule states that if the trend line is positive and steep enough to reach the goal line by the end of the monitoring period, the progress is adequate; if the trend line is negative or flat, or not steep enough to reach the goal line by the end of the monitoring period, the progress is inadequate; and if the trend line is variable or inconsistent, the progress is uncertain.

### Earned Value Analysis in SPM

- Earned Value Analysis (EVA) is a technique used in project management for monitoring and controlling purposes. It allows the project manager to measure the amount of work actually performed on a project beyond the basic review of cost and schedule reports.
- EVA combines the measures of the project management triangle: scope, time and costs. It uses three key values to calculate the project performance and progress: Planned Value (PV), Actual Cost (AC) and Earned Value (EV).
- PV is the budgeted cost of the work scheduled to be completed by a given date. It represents the baseline or planned expenditure for the project.
- AC is the actual cost of the work performed by a given date. It represents the actual expenditure for the project.
- EV is the budgeted cost of the work actually completed by a given date. It represents the value of the work delivered by the project.
- EVA uses these values to calculate various indicators and ratios that help the project manager to assess the project performance and progress. Some of the most common indicators and ratios are:
  - Schedule Variance (SV) = EV - PV. It shows whether the project is ahead or behind schedule. A positive SV means the project is ahead of schedule, while a negative SV means the project is behind schedule.
  - Cost Variance (CV) = EV - AC. It shows whether the project is under or over budget. A positive CV means the project is under budget, while a negative CV means the project is over budget.
  - Schedule Performance Index (SPI) = EV / PV. It shows how efficiently the project is using its time. An SPI of 1 means the project is on schedule, while an SPI greater than 1 means the project is ahead of schedule, and an SPI less than 1 means the project is behind schedule.
  - Cost Performance Index (CPI) = EV / AC. It shows how efficiently the project is using its resources. A CPI of 1 means the project is on budget, while a CPI greater than 1 means the project is under budget, and a CPI less than 1 means the project is over budget.
  - Estimate at Completion (EAC) = BAC / CPI. It shows the estimated total cost of the project at completion, based on the current performance. BAC is the budget at completion, or the total planned cost of the project.
  - Estimate to Complete (ETC) = EAC - AC. It shows the estimated remaining cost of the project to complete, based on the current performance.
  - Variance at Completion (VAC) = BAC - EAC. It shows the expected difference between the planned and the actual cost of the project at completion, based on the current performance. A positive VAC means the project is expected to finish under budget, while a negative VAC means the project is expected to finish over budget.
  - To Complete Performance Index (TCPI) = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC). It shows the required performance level for the remaining work of the project to meet the budget goal. A TCPI of 1 means the project can meet the budget goal with the current performance level, while a TCPI greater than 1 means the project needs to improve its performance level, and a TCPI less than 1 means the project can afford to lower its performance level.
- EVA can be used to generate various reports and graphs that help the project manager to communicate the project status and performance to the stakeholders. Some of the most common reports and graphs are:
  - EVA table: A table that shows the PV, AC, EV and the calculated indicators and ratios for each reporting period and cumulatively.
  - EVA chart: A chart that plots the PV, AC and EV curves over time, and shows the SV and CV at each reporting period.
  - S-curve: A chart that plots the cumulative PV, AC and EV curves over time, and shows the cumulative SV and CV at each reporting period.
  - SPI and CPI chart: A chart that plots the SPI and CPI values over time, and shows the trend and the deviation from the target value of 1.
  - EAC and ETC chart: A chart that plots the EAC and ETC values over time, and shows the forecast and the deviation from the BAC and the AC.
  - VAC and TCPI chart: A chart that plots the VAC and TCPI values over time,



### Prioritizing Monitoring in SPM

Prioritizing monitoring is the process of organizing the order in which tasks and activities need to be monitored in a software project. This helps to ensure that the most important tasks and activities get the most attention and that resources and efforts are being used efficiently.

Some of the steps involved for prioritizing monitoring are:

- Collection – you must collect and gather all the data about your projects, such as scope, schedule, budget, quality, risks, and stakeholders.
- Ranking – you must develop and use a ranking model that includes criteria for prioritizing, such as urgency, impact, complexity, dependency, and alignment with strategic goals. You can use different methods for ranking, such as scoring, ranking, or categorizing.
- Verification – you must approve the ranked projects and communicate the results to the relevant stakeholders. You must also review and update the ranking periodically to reflect any changes in the project environment or requirements.

Some of the benefits of prioritizing monitoring are:

- It helps to focus on the most critical tasks and activities that affect the project success.
- It helps to avoid wasting time and resources on low-priority tasks and activities that have little or no impact on the project outcome.
- It helps to manage the expectations of the stakeholders and ensure their satisfaction and support.
- It helps to identify and mitigate potential risks and issues that may arise during the project execution.

Some of the challenges of prioritizing monitoring are:

- It may be difficult to collect and analyze all the relevant data for each project, especially if there are many projects or if the data is incomplete or inconsistent.
- It may be subjective and biased to rank the projects based on different criteria and perspectives, especially if there are conflicting interests or opinions among the stakeholders.
- It may be dynamic and uncertain to verify and update the ranking, especially if there are frequent changes or uncertainties in the project environment or requirements.

Some of the best practices for prioritizing monitoring are:

- Define clear and measurable criteria for prioritizing and use a consistent and transparent method for ranking.
- Involve and consult the key stakeholders and experts in the prioritizing process and ensure their agreement and buy-in.
- Document and communicate the prioritizing results and rationale and solicit feedback and suggestions for improvement.
- Monitor and review the prioritizing results and adjust them as needed based on the project performance and feedback.

### Change Control in SPM

- Change control is a process of managing and documenting any changes that occur during the life cycle of a software project (SPM).
- Change control aims to ensure that the changes are aligned with the project objectives, scope, quality, and schedule, and that they do not introduce any risks or errors to the software product or the project environment.
- Change control involves the following steps:
  - Propose change: The change requestor identifies the need for a change and submits a change request form that describes the change, its rationale, its impact, and its priority.
  - Impact summary: The change manager or a change control board reviews the change request and evaluates its feasibility, benefits, costs, risks, and dependencies. The change manager or the board also determines the approval authority and the stakeholders who need to be involved or informed of the change.
  - Making a decision: The change manager or the board approves, rejects, or defers the change request based on the impact summary and the project constraints. The change requestor and the stakeholders are notified of the decision and the reasons behind it.
  - Make the change: If the change request is approved, the change manager or the board assigns the change to the responsible team or individual, who implements the change according to the agreed plan and timeline. The change manager or the board monitors the progress and quality of the change implementation and ensures that the change is tested and verified before it is deployed to the production environment.
  - Closure: The change manager or the board confirms that the change has been successfully implemented and meets the expected outcomes. The change manager or the board also updates the project documentation, such as the requirements, design, test cases, and user manuals, to reflect the change. The change manager or the board closes the change request and records the lessons learned from the change process.
- Change control is an essential part of software project management, as it helps to maintain the integrity, consistency, and quality of the software product and the project environment. Change control also helps to avoid scope creep, schedule delays, cost overruns, and customer dissatisfaction that may result from uncontrolled or poorly managed changes. Change control also supports the strategic portfolio management (SPM) of the organization, as it enables the planners to visualize the strategies and targets, know whether they are meeting the objectives, and make decisions with confidence. Change control also allows the planners to respond quickly when changes are needed and to align the teams, drive efficiency, and stay on strategy.

Software Configuration Management (SCM) is a process to systematically manage, organize, and control the changes in the documents, codes, and other entities during the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC). The primary goal is to increase productivity with minimal mistakes.

Some of the benefits of SCM are:

- It ensures the consistency and quality of the software products.
- It facilitates the coordination and collaboration among the team members and stakeholders.
- It enables the traceability and accountability of the software changes.
- It supports the reuse and integration of the software components.
- It reduces the risks and costs of the software development and maintenance.

The main activities of SCM are:

- **Identification**: It involves defining and naming the software items and their versions, attributes, and relationships.

- **Audit**: It involves verifying and validating the completeness and correctness of the software items and their changes.
- **Review**: It involves evaluating and approving the software items and their changes.

The SCM process planning begins at the early phases of a project. The outcome of the planning phase is the SCM plan which might be stretched or revised during the project. The SCM plan can follow a public standard like the IEEE 828 or organization specific standard .

- **Standards and guidelines**: It defines the standards and guidelines that are adhered to in the SCM activities, such as documentation, quality, security, etc.
- **Schedule and resources**: It defines the schedule and resources that are allocated for the SCM activities, such as milestones, deliverables, budget, etc.
- **Measurement and reporting**: It defines the measurement and reporting mechanisms that are used to monitor and control the SCM activities, such as metrics, indicators, reports, etc.

### Best Methods of Staff Selection in SPM

Staff selection is the process of choosing the most suitable candidates for a job from a pool of applicants. Staff selection is a crucial step in software project management (SPM) as it affects the quality, productivity, and performance of the project team. Therefore, it is important to use effective and reliable methods of staff selection that can identify the best fit for the job based on the required skills, knowledge, abilities, and personality traits.

Some of the best methods of staff selection in SPM are:

- **Assess cognitive ability**: Cognitive ability refers to the mental processes of reasoning, problem-solving, learning, and decision-making. Cognitive ability tests measure the general intelligence and aptitude of the candidates and can predict their potential to perform complex tasks and learn new skills. Cognitive ability tests can be administered online or in-person and can include verbal, numerical, abstract, or spatial reasoning questions. Cognitive ability tests are one of the most valid and reliable methods of staff selection as they can correlate with job performance across various domains and levels.

- **Evaluate learning agility**: Learning agility is the ability and willingness to learn from experience and apply that learning to new and different situations. Learning agility tests measure the candidates' adaptability, curiosity, feedback-seeking, and experimentation behaviors. Learning agility tests can be administered online or in-person and can include self-assessment, situational judgment, or simulation questions. Learning agility tests are one of the most effective methods of staff selection as they can predict the candidates' potential to cope with change, uncertainty, and complexity in the dynamic software project environment.

- **Situational judgment test (SJT)**: SJT is a type of test that presents the candidates with realistic scenarios and asks them to choose the most appropriate or effective response from a set of options. SJT measures the candidates' judgment, decision-making, and interpersonal skills in the context of the job role and the organizational culture. SJT can be administered online or in-person and can include text, audio, or video-based scenarios. SJT is one of the most practical and relevant methods of staff selection as it can assess the candidates' fit with the job requirements and the organizational values.

- **Personality test**: Personality test is a type of test that measures the candidates' personality traits, such as extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, emotional stability, and openness to experience. Personality tests can be administered online or in-person and can include self-report, observer-report, or projective questions. Personality tests are one of the most useful methods of staff selection as they can assess the candidates' compatibility with the team, the manager, and the organizational culture. Personality tests can also predict the candidates' motivation, commitment, and job satisfaction.

- **Work sample test**: Work sample test is a type of test that requires the candidates to perform a task or a project that is similar to the actual work they will do on the job. Work sample tests measure the candidates' technical skills, knowledge, and abilities in a realistic and objective way. Work sample tests can be administered online or in-person and can include coding, debugging, designing, or testing tasks or projects. Work sample tests are one of the most accurate and fair methods of staff selection as they can evaluate the candidates' actual performance and output.

- **Interview**: Interview is a type of method that involves a face-to-face or a virtual interaction between the candidates and the interviewer(s). Interview can be structured, semi-structured, or unstructured and can include behavioral, situational, technical, or competency-based questions. Interview can also be panel, group, or one-on-one and can involve multiple rounds or stages. Interview is one of the most common and essential methods of staff selection as it can assess the candidates' communication, presentation, and interpersonal skills. Interview can also provide an opportunity for the candidates and the interviewer(s) to ask questions, clarify doubts, and build rapport.

These are some of the best methods of staff selection in SPM that can help to select the most qualified, suitable, and motivated candidates for the software project team. However, it is important to note that no single method of staff selection is perfect or sufficient by itself. Therefore, it is recommended to use a combination of multiple methods of staff selection that can complement each other and provide a comprehensive and holistic evaluation of the candidates. Moreover, it is important to ensure that the methods of staff selection are valid, reliable, fair, and consistent and that they align with the job description, the job holder profile, and the organizational goals [^4^



### Motivation in SPM

- Motivation is the psychological process that influences the direction, intensity, and persistence of behavior toward a goal.
- Motivation can be intrinsic or extrinsic. Intrinsic motivation is the desire to perform a task for its own sake, because it is interesting, enjoyable, or satisfying. Extrinsic motivation is the desire to perform a task for external rewards or to avoid negative consequences.
- Motivation can be influenced by several factors, such as needs, goals, expectations, feedback, rewards, and self-efficacy.
- Needs are the physiological or psychological deficiencies that drive behavior. Some of the most influential theories of needs are Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Alderfer's ERG theory, McClelland's acquired needs theory, and Herzberg's two-factor theory.
- Goals are the desired outcomes or targets that individuals or groups pursue. Goals can affect motivation by providing direction, challenge, feedback, and commitment. Some of the most influential theories of goal setting are Locke and Latham's goal setting theory, Bandura's social cognitive theory, and Vroom's expectancy theory.
- Expectations are the beliefs about the likelihood of achieving a goal or receiving a reward. Expectations can affect motivation by influencing the effort, persistence, and performance of individuals or groups. Some of the most influential theories of expectations are Skinner's operant conditioning theory, Thorndike's law of effect, and Adams' equity theory.
- Feedback is the information about the results or outcomes of one's actions or performance. Feedback can affect motivation by enhancing learning, correcting errors, reinforcing behavior, and increasing self-efficacy. Some of the most influential theories of feedback are Bandura's social cognitive theory, Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory, and Kluger and DeNisi's feedback intervention theory.
- Rewards are the positive consequences or incentives that follow a behavior or performance. Rewards can affect motivation by increasing satisfaction, recognition, and reinforcement. Some of the most influential theories of rewards are Skinner's operant conditioning theory, Thorndike's law of effect, and Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory.
- Self-efficacy is the belief in one's ability to perform a task or achieve a goal. Self-efficacy can affect motivation by influencing the choice, effort, persistence, and performance of individuals or groups. Some of the most influential theories of self-efficacy are Bandura's social cognitive theory, Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory, and Dweck's mindset theory.



### The Oldham-Hackman Job Characteristic Model in spm

- The Oldham-Hackman Job Characteristic Model, also known as the Job Characteristics Theory or the Core Characteristics Model, is a normative approach to job enrichment created by organizational psychologists J. Richard Hackman and Greg R. Oldham  .
- The model focuses on the design of individual tasks within a job role, rather than the job as a whole, and how they affect the motivation, performance and satisfaction of employees  .
- The model proposes that there are five core job characteristics that influence three critical psychological states of employees, which in turn affect the work outcomes    .
- The five core job characteristics are:
  - Skill variety: the degree to which a job requires a variety of different activities and skills    .
  - Task identity: the degree to which a job requires completing a whole and identifiable piece of work    .
  - Task significance: the degree to which a job has a substantial impact on the lives or work of other people    .
  - Autonomy: the degree to which a job provides freedom, independence and discretion to the individual in scheduling the work and determining the procedures to be used    .
  - Feedback: the degree to which carrying out the work activities required by a job results in the individual obtaining direct and clear information about the effectiveness of his or her performance    .
- The three critical psychological states are:
  - Experienced meaningfulness of the work: the degree to which the individual experiences the job as intrinsically meaningful and worthwhile    .
  - Experienced responsibility for outcomes of the work: the degree to which the individual feels personally accountable and responsible for the results of the work he or she does    .
  - Knowledge of the actual results of the work activities: the degree to which the individual knows and understands, on a continuous basis, how effectively he or she is performing the job    .
- The work outcomes are:
  - High internal work motivation: the degree to which the individual is motivated to perform the job well for its own sake, rather than for external rewards or pressures    .
  - High-quality work performance: the degree to which the individual performs the job effectively and efficiently, meeting the standards and expectations of the organization    .

Software project management (spm) is the process of planning, organizing, leading and controlling software projects. - Honesty: Software project managers should be truthful and transparent in their communication and reporting of the project status, progress, issues, risks and outcomes. They should also comply with the relevant laws and policies regarding data protection and intellectual property rights.

They should also monitor and evaluate the performance and feedback of the project team and stakeholders and take corrective and preventive actions when necessary.

They should not engage in or support any illegal, unethical or unprofessional activities or practices that may harm or compromise the project or the stakeholders. - Integrity: Software project managers should adhere to the ethical and professional principles and standards of the software industry and the society. ### Decision Making in SPM

SPM stands for Software Product Management, which is the discipline of managing software products throughout their life cycle, from ideation to retirement. ### Organizational Structures in SPM

Organizational structures in SPM (Supplier Relationship Management) are the ways in which the organization arranges its resources and activities to manage its relationships with its suppliers. Different organizational structures have different advantages and disadvantages for SPM, depending on the context and the goals of the organization.

Some of the common organizational structures for SPM are:

- **Functional/Hierarchical**: This structure is based on the division of labor and specialization of functions, such as procurement, finance, quality, etc. Each function has its own authority and responsibility for SPM activities, and reports to a higher-level manager. This structure is simple and clear, but it may create silos and conflicts among functions, and reduce the coordination and integration of SPM across the organization.

- **Matricial**: This structure is based on the combination of functional and project-based units, where each project has a cross-functional team of experts from different functions, led by a project manager. The project manager has the authority and responsibility for SPM activities related to the project, and reports to a higher-level manager. This structure is flexible and responsive, but it may create ambiguity and confusion about the roles and responsibilities of the project and functional managers, and increase the complexity and overhead of SPM.

- **RACI**: This structure is based on the assignment of four roles for each SPM activity: Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. The Responsible role is the person who performs the activity, the Accountable role is the person who approves and oversees the activity, the Consulted role is the person who provides input and feedback for the activity, and the Informed role is the person who is notified of the outcome of the activity. This structure is useful for clarifying and communicating the expectations and accountability of each stakeholder for SPM, but it may require a lot of coordination and communication, and it may not reflect the actual power and influence of the stakeholders.

- **Agile**: This structure is based on the adoption of agile principles and practices for SPM, such as self-organizing teams, iterative and incremental delivery, customer collaboration, and continuous improvement. The SPM team is composed of representatives from different functions and suppliers, who work together to deliver value to the customer and the organization. The SPM team has the authority and responsibility for SPM activities, and reports to a product owner or a sponsor. This structure is adaptive and innovative, but it may challenge the traditional culture and processes of the organization, and require a high level of trust and commitment from the team and the suppliers.
